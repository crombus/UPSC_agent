**R3-Q01 — A.** The 18-minute figure is self-reported. Respondents' estimates may be biased, and without timed data the saving cannot be verified. This is the most serious weakness.

- **B. The report gives the corridor's ridership rise as 22 per cent, which is too high to be accurate.** — Unsupported: the passage gives no reason to doubt the ridership figure.
- **C. The bus lane was built in the same year as a metro line, so no comparison is possible at all.** — Out of passage: the passage does not mention a metro line.
- **D. Corridor traffic fell by 5 per cent, which shows that car journeys were not affected by the lane.** — Contradicted: a 5 per cent fall in total traffic is consistent with some car journeys being replaced, so it does not show that car journeys were unaffected.
- **Trap:** Choosing a plausible but unsupported criticism of a figure the passage reports accurately.
- **Shortcut:** Ask how the key number was measured; a self-estimate is the weakest link.
- **Time target:** 75 seconds

**R3-Q02 — A.** The two trends fit a switching story, and the passage notes that the report does not verify it. The option keeps that hedge.

- **B. The 18-minute saving applies to every rider on the corridor, including the 150 who always used buses.** — Contradicted: the 18-minute saving is reported for riders who switched from cars, not for all riders.
- **C. Total traffic on the corridor fell by more than ridership rose over the same year.** — Distortion: traffic fell by 5 per cent and ridership rose by 22 per cent, so the rise in ridership was larger.
- **D. Bus journeys on the corridor are now faster than car journeys for every rider.** — Over-generalisation: the passage reports an average for switchers, not a universal result for every rider.
- **Trap:** Reading a consistent pattern as proof, or applying a subgroup average to everyone.
- **Shortcut:** Keep the hedge and the subgroup; match the numbers to the stated trends.
- **Time target:** 75 seconds
