# CSAT 08 — Revision 1: worked answers (original items)

Each entry gives the key, the reason it is right, a short review of every option, the examiner trap, a shortcut and a time target. Every numerical or logical key was checked by computation in `AUDIT.md`; reading-based RC/DM keys were checked against the passage text.

**R1-Q01 — D.** The passage reports that the most active members had typically been well connected before joining, in evaluations from two cities. The statement keeps the hedge 'typically' and the evaluation scope, so it goes no further than the text.

- **A. Time banks have been abandoned in most of the cities that have tried them, according to the passage.** — Over-generalisation: the passage reports no count of abandoned schemes. It says only that goodwill 'tends to fade'.
- **B. Time banks have been shown to raise members' cash incomes by reducing their unemployment.** — Out-of-passage and contradicted: the schemes pay nothing in cash, so the passage gives no income effect at all.
- **C. Time banks work only in cities where volunteer goodwill has been sustained for many years.** — Absolute 'only' turns the passage's durability worry into a sufficiency claim the author never makes.
- **Trap:** Reading 'tends to fade' as proof of failure, or reading 'typically' as 'always'.
- **Shortcut:** Locate the sentence with a hedge ('typically', 'two cities') and match it; reject absolutes such as 'only' and 'most cities'.
- **Time target:** 70 seconds

**R1-Q02 — D.** The passage gives the supporters' case, the critics' case (scarce skills, durability) and evaluation evidence, without endorsing either side outright. That is a balanced stance.

- **A. Enthusiastic: it presents the scheme as a complete replacement for paid public services.** — Distortion: the author never endorses replacing paid services. The supporters say the scheme does not compete with paid work, which is a different claim.
- **B. Dismissive: it treats the scheme as a failure that no supporter's argument can rescue.** — Contrary to the text: the passage presents supporters' benefits and reports evidence without declaring the scheme a failure.
- **C. Nostalgic: it prefers an older system of purely voluntary neighbourly help without credits.** — Scope creep: the passage never mentions a pre-credit voluntary system or expresses a preference for one.
- **Trap:** Mistaking the critics' paragraph for the author's own verdict. Attitude questions ask for the whole passage, not its most vivid sentence.
- **Shortcut:** Ask whether the author endorses, rejects or weighs both sides; discard any option that adds a policy the passage never mentions.
- **Time target:** 65 seconds

**R1-Q04 — A.** A divisor is a perfect square exactly when every prime exponent is even. Choices: 2-exponent 0,2,4,6,8,10 (six); 3-exponent 0,2,4 (three); 5-exponent 0,2 (two). Product 6 × 3 × 2 = 36.

- **B. 18** — Drops the prime 5 entirely (6 × 3 × 1). The 5-part still contributes two square choices (0 and 2).
- **C. 24** — Omits the square 3^4 from the 3-part (6 × 2 × 2): the choice 3-exponent 4 is lost.
- **D. 54** — Allows the 5-exponent 1, which is not even. Counts 6 × 3 × 3 = 54, i.e. all three choices for 5 instead of only the even ones.

**R1-Q05 — C.** Some lamps are rings, and every ring is a plate, so the lamps that are rings are plates: some lamps are plates. Conclusion II needs every lamp to be a plate, which the premises do not force.

- **A. Only conclusion II follows** — Reverses the logic: 'all' in the premise applies to rings, not to lamps. The universal claim about lamps is not forced.
- **B. Both conclusions follow** — Treats 'some lamps are rings' as 'all lamps are rings', which would make every lamp a plate. The premise is existential.
- **D. Neither conclusion follows** — Ignores the chain. An existential premise plus a universal premise does yield an existential conclusion.
- **Trap:** Reading 'some' as 'all' so that the universal conclusion appears to follow.
- **Shortcut:** Build a Venn sketch: one overlap of lamps and rings, then shade rings entirely inside plates; test each conclusion against that sketch.
- **Time target:** 70 seconds

**R1-Q36 — D.** Alligation: P : Q = (40 − 28) : (28 − 20) = 12 : 8 = 3 : 2. Of 25 litres, P is 15 litres. Check: 0.2 × 15 + 0.4 × 10 = 7 = 0.28 × 25.

- **A. 10 litres** — Reverses the ratio (2 : 3 instead of 3 : 2), giving P = 10 litres.
- **B. 12.5 litres** — Assumes equal parts of P and Q, which would be correct only for a 30 per cent target.
- **C. 20 litres** — Treats 80 per cent of the total as P, which matches neither mixture's strength.
- **Trap:** Inverting the alligation ratio so that the weight of the stronger mixture is applied to the wrong difference.
- **Shortcut:** The quantity of P is in the ratio of (Q's strength − target) to (target − P's strength).
- **Time target:** 85 seconds

**R1-Q37 — B.** Shifts from positions (times) to interests (why each time matters). This is the first step of principled negotiation and often reveals a workable option.

- **A. Give the room to the team with more senior members, and inform the other team afterwards.** — Settles by seniority, which ignores the needs of the team that loses and bypasses consultation.
- **C. Book the room at noon without consulting either team, so that neither side can object.** — Unilateral imposition with no consultation. It guarantees objection and damages cooperation.
- **D. Postpone the decision until both teams drop their demands, however long that takes.** — Waiting for both sides to give up their demands produces deadlock, not agreement.
- **Trap:** Treating a stated position as the real interest and bargaining over time slots only.
- **Shortcut:** Ask 'why does this time matter?' before proposing any slot.
- **Time target:** 70 seconds

**R1-Q38 — B.** Neutral and single-barrelled: it asks for direction and magnitude of change without assuming the outcome.

- **A. Don't you agree that the new bus route has improved your daily commute?** — Leading: 'Don't you agree' presupposes improvement.
- **C. Is the new bus route faster and cheaper than the old one, and by how much?** — Double-barrelled: asks about speed and cost together, so one answer cannot cover both.
- **D. Would you not say that the new route has been a clear success for commuters?** — Leading: 'would you not say ... a clear success' pushes the respondent to the desired conclusion.
- **Trap:** Choosing the most positive or most detailed question rather than the one that is neutral and single-barrelled.
- **Shortcut:** Check for a presupposed conclusion and for two questions joined by 'and'.
- **Time target:** 65 seconds

**R1-Q39 — D.** |2x − 5| ≤ 7 gives −1 ≤ x ≤ 6. Of the integers −1 to 6, x² > 4 keeps only 3, 4, 5, 6: four integers.

- **A. 8** — Ignores the second condition and counts the whole range −1 to 6.
- **B. 5** — Uses x² ≥ 4 instead of x² > 4, which adds x = 2.
- **C. 3** — Treats the bounds of the first inequality as strict, dropping x = 6.
- **Trap:** Losing an endpoint when an inequality is non-strict, or ignoring the second condition.
- **Shortcut:** Solve each condition separately, list the integers in the first range, then filter.
- **Time target:** 75 seconds

**R1-Q40 — C.** Coordinates: start (0,0); north 5 gives (0,5); right turn is east, 3 km gives (3,5); left turn is north, 5 km gives (3,10). Distance √(3² + 10²) = √109 km.

- **A. 8 km, the sum of the first two legs** — Adds the lengths of the legs (5 + 3), which is the path length, not the straight-line distance.
- **B. √34 km, after the first two legs only** — Omits the final leg: √(3² + 5²) = √34 km.
- **D. 10 km, ignoring the sideways leg** — Ignores the sideways leg and takes only the northward total of 10 km.
- **Trap:** Reporting the distance walked, or dropping the leg that changes direction.
- **Shortcut:** Turn each leg into coordinates, then apply Pythagoras to the net displacement.
- **Time target:** 85 seconds
