### Passage I

In an imagined ferry service, no ticket is issued without a recorded payment. Payment does not guarantee a ticket: once the last seat is booked, a payment is returned. A traveller objects that a payment receipt surely must amount to a reservation. The clerk points to the separate seat ledger: the receipt confirms that money was recorded, while the ticket is issued only when a seat can be assigned.

A notice adds that a traveller whose payment is returned may seek another departure, but it makes no claim about the availability of seats on that departure. The clerk cannot promise the next departure will have room. Nor does the service treat a returned payment as proof that the traveller never tried to book.

**R3-Q01.** Which conclusion follows from the passage?

A. Recorded payment establishes a place on the requested departure.
B. A returned payment indicates another departure has space.
C. A ticket cannot be issued if no payment is recorded.
D. A payment receipt confirms a provisional reservation of a seat.

**R3-Q02.** Which statement is **contradicted** by the passage?

A. A payment may be returned when the last seat is booked.
B. A ticket can be issued despite no recorded payment.
C. Another departure may be sought after a returned payment.
D. Payment alone does not establish that a ticket is issued.

### Passage III

An imagined pottery workshop compared two ways of drying clay. Faster air movement shortened drying time, but pieces placed near an open window cracked more often. An apprentice proposed moving all pieces far from the window; another wanted to ignore cracks as the price of working quickly. Neither had inspected where the cracked pieces had stood. The instructor pointed out that the useful question was not simply whether a window was open, but how pieces were placed and checked while they dried.

She did not ban moving air or declare that slow drying would prevent every crack. She asked learners to adjust placement and monitor the pieces during the next trial. Speed was useful only when it did not spoil the work it was supposed to prepare; a rule about every piece, she said, would require more than this comparison.

**R3-Q05.** What is the principal message?

A. Faster drying is generally better preparation than monitored placement.
B. Drying time matters, but placement and damage must be considered.
C. Cracking depends on placement rather than on drying conditions.
D. Moving air is useful only if every piece is far from a window.

**R3-Q06.** The instructor's advice assumes that:

A. learners already know exactly which pieces will crack.
B. the workshop can remove moving air from every room.
C. placement or monitoring can help manage the cracking risk.
D. drying speed has no effect on how work is prepared.

### Passage IV

### Passage VI

At a fictional government school, a teacher placed a short explanation beside each student's drawing in a hallway exhibition. Some visitors who had previously passed the drawings without pausing now read the descriptions. A student worried that the explanations would dictate what everyone saw. The teacher replied that the descriptions should record the maker's intention, not close other readings; she offered to put blank cards nearby for visitors' responses.

One visitor wrote about a colour that the maker had not discussed; another asked a question instead of offering an interpretation. The teacher did not say either response corrected the maker, and no one claimed that every visitor now read every drawing. The school kept both the descriptions and the blank cards. The teacher later refused a suggestion that the visitor cards should be graded for matching the maker's intention: that would turn a point of entry into a standard that allowed only one way of looking.

**R3-Q11.** Which detail is explicitly stated?

A. The school removed every maker's description after the student's objection.
B. The teacher required visitors to agree with the maker on every drawing.
C. The student proposed grading visitors on their written responses.
D. Blank cards were offered so visitors could record their own responses.

**R3-Q12.** Which idea best encompasses the whole passage?

A. Visitors' responses should be graded for accuracy against makers' intentions.
B. Explanations beside drawings can invite attention without closing alternative interpretations.
C. Makers' descriptions are unnecessary when drawings are displayed publicly.
D. Student drawings must be removed when visitors disagree about their meaning.

### Passage VII

An imagined ward planted trees along a busy lane to reduce midday heat. Trees grew in two stretches, but the stretch beside a wall remained hot because the wall held warmth late into the afternoon. A councillor argued that the planting was therefore pointless. The surveyor disagreed: shade helped some walkers, yet wall materials and walking times also mattered. She proposed measuring conditions at several hours before deciding where more shade would be useful. Neither claimed that changing the wall alone would cool every part of the lane.

**R3-Q13.** Which practical proposal best follows from the surveyor's reasoning?

B. Record wall-side heat but give less weight to the shade already observed.

**R3-Q14.** Which inference about the councillor's argument is best supported?

C. The councillor uses local heat to dismiss planting's wider benefits.
D. The councillor proposes changing walking hours instead of planting.

**R3-Q15.** With little time left, a reader is choosing between “planting has no value” and “planting may help some walkers, though the wall also matters.” Which sentence is the decisive discriminator?

A. The ward planted trees along a busy lane.
B. Trees grew in two stretches of the lane.
C. The wall held warmth into the late afternoon.

### Passage VIII

In an imagined township, a committee invited residents to comment on a proposed relocation of a weekly gathering. It published a short record of each submission. The record used a separate column for people who supported relocation, people who opposed it and people who asked for further information. A committee member suggested counting the last group as supporters: asking a question, she said, showed interest in the new site. Another member observed that interest in a proposal was not agreement with it; some questions asked whether older residents would still be able to attend. The committee retained the separate column.

The committee also received two letters endorsing relocation, both of which asked that a sheltered waiting area be provided first. The chair proposed publishing the letters simply as “support”. A resident objected that such a label would preserve the direction of the letters but lose their condition. The chair conceded that neither of the writers had approved an *unconditional* move. Before a decision, the committee resolved to publish both the number of submissions in each category and the conditions attached to them. It did not resolve to give every submission equal weight or to let a tally automatically decide the final location. Another member proposed visiting older residents, saying this could add voices to the record before the decision. The committee's concern was to report what was said without silently promoting questions or qualified support into agreement.

**R3-Q17.** Which description most faithfully captures the committee's final reporting approach?

A. Keep the inquiries separate but list the conditional letters as support without details.
B. Preserve the conditions but count information requests as support in the tally.
C. Keep only the final tally, since reasons cannot affect a counted position.
D. Preserve each submission's stated stance while clearly distinguishing inquiries and conditional endorsements.

**R3-Q18.** What most closely follows about the two letters?

A. Calling them “support” without qualification would omit their stated waiting-area condition.
B. Calling them “support” would prove the waiting area was already provided.
C. Calling them “opposition” would make their concerns disappear from the tally.
D. Calling them “questions” would establish that their writers preferred no move.

**R3-Q19.** The argument for visiting older residents before deciding the location rests on which unstated premise?

A. Relevant older residents' views may be absent from submissions.
B. Visits can establish which site all residents consider preferable.
C. Written submissions are less reliable than in-person comments.
D. Residents seeking information share the older residents' concerns.

### Passage IX

A fictional school asked children to count moths near two lights on its grounds. On the first evening, more moths were counted near the brighter light. On the next evening, the children arrived late at the second light; by then the first group had been watching for a longer period. The teacher declined to combine both evenings into a claim that brightness alone explained the counts. She did not say brightness was irrelevant. Instead she asked that both lights be observed for the same duration and at the same hours before another comparison was made.

One student wanted to omit the first evening because the second was unevenly observed. The teacher pointed out that a flaw in one evening's procedure did not erase the separate observation made on the first. Another student proposed reporting the total counts without mentioning the different observation periods. The teacher said that would make a comparison appear more settled than it was. Her report kept the counts, distinguished the evenings and named the limitation. It offered no account of moth behaviour outside these two evenings, and it made no recommendation about what sort of lighting the school should install.

**R3-Q20.** Which statement is **inconsistent** with the teacher's report?

A. The first evening produced different counts at the two lights.
B. Both evenings used equal observation periods at the two lights.
C. The second evening involved a later arrival at one of the lights.
D. The teacher retained the first evening's observation in the report.

**R3-Q21.** Which inference is best supported?

A. Brightness is irrelevant to comparing either evening's readings.
B. Equal observation periods would settle whether brightness explains the counts.
C. The first evening's counts show brightness chiefly drove the difference.
D. Pooling the two evenings without the duration difference would mislead.

**R3-Q22.** The teacher's attitude to the recorded counts is best described as:

A. dismissive of any observation made before a perfect experiment.
B. confident that brightness alone explains both evenings' results.
C. receptive to observations but cautious about comparison across them.
D. indifferent to how the duration of a count alters its interpretation.

### Passage X: two fictional notes on a shared cooking arrangement

**Passage 1.** A group tried preparing evening food together on certain days. Some members spent less time at the stove than when each cooked separately. One participant argued that this necessarily gave them more leisure. Another replied that a shorter cooking task might free time for rest, but a member might instead be assigned other duties. The group had not recorded how the freed time was used. It agreed to ask members about that before describing the arrangement as increasing leisure; it did not deny that cooking time itself had fallen for some members.

**Passage 2.** Organisers of another shared kitchen could plan menus only when ingredients arrived on the agreed day. On several days the ingredients arrived on time, but the cooking team could not begin because the cooking space was occupied. The organisers said reliable deliveries were necessary for their plan, yet not enough by themselves to make the plan work. They proposed coordinating the kitchen's use as well as the supplies, without claiming that any particular menu was better than an individual meal.

**R3-Q23.** Which inference is supported by **Passage 1 alone**, without using Passage 2's kitchen schedule?

A. Some of the saved time was recorded as rest after cooking.
B. Other assigned duties were observed taking up the saved time.
C. Members must be asked before claiming saved cooking time increased leisure.
D. The group concluded that cooking together increased overall free time.

**R3-Q24.** Consider the following statements:

1. Passage 1 reports less stove time for some members but does not establish more leisure for them.
2. Passage 2 treats timely ingredients as necessary but insufficient for cooking to begin.
3. Both notes distinguish an observed or required input from a wider desired result.

Which of the statements is/are supported?

A. 1 and 3 only
B. 1 and 2 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**R3-Q25.** Under a time ceiling, which short expression distinguishes “timely deliveries matter” from “timely deliveries guarantee cooking can begin,” as Passage 2 uses the claims?

A. The word “menus” in the organisers' description of planning.
B. The word “agreed” in the description of delivery day.
C. The word “several” in the description of timely arrivals.
D. The word “necessary” paired with the phrase “not enough.”
