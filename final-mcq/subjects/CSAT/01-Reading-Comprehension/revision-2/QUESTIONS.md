# csat-01 — Revision 2: Reading Comprehension

All passages and questions are original. Use only what the text licenses, including when you might know more about the subject. Read the stem first; paired questions share a passage.

### Passage I

An imagined mandi issues a pass to small growers unless their registration is incomplete. A valid registration makes a grower eligible to be considered, but a pass also requires available unloading space. A notice explains that an eligible grower who finds no space may seek entry on a different day; it does not promise space then. The registration list records eligibility, not whether tomorrow's unloading bays are occupied.

One officer argues that the list alone can predict how many growers will be admitted tomorrow. Another replies that a list cannot predict available space. The two agree that a complete registration matters; they disagree on whether it settles daily admission. Neither says every registered grower will arrive tomorrow or that the number of bays alone will determine how many growers seek a pass.

**R2-Q01.** Which statement about a grower with incomplete registration is consistent with the passage?

A. The grower is admitted when unloading space is available.
B. The grower receives a pass if the officer expects space.
C. The grower is ineligible for a pass under the rule.
D. The grower receives a pass after the list is published.

**R2-Q02.** The second officer's objection most directly assumes that:

A. the registration list contains no eligible small growers.
B. every registered grower will use the pass on the same day.
C. unloading space is always smaller than registered demand.
D. unloading space can affect admission independently of registration.

### Passage II

A fictional museum placed brief translations next to handwritten letters donated by town residents. Visitors stayed longer at the cases, yet the curator cautioned against replacing the handwriting with printed text: the crossed-out words revealed how a writer reconsidered a thought. A visitor said the translation let her follow the writer's argument; another pointed to an erased phrase that changed how she understood its certainty.

The curator did not claim that every crossing-out was significant or that translations systematically misled visitors. She favoured translations as an invitation to look closer, not as a transparent substitute for the originals. When a volunteer suggested displaying only printed copies to spare the letters from handling, the curator asked whether access to the originals could be protected without removing them from view. The issue, she said, was not how long people stood by a case but what kind of reading the exhibit made possible.

**R2-Q03.** Which option best captures the curator's central position?

A. Longer visits alone establish that printed letters are better exhibits.
B. Translations broaden entry, while manuscript details retain their value.
C. Handwriting matters only when a visitor cannot read a translation.
D. Letters should be removed because translations hide their revisions.

**R2-Q04.** The curator's attitude toward the translations is:

A. unqualified praise for their accuracy as full replacements.
B. indifference to their effect on visitors' reading habits.
C. outright hostility to any printed aid beside a letter.
D. qualified approval of their role in approaching originals.

### Passage III: two field notes from an imagined water scheme

**Passage 1.** New taps shortened the walk for water in one hamlet. Some households nevertheless kept using the old well because the taps operated unpredictably.

**Passage 2.** Repair teams made the scheme's taps more reliable in another hamlet. Yet the long route to the tap still limited its usefulness to households with less time.

**R2-Q05.** Which mapping accurately assigns the stated constraints?

A. Irregular operation is in Passage 1; travel burden is in Passage 2.
B. Irregular operation is in Passage 2; travel burden is in Passage 1.
C. Both irregular operation and travel burden are stated in Passage 1.
D. Neither constraint is stated in either passage.

**R2-Q06.** What is the most defensible conclusion from the two passages?

A. Repairs suffice because both hamlets have shortened routes to taps.
B. Shorter routes suffice because both hamlets have reliable taps.
C. Both proximity and reliability can influence a tap's usefulness.
D. Either proximity or reliability alone determines how households use taps.

### Passage IV

Each spring a potter carried an empty bowl to the fair and set it among finished wares. “This one,” she told a curious child, “holds the space for what I have not learned.” Years later the child, now a potter, kept an unpainted bowl on her own shelf. Neither claimed that an empty bowl was a better vessel than a finished one.

**R2-Q07.** The passage's tone is best described as:

A. contemptuous of craftsmen who complete their work.
B. anxious about the dwindling demand at the fair.
C. sarcastic about the child's eventual choice of work.
D. reflective about learning while valuing accomplished work.

**R2-Q08.** What does the empty bowl signify most closely?

A. A product withheld from buyers to raise its price.
B. A reminder that a craftsperson can still learn more.
C. A vessel that outperforms every completed fair exhibit.
D. A memorial to a potter who stopped visiting the fair.

### Passage V

At a fictional hill laboratory, a recorder notes the temperature of air outside a greenhouse every hour. On one afternoon, the outside reading rose while the inside reading did not. The team therefore declined to describe outside change as a sufficient explanation for inside change; roof vents could alter the inside response. It did not conclude that outside temperature never matters.

**R2-Q09.** Consider the following statements:

1. The outside and inside readings moved differently on the afternoon described.
2. The team rejects treating an outside rise as a sufficient explanation for an inside rise.
3. Roof vents are presented as a possible modifier of the inside response.

Which of the statements is/are supported?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**R2-Q10.** Which inference is warranted?

A. Vent position determines the size of every inside change.
B. An outside rise need not produce a matching inside rise.
C. An inside rise will follow a delayed outside rise.
D. Outside air and roof vents contribute equally to inside changes.

### Passage VI

She says this reduces costly mistakes without denying that mistakes are part of learning. The manager agrees on a trial, provided the discarded pieces resemble the cloth the workers will actually handle.

**R2-Q11.** The trainer's proposal most directly assumes that:

A. skills practised on discarded fabric can transfer to regular cloth.
B. all mistakes can be removed from work by additional practice.
C. saleable cloth is cheaper to replace than discarded pieces.
D. workers already operate the machines without any mistakes.

**R2-Q12.** What is the manager's principal reason for the condition attached to the trial?

A. To remove all training before workers cut saleable cloth.
B. To replace cutting machines with fabric that is discarded.
C. To increase the number of garments sold during practice.
D. To make practice relevant to the workers' actual task.

### Passage VII

An imagined panchayat offered a long application form for a community room. A translated version helped readers understand the questions, but several applicants still arrived with missing attachments. A clerk suggested a short checklist beside each form: it would name the required attachments without hiding the form's conditions. Another clerk preferred a spoken explanation alone. The first replied that spoken help is welcome but can vary between visits; a printed checklist lets an applicant verify what was requested afterward.

One applicant had understood what the form asked but not which supporting pages needed to be brought from home. Another wanted the clerk to explain how a condition applied to a particular case; the checklist would not settle that question by itself. The two clerks therefore agreed to retain personal assistance while testing whether a stable written list reduced omitted attachments. They did not say that every incomplete application resulted from translation, and no clerk proposed waiving any required document.

**R2-Q13.** Which detail is explicitly stated?

A. Applicants were exempted from attaching documents after translation.
B. Some applications still lacked attachments despite translated questions.
C. Spoken guidance was prohibited for future room applications.
D. The checklist replaced the original application's conditions.

**R2-Q14.** Which practical message best fits the passage?

A. Remove written conditions to speed up every room application.
B. Give spoken guidance instead of any written attachment list.
C. Pair accessible wording with a stable list of requirements.
D. Rely on translation alone to avoid missing attachments.

**R2-Q15.** With little time left on Passage I, a reader has narrowed the answer to “registration alone predicts admission” and “registration alone cannot predict admission.” Which textual check resolves the tie?

A. Whether registration is mentioned in the first sentence.
B. Whether a pass also requires available unloading space.
C. Whether a registration list can be printed overnight.
D. Whether the officers disagree about registration itself.

**R2-Q16.** Which assertion is **inconsistent** with Passage VI?

A. Discarded pieces might resemble the cloth later cut for sale.
B. The trainer accepts that mistakes may accompany learning.
C. The manager rejects a trial using discarded pieces outright.
D. The trainer expects practice to reduce costly cutting errors.

### Passage VIII

At an imagined town office, applicants for housing repairs began receiving numbered tokens on arrival. A board listed the next token and a clerk recorded each applicant's time from token issue to consultation. After the change, the recorded average wait fell. The office called this “fairer access.” A visitor who had waited before the tokens were introduced agreed that the queue had become easier to follow, but asked what “fairer” meant. Previously, people who left after a long wait were not recorded; the new ledger likewise included only those who reached the clerk. A shorter recorded wait could coexist with more people giving up, and a transparent queue could coexist with an inaccessible application form.

The visitor proposed recording departures as well as consultations and asking applicants whether they could understand the form. The clerk responded that such questions would not invalidate the shorter wait among those seen. The visitor agreed: her point was not that the recorded fall was imaginary. She argued that it established something narrower than fair access for everyone trying to apply. The office decided to keep the tokens while examining the missing observations; neither participant said that a longer wait necessarily means less fairness in every respect.

**R2-Q17.** Which account of the disagreement is most accurate?

A. The visitor accepts the shorter wait but disputes whether anyone benefited.
B. The clerk treats departures as decisive, whereas the visitor considers forms alone.
C. Both accept the fall but differ over what it proves about access.
D. Both accept easier queues, but only the clerk wants departure counts examined.

**R2-Q18.** Which inference from the record is most defensible?

A. A lower recorded wait for those consulted need not describe those who left.
B. A lower recorded wait describes those who left as well as those consulted.
C. A lower recorded wait measures whether applicants understood the form.
D. A lower recorded wait includes everyone who tried to apply that day.

**R2-Q19.** The visitor's proposal to add departures to the ledger assumes that:

A. every departure is caused exclusively by an unreadable form.
B. recorded consultation times become useless once exits are tracked.
C. every departing applicant returns later to finish the process.
D. some exits can be registered without a completed consultation.

### Passage IX

In a fictional mountain village, volunteers take readings at two points on a stream each morning. At the upstream point the water rose on three mornings; on those days it rose downstream as well. A team member claimed that any upstream rise would always be followed by a downstream rise. Another pointed out that they had not observed all mornings or all weather conditions and asked the team to keep taking readings. No one proposed that the downstream gauge was faulty.

The volunteers also used a small mark beside each reading to note whether a footbridge was open. The mark was intended to help villagers plan a walk, not to forecast water levels. Once, the bridge remained closed even after both gauges fell, because its inspection was unfinished. The team issued a notice saying a low gauge reading was relevant to planning, but not sufficient to establish that the bridge could be used. One volunteer suggested removing the gauge readings from the notice altogether; the others disagreed, saying that a relevant but incomplete observation should be accompanied by its limit, not discarded.

**R2-Q20.** Consider the following statements:

1. Each observed upstream rise on the specified three mornings accompanied a downstream rise.
2. A fall at both gauges always means the footbridge is open.
3. The mark beside each reading records whether the bridge is open.

Which of the statements is/are supported?

A. 1 only
B. 1 and 3 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

**R2-Q21.** What is the team's most defensible practical message?

A. Omit gauge readings because they cannot by themselves certify bridge access.
B. Include gauge readings while stating their limits for bridge access.
C. Treat upstream rises as a reliable forecast under every future condition.
D. Replace bridge inspections with downstream readings after the water falls.

**R2-Q22.** Which assertion is **inconsistent** with the passage?

A. The observed upstream rises occurred on a limited set of mornings.
B. The bridge's opening status was noted beside the water readings.
C. The bridge could stay closed after both gauges showed a fall.
D. The downstream gauge was deemed faulty and so it was discontinued.

### Passage X: two fictional accounts of a neighbourhood garden

**Passage 1.** A committee counted visits to a community garden after removing a gate that had long been locked at lunchtime. It recorded more visits at midday. A member inferred that the gate had been the only obstacle for residents who wanted to use the garden. Another member replied that the count identified an increase in visits, not the identities or reasons of those who had stayed away before. The committee then asked whether benches and shade were available at the hours people now used the space; it did not decide that every visitor needed either.

**Passage 2.** Garden caretakers welcomed the lunchtime visitors but noted that litter near one bench had increased. They asked for a way to keep the garden usable when volunteers were absent. A resident suggested permanently locking the gate again; the caretakers rejected this as a substitute for maintaining the newly opened space. Their statement did not estimate how many visitors left litter, nor did it show whether a shaded bench would change the work required to clean the garden. They wanted to preserve access while planning for upkeep.

**R2-Q23.** Which position is supported by **Passage 2 alone**, without relying on Passage 1's visit count?

A. The caretakers seek to retain access while arranging for upkeep.
B. Midday visits rose after the committee removed the gate.
C. Closing the gate would substitute for upkeep when volunteers are absent.
D. Shade could reduce the work of clearing litter near the bench.

**R2-Q24.** What is the most defensible inference about the midday visit count?

A. It identifies the residents newly able to visit after the gate opened.
B. It measures the proportion of visitors who caused the increased litter.
C. It shows visits rose, but does not identify why each visitor came.
D. It proves every visitor finds the newly opened garden comfortable.

**R2-Q25.** Under a time ceiling, two readings remain: “caretakers prefer permanent closure” and “caretakers reject permanent closure as a substitute for upkeep.” Which clause in Passage 2 best discriminates?

A. The caretakers spoke favourably about the new lunchtime visitors.
B. More litter was noticed near a garden bench.
C. The caretakers rejected locking as a substitute for upkeep.
D. Volunteers cannot always be present in the garden.
