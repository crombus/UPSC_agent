All passages and questions are original. Answer from the passages alone; external information is irrelevant. Read each stem before its passage. For a shared passage, use the same text for both questions.

### Passage I

An imagined district warehouse began displaying the daily arrival of grain from nearby growers. Traders welcomed the figures because they could schedule vehicles; growers welcomed them because fewer trips ended in a full yard. Yet the display occasionally failed precisely when arrivals were greatest. The committee therefore recommended a backup board at the gate rather than closing the digital display. It argued that access to timely figures, not loyalty to a particular device, was the object.

**R1-Q01.** Which of the following best expresses the central idea?

A. Traders' vehicle scheduling should determine the design of warehouse displays.
B. The committee considers gate notices more accurate than digital displays.
C. Growers benefit more from arrival information than traders do.
D. Reliable access to arrival information matters more than its medium.

**R1-Q02.** The committee's recommendation rests most directly on which unstated assumption?

A. A backup board will eventually replace every digital display in the district.
B. Someone can place sufficiently timely arrival figures on the gate board.
C. Growers will cease checking arrivals before travelling to the warehouse.
D. Traders will give growers priority whenever the digital display fails.

### Passage II

In a fictional coastal village, volunteers fenced a patch of marsh against trampling. During dry months, birds returned to that patch but not to every nearby patch. Some visitors concluded that keeping feet off the ground had restored the birds' habitat. The volunteers refused to call this proof that fences alone restore marshes: tidal flow differed between patches. They had counted birds, not measured every change in the marsh.

The group proposed comparing patches with similar tidal access before recommending a fence for the whole shoreline. A volunteer observed that such a comparison might still require attention to which patches people could reach easily. Others agreed to note where visitors walked; no one suggested that every nearby patch had the same tidal conditions or that the original bird count should be discarded.

**R1-Q03.** Which inference is most defensible?

A. The differences between patches rule out any useful comparison.
B. Tidal flow, rather than reduced trampling, caused the return.
C. The observed return cannot isolate the effect of fencing.
D. The bird count shows that trampling had no effect on habitat.

**R1-Q04.** Which of the following is **inconsistent** with the passage?

A. The volunteers already attribute the entire recovery to fencing alone.
B. Tidal access could matter when interpreting the birds' return.
C. The volunteers want comparable patches before wider advice.
D. Birds were seen in the protected patch during dry months.

### Passage III

An imagined clinic stores vaccines in a cooling cabinet. Staff may send a delivery out **only if** the cabinet's indicator has remained in range; a reading in range does not, by itself, authorise delivery, because the stock record must also be checked. One worker says a green light should be enough to speed up dispatch; another replies that a cabinet reading says nothing about whether the parcels on the shelf match the scheduled stock. Neither suggests that checking the record would make an out-of-range reading harmless.

If the indicator leaves the range, the delivery is held for review. The procedure specifies no method for repairing a cabinet or predicting its future performance; it sets conditions for deciding whether a delivery may leave now.

**R1-Q05.** What does “only if” make the in-range indicator in this passage?

A. A complete authorisation for dispatching the delivery.
B. A substitute for checking the clinic's stock record.
C. A guarantee that the cabinet will remain operational.
D. A condition that any authorised dispatch must satisfy.

**R1-Q06.** Consider the following statements:

1. An out-of-range reading prevents immediate dispatch under the procedure.
2. The stock record must be checked before a delivery may leave.
3. The procedure does not specify a method for repairing a failed cabinet.

Which of the statements is/are supported?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 1 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

### Passage IV

When the last bus left the small hill-town library, the caretaker still kept one lamp lit. He did not expect another reader that evening; he wanted the windows to say that the town had not quite surrendered its habit of asking questions. By morning the lamp was out, but the notebook on the desk had gained a page of pencilled doubts.

**R1-Q07.** The author's attitude to the caretaker's action is best described as:

A. quietly appreciative of the caretaker's gesture and its meaning.
B. sceptical that a small gesture can sustain a town's reading habit.
C. detached and merely reporting the library's closing routine.
D. ironic about a town that has entirely abandoned its questions.

**R1-Q08.** In context, “had not quite surrendered” most nearly suggests that the town:

A. had agreed to hand the library to another authority.
B. had lost all interest in learning after the last bus.
C. retained a fragile practice of questioning and reading.
D. maintained an uninterrupted crowd of nightly readers.

### Passage VI

At a fictional field station, a sensor flags unusually dry soil at one depth. Such a flag can prompt a closer inspection, but the station never treats a single flag as a measurement of water across the entire field. When two readings disagree, staff inspect both locations rather than discard the smaller reading. Their procedure is intended to identify variation before deciding where to irrigate.

**R1-Q11.** Which of the following is stated in the passage?

A. Staff inspect both places when their sensor readings differ.
B. Staff irrigate every part of the field after a dry-soil flag.
C. A single sensor records water conditions across the field.

**R1-Q12.** Which inference follows most closely?

B. Irrigation should follow as soon as both places are inspected.

### Passage VII

An imagined town's archives placed summaries beside old council minutes. Visitors could still consult the full minutes; the summaries were meant to help them find relevant pages, not to stand in for those pages. A reviewer welcomed the easier search but warned that a summary might omit a disputed qualification. Even a summary that directed a reader to the right meeting could compress an unresolved proposal into a sentence that sounded like a final decision.

The archivist replied that a visitor with little time needed a way to locate the meeting before studying it. The reviewer agreed and asked that the summaries remain. Her objection was not to summarising, but to treating a finding aid as the final account of what the council did. She asked readers to check the original minute before citing a council decision; she made no claim that every summary was inaccurate.

**R1-Q13.** What is the reviewer's principal purpose?

A. To end the public display of old council minutes.
B. To prove that all archive summaries contain errors.
C. To recommend searching with summaries but citing originals.
D. To demand that visitors avoid all archive summaries.

**R1-Q14.** Which statement is best supported?

A. The reviewer says summaries may replace original minutes when short.
B. The reviewer distinguishes finding a page from citing its decision.
C. The reviewer says disputed qualifications appear in every summary.
D. The reviewer treats ease of search as irrelevant to the archive.

**R1-Q15.** With the time ceiling almost reached, two choices remain: “summaries make originals unnecessary” and “summaries assist search but originals should be checked for citation.” Which phrase alone decides between them?

A. “not to stand in for those pages”.
B. “placed summaries beside old council minutes”.
C. “Visitors could still consult the full minutes”.
D. “A reviewer welcomed the easier search”.

### Passage VIII

In an imagined town, the council extended a bus service to its night market for two trial evenings. Several stallholders who remained open late reported more customers than on the previous evenings. The council's notice described this as an encouraging sign that travel was becoming easier. A reviewer objected to the notice's next sentence, which said that the later bus **caused every stallholder** to gain customers. Only stallholders present at closing time were asked; those who closed early were not counted. Moreover, a street performance had drawn visitors on both trial evenings. Neither the reviewer nor the council claimed to know how many of the reported customers would otherwise have visited.

The reviewer proposed repeating the trial on evenings with and without a street performance and asking *all* stallholders, including those who close before the last bus. A resident added that some households along the route had complained of noise. The reviewer said these complaints should be recorded separately, rather than treated as evidence that the later bus brought no customers. She was not arguing for the bus to be withdrawn: deciding whether and where to extend it, she said, required evidence on access, trade and inconvenience that was not selectively gathered.

**R1-Q16.** Which is the most faithful account of the reviewer's position?

A. The bus may ease access, but noise complaints outweigh trade evidence.
B. The trial suggests access improved for all stalls, though other causes may matter.
C. The trial is promising, but benefits and costs need less selective evidence.
D. The performance may add visits, so the bus should be withdrawn before retesting.

**R1-Q17.** Which inference is best supported concerning the claim about *every* stallholder?

A. The claim cannot be tested because all stalls closed before the last bus.
B. The interviews exclude some stallholders whom that universal claim purports to cover.
C. The claim is proved false because early-closing stalls lost all their buyers.
D. The interviews cover most stalls, even if the trial omitted a few.

**R1-Q18.** For the proposed comparison to help isolate the role of the performance, what unstated requirement is most important?

A. The performance must have attracted more visits than the bus.
B. Interviewing only late-opening stalls represents all stallholders.
C. Noise levels must be alike for customer visits to be compared.
D. Other conditions affecting visits should be sufficiently comparable.

### Passage IX

An apprentice weaver in a fictional town left a pale thread running through an otherwise dark cloth. Her employer pointed at it during inspection; the apprentice expected the piece to be discarded. Instead the employer asked her to trace where the thread had entered the loom. She could identify the place but not explain how it had escaped notice for so long. Rather than praise the pale line as a new pattern, the employer placed the cloth beside an earlier piece that had also gone wrong. In the first piece the mistake had been hidden under a border; this one remained plainly visible.

Months later, when the apprentice taught a newcomer, she showed both pieces. She said that concealing the first error had made it harder to understand, whereas the visible line had helped her locate a lapse in attention. She did not claim that every error should be displayed or that flawed cloth should be sold. The narrator closes with the apprentice folding the pieces carefully, “as though the unfinished explanation, not the fabric, required protecting.” No later account of what was done with either cloth is supplied.

**R1-Q19.** The narrator's attitude to the visible mistake is best described as:

A. pleased that a flawed piece can be sold without alteration.
B. attentive to its teaching value without celebrating the flaw.
C. doubtful that a newcomer can learn from any prior error.
D. indifferent to the difference between hiding and tracing it.

**R1-Q20.** In context, what does “unfinished explanation” principally refer to?

A. The cost of replacing the finished cloth at the next inspection.
B. The employer's refusal to inspect the earlier defective border.
C. The newcomer's lack of interest in the two woven examples.
D. The still-incomplete account of how the pale thread was missed.

**R1-Q21.** Consider the following statements:

1. The apprentice identified where the pale thread had entered the loom.
2. The apprentice demonstrated that every flaw should be put on display.
3. The passage establishes that the cloth with the pale line was sold.

Which of the statements is/are supported?

A. 1 only
B. 1 and 2 only
C. 2 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

### Passage X: two fictional accounts of a flood-warning exercise

**Passage 1.** An office tested alerts sent before water reached a riverside lane. Recipients liked knowing that a route might soon be closed, but some asked which alternative path the warning referred to. The office concluded that a prompt alert and intelligible route information were separate parts of a usable warning. Its exercise did not test whether a named path remained open after dark. It proposed adding a map, while cautioning that a map would have to be updated when a path changed.

**Passage 2.** Residents in the same exercise could identify the mapped alternative path, but several said its entrance was difficult to find in poor light. One resident preferred a guide stationed near the turning; another worried that a guide might not always be present. The residents did not call the alert useless, nor did they verify whether a printed map would solve the night-time problem. They asked the office to test directions with people who had not already walked the route, including after dark.

**R1-Q22.** Which proposition is supported by **Passage 2 alone**, rather than borrowing a claim from Passage 1?

A. Timely alerts and intelligible route directions serve separate warning needs.
B. A map should be updated when the named path changes.
C. The entrance can be hard to find in poor light.
D. A guide has been tested with first-time users after dark.

**R1-Q23.** Which assertion **contradicts** the two accounts read together?

A. The exercise established that a named path stays open after dark.
B. Some recipients wanted directions in addition to a prompt warning.
C. Residents asked that unfamiliar users test the route after dark.
D. A path shown on a map may need updating when its status changes.

**R1-Q24.** Under a time ceiling, two readings remain: “the residents rejected the warning” and “the residents questioned whether directions worked at night.” Which textual discriminator settles the choice?

A. The office tested alerts before water reached the lane.
B. The residents refused to describe the alert as useless.
C. One resident suggested posting a guide near the turning.
D. A map was proposed after recipients asked about routes.
