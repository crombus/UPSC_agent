## Source and precision rules

- Khandesh was a Faruqi gateway state, not a Bahmani successor. Burhanpur-Asirgarh linked the Malwa/Gujarat and Agra-Surat approaches to the Deccan, but gateway control did not equal plateau-wide control.
- Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda are the principal Deccani powers in the Mughal-intervention phase. Berar and Bidar appear only as required background.
- Deccani, afaqi/gharib, Habshi, Afghan, Maratha and sectarian labels describe changing service and patronage coalitions, not fixed communal armies.
- Chand Bibi is treated as a regent, defender and negotiator constrained by factional politics. The 1596 arrangement recognised Bahadur under her regency, ceded Berar and accepted Mughal suzerainty; it was neither total surrender nor unconstrained victory.
- The assigned Part II OCR calls the 1597 Berar-theatre engagement `Sonepat`; no bank identifies it with Sonipat in Haryana.
- Ahmadnagar fort fell in 1600 and Asirgarh/Khandesh in 1601, but a Nizam Shahi remnant survived. Fort occupation, dynastic legitimacy, countryside control and durable revenue are tested separately.
- Malik Ambar is identified as an Ethiopian/Habshi Nizam Shahi leader and Peshwa who used a multi-group coalition including Maratha bargis and deshmukh networks. He is not described as a Maratha king.
- `Guerrilla` is used only as a qualified shorthand for mobility, intelligence and supply-cutting alongside forts, sieges, negotiation and conventional battle.
- Later Marathi evidence supports an attributed measurement and zabti-type reconstruction under Malik Ambar, but not a uniform Todar Mal copy, a securely universal one-third rate or empire-wide implementation.
- Standard chronology places Malik Ambar's death in **1626**. The assigned Part II OCR's **1627** is retained only as an explicit source variant, never silently adopted as the sole date.
- Jahangir's 1616-21 successes and limited settlements are treated as recovery plus deliberate restraint, not either total conquest or simple military impotence.
- The pre-Shivaji Maratha bridge is bounded to service, jagir, bargi, deshmukh and claimant networks. Mature Shivaji and later Mughal-Maratha warfare remain with Topic 23.
- Daulatabad's fall in 1633 ended the ruling Nizam Shahi line but did not instantly end Shahji's claimant resistance.
- The 1636 Bijapur and Golconda ahdnamas are tested as coercive suzerainty/protection and buffer management, not provincial annexation or an equal modern treaty.
- Bijapur's indemnity, arbitration/non-interference obligations and Shahji condition are kept distinct from Golconda's khutba, tribute and protection terms.
- Aurangzeb's first (1636-44) and second (1652-57) Deccan viceroyalties are confined to provincial implementation, fiscal strain and the 1656-57 interventions. His later imperial programme is excluded.
- Mir Jumla appears only through his Golconda rupture, Karnataka/Coromandel interests and effect on the 1656 balance.
- Persian chronicles, Deccani histories, later Marathi records, European commercial reports, treaties, coins/khutba, forts and court painting are tested by genre and provenance. Symbolic recognition is never treated as proof of effective administration.
