## Precision and ownership rules

- `Foreign policy` is a qualified analytical label, not proof of a modern nation-state, ministry, resident embassy system or fixed border law.
- Qandahar is the early-modern fort and route junction; it is not Gandhara.
- Kabul was the indispensable Mughal base; Qandahar was its fortified outer support; Balkh was a more distant client/buffer problem.
- The Timurid inheritance mattered to legitimacy and prestige but did not create an uninterrupted Central Asian reconquest programme.
- Safavid Iran was both a counterweight to Uzbeks and a rival over Qandahar. Sunni-Shia labels did not mechanically determine alignment.
- Ottoman prestige, Ottoman-Safavid conflict, Red Sea/Gulf power and Portuguese pressure supplied context, but no sustained Ottoman-Mughal-Uzbek operational bloc existed.
- Embassies were temporary missions led by an `elchi` or `safir`; gifts and titles staged rank but did not prove subordination or abandonment of a claim.
- The 1572-73 and 1583 Balkh dates describe two Uzbek seizures separated by Shahrukh Mirza's recovery.
- Akbar's 1595 Qandahar acquisition was a local transfer/surrender in a changing regional balance, not a conquest of Persia.
- The 1622 Qandahar loss is kept multi-causal. Topic 20's Nur Jahan and succession politics appear only as a bounded coordination factor.
- The `Iqbalnama-i Jahangiri` figure of 3,000 and Jahangir's 300-400 servants remain distinct source claims.
- Shah Jahan's 1636 three-sided proposal is evidence of diplomatic aspiration, not proof of an operating alliance.
- Ali Mardan Khan's 1638 defection transferred Qandahar; it was not a successful Mughal siege.
- The Balkh campaign began with a rational client-buffer design, became direct occupation after Murad's coercive move, achieved real battlefield success under Aurangzeb, and remained politically-logistically unsustainable.
- The Part II figure of 50,000 horse and 10,000 foot belongs only to the 1646 campaign narrative.
- Qandahar recovery chronology is fixed: Aurangzeb in 1649 and 1652; Dara Shukoh in 1653.
- Qandahar siege failure is not reduced to generic artillery weakness; prepared fortification, engineering, Lahore supply, scorched earth, season and defender resolve interacted.
- Aurangzeb's later internal, Rajput, religious and Deccan policies remain outside this owner. Only the 1664 Iranian threat, resumed diplomacy and 1680 Ottoman embassy appear as an external-policy coda.
- Topic 18 Deccan narrative is not imported. Northwest-Deccan comparison is used only to distinguish capacity and logistics.
- Portuguese material is bounded to cartaz, shipping, pilgrimage and Ottoman maritime context. English evidence appears only through traveller/merchant source criticism. Dutch company and broader European-economic history are not imported without owner evidence.
- Mughal society/economy beyond the route and merchant-protection dimension remains with Topic 24; imperial decline and Nadir Shah remain with Topic 25.
