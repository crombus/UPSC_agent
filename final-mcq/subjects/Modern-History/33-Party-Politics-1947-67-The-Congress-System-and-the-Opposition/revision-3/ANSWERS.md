# Topic 33 Revision 3 - Answers

## Party Politics 1947-67 - The Congress System and the Opposition

### R3-001 - A

**[Original]**

- **A - correct:** Congress repeatedly formed governments, but rivals remained legal, elections remained consequential and an opposition party could win a state mandate; that is dominance within competition.
- **B - incorrect:** A single-party state excludes or subordinates rival parties; the period instead had active Socialist, Communist, conservative and regional competitors.
- **C - incorrect:** Legislatures, parties and elections were central institutions, so the system was not organised as partyless consultation.
- **D - incorrect:** Congress normally possessed commanding legislative seat majorities rather than routinely heading minority coalitions.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., *India After Independence*, OCR PDF pp. 234-237; electoral extract pp. 173-176.

**Examiner trap:** Treating long tenure as proof that legal competition and alternation potential did not exist.

### R3-002 - B

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Strict doctrine may suppress bargaining inside a party, but excluded tendencies can exit and organise separately rather than disappear.
- **B - correct:** Congress retained diverse left, centre, right and regional tendencies; ideological rigidity would have reduced that internal accommodation.
- **C - incorrect:** A narrower Congress would have given rivals more, not less, room to claim distinct programmes and constituencies.
- **D - incorrect:** First-past-the-post operates through constituency pluralities and would not vanish because one party became doctrinally rigid.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 235-237; Topic 33 Advanced owner, §§1-2.

**Examiner trap:** Assuming organisational uniformity eliminates conflict instead of relocating it outside the party.

### R3-003 - A

**[Original]**

- **A - correct:** Congress factions carried ideological, regional and leadership rivalry inside the dominant party, partly performing functions associated with inter-party competition.
- **B - incorrect:** Factions lacked separate constitutional party status and contested under the common Congress organisation.
- **C - incorrect:** Congress had formal membership and disciplinary boundaries even while tolerating informal tendencies and open disagreement.
- **D - incorrect:** Internal rivalry operated within electoral and parliamentary government, not as a substitute for those institutions.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 236-237, 243-245; Topic 33 Basic owner, §§8.4 and 8.8.

**Examiner trap:** Confusing a faction with a legally separate political party.

### R3-004 - D

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Administrative centralisation concerns allocation of governmental authority, not absorption of a rival demand and leadership.
- **B - incorrect:** Adopting a moderated demand did not transform Congress into the organisation from which the demand originated.
- **C - incorrect:** The described method preserves participation and uses concessions; suppression would exclude or prohibit the movement.
- **D - correct:** Co-option worked by absorbing programmes, cadres, social bases or movements through compromise and conciliation.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 236-237; Topic 33 Basic owner, §§8.4 and 8.6.

**Examiner trap:** Reducing co-option either to total surrender by Congress or to coercive prohibition.

### R3-005 - A

**[Original]**

- **A - correct:** The settlement preserved parliamentary responsibility: ministers answered to the elected legislature, while the party framed broad goals and managed organisation.
- **B - incorrect:** Confidential government papers were not made automatically available to non-ministerial party officers.
- **C - incorrect:** The Working Committee did not replace the Cabinet as the constitutionally responsible executive.
- **D - incorrect:** A legislative majority sustains government, but it does not remove the need for confidence and ministerial responsibility.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 237-243, especially pp. 238 and 242.

**Examiner trap:** Importing organisational supremacy into a parliamentary system where executive accountability runs through the legislature.

### R3-006 - C

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Factional influence, central and state leaders, and electoral calculations meant nominations did not mechanically follow local popularity.
- **B - incorrect:** Ticket allocation was itself a major arena of personal, regional and factional bargaining.
- **C - correct:** Aspirants valued the Congress label and therefore had incentives to bargain, remain loyal or accept accommodation within the umbrella.
- **D - incorrect:** The Election Commission conducted elections; it did not choose a party's candidates or adjudicate routine nomination rivalries.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 241-244; Topic 33 Complete Topic Package, consolidated anchor on Congress nomination control.

**Examiner trap:** Treating candidate selection as an administrative detail rather than a mechanism for containing factional competition.

### R3-007 - B

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Congress was organisationally loose and ideologically broad; disciplined cadre organisation was more characteristic of some challengers.
- **B - correct:** Congress joined nationwide village reach and a cross-class coalition, whereas rivals often combined commitment with limited regions or social constituencies.
- **C - incorrect:** Major opposition parties discussed national development, democracy, economic policy and constitutional questions even when their bases were uneven.
- **D - incorrect:** Congress, not its rivals, inherited the broadest rural network and national-movement legitimacy.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 234-237, 251-272.

**Examiner trap:** Equating cadre discipline with social and territorial comprehensiveness.

### R3-008 - A

**[Original]**

- **A - correct:** Under first-past-the-post the leading candidate wins the constituency; divided rivals can therefore permit victory below an absolute majority.
- **B - incorrect:** Votes cast for different candidates are not pooled merely because all candidates oppose Congress.
- **C - incorrect:** The system did not award compensatory seats to balance national vote shares.
- **D - incorrect:** Plurality victory did not require an automatic runoff when no candidate crossed half the vote.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., electoral OCR extract pp. 169-174; Topic 33 Basic owner, §§8.4 and 8.6.

**Examiner trap:** Reading seat share as if the electoral system proportionally translated the combined opposition vote.

### R3-009 - D

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Coordination could alter close contests, but the sources do not justify certainty about immediate national defeat.
- **B - incorrect:** Seat adjustments can coexist with deep disputes over ideology, leadership and policy.
- **C - incorrect:** Congress's organisation, legitimacy and coalition-building would still matter even with fewer opposition candidates.
- **D - correct:** Fewer competing opposition nominees could concentrate anti-Congress votes and reduce plurality losses caused by fragmentation.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 234-235; electoral extract pp. 173-175; Topic 33 Basic owner, §8.6.

**Examiner trap:** Turning a bounded electoral counterfactual into a claim of inevitable regime change.

### R3-010 - B

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Opposition criticism could persuade and expose, but it carried no constitutional veto over Cabinet policy.
- **B - correct:** Capable members used parliamentary procedure, and outside movements could reinforce similar ideological currents already present within Congress.
- **C - incorrect:** Congress controlled its own nominations; opposition parties neither selected Congress candidates nor managed its factions.
- **D - incorrect:** Governors did not automatically transform opposition demands into Union decisions.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., OCR PDF pp. 234-235; electoral extract pp. 174-177.

**Examiner trap:** Measuring influence only by the capacity to form the Union government.

### R3-011 - B

### R3-012 - D

**[Original]**

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., electoral OCR extract pp. 169-175.

### R3-013 - B

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Ideological conflict remained intense; what changed was acceptance of rules for pursuing it.
- **B - correct:** Repeated participation and acceptance of results helped establish elections as the legitimate method of changing governments.
- **C - incorrect:** Communists and communal parties remained organisationally distinct from Congress.
- **D - incorrect:** Governments were selected through electoral and legislative processes, not judicial appointment.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., electoral OCR extract pp. 175-176.

**Examiner trap:** Confusing agreement on constitutional procedure with agreement on political goals.

### R3-014 - A

**[Original]**

- **A - correct:** Symbols made candidates identifiable, registration brought citizens onto the rolls, and secrecy protected individual choice without a literacy test.
- **B - incorrect:** Collective voting by notables would deny each adult an equal and personal franchise.
- **C - incorrect:** Tax or literacy qualifications would contradict universal adult franchise.
- **D - incorrect:** Legislatures were directly elected rather than chosen by local councils.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., electoral OCR extract pp. 169-171.

**Examiner trap:** Assuming administrative difficulty should be solved by restricting citizenship rather than adapting election procedure.

### R3-015 - D

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** Social inequality constrained choice but did not erase the legal and political significance of equal voting rights.
- **B - incorrect:** Secret voting protected choice at the polling stage; it did not abolish dependence, caste or local hierarchy.
- **C - incorrect:** Influence by notables and vote banks is not evidence that landlords formally administered the election.
- **D - correct:** The vote created political equality while citizens continued to live within unequal economic and social structures.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., electoral OCR extract pp. 171-175.

**Examiner trap:** Treating either legal equality or social inequality as the whole democratic record.

### R3-016 - C

**[Original]**

- **A - incorrect:** An opposition party winning and forming a ministry was evidence that electoral competition could produce alternation at state level.
- **B - incorrect:** The ministry did constitutionally assume office; later dismissal does not cancel that fact.

- **D - incorrect:** The conflict surrounding the ministry connected state politics to wider party competition.

**Source:** Bipan Chandra et al., electoral OCR extract pp. 175-178; OCR PDF pp. 258-259.

**Examiner trap:** Allowing the achievement or the blemish to erase the other half of the episode.

### R3-017 - A

**Examiner trap:** Discussing federalism only as a division of subjects and ignoring the political treatment of an adverse state mandate.
