### R3-001 — B

✅ **Evidence:** Bipan ch. V, PDF pp.95–96, distinguishes Company dividends and servants' illicit private fortunes from its later request for government assistance. ⚠️ The treasury-versus-servant distinction is an analytical accounting inference.

- **A — incorrect:** Company dividends were distributed despite later corporate distress; a payout does not prove that its treasury could meet debts without public assistance.
- **B — correct:** Individual employees' fortunes from unequal private trade and gifts could coexist with a distressed corporate treasury that later sought a loan.
- **C — incorrect:** Employees' private gifts and trade gains were not automatically Diwani receipts accounted to the Company as corporate revenue.
- **D — incorrect:** A corporate right to collect territorial revenue did not preclude servants' separate fortunes through gifts or private trading.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Private gains by Company servants did not make the corporate treasury solvent; do not treat the two accounts as interchangeable.

### R3-002 — A

✅ **Evidence:** Bipan ch. V, PDF p.94, expressly contrasts Indian officials working under British overall control in 1765–72 with direct Company administration through its own servants from 1772.

- **A — correct:** Operational administration passed from supervised Indian officials to Company's own servants in 1772; British overall control did not first appear in that year.
- **B — incorrect:** Indian officials had previously functioned under British control, not gained overall supervision over the Company's servants in 1772.
- **C — incorrect:** London Directors remained at metropolitan headquarters; the change concerned who administered Bengal on the ground.
- **D — incorrect:** It reverses the documented shift from Indian operational officials to Company servants without explaining continuing British control.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** British overall control preceded 1772; the reform changed who executed Bengal administration on the ground.

### R3-003 — C

✅ **Evidence:** Bipan ch. V, PDF pp.96–97: 1773 Directors' ministerial correspondence reporting versus 1784 Board's urgent policy orders through a secret Directors' committee.

- **A — incorrect:** A Directors-to-ministers disclosure duty in 1773 did not itself constitute the later Board or its urgent confidential direction channel.
- **B — incorrect:** Reporting preceded 1784; that Act added a means for the Board to send urgent orders via a Directors' committee.
- **C — correct:** Reporting Indian affairs to ministers gave scrutiny in 1773, whereas the 1784 Board acquired a channel to *direct* important urgent policy.
- **D — incorrect:** The Board could direct political affairs via Directors' confidential committee; it did not replace Directors as author of *all* Company correspondence to India.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Reporting Company correspondence for ministerial scrutiny in 1773 was not yet the Board’s confidential-direction machinery of 1784.

### R3-004 — A

✅ **Evidence:** Basic §§1,4; Bipan ch. V, PDF p.96.

- **A — correct:** The 1773 arrangement vested Bengal governance and limited presidency superintendence; 1833 later supplied central lawmaking across Company territories.
- **B — incorrect:** The 1833 central legislative authority was not already inherent in the Bengal title from 1773.
- **C — incorrect:** Bengal's limited presidency supervision already arose under the 1773 arrangement, before 1833 legislation.
- **D — incorrect:** The 1833 central Governor-General in Council acquired authority to **make**, not merely review, general laws within its statutory remit.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Supervising the other presidencies in specified affairs did not give the 1773 Bengal executive general central legislative power.

### R3-005 — D

✅ **Evidence:** [1773 Act](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/mod/1773indiaact.asp), Governor-General bound by majority of those present; [1858 Act §§XXIII, XXV](https://statutes.org.uk/site/the-statutes/nineteenth-century/1858-21-22-victoria-c-106-government-of-india-act/), Secretary of State's final determination on ordinary questions with reasons recorded if opposing a majority of recorded council opinions.

- **A — incorrect:** The 1773 Bengal Governor-General was bound by the majority, but the Secretary's 1858 ordinary decision was not generally bound by his London advisers' contrary opinion.
- **B — incorrect:** A casting voice breaks equality, not a Bengal council majority; the 1858 question does not require Calcutta Court approval of a ministerial order.
- **C — incorrect:** The 1773 Bengal majority rule applies, and when a London council majority recorded objections the 1858 minister had to record reasons for acting against it.
- **D — correct:** The Bengal Governor-General could not treat a losing ordinary majority vote as a tie; on an ordinary 1858 London question the Secretary's determination was final, with recorded reasons required if he rejected a majority's recorded objections.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** A binding majority of the Bengal executive and a recorded majority of London advisers constrained different officeholders in different ways.

### R3-006 — D

✅ **Evidence:** [1774 original-period *Letters Patent*, printed pp.1–3, scan leaves 11–13](https://archive.org/details/letterspatentes00walegoog/page/n13/mode/1up) visually recites the earlier parliamentary empowering Act and expressly creates a Court of Record with **one Chief Justice and three puisne judges**; [1773 Act §XIII, period statutory OCR](https://archive.org/download/10225491bsb/10225491bsb_djvu.txt) provides the earlier Crown authority. [Bench-name overview](https://www.gktoday.in/regulating-act-and-constitutional-control/) identifies Impey, Chambers, Le Maistre and Hyde; the **named inaugural appointees**, unlike the four-place bench, have not been established by the patent's first three page images.

- **A — incorrect:** The 1773 authority is correct but there were three other judges, not two.
- **B — incorrect:** The three other judges are correctly counted, but a royal patent did not empower Parliament; the parliamentary Act empowered the Crown.
- **C — incorrect:** The bench count is correct, but Parliament empowered the **Crown**, not the Company, to issue the founding patent.
- **D — correct:** Parliament's 1773 Act empowered the Crown; the visually checked 1774 patent constitutes a bench of one Chief Justice and three other judges. Impey's first appointment is separately corroborated by the owner/book and bench overview, not by those scanned patent pages.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Preserve both documentary layers and the **Chief Justice plus three**, not three judges in total; R3-077 instead asks about the later Council's appellate-court status.

### R3-007 — C

✅ **Evidence:** [13 Geo. III c.63, §VII, original 1817 period print p.146, scan leaf 181](https://archive.org/download/10225491bsb/page/n181): eastern acquisitions and revenues vest in **Governor-General and Council** *during such time* as they remain in Company possession. This original page has been visually checked (not merely inferred from the [Horn–Ransome excerpt](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/mod/1773indiaact.asp)).

- **A — incorrect:** The clause ties the territorial vesting to Company possession, not Hastings's individual tenure.
- **B — incorrect:** The later Board's establishment does not state or replace this clause's express condition.
- **C — correct:** The statutory vesting operates while the acquisitions and revenues remain with the Company; it does not assert perpetual Company possession or direct Crown administration in 1773.
- **D — incorrect:** The clause does not make Directors' consent the condition after territorial possession ends.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** This is a condition on the statutory vesting, not a claim that later loss of possession occurred during the 1773 government or that the Act promised perpetual Company rule.

### R3-008 — B

✅ **Evidence:** [1781 Act, 21 Geo. III c.70, §VIII, original 1817 print p.205, scan leaf 240](https://archive.org/download/10225491bsb/page/n240) visibly excludes the Supreme Court from revenue matters; §X separately addresses mere Company employment; WB drill 6.

- **A — incorrect:** The revenue exclusion depends on subject matter, not solely on the officer's residence; the unrelated private claim is separately assessed.
- **B — correct:** Collection disputes are excluded by the 1781 revenue limitation, while an otherwise competent private suit against a Calcutta resident remains possible.
- **C — incorrect:** Calcutta residence does not cancel the specific statutory exclusion for collection/revenue issues.
- **D — incorrect:** The officer's employment does not automatically shield unrelated private contractual dealings from the Court's otherwise valid jurisdiction.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Revenue subject matter and a defendant’s official status are different jurisdictional tests; a separate private claim is not automatically barred.

### R3-009 — C

✅ **Evidence:** 1781 Act §§XXI–XXIII (period-volume OCR, printed pp.206–207) expressly recognises the Governor-General and Council as provincial-civil-appeal court of record and gives it regulatory authority; WB evidence bank 4.

- **A — incorrect:** The threshold includes suits worth **exactly** £5,000; it is not limited to values strictly greater.
- **B — incorrect:** The provincial appeal lies to the Governor-General and Council, not through the Calcutta Supreme Court first.
- **C — correct:** Section XXI routes provincial civil appeals through the Council and allows further Crown appeal at £5,000 **and upwards**.
- **D — incorrect:** The Council's appellate authority is qualified by a high-value further Crown appeal, not final at every value.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** The £5,000 threshold concerns further Crown review of qualifying provincial civil appeals, not the existence of the Council appeal.

### R3-010 — D

✅ **Evidence:** Bipan ch. V, PDF p.97; Basic §3 (Board membership and two Cabinet ministers).

- **A — incorrect:** The President of the Board of Trade substitutes for the Chancellor of the Exchequer, the second minister identified in the account.
- **B — incorrect:** The Lord President of the Council substitutes for the Secretary of State named among these commissioners.
- **C — incorrect:** The First Lord of the Admiralty substitutes for the Chancellor of the Exchequer.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** The ministers on the London Board were the Secretary of State and Chancellor of the Exchequer, not officers governing in India.

### R3-011 — A

✅ **Evidence:** [1784 Act 24 Geo. III sess. 2 c. 25 §XIV, Keith period reprint, printed p.100, OCR](https://archive.org/download/speechesdocument01keituoft/speechesdocument01keituoft_djvu.txt): if Directors regard the Board's direction as outside civil/military government and revenues, they **may petition His Majesty in Council**, whose subject-matter decision is final. ⚠️ The disputed instruction is hypothetical; no finding about a real commercial order is claimed.

- **A — correct:** The statute identifies His Majesty **in Council** as the final arbiter of whether the subject lies within the Board's statutory direction field.
- **B — incorrect:** The Bengal Governor-General does not decide the London Board/Directors subject-matter boundary under §XIV.
- **C — incorrect:** Company proprietors are neither the petition recipient nor the final arbiter of this dispute.
- **D — incorrect:** The Directors' secret committee transmits specified confidential orders under §§XV–XVI, but does not finally decide §XIV petitions.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Do not confuse a confidential **dispatch channel** (§XV) with the Crown-in-Council **scope appeal** (§XIV). The petition does not itself prove the Directors' objection is correct.

### R3-012 — C

✅ **Evidence:** [1773 Act](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/mod/1773indiaact.asp), majority/casting-vote mechanism; Bipan ch. V, PDF p.97, Governor-General plus three councillors from 1784.

- **A — incorrect:** The Governor-General votes as a member; before his ballot only three councillors have voted, one for and two against.
- **B — incorrect:** GG plus one ally are **two** ordinary votes, not three; they face the other two councillors.
- **C — correct:** Four voters divide 2–2; the Governor-General's casting voice then resolves equality even though only one councillor supported him.
- **D — incorrect:** The opposed councillors' votes do not automatically supplant the statutory tie-breaking mechanism.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** The Governor-General had an ordinary vote as well as a casting vote **only upon a tie** under the reduced council arrangement.

### R3-013 — D

✅ **Evidence:** [1784 Act §XXII, Keith reprint printed p.104](https://archive.org/download/speechesdocument01keituoft/speechesdocument01keituoft_djvu.txt), directly checked in OCR: Crown by sign manual countersigned by Secretary of State **or** Court of Directors by writing could remove or recall specified Company civil/military officeholders. Bipan ch. V, PDF p.97, accurately says Directors retained valuable appointment rights but does not negate this express Crown recall route.

- **A — incorrect:** Section XXII gives the Crown an **independent** power to remove or recall officers; royal action was not confined to confirming Directors' choices.
- **B — incorrect:** The Crown has a separate recall route, but Directors also retain an **independent written recall** route, not merely the power to fill a vacancy.
- **C — incorrect:** The provision does not partition removal power by **civil versus military** service; its covered officers include both kinds under either statutory route.
- **D — correct:** Crown sign-manual/countersigned writing and Directors' written instruments provide distinct removal routes for the covered officers, whether civil or military. Section XXII specifies *who can recall*; R3-043 instead tests **when the writing takes effect**.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Directors’ appointment rights do not imply a monopoly over recall: the 1784 statute also preserved a Crown route.

### R3-014 — B

✅ **Evidence:** 1793 Act 33 Geo. III c.52, preamble and §I (period-volume OCR, printed p.288); Basic §2; WB evidence bank 2.

- **A — incorrect:** The preamble preserved, rather than extinguished, competing claims while allowing continued trade and possession.
- **B — correct:** Continuing exclusive trade and territorial possession did not settle other parties' territorial claims against them.
- **C — incorrect:** It continued possession as well as exclusive trade, without resolving title by that continuation.
- **D — incorrect:** The Crown did not renounce its claim as a condition for the Company's continued possession.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Continuing Company privileges did not adjudicate away other territorial claims reserved by the 1793 preamble.

### R3-015 — C

✅ **Evidence:** Basic §§1–2; 1813 Act 53 Geo. III c.155, trading provisions; Bipan ch. V, PDF pp.94,97.

- **A — incorrect:** Opening trade to private British merchants did not restore Bengal's territorial revenue administration to the Mughal court.
- **B — incorrect:** Merchants gained access to trade; they did not individually inherit the Company's Diwani fiscal power.
- **C — correct:** The 1813 commercial opening changed who could trade in ordinary Indian goods, not who governed and collected territorial revenue.
- **D — incorrect:** Revenue authority existed before 1813 and was not suspended pending a later charter.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Opening trade to private merchants in 1813 did not surrender the Company’s territorial revenue administration.

### R3-016 — B

✅ **Evidence:** [1813 Act §XCV, original 1817 print p.1155, scan leaf 1364](https://archive.org/download/10225491bsb/page/n1364) expressly saves Crown sovereignty (not a direct Crown takeover); Basic §§3–5; [1858 Act preamble and §§I–II](https://statutes.org.uk/site/the-statutes/nineteenth-century/1858-21-22-victoria-c-106-government-of-india-act/) recalls earlier government *in trust for Her Majesty* before transferring governing powers.

- **A — incorrect:** The 1813 declaration did not assign sovereign title to the Company; 1833's trust concerned territorial government, not a Crown trading trust.
- **B — correct:** Crown sovereignty could be declared in 1813 while Company government continued in trust for the Crown under the later charters; the 1858 Act then vested that government in the Crown.
- **C — incorrect:** Crown ministers did not take over direct government in 1813; the Company governed as intermediary before 1858.
- **D — incorrect:** It reverses both early sovereign title and the 1833/1858 division of actual government.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Recognition of Crown sovereignty and Company government in trust preceded, rather than themselves accomplished, the 1858 transfer.

### R3-017 — B

✅ **Evidence:** 1813 Act 53 Geo. III c.155, education appropriation; Basic §2.

- **A — incorrect:** The charter retained the tea and China exceptions while providing an annual education allocation.
- **B — correct:** The educational provision coexisted with the Company's continuing tea and China trading privileges; neither depended on abolishing the other.
- **C — incorrect:** Company administration continued after 1813; direct Crown government was not a prerequisite for the education provision.
- **D — incorrect:** The provision established an annual amount for literature and learning, not a single compensation payment for trade changes.

### R3-018 — C

✅ **Evidence:** Basic §2; Bipan ch. V, PDF p.97.

- **A — incorrect:** The 1813 measure opened ordinary India trade but retained tea/China; it was not full commercial exit, and tea was not restored by 1833.
- **B — incorrect:** The 1813 charter did not install direct Crown administration; the 1858 Act made that transfer.

- **D — incorrect:** Commercial wind-up in 1833 was not transfer of Indian government to the Crown; that came in 1858.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** A Company’s trading exit in 1833 is not the same legal event as its loss of governmental authority in 1858.

### R3-019 — C

✅ **Evidence:** 1773 Act, 1833 Act §§39–41 and 1858 Proclamation's express appointment of Canning as Viceroy (primary reproductions linked above).

- **A — incorrect:** The 1833 enactment provided the all-India Governor-General title; it did not designate a Viceroy or use the 1858 Proclamation to create that office.
- **B — incorrect:** The 1858 Act transferred government; the separate Proclamation expressly called Canning Viceroy.
- **C — correct:** The 1833 Act provided Governor-General of India; the 1858 Proclamation styled Canning its first Viceroy and Governor-General.

### R3-020 — C

✅ **Evidence:** [1833 Act 3 & 4 Will. IV c.85 §XLIII, Keith reproduction](https://indiaofthepast.org/british-parliament/major-events-pre-1950/government-india-act-1833): Governor-General **in Council** could make laws “for all servants of the said Company within the dominions of princes and states in alliance with the said Company.” ⚠️ The posting is an invented application; whether any particular enacted law applies depends on its actual terms.

- **A — incorrect:** The clause extends to **Company servants** in allied dominions, not to every person there.
- **B — incorrect:** Its test is service under the Company, not being a prince's subject; it also covers Company servants who are not princely subjects.
- **C — correct:** Section XLIII expressly includes Company servants in allied princely dominions in the potential reach of central laws.
- **D — incorrect:** The section specifies this limited reach beyond Company-administered territory, so an absolute territorial bar fails.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Limited extra-territorial reach over **Company servants** does not license legislation for all inhabitants of a princely state; R1-020 separately tests who has central lawmaking authority.

### R3-021 — D

✅ **Evidence:** 1833 Act §40 (law-making-only participation) and 1853 Act §XXI (express repeal in period-volume OCR, printed p.621); Basic §4.

### R3-023 — D

✅ **Evidence:** [1833 Act §LXXXVII](https://indiaofthepast.org/british-parliament/major-events-pre-1950/government-india-act-1833) protects eligible people against disqualification **by reason only** of religion, place of birth, descent or colour; [1853 Act §§XXXVIII–XLI, period-volume OCR pp.623–624](https://archive.org/download/india.history.resource.91520/91520_djvu.txt) retains qualified appointment through Board-regulated examinations.

- **A — incorrect:** The four identity grounds are indeed protected; §87 did **not** eliminate later examination standards.
- **B — incorrect:** Protected identity grounds cannot justify rejection alone, but that protection does not make the unqualified second applicant necessarily eligible for selection.
- **C — incorrect:** It reverses both rules: the four named identity grounds cannot alone bar office, whereas a valid qualification standard can apply.
- **D — correct:** Religion, place of birth, descent and colour cannot **alone** disqualify; a valid examination qualification still governs the second applicant.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** All **four** 1833 grounds remain affirmative in R3; neither the statute nor the 1853 exam arrangements promise an appointment without satisfying valid qualifications.

### R3-026 — A

✅ **Evidence:** [1833 Act §XLIII](https://indiaofthepast.org/british-parliament/major-events-pre-1950/government-india-act-1833) extends lawmaking across the territories to British/native/foreign persons and chartered or other courts, with enumerated constitutional limits (cf. R3-064).

- **B — incorrect:** The clause includes foreigners and chartered courts; it is not restricted to Company subjects and Company courts.
- **C — incorrect:** The statutory rule reaches foreigners generally within the territories, without making each presidency's separate consent a prerequisite.
- **D — incorrect:** General laws could reach chartered courts across the Company's territories; the scope was not restricted to the particular presidency containing a court.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** The 1833 general lawmaking remit included foreigners and chartered courts; do not infer exemptions from their legal status.

### R3-027 — A

✅ **Evidence:** [1858 Act §XV](https://statutes.org.uk/site/the-statutes/nineteenth-century/1858-21-22-victoria-c-106-government-of-india-act/): Company's home establishment and Board staff initially form the Secretary of State in Council's establishment, subject to subsequent arrangement/reduction.

- **D — incorrect:** Initial continuity did not prevent the Secretary of State from later arranging and reducing the transferred establishment under §XV.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** Home-establishment employees could continue under the new ministerial office without immunity from lawful reorganisation.

### R3-028 — D

- **B — incorrect:** The Directors' secret committee was a prior Company channel, superseded for these despatches by the new ministerial address.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** A despatch’s addressee and the signatory authorising an outgoing order are separate requirements after 1858.

### R3-029 — C

✅ **Evidence:** [Queen's Proclamation, November 1858, paragraph beginning “Firmly relying Ourselves”](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Proclamation_by_the_Queen_in_Council,_to_the_princes,_chiefs,_and_people_of_India), directly refetched: while professing Christianity it says “we disclaim alike the **right and desire to impose our convictions** on any of our subjects”; its separate princely paragraph accepts Company treaties. This question asks the *two-part disavowal*, not the London transfer mechanism of the 1858 Act or R2-049's no-favour/no-molestation directive.

- **A — incorrect:** The Proclamation does not claim a right or a desire to impose Crown convictions on subjects.
- **B — incorrect:** Its disavowal includes the **right** to impose convictions, not only the desire to do so.
- **C — correct:** The text expressly disclaims **both right and desire** to impose the Crown's convictions, notwithstanding its professed Christian belief.
- **D — incorrect:** It disclaims the **desire** as well as the right to impose convictions; acknowledging Christianity does not reserve a wish for coercion.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** A sovereign's professed religious belief in this Proclamation did **not** reserve a right or desire to impose that belief; the distinct promises against official favour and interference are separately tested elsewhere.

### R3-083 — C

✅ **Evidence:** [1858 Queen's Proclamation, inherited-lands paragraph](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Proclamation_by_the_Queen_in_Council,_to_the_princes,_chiefs,_and_people_of_India): inherited land rights are respected “subject to the equitable demands of the State”; the same paragraph separately directs **due regard** to ancient rights, usages and customs “generally, in framing and administering the law.” The drafter is hypothetical.

- **A — incorrect:** The sentence expressly covers *framing* the law too; applying existing law is only one half.
- **B — incorrect:** The sentence expressly covers *administering* the law too; drafting rules is only one half.
- **C — correct:** Due regard applies generally in **both** making and administering law; the separate equitable-State-demand qualification still limits the inherited-land promise.
- **D — incorrect:** “Generally” is broader than inherited-land disputes alone, though the instruction appears alongside that land-rights promise.

⚠️ **Examiner trap:** A paired provision's location in the land paragraph does not reduce “generally, in framing and administering the law” to land cases only.
