### 1. C

- **A:** No: Tithes were ecclesiastical income, not an unrestricted substitute for royal tax reform.
- **B:** No: Its rich and poor alike remained outside the privileged estates.
- **C:** Yes: revenue and privilege were institutionally entangled.
- **D:** No: Dues were peasant burdens, not a workable answer to privileged exemption.
- **Evidence:** B §§3, 8.4; A §§1–2. **Examiner trap:** Distinguish Church revenue, seigneurial dues and royal taxation.

### 2. D

- **A:** No: A royal fiscal summons did not itself vest national sovereignty in nobles.
- **B:** No: Voting by order risked outvoting the numerically larger Third Estate.
- **C:** No: Tax concessions would not resolve who represented the nation.
- **D:** Yes: representation and sovereignty became the central stakes.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 3–4. **Examiner trap:** Representation by estate versus by head turned a fiscal summons into a sovereignty dispute.

### 3. C

- **A:** No: Enlightenment critique did not cause harvest or food-price stress.
- **B:** No: Financial pressure did not automatically dislodge the privileged estates.
- **C:** Yes: intellectual critique interacted with material crisis.
- **D:** No: Rural burdens mattered, but deputies made a separate institutional claim.
- **Evidence:** B §§3–4, 8.4; A §1. **Examiner trap:** Ideas delegitimised privilege; bread and revenue pressures supplied different forces.

### 4. B

- **A:** No: The Third Estate declared itself an Assembly before its oath.
- **B:** Yes: declaration, commitment to constitution and crowd intervention followed.
- **C:** No: The Bastille followed both deputies' institutional steps.
- **D:** No: The Tennis Court Oath preceded the Parisian assault.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 4, 9. **Examiner trap:** For chronology retain the Assembly's June declaration before its June oath; neither option supplies dates.

### 5. C

- **A:** No: The decisive anti-feudal legislative turn followed the Bastille.
- **B:** No: The rights declaration was an Assembly act rather than the storming itself.
- **C:** Yes: this is the owner's explicit examiner trap.
- **D:** No: The Directory was a much later republican government.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 6; W Q21–24. **Examiner trap:** A popular event's symbolic force is not the same as an Assembly's enactment.

### 6. B

- **A:** No: Formal rights did not themselves establish universal suffrage.
- **B:** Yes: proclaimed rights did not erase political exclusions.
- **C:** No: The declaration was compatible with a subsequent constitutional monarchy.
- **D:** No: Colonial slavery and universal rights remained in tension.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 8.5–8.6; A §5. **Examiner trap:** Separate rights proclaimed in 1789 from the narrower 1791 electoral settlement.

### 7. C

- **A:** No: Rural grievances focused on land burdens rather than deputy allocation.
- **B:** No: The targets included seigneurial privileges, not their preservation.
- **C:** Yes: rural mobilisation widened the rupture beyond elite debate.
- **D:** No: Peasant pressure mattered, but deputies passed the decrees.
- **Evidence:** B §§3, 8.4–8.7; A §2. **Examiner trap:** Peasant mobilisation exerted pressure; it did not itself legislate.

### 8. D

- **A:** No: The Directory came after the republic and Terror.
- **B:** No: The presence of a king made the regime a constitutional monarchy.
- **C:** No: The Convention belongs to the later republican phase.
- **D:** Yes: 1791 was neither republican nor universally enfranchised.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 4, 8.5; W Q29–32. **Examiner trap:** Identify the actual regime, not an adjacent institution or a qualified later franchise.

### 9. C

- **A:** No: The constitutional monarchy survived the 1789 declaration.
- **B:** No: The oath asserted constitutional authority, not military rule.
- **C:** Yes: these pressures helped drive republican radicalisation.
- **D:** No: The Directory followed that abolition.
- **Evidence:** B §§4, 8.6; A §§1, 3. **Examiner trap:** War and distrust belong to the transition from monarchy, not to a later republican regime.

### 10. B

- **A:** No: The fiscal crisis is a background cause, not the complete Terror mechanism.
- **B:** Yes: context explains violence without excusing it.
- **C:** No: Wartime and factional pressures cannot be replaced by this single claim.
- **D:** No: Rights rhetoric did not mandate the coercive practices of Terror.
- **Evidence:** B §§8.6, 8.8; A §§1, 3. **Examiner trap:** Explaining wartime coercion is not an endorsement of it.

### 11. B

- **A:** No: The Jacobin project proposed broader male participation.
- **B:** Yes: the proposed Jacobin constitution linked political and social rights; it was not implemented amid coercive emergency rule.
- **C:** No: Its social vision explicitly linked government to livelihood.
- **D:** No: The expanded political claims still excluded women.
- **Evidence:** A §3; B §§8.5–8.6. **Examiner trap:** Constitutional aspiration is not proof of implemented social equality.

### 12. D

- **A:** No: The Directory was a retreat from the Terror's regime.
- **B:** No: It was a republican executive, not a restored monarchy.
- **C:** No: The regime's instability gave military actors more leverage.
- **D:** Yes: political fatigue and instability helped the coup.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 8.6; A §4. **Examiner trap:** Distinguish a propertied republican retreat from restoration or lasting stability.

### 13. D

- **A:** No: Republic and Directory intervened before Napoleon's empire.
- **B:** No: The Directory and then Consulate intervened.
- **C:** No: Brumaire installed a Consulate, not a Bourbon restoration.
- **D:** Yes: the 1799 coup closed the Directory phase.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 4, 9; W Q49–52. **Examiner trap:** Brumaire established the Consulate, not the imperial title acquired later.

### 14. D

- **A:** No: Codification was among his durable projects, not something abandoned.
- **B:** No: nominal representative bodies survived, but elections and political opposition were not unrestricted under imperial rule.
- **C:** No: Anti-feudal reform persisted while political liberties narrowed.
- **D:** Yes: social/legal continuity paired with authoritarian rule.
- **Evidence:** B §§5, 8.7; A §4. **Examiner trap:** Judge separately legal reform and political sovereignty.

### 15. B

- **A:** No: The Code was civil law and did not institute female suffrage.
- **B:** Yes: formal legal reform was gender-selective.
- **C:** No: Civil equality is not abolition of wealth differences or retention of estates.
- **D:** No: Anti-feudal legal change coexisted with limits on married women.
- **Evidence:** B §§5, 8.5; A §4. **Examiner trap:** Equality of male civil status does not guarantee equality in family law.

### 16. A

- **A:** Yes: rights and imperial policy diverged over time.
- **B:** No: Revolutionary abolition came earlier, even though final abolition came later.
- **C:** No: There was a revolutionary abolition and a Napoleonic reversal.
- **D:** No: Napoleon reversed rather than completed revolutionary abolition.
- **Evidence:** B §§8.5, 8.8; W Q69–72. **Examiner trap:** Do not mistake the first revolutionary abolition for final abolition.

### 17. C

- **A:** No: National suffrage was not first granted to women in 1791.
- **B:** No: The radical phase still did not grant equal female political rights.
- **C:** Yes: action and formal membership did not coincide.
- **D:** No: Women acted in revolutionary politics without securing equal franchise.
- **Evidence:** B §§2, 8.5; A §2. **Examiner trap:** Political mobilisation need not yield formal electoral inclusion.

### 18. A

- **A:** Yes: Waterloo ended the imperial phase, not the whole settlement.
- **B:** No: Defeat did not erase every codified legal change.
- **C:** No: A wholesale return to pre-1789 feudal privilege did not occur.
- **D:** No: Jacobin rule had ended before Napoleon's rise.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 5, 8.5; W Q57–60. **Examiner trap:** A change of ruler is not total reversal of social law.

### 19. C

- **A:** No: Women and poorer people faced continuing exclusions.
- **B:** No: Legal status did not determine economic distribution.
- **C:** Yes: principle and critique together explain relevance.
- **D:** No: The Code coexisted with an authoritarian regime.
- **Evidence:** B §§8.5, 8.7; A §5. **Examiner trap:** Legacy lies in claims and contestation, not immediate universal implementation.

### 20. B

- **A:** No: American independence did not directly dismantle French feudal rights.
- **B:** Yes: distinct social targets qualify shared rights language.
- **C:** No: The French conflict turned on a domestic monarchy and estates.
- **D:** No: This reverses the social-political settings of the two revolutions.
- **Evidence:** B §§8.1, 8.5; A §§5, 7. **Examiner trap:** Shared rights language does not erase different institutional targets.

### 21. A

- **A:** Yes: regicide followed the abolition of monarchy.
- **B:** No: Constitutional monarchy preceded republican regicide.
- **C:** No: The declaration belongs to the earlier 1789 constitutional phase.
- **D:** No: Regicide belongs to the earlier republican phase.
- **Evidence:** B §§1, 9. **Examiner trap:** Abolition of monarchy and execution of Louis XVI are not the same event.

### 22. D

- **A:** No: Reform challenged, rather than re-entrenched, clerical legal privilege.
- **B:** No: Coercive policy did not extinguish religion itself.
- **C:** No: One political regime is not a necessary basis of secular public law.
- **D:** Yes: public law and coercion must be distinguished.
- **Evidence:** B §§3, 8.5; A §5. **Examiner trap:** Separate secular legal authority from forced suppression of worship.
