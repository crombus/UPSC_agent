                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The early analytic movement rejects grand idealist system-building through three
distinct programmes. Moore defends ordinary certainty and separates consciousness from
its object. Russell reveals quantified logical form beneath misleading grammar and uses
contextual analysis to reduce ontological commitments. Early Wittgenstein treats
meaningful propositions as logical pictures of possible facts and draws a boundary
between what language can say and what its logical form can only show. Their common
method is analysis; their objects, arguments and conclusions must not be merged.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Moore restores confidence in ordinary knowledge but does not defeat scepticism on
neutral ground. Russell's contextual analysis permanently transforms logic and
semantics even though his sense-data foundation and final atoms remain unstable. Early
Wittgenstein offers the boldest account of propositional representation and its limits,
but its simple objects, elementary independence and truth-functional completeness are
under-specified. Analytic philosophy's durable achievement is methodological: do not
infer ontology from grammar before logical and conceptual analysis.
