                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Empiricism makes experience the source and test of ideas. Locke begins with sensation
and reflection yet retains material substance, primary qualities and a consciousness-
based self. Berkeley applies the idea principle more consistently and rejects
unperceived material substratum while retaining spirits and God. Hume applies the copy
principle most rigorously and dissolves necessary causation, substantial self and
rationally justified induction into habits of association and mitigated natural belief.
The tradition advances through increasingly consistent criticism of its own assumptions.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Empiricism disciplines metaphysics by demanding an experiential source for ideas.
Locke retains a moderate realism but creates the veil-of-perception problem; Berkeley
removes matter and preserves an ordered world through spirits and God; Hume exposes
the absence of perceived necessity and substantial unity, ending in mitigated
scepticism. The tradition's strength is critical economy, while its limit is that
experience atomised into impressions cannot by itself explain necessity, objectivity
or the unity presupposed by knowledge.
