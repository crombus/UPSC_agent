SOCIAL AND POLITICAL IDEALS: EQUALITY, LIBERTY AND JUSTICE
|
+-- CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
|   +-- Equality determines whose interests count and on what metric; liberty protects
|       agency against interference, domination and incapacity; justice specifies fair
|       institutions, procedures and distributions.
|   +-- None is self-sufficient: liberty without fair conditions entrenches power, equality
|       without freedom suppresses agency, and both require justice to order competing claims.
|

|   |   Dworkin; negative/positive/republican liberty, Berlin, Mill, Green, Hobbes, Locke,
|   |   Rousseau; Plato, Aristotle, utilitarian, Rawls, Nozick, Sen and Ambedkar on justice.
|   +-- Forms of Government owns institutional democracy.
|   +-- Individual and State owns rights, duties, accountability and political obligation.
|   +-- Political Ideologies owns full Marxism/socialism.
|   +-- Political Theory atlas owns broader conceptual genealogy; this Paper II chart
|       prioritises syllabus thinkers and applied normative comparison.
|
+-- SOURCE / ATTRIBUTION CAUTION
|   +-- Plato: Republic; Aristotle: Nicomachean Ethics and Politics.
|   +-- Rousseau: Discourse on Inequality and Social Contract.
|   +-- Mill: On Liberty, Considerations on Representative Government and related writings.
|   +-- Marx: Critique of the Gotha Programme and broader critique of capitalism.
|   +-- Berlin: Two Concepts of Liberty; Rawls: A Theory of Justice; Nozick: Anarchy,
|       State, and Utopia; Sen: The Idea of Justice and capability writings.
|   +-- Famous slogans must be attributed carefully; use verified paraphrase where wording
|       or translation varies.
|
+-- I. EQUALITY: BASIC MEANING
|   +-- Equality is not sameness of all treatment or outcome.
|   +-- It denies irrelevant hierarchy and demands equal moral standing.
|   +-- Formal equality:
|   |   +-- Same rule/status before law.
|   +-- Political equality:
|   |   +-- Equal citizenship, vote and opportunity to influence.
|   +-- Social equality:
|   |   +-- Absence of status humiliation, caste/race/gender subordination.
|   +-- Economic equality:
|       +-- Limits unjust disparities and secures material basis of agency.
|   +-- Equal treatment can reproduce inequality where starting positions differ.
|
+-- II. OPPORTUNITY, OUTCOME AND EQUITY
|   +-- Formal opportunity:
|   |   +-- Offices open under identical rules.
|   +-- Fair opportunity:
|   |   +-- Comparable prospects despite class/social origin.
|   +-- Outcome equality:
|   |   +-- Narrows final distribution; complete uniformity rarely defended.
|   +-- Equity:
|       +-- Context-sensitive allocation correcting relevant disadvantage.
|   +-- India application:
|       +-- Reservation can be defended as substantive equality/equal citizenship, not an
|           exception to equality understood only as identical treatment.
|   +-- Caution:
|       +-- Constitutional/legal details are date-sensitive; philosophical point is the
|           distinction between formal neutrality and corrective justice.
|
+-- III. ROUSSEAU ON INEQUALITY
|   +-- Natural/physical inequality:
|       +-- Age, health, bodily strength and capacities.
|   +-- Moral/political/artificial inequality:
|       +-- Wealth, honour, power and authority established by convention.
|   +-- Private property and social comparison intensify dependence.
|   +-- Amour de soi:
|       +-- Natural self-concern compatible with pity.
|   +-- Amour-propre:
|       +-- Comparative esteem that fuels rivalry and domination.
|   +-- Legitimate association must transform dependence into equal citizenship under
|       laws one prescribes as member of the general will.
|   +-- Objection:
|       +-- General will may silence dissent.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- It concerns common interest, not numerical will of all.
|
+-- IV. MILL ON EQUALITY
|   +-- Equal consideration and opposition to inherited privilege.
|   +-- Equal liberty enables individuality and experiments in living.
|   +-- Women's subjection is socially produced, not natural proof of inferiority.
|   +-- Representative institutions require broad participation and education.
|   +-- Equality before law and opportunity are central.
|   +-- Qualified departures:
|       +-- Mill sometimes permits competence-weighted institutional devices and retains
|           controversial civilisational assumptions; do not portray him as complete egalitarian.
|   +-- Salient verdict:
|       +-- Equality protects persons from status domination while diversity of talent
|           and life-plan remains valuable.
|
+-- V. MARX: EQUALITY AND EQUITY
|   +-- Formal legal equality under capitalism can coexist with material exploitation.
|   +-- Equal exchange:
|       +-- Labour contract appears voluntary/equal.
|       +-- Ownership structure makes worker dependent.
|   +-- Lower communist phase:
|       +-- Distribution according to contribution retains unequal effects because persons
|           differ in needs/capacities.
|   +-- Higher phase:
|       +-- Contribution according to ability, distribution according to need.
|   +-- Equity:
|       +-- Need-sensitive treatment may require unequal shares.
|   +-- Marx does not simply demand arithmetically equal goods.
|
+-- VI. EQUALITY OF WHAT?
|   +-- Welfare:
|       +-- Equal satisfaction; risks adaptive preferences and expensive tastes.
|   +-- Resources / Dworkin:
|       +-- Equal initial resources, ambition-sensitive but endowment-sensitive correction.
|       +-- Hypothetical auction/insurance clarify responsibility and luck.
|   +-- Primary goods / Rawls:
|       +-- Rights, liberties, opportunities, income/wealth and social bases of self-respect.
|   +-- Capabilities / Sen:
|       +-- Real freedom to achieve valuable functionings.
|       +-- Accounts for conversion differences: disability, gender, location and social norms.
|   +-- Cohen:
|       +-- Advantage/access-to-advantage and critique of incentive inequality.
|   +-- Relational equality:
|       +-- Ending hierarchy, domination and humiliation, not only distributing a metric.
|
+-- VII. LIBERTY: BASIC MAP
|   +-- Negative liberty:
|       +-- Sphere free from interference.
|   +-- Positive liberty:
|       +-- Capacity/self-mastery to direct one's life.
|   +-- Republican liberty:
|       +-- Freedom from subjection to arbitrary power, even when interference is absent.
|   +-- Legal liberty:
|       +-- Protected permissions/rights.
|   +-- Effective liberty:
|       +-- Actual means and capacities to use formal freedom.
|
+-- VIII. BERLIN
|   +-- Negative:
|       +-- Area within which person can act unobstructed by others.
|   +-- Positive:
|       +-- Being one's own master/source of action.
|   +-- Warning:
|       +-- Positive freedom can be appropriated by rulers claiming to know one's "real self"
|           and coercing for supposed liberation.
|   +-- Value pluralism:
|       +-- Human goods may conflict without one final harmonising metric.
|   +-- Objection:
|       +-- Negative liberty ignores private/economic domination.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- Berlin does not deny welfare; he protects conceptual clarity and anti-coercion.
|
+-- IX. MILL'S HARM PRINCIPLE
|   +-- Coercion justified to prevent harm to others, not merely person's own good.
|   +-- Self-regarding conduct:
|       +-- Presumptive individual sovereignty.
|   +-- Other-regarding conduct:
|       +-- Regulation possible where interests/rights of others are harmed.
|   +-- Reasons for liberty:
|       +-- Fallibility.
|       +-- Partial truth.
|       +-- Living conviction.
|       +-- Individuality/social progress.
|   +-- Difficulties:
|       +-- Harm versus offence.
|       +-- Indirect/systemic harm.
|       +-- Dependence and impaired autonomy.
|
+-- X. HOBBES, LOCKE, ROUSSEAU
|   +-- Hobbes:
|       +-- Liberty as absence of external impediments.
|       +-- Strong sovereign secures usable freedom from violent insecurity.
|   +-- Locke:
|       +-- Natural freedom bounded by natural law.
|       +-- Government protects life, liberty and property through consent.
|   +-- Rousseau:
|       +-- Moral/civil freedom as obedience to self-prescribed general law.
|       +-- Freedom is not unregulated appetite.
|   +-- Tension:
|       +-- Security, rights and collective self-rule yield different state-liberty relations.
|
+-- XI. T. H. GREEN
|   +-- Freedom is positive power to do/be something worth doing/being.
|   +-- Addiction, ignorance and poverty can disable freedom without direct coercion.
|   +-- State may remove obstacles and build enabling conditions.
|   +-- Not unlimited paternalism:
|       +-- Intervention justified by common conditions of self-development.
|   +-- Objection:
|       +-- Officials may impose contested conception of good.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- Enabling education, health and basic security expands rather than replaces agency.
|
+-- XII. REPUBLICAN NON-DOMINATION
|   +-- Pettit:
|       +-- Freedom means not living under arbitrary, uncontrolled power.
|   +-- Benevolent master may not interfere but slave remains unfree.
|   +-- Institutions need:
|       +-- Rule of law.
|       +-- Contestability.
|       +-- Checks.
|       +-- Non-arbitrary public justification.
|   +-- Is it a third liberty?
|       +-- Republican view: domination differs from actual interference.
|       +-- Rival view: it is a demanding specification of negative liberty.
|
+-- XIII. TECHNOLOGICAL SOCIETY
|   +-- Threats:
|       +-- Surveillance.
|       +-- Behavioural profiling.
|       +-- Algorithmic manipulation.
|       +-- Platform dependency.
|       +-- Concentrated information power.
|   +-- Formal consent may not equal informed autonomous choice.
|   +-- Technology also expands expression, access and association.
|   +-- Realisable liberty requires:
|       +-- Privacy.
|       +-- Transparency.
|       +-- Contestability.
|       +-- Digital capability.
|       +-- Limits on state/corporate domination.
|   +-- Avoid date-sensitive factual assertions unless freshly verified.
|
+-- XIV. LIBERTY AND EQUALITY
|   +-- Conflict:
|       +-- Unrestricted property/contract freedom can widen inequality.
|       +-- Aggressive equalisation can restrict choice and incentive.
|   +-- Complementarity:
|       +-- Minimum resources make liberty effective.
|       +-- Equal civic status protects freedom from domination.
|       +-- Liberty permits persons to challenge unequal structures.
|   +-- Complete liberty is impossible:
|       +-- One person's unconstrained power can destroy another's freedom.
|   +-- Legitimate limits:
|       +-- General, public, proportionate and rights-compatible.
|
+-- XV. JUSTICE: CORE DISTINCTIONS
|   +-- Formal justice:
|       +-- Like cases treated alike under relevant rule.
|   +-- Procedural justice:
|       +-- Fair decision process.
|   +-- Substantive justice:
|       +-- Fairness of outcomes/institutions themselves.
|   +-- Legal justice:
|       +-- Rights and lawful adjudication.
|   +-- Political justice:
|       +-- Equal voice and accountable power.
|   +-- Socio-economic justice:
|       +-- Fair life chances/resources.
|   +-- Recognition/status justice:
|       +-- Equal respect and non-humiliation.
|
+-- XVI. PLATO
|   +-- Justice in city:
|       +-- Each class performs proper function without usurpation.
|   +-- Justice in soul:
|       +-- Reason rules, spirit supports, appetite obeys.
|   +-- Harmony and functional specialisation.
|   +-- Strength:
|       +-- Justice orders whole, not isolated transactions.
|   +-- Criticism:
|       +-- Rigid hierarchy, paternalism and suppression of individual mobility.
|   +-- Rawls continuity:
|       +-- Both seek principles ordering basic structure.
|   +-- Break:
|       +-- Rawls begins from free/equal persons, not natural class hierarchy.
|
+-- XVII. ARISTOTLE
|   +-- Distributive justice:
|       +-- Proportionate allocation according to relevant merit.
|       +-- Equals equally, unequals proportionately unequally.
|   +-- Corrective justice:
|       +-- Arithmetic restoration between parties after wrongful gain/loss.
|   +-- Criteria problem:
|       +-- Merit, need, contribution and equality produce rival distributions.
|   +-- Corrective/compensatory application:
|       +-- Restore position, repair injury or offset structural disadvantage.
|   +-- Criticism:
|       +-- Existing hierarchy can define "merit" and reproduce exclusion.
|
+-- XVIII. UTILITARIAN JUSTICE
|   +-- Just institutions maximise aggregate welfare.
|   +-- Impartiality:
|       +-- Each person's welfare counts.
|   +-- Strength:
|       +-- Consequences and public benefit matter.
|   +-- Aggregation objection:
|       +-- Large benefits to majority can outweigh severe burden on minority.
|   +-- Separateness-of-persons objection:
|       +-- Society cannot treat individuals as one aggregate experiencer.
|   +-- Rule utilitarian reply:
|       +-- Stable rights-protecting rules usually maximise welfare.
|
+-- XIX. RAWLS: JUSTICE AS FAIRNESS
|   +-- Original position:
|       +-- Fair choice situation.
|   +-- Veil of ignorance:
|       +-- Parties do not know class, caste, gender, talents, religion or life-plan.
|   +-- Know general social facts and seek primary goods.
|   +-- Two principles:
|       +-- Equal basic liberties compatible with same scheme for all.
|       +-- Social/economic inequalities permitted only if:
|           +-- Offices under fair equality of opportunity.
|           +-- Greatest benefit to least advantaged: difference principle.
|   +-- Priority:
|       +-- Basic liberty has lexical priority over distributive gains.
|       +-- Fair opportunity precedes difference principle.
|   +-- Reflective equilibrium:
|       +-- Mutual adjustment of principles and considered judgements.
|
+-- XX. RAWLS OBJECTIONS / REPLIES
|   +-- Nozick:
|       +-- Patterned redistribution violates historical entitlements.
|   +-- Communitarian:
|       +-- Original-position self is abstract/unencumbered.
|   +-- Feminist:
|       +-- Family and care inequalities underdeveloped.
|   +-- Cohen:
|       +-- Incentive inequalities compromise egalitarian ethos.
|   +-- Sen:
|       +-- Focus on perfect institutions/primary goods misses actual capabilities.
|   +-- Rawlsian replies:
|       +-- Basic structure regulates background justice.
|       +-- Primary goods are public metric.
|       +-- Political conception avoids comprehensive moral doctrine.
|
+-- XXI. NOZICK
|   +-- Entitlement theory:
|       +-- Justice in acquisition.
|       +-- Justice in transfer.
|       +-- Rectification of injustice.
|   +-- Historical, not end-state/patterned.
|   +-- Wilt Chamberlain example:
|       +-- Voluntary exchanges disrupt any pattern.
|   +-- Minimal state:
|       +-- Protects against force, theft, fraud and enforces contracts.
|   +-- Taxation for redistributive purposes compared to forced labour in his argument.
|   +-- Objections:
|       +-- Original acquisition doubtful.
|       +-- Structural coercion affects voluntariness.
|       +-- Historical injustice makes current holdings suspect.
|       +-- Minimal state cannot secure fair opportunity.
|
+-- XXII. SEN
|   +-- Nīti:
|       +-- Organisational/institutional correctness.
|   +-- Nyāya:
|       +-- Realised justice in people's lives.
|   +-- Comparative approach:
|       +-- Remove manifest injustice rather than first identify perfectly just society.
|   +-- Capability:
|       +-- Real opportunity to achieve valued functionings.
|   +-- Public reasoning:
|       +-- Open, plural and comparative evaluation.
|   +-- Rawls improvement:
|       +-- Moves from holdings to conversion and actual freedom.
|   +-- Objection:
|       +-- No fixed capability list or complete institutional blueprint.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- Democratic public reasoning should determine priorities contextually.
|
+-- XXIII. AMBEDKAR
|   +-- Liberty, equality and fraternity form an inseparable trinity.
|   +-- Political democracy cannot endure without social democracy.
|   +-- Caste destroys equal status and fraternity even under formal rights.
|   +-- Constitutional morality:
|       +-- Institutions require habits of restraint, equality and public reason.
|   +-- India-facing justice:
|       +-- Anti-discrimination plus representation and corrective measures.
|   +-- Do not reduce Ambedkar to an example appended after Western theory; he exposes the
|       social preconditions that abstract contractual models can overlook.
|
+-- XXIV. WHY JUSTICE ORDERS LIBERTY AND EQUALITY
|   +-- Equality alone cannot identify relevant metric or permissible differences.
|   +-- Liberty alone cannot decide whose freedom prevails amid conflict.
|   +-- Justice:
|       +-- Determines equal basic status.
|       +-- Specifies justified liberty limits.
|       +-- Selects distributive metric.
|       +-- Protects minorities.
|       +-- Repairs historical wrong.
|       +-- Requires procedures and substantive outcomes.
|   +-- Justice is not a third isolated good but the ordering framework of social cooperation.
|
