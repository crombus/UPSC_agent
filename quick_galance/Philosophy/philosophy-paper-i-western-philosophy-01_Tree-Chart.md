                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Plato explains knowledge, universality and value through transcendent, timeless Forms
of which sensible particulars are imperfect instances. Aristotle accepts intelligible
form but relocates it within concrete substances, explaining change through matter,
form, privation, four causes and potentiality-actuality. Their disagreement is not
whether form matters, but whether it exists separately, how it causes particulars, what
counts as substance and how stable knowledge can accommodate a changing world.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Plato secures the objectivity of knowledge and value by separating Forms from the
changing sensible world, but the separation makes participation and causation obscure.
Aristotle's immanent form, hylomorphism and potentiality-actuality explain concrete
substance and change more powerfully, yet leave difficult questions about universal
form and teleology. Aristotle does not simply reject Plato: he preserves the demand
for intelligibility while transforming its ontology from two worlds into one.
