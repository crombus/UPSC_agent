# Philosophy Optional — Paper II — Philosophy of Religion 08 — Religion and Morality

RELIGION AND MORALITY: DEPENDENCE, AUTONOMY AND INTERACTION
|
+-- CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
|   +-- Religion may ground, motivate, interpret or socially reinforce morality, but these
|       are distinct dependence claims. Moral reasons can be autonomous while religious
|       traditions remain morally valuable and themselves answerable to ethical criticism.
|
+-- SCOPE / OWNERSHIP FIREWALL
|   +-- Owns divine command, Euthyphro, natural law, Kantian autonomy, secular ethics,
|       dharma/karma, religion's moral influence, Nietzsche, religious violence,
|       just-war/jihād/dharma-yuddha comparisons and religiosity/immorality.
|   +-- Evil, faith and political secularism remain separate owners.
|
+-- SOURCE / ATTRIBUTION CAUTION
|   +-- Plato's Euthyphro, Aquinas, Kant, Mill, Nietzsche and Indian traditions.
|   +-- Religious ethical traditions are internally plural; justified-war concepts are
|       constraint frameworks, not blanket endorsements of violence.
|
+-- I. FOUR DEPENDENCE CLAIMS
|   +-- Ontological: moral facts depend on God.
|   +-- Semantic: moral terms derive meaning from religion.
|   +-- Epistemic: morality can be known only through revelation.
|   +-- Motivational: people need religious sanction to act morally.
|   +-- Refuting one does not refute all.
|
+-- II. DIVINE COMMAND
|   +-- Right action is commanded by God; wrong forbidden.
|   +-- Supplies authority, objectivity and motivation.
|   +-- Euthyphro dilemma:
|       +-- Is act good because commanded?
|       +-- Or commanded because independently good?
|   +-- First horn risks arbitrariness; second makes morality independent.
|   +-- Modified theory: commands express necessarily good divine nature.
|   +-- Residual: account of divine goodness must avoid circularity.
|
+-- III. NATURAL LAW / AUTONOMY
|   +-- Aquinas: practical reason participates in eternal law; basic goods naturally knowable.
|   +-- Morality is theistically grounded but not dependent on fresh command for each duty.
|   +-- Kant: autonomy is rational self-legislation under universal moral law.
|   +-- Heteronomous reward/fear cannot constitute moral worth.
|   +-- Secular grounds: welfare, dignity, contractual reciprocity, virtue and flourishing.
|
+-- IV. INTERACTION
|   +-- Religion shapes moral imagination, community, exemplars and motivation.
|   +-- Morality evaluates religious beliefs/practices.
|   +-- Religion can inspire compassion and sacrifice.
|   +-- It can also intensify exclusion when sacred authority immunises commands.
|   +-- Best model is mutual influence without moral surrender.
|
+-- V. INDIAN GROUNDING
|   +-- Dharma: role/order/duty traditions, not one uniform rule.
|   +-- Karma links intentional action and consequence without necessarily divine command.
|   +-- Buddhism grounds ethics in suffering, intention, compassion and dependent origination.
|   +-- Jainism grounds radical non-injury in plurality of jīvas.
|   +-- Mīmāṃsā grounds dharma in authorless Vedic injunction.
|   +-- Theism is therefore not necessary for Indian moral normativity.
|
+-- VI. WHY BE MORAL?
|   +-- Prudence/reward is unstable if morality demands sacrifice.
|   +-- Rational consistency and respect for persons.
|   +-- Sympathy/virtue and social reciprocity.
|   +-- Identity/community commitment.
|   +-- Divine relation/judgement.
|   +-- No one motive explains all moral fidelity; secular ethics can ground obligation
|       while religion may deepen motivation.
|
+-- VII. NIETZSCHE
|   +-- Genealogy asks whose life/interests a morality serves.
|   +-- Slave morality transforms weakness/ressentiment into moral superiority.
|   +-- Ascetic ideal devalues embodied life.
|   +-- Death of God destabilises inherited moral authority and risks nihilism.
|   +-- Revaluation seeks life-affirming creation of values.
|   +-- Criticism: can weaken equality/compassion and enable domination.
|
+-- VIII. RELIGIOUS VIOLENCE
|   +-- Claimed supports: divine command, sacred duty, defence of community/order.
|   +-- Philosophical constraints:
|       +-- Just cause.
|       +-- Legitimate authority.
|       +-- Right intention.
|       +-- Last resort.
|       +-- Proportionality.
|       +-- Discrimination/non-combatant protection.
|   +-- Jihād and dharma-yuddha have plural textual traditions; never reduce to violence.
|   +-- No revelation can bypass interpretation, responsibility and moral scrutiny.
|
+-- IX. RELIGIOSITY / IMMORALITY
|   +-- Religious identity may coexist with wrongdoing through hypocrisy, fanaticism,
|       obedience, moral licensing or group partiality.
|   +-- This does not show religion necessarily causes immorality.
|   +-- Genuine religion arguably requires moral transformation.
|   +-- The 2018 held wording says "immorality" and must not be silently changed.
|
+-- OBJECTIONS / REPLIES
|   +-- Without God all is permitted -> secular reasons and non-theistic traditions refute necessity.
|   +-- Secular ethics cannot motivate always -> religion also fails to guarantee conduct.
|   +-- Morality judges revelation arrogantly -> interpretation already uses moral concepts.
|   +-- Autonomous ethics lacks ultimate meaning -> grounding and existential meaning differ.
|
+-- VERIFIED THINKER / TEXT / EXAMPLE BANK
|   +-- Plato, Aquinas, Kant, Mill, Nietzsche; Hindu, Buddhist, Jaina and Mīmāṃsā traditions.
|   +-- Examples: Euthyphro dilemma, reward versus duty, moral critique of sacred command.
|   +-- No fabricated quotations.
|
+-- HIGH-RISK CONCEPTUAL TRAPS
|   +-- Grounding, knowing and motivating morality are not identical.
|   +-- Divine command is not natural law; autonomy is not moral subjectivism.
|   +-- Karma is not divine reward; religion is not automatically moral.
|   +-- Nietzsche does not merely advocate immorality.
|   +-- Just-war constraints do not sanctify any claimed religious conflict.
