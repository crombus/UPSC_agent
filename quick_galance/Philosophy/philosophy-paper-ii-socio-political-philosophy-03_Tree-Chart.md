# Philosophy Optional — Paper II — Socio-Political Philosophy 03 — Individual and State

## Quick-Glance Tree Chart

INDIVIDUAL AND STATE: RIGHTS, DUTIES, ACCOUNTABILITY AND POLITICAL OBLIGATION
|
+-- CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
|   +-- A legitimate state must secure persons as rights-bearing agents while requiring
|       duties needed for reciprocal freedom and common institutions.
|   +-- Rights identify protected claims and spheres; duties specify obligations; accountability
|       links public power and civic action to reason-giving and correction.
|   +-- Neither atomistic individualism nor unlimited statism is adequate: political
|       authority is justified only when it protects dignity, enables agency and remains
|       contestable through law, participation and principled resistance.
|
+-- SCOPE / OWNERSHIP FIREWALL
|   +-- Owns: natural/legal/moral/human rights; Hohfeld incidents; will/interest theories;
|   |   universalism/cultural relativism; duties and Gandhian duty-centred thought;
|   |   answerability/enforcement/accountability; liberal, idealist, welfare and
|   |   communitarian state; social contract; statism/individualism; political obligation;
|   |   consent, fair play, natural duty, association; civil disobedience/resistance.
|   +-- Social and Political Ideals owns equality/liberty/justice theories.
|   +-- Sovereignty owns final authority.
|   +-- Forms of Government owns regime classification.
|   +-- Political Ideologies owns full anarchist/Marxist doctrine.
|
+-- SOURCE / ATTRIBUTION CAUTION
|   +-- Hobbes: Leviathan; Locke: Two Treatises; Rousseau: Social Contract.
|   +-- Hohfeld: Fundamental Legal Conceptions; Bentham's natural-rights critique must be
|       paraphrased carefully rather than reduced to one slogan.
|   +-- Mill, Green, Hegel, Gandhi, Ambedkar, Dworkin, Hart and Raz enter bounded debates.
|   +-- Indian constitutional examples illustrate philosophical relations; specific legal
|       status and case holdings require current verification.
|
+-- I. THE BASIC RELATION
|   +-- Individualist pole:
|       +-- Person has moral worth prior to political office.
|       +-- State is instrument for protection/coordination.
|   +-- Statist/organic pole:
|       +-- Person develops capacities through institutions and common life.
|       +-- State embodies objective ethical order in stronger idealist accounts.
|   +-- Balanced relation:
|       +-- Individual needs collective institutions.
|       +-- State derives justification from persons, not its own unlimited glory.
|
+-- II. WHAT IS A RIGHT?
|   +-- Justified normative claim protecting interest, choice, status or sphere of action.
|   +-- Rights imply reasons constraining others/institutions.
|   +-- They may be:
|       +-- Moral before legal recognition.
|       +-- Legal through enacted/adjudicated institutions.
|       +-- Positive claims to provision.
|       +-- Negative protections against interference.
|       +-- Individual or group-differentiated.
|   +-- A right is not every desire or benefit.
|
+-- III. NATURAL RIGHTS
|   +-- Locke:
|       +-- Persons naturally free and equal.
|       +-- Life, liberty and property protected by natural law.
|       +-- Government arises by consent as fiduciary power.
|       +-- Breach permits resistance.
|   +-- Strength:
|       +-- Moral limits precede state.
|   +-- Problems:
|       +-- Historical/metaphysical basis disputed.
|       +-- Property acquisition can entrench inequality.
|       +-- Social dependency complicates pre-political individual.
|
+-- IV. LEGAL/POSITIVIST RIGHTS
|   +-- Rights exist through valid law/institution.
|   +-- Bentham:
|       +-- Critiques abstract natural rights detached from legal specification/enforcement.
|   +-- Strength:
|       +-- Clarifies claim-holder, duty-bearer, remedy and institutional reality.
|   +-- Weakness:
|       +-- Cannot by itself criticise unjust law.
|       +-- If legal existence equals moral legitimacy, resistance loses normative basis.
|
+-- V. MORAL AND HUMAN RIGHTS
|   +-- Moral rights:
|       +-- Justified independently of current enactment.
|   +-- Human rights:
|       +-- Held by persons as human beings, protecting dignity and basic agency.
|   +-- Universality:
|       +-- Common minimum not dependent on citizenship.
|   +-- Cultural-relativist challenge:
|       +-- Rights language may reflect individualist Western histories.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- Cross-cultural participation, suffering and dignity support revisable universalism.
|       +-- Implementation may vary; protection against cruelty/subordination cannot be
|           surrendered to unaccountable custom.
|
+-- VI. HOHFELD'S FOUR INCIDENTS
|   +-- Claim-right:
|       +-- Correlative duty in another.
|   +-- Liberty/privilege:
|       +-- Absence of duty not to act.
|       +-- Correlative no-right in another.
|   +-- Power:
|       +-- Ability to alter legal/normative relations.
|       +-- Correlative liability.
|   +-- Immunity:
|       +-- Protection against another's power.
|       +-- Correlative disability.
|   +-- One ordinary "right" may combine incidents.
|   +-- Trap:
|       +-- Not every liberty has a correlative duty to assist.
|
+-- VII. WILL AND INTEREST THEORIES
|   +-- Will/choice theory:
|       +-- Right gives holder normative control over another's duty.
|       +-- Highlights autonomy and waiver.
|       +-- Difficulty: infants, severely disabled persons and inalienable rights.
|   +-- Interest theory:
|       +-- Right protects sufficiently important interest to impose duties.
|       +-- Covers vulnerable/non-choosing bearers.
|       +-- Difficulty: many interests are important without generating rights.
|   +-- Hybrid:
|       +-- Agency explains many rights; basic interests explain universal protections.
|
+-- VIII. RIGHTS AND DUTIES
|   +-- Correlativity:
|       +-- Claim-right entails another's duty.
|   +-- But:
|       +-- Liberty-right correlates with no-right, not duty.
|       +-- General moral duties may lack one determinate right-holder.
|   +-- Rights of citizens can imply duties of state, other citizens or institutions.
|   +-- Having rights does not by itself make citizen accountable to state:
|       +-- Accountability depends on role, law and reciprocal cooperation.
|   +-- State also owes account to rights-holders.
|
+-- IX. DUTY
|   +-- Obligation to act/refrain for justified reason.
|   +-- Legal duty:
|       +-- Institutionally specified and sanctionable.
|   +-- Moral duty:
|       +-- Grounded in conscience, dignity, justice or relationship.
|   +-- Positive duty:
|       +-- Assist/provide.
|   +-- Negative duty:
|       +-- Refrain from harming/interfering.
|   +-- Perfect duty:
|       +-- Specific act/recipient.
|   +-- Imperfect duty:
|       +-- Latitude in how/when fulfilled.
|
+-- X. GANDHI'S DUTY-CENTRISM
|   +-- Rights flow from duties well performed.
|   +-- Swaraj:
|       +-- Self-rule includes self-restraint, not only transfer of state power.
|   +-- Ahiṃsā and satya discipline claims.
|   +-- Rights without duties encourage possessive individualism.
|   +-- Duties without resistance are not Gandhian:
|       +-- Satyāgraha opposes unjust authority through truthful non-violence.
|   +-- Criticism:
|       +-- Duty-first language can burden oppressed persons.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- Duties fall especially on power-holders and are bounded by equal dignity.
|
+-- XI. INDIAN TRADITION AND RIGHTS
|   +-- Dharma traditions emphasise relational duties and role obligations.
|   +-- This does not prove hostility to individual rights.
|   +-- Resources for dignity:
|       +-- Ahiṃsā.
|       +-- Compassion.
|       +-- Self-restraint.
|       +-- Bhakti critiques of status.
|       +-- Buddhist/Jaina concern for suffering.
|   +-- Critical caution:
|       +-- Traditional duty orders also supported caste, gender and status hierarchy.
|   +-- Constitutional rights transform tradition through equal citizenship.
|
+-- XII. FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES: PHILOSOPHICAL BASIS
|   +-- Civic reciprocity.
|   +-- Maintenance of common institutions.
|   +-- Respect for plural citizenship and public goods.
|   +-- Ecological/intergenerational responsibility.
|   +-- Duties cannot become:
|       +-- Pretext to cancel enforceable rights.
|       +-- Unilateral obedience demanded only from citizens.
|   +-- Exact constitutional scope/status is a legal question requiring current authority.
|
+-- XIII. ACCOUNTABILITY
|   +-- Answerability:
|       +-- Duty to explain and justify conduct.
|   +-- Enforceability:
|       +-- Possibility of investigation, correction and sanction.
|   +-- Responsibility:
|       +-- Attribution of role, action and consequence.
|   +-- Accountability cycle:
|       +-- Standards -> information -> explanation -> judgement -> correction/sanction ->
|           learning.
|   +-- Transparency is necessary but not sufficient:
|       +-- Information without forum or consequence may not produce accountability.
|
+-- XIV. POLITICAL / LEGAL / MORAL ACCOUNTABILITY
|   +-- Political:
|       +-- Elections, legislature, public opinion and representative responsibility.
|   +-- Legal:
|       +-- Courts, review, liability and lawful procedure.
|   +-- Administrative:
|       +-- Audit, hierarchy, grievance mechanisms and reasoned decisions.
|   +-- Social:
|       +-- Public scrutiny, media, civil society and social audit.
|   +-- Moral:
|       +-- Conscience and responsibility exceeding minimal legality.
|   +-- Horizontal versus vertical:
|       +-- Institution checking institution versus citizen controlling representative.
|
+-- XV. LIBERAL STATE
|   +-- Individuals are free/equal rights-bearers.
|   +-- Limited government, rule of law and private sphere.
|   +-- Locke:
|       +-- Protective fiduciary state.
|   +-- Mill:
|       +-- Harm principle and individuality.
|   +-- Neutrality:
|       +-- State should not impose one comprehensive good.
|   +-- Problems:
|       +-- Formal neutrality can preserve structural inequality.
|       +-- Private power can dominate.
|
+-- XVI. PATERNALISM / PERFECTIONISM
|   +-- Paternalism:
|       +-- Restricting person's liberty for that person's good.
|   +-- Soft paternalism:
|       +-- Intervention where choice is uninformed/non-voluntary.
|   +-- Hard paternalism:
|       +-- Override competent voluntary choice.
|   +-- Perfectionism:
|       +-- State promotes objectively valuable forms of life.
|   +-- Liberal objection:
|       +-- Officials may usurp autonomy.
|   +-- Positive-liberty reply:
|       +-- Enabling conditions and protection from manipulation can deepen autonomy.
|
+-- XVII. IDEALIST STATE
|   +-- Hegel:
|       +-- State is actuality of ethical life, mediating family and civil society.
|       +-- Freedom becomes concrete through rational institutions.
|       +-- Not simple worship of any existing regime.
|   +-- T. H. Green:
|       +-- State creates conditions for moral self-development.
|       +-- Authority justified by common good, not force.
|   +-- Do not flatten:
|       +-- Hegelian ethical totality and Green's liberal idealism differ.
|   +-- Criticism:
|       +-- State may define "real freedom" paternalistically.
|
+-- XVIII. WELFARE AND COMMUNITARIAN VIEWS
|   +-- Welfare state:
|       +-- Social rights and services make formal liberty usable.
|   +-- Communitarian critique:
|       +-- Persons are embedded in histories, practices and relations.
|       +-- Liberal self may be too atomistic.
|   +-- Liberal reply:
|       +-- Community matters, but rights protect dissenters within communities.
|   +-- Balanced position:
|       +-- Social membership enables agency but cannot fix identity beyond revision.
|
+-- XIX. SOCIAL CONTRACT: ORIGIN AND DEVELOPMENT
|   +-- Hobbes:
|       +-- Insecurity/state of war -> mutual covenant -> undivided sovereign.
|       +-- Subjects contract with one another, not ordinarily with sovereign.
|   +-- Locke:
|       +-- Natural rights but inconvenient enforcement -> limited fiduciary government.
|       +-- Consent and right of resistance.
|   +-- Rousseau:
|       +-- Legitimate association under general will.
|       +-- Each obeys law as equal co-author.
|   +-- Kantian/Rawlsian transformation:
|       +-- Contract becomes device of public justification, not literal historical event.
|
+-- XX. SOCIAL CONTRACT AND HUMAN RIGHTS
|   +-- Contribution:
|       +-- Consent, equality and limits on rulers.
|   +-- Limits:
|       +-- Actual history includes conquest/exclusion.
|       +-- Women, enslaved, colonised and propertyless often excluded.
|       +-- Rights cannot depend solely on actual consent.
|       +-- Future generations/non-citizens may lack contractual voice.
|   +-- Reconstructed reply:
|       +-- Hypothetical contract tests whether institutions could be justified to free/equal
|           persons, not whether ancestors signed.
|
+-- XXI. PLATO AND ARISTOTLE: STATISM / INDIVIDUALISM
|   +-- Plato:
|       +-- Justice as functional unity; guardian order strongly subordinates private life.
|       +-- Risks statism and rigid hierarchy.
|   +-- Aristotle:
|       +-- Polis natural and prior in explanatory sense.
|       +-- Household, property, friendship and mixed constitution retain plural spaces.
|       +-- Citizen participation and middle class moderate extremes.
|   +-- Aristotle's middle course:
|       +-- Individual cannot flourish outside polis.
|       +-- Polis exists for good life, not absorption of all associations.
|   +-- Limit:
|       +-- Historical exclusion from citizenship weakens universal claim.
|
+-- XXII. ANARCHIST CHALLENGE
|   +-- State claims coercive authority not morally self-validating.
|   +-- Voluntary cooperation and federations may replace hierarchy.
|   +-- Philosophical anarchism:
|       +-- No general duty to obey merely because law is law.
|   +-- State reply:
|       +-- Public goods, security, adjudication and structural justice need durable authority.
|   +-- Middle:
|       +-- Authority is presumptively contestable and must justify each coercive domain.
|
+-- XXIII. MARXIST CHALLENGE
|   +-- Liberal state presents itself as universal while protecting class relations.
|   +-- Formal rights can mask material dependence.
|   +-- Political emancipation is incomplete without social/economic emancipation.
|   +-- State expected to wither after class antagonism.
|   +-- Criticism:
|       +-- Rights/state constraints remain necessary against concentrated collective power.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- Rights must be materially enabled and not fetishised as sufficient.
|
+-- XXIV. UNCONDITIONAL RIGHTS AND ANARCHY
|   +-- If every right absolute:
|       +-- Conflicting rights cannot be adjudicated.
|       +-- Public health/security/taxation may become impossible.
|   +-- But "unconditional" can mean:
|       +-- Non-derogable core protecting dignity.
|   +-- Rights are structured:
|       +-- Internal limits.
|       +-- Proportional restrictions.
|       +-- Correlative duties.
|       +-- Emergency safeguards.
|   +-- Therefore strong rights do not logically entail anarchy; unstructured absolute
|       claims create conflict, while rule-governed rights constitute order.
|
+-- XXV. POLITICAL OBLIGATION
|   +-- Question:
|       +-- Why obey law beyond fear or agreement with each rule?
|   +-- Consent:
|       +-- Express or tacit authorisation.
|       +-- Problem: residence is not always voluntary consent.
|   +-- Fair play:
|       +-- Beneficiaries of cooperative scheme owe their share.
|       +-- Problem: unsolicited benefits do not always generate duty.
|   +-- Natural duty of justice:
|       +-- Support reasonably just institutions.
|       +-- Problem: explains just states better than unjust ones.
|   +-- Associative obligation:
|       +-- Membership/citizenship generates role duties.
|       +-- Problem: inherited membership can legitimise domination.
|   +-- Gratitude/benefit:
|       +-- State services generate reciprocal duty.
|       +-- Problem: gratitude is not blanket obedience.
|   +-- Common-good necessity:
|       +-- Coordination needs authoritative rules.
|       +-- Problem: necessity of coordination does not validate every command.
|
+-- XXVI. DWORKIN AND LAW AS INTEGRITY
|   +-- Law includes principles, not rules alone.
|   +-- Hard cases require constructive interpretation of institutional history.
|   +-- Government must treat citizens with equal concern and respect.
|   +-- Integrity:
|       +-- Coherent principled justification across law.
|   +-- Relevance:
|       +-- Obligation strengthens when coercive order speaks in principled, equal voice.
|   +-- Criticism:
|       +-- Coherence may preserve unjust inherited doctrine.
|   +-- Reply:
|       +-- Best interpretation includes moral justification, not fit alone.
|
+-- XXVII. RESISTANCE LADDER
|   +-- Lawful dissent:
|       +-- Speech, petition, litigation, electoral change.
|   +-- Conscientious objection:
|       +-- Personal refusal, often not aimed at changing general law.
|   +-- Civil disobedience:
|       +-- Public, conscientious, nonviolent breach aimed at changing law/policy.
|   +-- Non-cooperation/satyāgraha:
|       +-- Truthful disciplined withdrawal and willingness to bear penalty.
|   +-- Revolutionary resistance:
|       +-- Seeks replacement of political order; highest justificatory burden.
|   +-- Justification factors:
|       +-- Serious injustice, last resort, publicity, proportionality, nonviolence,
|           reasonable prospect and respect for equal rights.
|
+-- XXVIII. INDIA-CENTRIC APPLICATION
|   +-- Fundamental rights and directive/social commitments illustrate liberty with enabling
|       conditions.
|   +-- Fundamental duties illustrate reciprocity but cannot erase rights.
|   +-- Public reason, judicial review, elections, audit and grievance systems institutionalise
|       accountability.
|   +-- Satyāgraha provides a duty-conscious theory of resistance.
|   +-- Caste/gender/community hierarchy tests whether group duties respect individual dignity.
|   +-- Legal particulars require current source verification.
|
+-- XXIX. CLASSIC OBJECTIONS / REPLIES
|   +-- Rights promote egoism.
|       +-- Reply: rights secure equal agency and can include social claims.
|   +-- Duties legitimise hierarchy.
|       +-- Reply: only duties consistent with dignity/public justification bind.
|   +-- Accountability impedes decisive government.
|       +-- Reply: ex ante clarity and reason-giving improve legitimate capacity.
|   +-- Contract is historical fiction.
|       +-- Reply: strongest version is normative test of justification.
|   +-- Universal rights impose one culture.
|       +-- Reply: universality can be dialogic and minimum, not culturally uniform.
|   +-- No general political obligation exists.
|       +-- Reply: just institutions and reciprocal cooperation generate conditional duties,
|           not unconditional submission.
