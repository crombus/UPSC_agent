                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Logical positivism joins radical empiricism with modern logic. A factual statement is
cognitively meaningful only if analytic or empirically verifiable; metaphysical
sentences are therefore classified not as false but as pseudo-propositions without
factual content. Necessary propositions are explained linguistically as analytic or
conventional rather than synthetic a priori truths. The programme clarifies science
and grammar but faces decisive internal pressures: universal laws, observation
foundations, strong-versus-weak verification, self-application and the analytic-
synthetic distinction.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Logical positivism transformed philosophy by demanding public criteria of factual
significance and by exposing how grammar can simulate ontology. Yet its ambition to
reduce every meaningful factual sentence to analytic truth or empirical verification
could not accommodate scientific universals, theory-laden observation or its own
criterion without retreat. Its strongest legacy is not the original principle as a
law of all meaning, but a disciplined demand to specify evidence, logical form and the
kind of significance a philosophical utterance claims.
