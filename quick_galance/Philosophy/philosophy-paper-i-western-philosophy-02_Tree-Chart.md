                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Rationalism holds that experience alone cannot ground necessity, universality or
indubitable first principles; reason, innate cognitive resources, intuition and
deduction disclose reality's intelligible structure. Descartes begins from doubt and
admits God, mind and matter; Spinoza rigorously reduces substance to one immanent
God-or-Nature; Leibniz replaces extended substance with infinitely many simple monads.
Their shared rationalist method therefore generates incompatible metaphysics,
mind-body theories and accounts of freedom.

Rationalism succeeds in explaining why necessary knowledge cannot be a mere copy of
contingent sensation, but it does not deliver one metaphysics. Descartes' foundational
certainty depends on a vulnerable divine guarantee; Spinoza gains systematic unity at
the cost of contingency and ordinary free will; Leibniz preserves individuality and a
compatibilist freedom through a harmony whose divine design is difficult to test. The
tradition's strength is rational architecture, and its weakness is the metaphysical
weight required to secure that architecture.
