                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Cārvāka restricts valid knowledge to perception and therefore refuses to admit
supersensible entities supported only by inference or testimony. Its epistemology
generates its four-element materialism, body-self theory, emergent account of
consciousness and rejection of ātman, karma, rebirth, mokṣa, Vedic authority and
Īśvara. Its strongest contribution is epistemic economy and the challenge to vyāpti;
its deepest weakness is self-reference, because a universal denial of inference appears
to use reasoning it rejects. Refined and Jayarāśi-style readings soften this problem.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Cārvāka is philosophically strongest as a demand that ontology not outrun warranted
experience and as a challenge to inference from finite cases to transcendent entities.
Its body-materialism and anti-ritualism follow coherently from that premise. As a total
epistemology, however, strict perception-only doctrine is self-referentially unstable
and too restrictive for science and social knowledge. A refined worldly empiricism and
Jayarāśi's dialectical scepticism preserve its critical force without defending every
doxographical caricature.
