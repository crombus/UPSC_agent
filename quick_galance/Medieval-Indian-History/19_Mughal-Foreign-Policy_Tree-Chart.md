                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Mughal foreign policy joined frontier security, balance of power,
dynastic prestige, intelligence and trade. Kabul-Qandahar formed a
defensible commercial gateway; Balkh was a forward buffer. The empire
usually preferred diplomacy and anti-sectarian realism, but Qandahar
and Balkh showed that prestige or battlefield success could not defeat
fortification, supply, climate and local political hostility.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Mughal foreign policy was broadly realist: it resisted sectarian blocs,
used diplomacy and intelligence, and treated Kabul-Qandahar as the
defensive-commercial hinge of India. Its failures came when forward
prestige exceeded logistical and political sustainability, as at Balkh
and the repeated Qandahar sieges, not from absence of strategic purpose.
