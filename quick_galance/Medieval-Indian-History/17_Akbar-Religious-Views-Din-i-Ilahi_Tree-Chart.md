                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Akbar's religious policy evolved from fiscal accommodation and debate
to an imperial ideology of sulh-i-kul. The Ibadat Khana exposed
sectarian conflict; the Mahzar limited clerical monopoly; Tauhid-i-Ilahi
created a small elite discipleship around ethical kingship. It was not
a mass new religion, papal infallibility or modern secularism.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Akbar's religious syncretism was less a creed than a progression from
inquiry to imperial ethics. By subordinating sectarian monopoly to
public order and sulh-i-kul, he supplied a plural empire with a common
political language; Tauhid-i-Ilahi remained a narrow discipleship whose
symbolic importance exceeded its membership or institutional reach.
