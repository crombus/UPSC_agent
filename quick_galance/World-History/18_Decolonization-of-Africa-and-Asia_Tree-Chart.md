                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Empire ended when nationalist mobilisation raised the political and coercive
cost of rule while war exhaustion reduced metropolitan capacity and willingness
to pay that cost. The postwar language of self-determination, Japanese
destruction of European invincibility, UN pressure and superpower competition
further weakened imperial legitimacy. Routes differed according to settler
presence, strategic value, social composition, insurgency and the existence of
a credible negotiating partner. Independence was a real transfer of sovereignty
but not an automatic transfer of institutional depth or economic autonomy:
colonial borders, narrow administrations, commodity dependence, unresolved
land distribution and external intervention constrained successor states.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Decolonisation was historically legitimate and increasingly unavoidable, but
its timing and form arose from an interaction between colonial resistance and
metropolitan capacity, not from generosity or a single global timetable.
Negotiated Ghana, partitioned India, insurgent Malaya, settler-war Algeria and
hurried Congo demonstrate distinct routes. Independence ended foreign
sovereignty while leaving boundaries, economies, administrations and land
structures built for imperial purposes. Those inheritances constrained new
states without predetermining failure: political choices, institutional
adaptation and external intervention explain why outcomes diverged. Political
decolonisation was rapid; economic and social decolonisation remained unfinished.
