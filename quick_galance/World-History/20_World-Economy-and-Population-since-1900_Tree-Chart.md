                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Since 1900 the world economy has moved from European-centred liberal
integration through war and Depression, into a deliberately designed postwar
order, then oil and debt shocks, and finally intensified globalisation whose
networks transmit prosperity and crisis alike. Integration did not produce
uniform convergence because industrial capacity, state policy, commodity
dependence and debt differed sharply. Population change followed an equally
uneven transition: public health lowered mortality before fertility adjusted,
creating rapid growth in some regions while others later aged. Economic output,
distribution, environmental limits, health and demographic rights must
therefore be analysed as one connected history rather than separate statistics.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The twentieth-century world economy became more organised and more integrated,
but not more uniform. Institutions after 1944 stabilised parts of capitalism,
industrial and policy capacity enabled some poorer states to converge, while
commodity dependence and debt narrowed others' choices. The Depression and
2008 crisis show that integration transmits collapse as well as growth.
Demographically, falling mortality was a human achievement whose delayed
fertility response created pressure, followed elsewhere by ageing. Sustainable
judgment must therefore combine output with distribution, public health,
rights and environmental limits. The New Deal illustrates the same balance:
it rescued confidence and democratic legitimacy without alone ending the slump.
