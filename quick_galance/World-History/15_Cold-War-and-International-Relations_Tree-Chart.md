                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The Cold War was organised hostility between the United States and the USSR
through ideology, alliances, economic instruments, arms competition and limited
wars without direct general war between them. It arose from a postwar security
dilemma: Soviet pursuit of an eastern European buffer appeared expansionist to
the West, while containment appeared encircling to Moscow. Ideology determined
how each side interpreted power, and nuclear danger restrained direct conflict
while globalising indirect conflict. Yet bipolarity never erased local agency,
non-alignment or fractures within communism. Detente regulated rivalry rather
than ending it; the Cold War ended when Soviet structural weakness, reform,
popular mobilisation and Moscow's refusal to use old-style force converged.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The Cold War was a security dilemma sharpened by incompatible ideologies and
made globally dangerous by nuclear and alliance power. It imposed a bipolar
structure, but never a perfectly bipolar world: Korea, Vietnam and the Middle
East retained local causes; non-aligned states sought bargaining space; and
China's conflict with the USSR fractured the communist camp. Detente showed
that rivals could regulate danger without reconciling. The conflict ended not
through one summit or a simple western victory, but when Soviet economic and
legitimacy problems met Gorbachev's reforms, popular mobilisation and the
decisive choice not to preserve the bloc through force.
