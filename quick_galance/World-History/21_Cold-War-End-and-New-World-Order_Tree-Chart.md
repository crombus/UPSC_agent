                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The Cold War ended through interaction, not a single victory. Soviet economic
stagnation and strategic burden made reform urgent; glasnost legitimised
criticism faster than perestroika restored performance; national movements and
republican institutions supplied alternatives; Yeltsin created a rival Russian
centre; and Gorbachev's refusal to preserve the bloc through mass force made
change reversible neither in Eastern Europe nor the USSR. German reunification
joined popular revolution to legal diplomacy. The post-1991 order produced a
US unipolar moment, Security Council alignment and European enlargement, but
Yugoslavia, disputed intervention, unequal globalisation and continuing Global
South agency disproved any final or universally accepted Western settlement.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The Cold War ended because a weakening Soviet system attempted reform, released
criticism and national mobilisation, and then chose not to restore control
through mass force. Gorbachev was indispensable but did not act alone; Eastern
European societies, republican institutions, Yeltsin, the failed coup and
international diplomacy converted reform into dissolution. The order that
followed was genuinely unipolar in military capability for a time and enabled
exceptional UN alignment and European enlargement. Yet Yugoslav war,
post-Soviet social disruption, contested NATO action, unequal globalisation and
continuing postcolonial agency showed that history had not reached a uniform
liberal endpoint.
