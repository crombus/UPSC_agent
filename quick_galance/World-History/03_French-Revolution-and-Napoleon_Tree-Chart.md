                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The French Revolution transformed the legal and political basis of society more
completely than it transformed wealth or social power. Legal privilege, estate
hierarchy and dynastic sovereignty were attacked by a multi-class coalition
whose leadership shifted under fiscal collapse, bread stress, war and popular
mobilisation. The Jacobin phase joined democratic innovation to coercive
emergency. Napoleon preserved anti-feudal law, merit and administrative
rationalisation while ending republican liberty through empire and conquest.
The Revolution survived both Terror and restoration because legitimacy itself
had moved from king and estate toward citizen and nation.

                 QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The French Revolution destroyed the legitimacy of legal privilege and dynastic
sovereignty more decisively than it eliminated economic or social hierarchy.
Its democratic claims could produce both citizenship and Terror; Napoleon could
preserve equality before law while extinguishing republican liberty. The
Revolution's enduring relevance therefore lies in a double inheritance: the
universal standards by which power is judged and the recurring struggle over
who is excluded from those standards.
