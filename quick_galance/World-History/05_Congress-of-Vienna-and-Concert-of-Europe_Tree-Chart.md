                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Vienna achieved substantial success on the problem it recognised: preventing
another French hegemony and managing disputes among great powers. It created
balance through dynastic restoration, buffers and territorial compensation,
then institutionalised consultation through the Concert. Its weakness was the
problem it refused to recognise: legitimacy among peoples. Liberal, national
and social demands were treated as police threats. The order survived through
reintegration and selective adaptation, but its denied nationality question
returned in 1830, 1848 and the later Italian and German unifications.

                 QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The Vienna order was neither a cynical failure nor a democratic peace. It
contained France without humiliating it, restored strategic balance and
normalised consultation, producing a durable framework for great-power
management. The same system denied national consent and used censorship and
intervention against liberal change. Its settlements survived when they
adapted, as in Belgium, and collapsed where national aspirations acquired force.
Vienna's legacy is thus effective diplomacy joined to an unresolved legitimacy
problem.
