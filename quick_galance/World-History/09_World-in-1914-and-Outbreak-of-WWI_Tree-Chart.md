                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Sarajevo triggered the First World War but did not by itself cause it. A
militarised European system of counterweighted alignments, naval and imperial
rivalry, unresolved national claims and Balkan insecurity made escalation
dangerously likely. Yet likelihood was not inevitability: earlier crises had
been contained. In July 1914, Vienna chose war against Serbia, Berlin enabled
that choice, Russia mobilised, German planning widened the conflict through
Belgium, and Britain entered. Balance of power explains the structure through
which the crisis spread, but not the whole purpose or motive of every belligerent.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The European balance-of-power system transformed local danger into general
danger, but it did not compel war or supply every state's purpose. The war
began in an Austro-Serb nationality conflict, widened through German support
and Russian mobilisation, and became western and global through operational
planning and Britain's response to Belgium. Responsibility is therefore
layered, with particularly heavy weight on Vienna's decision and Berlin's
support, while the contained crises before 1914 prove that July's catastrophe
remained a choice within a dangerous system rather than an automatic outcome.
