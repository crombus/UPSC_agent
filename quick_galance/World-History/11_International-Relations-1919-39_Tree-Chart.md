                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The interwar order did not move directly from Versailles to another world war.
It passed through a real but conditional stabilisation in the 1920s and a
cumulative collapse in the 1930s. The League pioneered international
cooperation and resolved limited disputes, but collective security required
states to accept military and economic risks in advance, which its leading
members would not do. American credit, Dawes and Locarno temporarily eased
tensions; Depression destroyed that foundation. Manchuria established
impunity, Abyssinia discredited sanctions, separate bargains fractured
deterrence, and appeasement treated expansionist aims as finite grievances.
Repeated non-enforcement taught aggressors that each further revision was cheap.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The interwar system was neither stillborn nor securely founded. The League
proved that international administration and limited dispute settlement were
possible, while Dawes and Locarno showed that reconciliation could work under
favourable economic conditions. But collective security failed when major
states withheld advance commitments and treated each crisis separately.
Depression narrowed cooperation, unpunished aggression altered expectations,
and appeasement removed deterrence while assuming demands were finite. Hitler's
expansionism drove the final crisis, but institutional weakness and repeated
state choices made that expansion progressively easier to execute.
