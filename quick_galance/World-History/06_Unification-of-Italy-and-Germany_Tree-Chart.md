                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
National feeling made unity imaginable, but organised states made it durable.
The Vienna order left Italians and Germans politically fragmented under
Austrian influence. The failure of liberal-national revolutions in 1848 shifted
initiative toward Piedmont-Sardinia and Prussia. Italy was made through a
visible compromise among Mazzinian legitimacy, Cavourian diplomacy,
Garibaldian mobilisation and monarchy. Germany was made more decisively through
Prussian administration, diplomacy and war. Both achieved political unity
without complete social or national integration, and German unification altered
the European balance much more sharply.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Italian and German unity were nationalist victories, but not simple victories
of popular nationalism. Ideas supplied legitimacy; capable monarchies selected
the boundaries, methods and institutions. Italy retained a visible mixture of
republican inspiration, popular conquest and elite diplomacy, whereas Prussia
more completely subordinated nationalism to state power. Both new states
remained internally unfinished, but Germany's concentration of military and
economic power decisively changed the European system without making the later
world war inevitable.
