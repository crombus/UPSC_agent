                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Twentieth-century Latin America was formally sovereign but often constrained
by informal US hegemony, unequal landholding, narrow export economies,
militarised politics and later debt dependence. External domination and
domestic inequality reinforced one another: local elites sought outside
protection, while external power defended strategic and commercial interests.
Yet influence never erased agency. Mexico institutionalised revolution, Brazil
industrialised under unequal and authoritarian arrangements, Guatemala's
reform was overturned, Cuba's revolution survived through Soviet alignment,
Nicaragua faced counter-pressure, and oil shaped Venezuelan populism. The
economic arc ran from commodity dependence through ISI to debt-led austerity
and liberalisation, with sovereignty real but policy space repeatedly narrowed.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Latin America's twentieth-century instability cannot be explained by either
US domination or domestic weakness alone. Informal hegemony constrained
sovereign choices, often through local elites and armies, while unequal land,
commodity dependence and fragile institutions created the openings through
which that leverage worked. Revolutionary and reformist projects repeatedly
demonstrated local agency, but their survival depended partly on the
international environment. ISI widened industrial capacity, debt narrowed
policy autonomy, and liberalisation restored openness without resolving
inequality. The region therefore shows constrained sovereignty, not absent
sovereignty, and persistent contestation rather than one uniform political path.
