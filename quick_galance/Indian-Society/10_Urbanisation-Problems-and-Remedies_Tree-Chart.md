                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Urbanisation is demographic, occupational, spatial and institutional change.
Large cities attract migrants because agglomeration offers diverse jobs,
matching, information networks and services, not wages alone. The same
concentration raises land and housing costs and can outpace formal service
supply, producing informality and segregation. Slums are not migrant moral
failure; they emerge from interacting income, land, tenure, housing and planning
systems. Smart or conventional infrastructure is just only when poor residents
gain service, tenure, livelihood access and voice.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Urbanisation is socially progressive when the productivity networks that draw
people are matched by affordable housing, services, tenure, mobility and voice.
Informality and segregation are produced by land and governance arrangements,
not by migrants or city size alone. Smart-city technology can improve delivery
but cannot substitute for inclusive planning. The decisive remedy is to align
municipal and metropolitan capacity with the functional city and protect
livelihood access while upgrading vulnerable settlements.
