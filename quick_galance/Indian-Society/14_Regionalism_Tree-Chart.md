                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Regionalism is the political or social articulation of attachment, interest
and grievance around a territorially grounded region. A region may be organised
through language, ecology, history, economy, ethnicity, tribe, culture or
administrative experience. Regional consciousness is not inherently
anti-national: it can deepen federal participation and correct uneven
development. It becomes exclusionary when cultural pride is converted into
hostility, nativism, discrimination, coercive autonomy or secession. Analysis
must separate diversity, regional identity, regional disparity and regionalism,
then trace how institutions translate each into accommodation or conflict.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Regionalism is best understood as a mode of federal claim-making rather than an
automatic threat to national unity. India's diversity becomes politically
destabilising when recognition is denied, development is persistently unequal,
or elites convert grievance into exclusion. Responsive federalism must combine
cultural recognition, balanced development, intergovernmental negotiation,
migrant citizenship and protection for minorities within every region.
