              HIGH-YIELD UPSC TRAPS
• 200 BCE–250 CE was not one uniform pan-Indian boom
• NBPW is fine ware within wider urban material culture
• pottery style does not automatically reveal ethnicity
• one artefact or workshop does not define a city
• every artisan group was not a shreni
• guilds were not modern companies or banks
• specific deposits do not prove economy-wide banking
• routes were corridors, not one empire's fixed highways
• ports depended on agrarian and craft hinterlands
• amphorae/rouletted ware do not prove colonies
• Roman exchange was one part of Indian Ocean commerce
• Pliny's drain claim is rhetoric, not trade statistics
• coin find does not prove prosperity, market use or rule
• monetization did not eliminate barter/payment in kind
• religious patronage was not purely commercial investment
• urban growth did not displace rural dependence
• state influence did not equal total economic control
• Hundi belongs mainly to a later chronological context
• Araghatta is an adjacent technology demand
• later Gupta ports cannot all be projected into this window
• third-century change was not synchronous urban collapse.

                 QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The post-Mauryan craft-commercial florescence was not produced by
coins, guilds, states or Roman trade in isolation. It rested on
agrarian hinterlands, raw materials, skilled labour, storage,
transport and everyday internal exchange. Shrenis, nigamas,
caravans, political courts and religious establishments helped
organize accumulation and circulation, while route junctions and
ports concentrated production, services and consumption. Diverse
coin traditions expanded monetary possibilities without eliminating
barter or payment in kind. Mediterranean, Central Asian and Southeast
Asian contacts intensified a fundamentally multi-directional Indian
Ocean economy. Urbanization consequently followed different regional
rhythms, generated both mobility and inequality, and after the third
century underwent selective contraction and functional change rather
than one universal commercial or urban collapse.
