                   CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The post-Gupta centuries were not a political or cultural void.
They witnessed regional reorganization through competing dynasties,
shifting capitals, samanta relationships, agrarian expansion, land
grants, Brahmana settlements, monasteries and new political centres.
Harsha created a major Kannauj-centred north Indian hegemony, but
neither restored the Gupta Empire nor ruled India uniformly.

                 QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Post-Gupta India is best understood as a period of regional
reorganization rather than civilizational decline. Harsha used the
Pushyabhuti-Maukhari connection, campaigns, samantas, alliances,
itinerant kingship and religious patronage to create a substantial
Kannauj-centred hegemony, but Gauda remained contested, Kamarupa was
an ally rather than a province, and Pulakeshin II marked the southern
limit. Bana, Xuanzang and Aihole together demonstrate why courtly,
pilgrim and rival records must be triangulated. Beyond Harsha,
eastern India developed through local dynasties, grants, agrarian
expansion, Brahmana settlements and monastic networks. The Palas
turned Bengal-Bihar into the decisive late-Indian institutional and
trans-Himalayan centre of Buddhism without making eastern society
uniformly Buddhist. Harsha's death therefore led not to universal
anarchy but to continuing regional competition, culminating in new
political systems and the later struggle for Kannauj.
