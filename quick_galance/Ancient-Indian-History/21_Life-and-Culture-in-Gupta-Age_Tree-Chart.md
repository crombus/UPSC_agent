              HIGH-YIELD UPSC TRAPS
• Golden Age was not golden for every social group
• cultural achievement does not equal social equality
• gold coins do not measure mass prosperity
• grants were neither harmless piety nor automatically full feudalism
• Kulyavapa/dronavapa are land measures, not coins
• agrarian terms do not imply one empire-wide rate
• one irrigation record does not prove a uniform system
• all artisan groups were not guilds
• commercial change was regional, not total collapse
• Dharmashastra is normative, not a social census
• varna and jati were not one fixed identical system
• untouchability did not simply begin in one Gupta reign
• dasa/dasi meanings depend on context
• women neither uniformly flourished nor uniformly declined
• Eran 510 does not prove universal sati
• Mitakshara/Dayabhaga are later, not Gupta law
• Sanskrit did not eliminate Prakrit or regional speech
• Kalidasa's exact court biography is uncertain
• Panini is pre-Gupta
• Aryabhata did not simply invent zero
• Brahmagupta's zero rules belong to 628 CE
• Charaka/Sushruta texts have layered chronologies
• Nalanda was not a modern university founded on one fixed date
• Gupta temples were not yet later giant complexes
• Ajanta's major fifth-century patronage is Vakataka
• no single uniform Gupta sculptural style existed
• royal Vaishnavism did not erase Buddhism/Jainism
• Faxian was a selective pilgrim, not an empire-wide observer.

                 QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The Gupta-Vakataka age deserves recognition as a major classical
phase because Sanskrit literature, mathematical astronomy, sculpture,
structural temples, religious iconography, metallurgy and learned
institutions achieved durable forms. Yet culture was inseparable from
material and social power. Land grants and agrarian expansion supported
patrons and institutions while strengthening intermediaries and claims
over peasant surplus and labour. Varna ideals, exclusion, servitude and
patriarchal constraints existed alongside mobility, women donors,
artisan corporations and plural religious patronage. Sanskrit acquired
transregional prestige without erasing Prakrits or regional traditions;
Aryabhata synthesized inherited and connected knowledge rather than
creating mathematics ex nihilo. Ajanta's Vakataka context, Nalanda's
gradual development and the limits of Faxian further demonstrate why
attribution and source criticism matter. The period was therefore
classically transformative, but its benefits and visibility were
selective, unequal and regionally varied.
