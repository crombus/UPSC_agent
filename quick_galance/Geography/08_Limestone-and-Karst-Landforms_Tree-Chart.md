                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Karst is a coupled surface-subsurface drainage system produced when weakly
acidic water dissolves soluble, jointed carbonate rock. Water disappears
through enlarged fractures, creates closed depressions and caves, and returns
at springs; carbonate may later precipitate as speleothem. This rapid conduit
hydrology gives high yields but little natural filtration and creates collapse,
pollution and quarrying risk. Cave deposits are also dated climate archives,
but host-rock age, cave excavation, speleothem growth, human occupation and
formal geological boundary are separate chronologies.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Karst demonstrates how rock chemistry can reorganise an entire drainage system:
the surface appears dry because water has moved into fractures and conduits.
That hidden connectivity creates spectacular landforms, productive springs,
rapid pollution transfer and collapse risk. Meghalaya's caves add global
palaeoclimate value through the Mawmluh GSSP, but one proxy cannot prove a
uniform drought or social collapse. Conservation must therefore operate from
catchment to cave, regulate extraction and visitation, protect recharge and
distinguish natural geomorphology from human rock-cut heritage.
