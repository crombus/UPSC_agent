                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
A glacier is flowing land ice governed by mass balance. Snow accumulates,
compacts into firn and ice, moves downslope, erodes by abrasion and plucking,
transports debris, and deposits unsorted till or sorted meltwater sediment.
Advance and retreat describe the position of the terminus, not reversal of ice
flow. Himalayan change must be analysed as a spatially uneven water-timing,
sediment, slope and hazard problem. A GLOF is produced when a vulnerable lake
and dam meet a trigger and exposed downstream assets; warming alone does not
prove the cause of a particular event.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Glacial landscapes are records of moving ice and changing climate, while
Himalayan glaciers remain active stores that redistribute water across seasons.
Their retreat is heterogeneous and does not imply that major rivers suddenly
cease, but it can weaken lean-season buffering, destabilise slopes and enlarge
moraine-dammed lakes. GLOF risk arises from the conjunction of lake, dam,
trigger, valley and exposure. Policy must therefore replace one glacier count
or retreat rate with basin-specific monitoring, hazard-informed infrastructure,
early warning and water management adapted to changing timing and uncertainty.
