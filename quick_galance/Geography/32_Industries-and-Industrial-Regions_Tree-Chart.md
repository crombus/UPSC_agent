                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Industrial location reflects the dominant cost and linkage at a particular
production stage. Raw material, energy, labour, water, market, transport,
capital, technology, policy and agglomeration exert different pulls.
Containerisation and value chains weakened some mine-site ties but strengthened
port, supplier, skill and reliability advantages. Industrial corridors are not
roads; they are planned networks of freight, utilities, nodes, labour and
urbanisation. A notified corridor or incentive does not prove production or
balanced regional development.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Industrial geography has shifted from mine and mill toward ports, corridors,
supplier ecosystems and knowledge workers, yet distance, energy and materials
remain real. Weber's enduring lesson is to identify the dominant cost, while
modern analysis adds networks, inertia and state-created agglomeration.
Corridors and incentives reduce locational friction only when local firms,
skills, utilities and feeder links convert a passing flow into durable regional
development.
