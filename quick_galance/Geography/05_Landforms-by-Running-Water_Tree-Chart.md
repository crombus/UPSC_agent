                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
A river is a basin-scale water-and-sediment system seeking graded adjustment,
not a fixed channel divided into rigid stages. Rain, snowmelt and groundwater
create discharge; gradient and channel form govern energy; rock and structure
guide erosion; calibre and supply govern transport; loss of competence produces
deposition. Uplift, capture, climate variability, dams, embankments and
inter-basin transfers continually disturb adjustment. A claimed donor surplus
is therefore seasonal and model-dependent, while downstream ecology, sediment,
livelihoods and federal consent remain part of the same river system.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Rivers integrate entire landscapes, so an intervention at one point changes
water, sediment, ecology and risk elsewhere. Their landforms record the balance
between energy and load, while India's drainage records both ancient structure
and continuing Himalayan uplift. Interlinking may improve reliability in a
carefully modelled basin pair, but no permanent surplus or universal flood-
drought cure can be assumed. The defensible approach combines basin-scale water
and sediment budgets, environmental flows, demand management, transparent
project status, federal consent and adaptive operation under climate uncertainty.
