                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Politics is a public process through which conflict over valued goods receives an
authoritative, generally binding settlement. Competing theories interpret that process
through different images of society: liberalism sees plural individuals and groups
whose interests can be reconciled; Marxism sees antagonistic classes and managed
domination; communitarianism sees socially embedded persons whose flourishing depends
on shared practices and a common good. No lens is universally sufficient: political
judgment must ask when conflict is negotiable, structurally unequal or capable of
cooperative transformation.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Politics cannot be reduced either to bargaining among formally equal interests or to
class domination alone. It also depends on authority, shared meanings, cooperation and
social bonds. Communitarianism corrects liberal atomism by showing that persons and
values are socially formed, but community is not self-validating: its common good must
remain open to dissent, equality and rights. The most defensible account treats liberty,
equality and fraternity as mutually corrective rather than mutually exclusive ideals.
