                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The state is a territorially organised political community possessing government,
sovereignty and a successful claim to legitimate coercive authority. It must be
distinguished from society, government, nation and civil society. Civil society can
mediate and resist state power, but it can also reproduce domination. Nation and state
often overlap imperfectly; nationalism seeks political organisation around national
identity, while internationalism responds to problems and obligations exceeding state
borders. Secularism and multiculturalism test how a common state can govern religious
and cultural plurality without abandoning equal citizenship or insulating internal
domination.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The modern state remains indispensable because territorial authority, law and
coercive coordination cannot be replaced by society or association alone. Yet it is
not the whole of social life, and its legitimacy depends on maintaining distinctions
between state and government, enabling autonomous civil society, accommodating plural
national and cultural identities, and cooperating beyond borders. Recognition and
national solidarity are defensible only when bounded by equal citizenship, individual
