                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
External sovereignty gives states legal independence, but effective autonomy depends
on material capacity and the structure of international relations. Colonial rule
suppressed sovereignty directly; neo-colonial dependence can constrain formally
independent states through capital, trade, technology and institutions; power blocs
narrow strategic choice; globalisation intensifies economic, communicative,
environmental and cultural interdependence. Sovereignty is therefore neither untouched
nor simply abolished: it is constrained, negotiated, pooled, delegated and transformed
under unequal conditions.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Globalisation exposes the inadequacy of treating sovereignty as complete insulation.
States remain legal actors and indispensable providers of law, welfare and enforcement,
but their effective choices are shaped by unequal markets, finance, technology,
security dependence and cross-border problems. The most defensible view is
transformational: sovereignty survives, yet its exercise is increasingly negotiated
through interdependence, institutions and asymmetric bargaining, making capacity and
accountability as important as formal independence.
