                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Justice is the reasoned ordering of benefits, burdens, rights, opportunities, power and
obligations. It cannot be reduced either to valid law, aggregate utility, fair procedure
or equal outcomes. Legal justice asks whether rules are fairly administered and morally
defensible; political justice democratises power; socio-economic justice transforms
conditions that deny weaker sections real opportunity. Justice integrates liberty,
equality and fraternity: fair procedure remains necessary, but becomes sufficient only
when the background order and minimum conditions are also defensible.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Justice requires more than obedience to valid rules and more than a preferred pattern
of outcomes. It joins impartial procedure with moral scrutiny of law and the background
conditions in which people compete, participate and live. Liberty, equality and
fraternity become politically meaningful only when ordered together, minimum needs and
equal standing are secured, relevant differences are justified publicly, and neither
administrative discretion nor inherited privilege escapes review.
