                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Contemporary democratic theory asks who actually governs in mass society and whether
electoral competition, group bargaining, participation, class transformation or public
deliberation best realises popular rule. Theories of social change then distinguish
revolutionary rupture from evolutionary and piecemeal reform. Development cannot be
reduced to growth or Westernisation: it must be judged through institutional capacity,
freedom, equality, participation, ecological sustainability, nation-building and human
well-being. Democracy supplies correction and legitimacy; development supplies
capacity and opportunity; each becomes dangerous when detached from the other.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Modern democracy cannot be reduced to elite competition, group bargaining, mass
participation or deliberation alone. Each addresses a real feature of large-scale
politics and a distinct danger. Social change likewise requires both structural
ambition and institutions capable of correction. Development is legitimate only when
growth and state capacity expand freedom, equality, participation and ecological
security. The democratic-developmental task is therefore to combine governability with
contestability, transformation with restraint, and present welfare with future justice.
