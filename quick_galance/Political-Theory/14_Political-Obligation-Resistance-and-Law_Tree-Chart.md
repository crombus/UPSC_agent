                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Political obligation asks why and how far citizens should obey authority. Compliance
caused by fear is not yet moral obligation, and valid enactment is not automatically a
just law. The strongest theories make obedience conditional on consent, rights, common
good, legality and public justification. Resistance becomes defensible when these
grounds fail, but civil disobedience is distinguished by publicity, principle,
non-violence, proportionality and acceptance of legal consequences. Jurisprudence and
punishment debates show the same pattern: legality, legitimacy, purpose and severity
must be analysed separately.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
A stable polity needs a presumption of obedience, but no government acquires moral
infallibility from legal validity alone. Political obligation is strongest where laws
protect rights, serve a publicly defensible common good and operate through rule of
law. When those conditions fail, resistance may be justified, yet civil disobedience
must remain public, principled, proportionate and accountable. The same discipline
governs punishment: responsibility matters, but severity must be lawful, proportionate,
humane and open to prevention, reform and correction.
