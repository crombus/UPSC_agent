                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Liberalism is a family of doctrines united by the moral worth of the individual,
liberty, consent, rights, rule-limited government and opposition to arbitrary power.
Its strands disagree over what most threatens liberty: coercive government, deprivation
and unequal social conditions, or an interventionist welfare state. Classical
liberalism restricts the state, welfare liberalism uses the state to enable freedom,
and neoliberal or libertarian arguments seek renewed limits on redistribution and
planning. Liberal constitutionalism is more durable than any single liberal economic
programme.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Liberalism's history is an argument within a shared commitment to individual liberty
and limited, justified power. Classical liberals fear the state, welfare liberals fear
the social conditions that make formal freedom hollow, and neoliberals fear welfare
coercion and planning failure. No strand resolves every tension between property,
equality and effective agency. Liberalism is therefore most defensible as a plural,
self-correcting constitutional tradition rather than a fixed laissez-faire formula.
