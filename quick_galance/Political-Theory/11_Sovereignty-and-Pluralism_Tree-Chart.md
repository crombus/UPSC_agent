                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Sovereignty is the state's final legal authority internally and its independence
externally. Classical monism requires one legally supreme, permanent and indivisible
source of law, but legal finality does not establish moral infallibility, unlimited
government or equal practical capacity. Popular sovereignty relocates legitimacy in
the people; pluralism shows that social authority, loyalty and law also arise through
associations. The defensible synthesis preserves state coordination and legal finality
while constitutionally limiting governments and requiring authority to justify itself.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Sovereignty remains indispensable as a legal account of final state authority and
external independence, but it becomes dangerous when transferred uncritically to
governments or treated as moral omnipotence. Popular sovereignty supplies democratic
legitimacy, while pluralism exposes the independent value and power of associations.
A constitutional polity therefore needs a legally coherent state, limited and
accountable government, protected associational autonomy and a residual public capacity
to coordinate conflict and defend weaker interests.
