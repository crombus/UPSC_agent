                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Power is the capacity to secure compliance or produce intended effects; legitimacy is
the belief that rule is rightful; authority is power accepted as legitimate. Political
power is supported by economic resources, organisation and ideology as well as formal
office. Class, elite, feminist and pluralist theories disagree over where power is
concentrated, while Gramsci, Lukes and Foucault show how consent, agendas, preferences
and self-discipline can conceal domination. Arendt, Macpherson and Gandhi recover
power as collective or developmental capacity rather than domination alone.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Power is unavoidable in collective life, but it is neither confined to government nor
made rightful by success. Authority requires legitimacy, and legitimacy itself must be
tested because consent can be shaped through culture, agendas, preferences and
surveillance. Class, elite, feminist and pluralist approaches expose different centres
of domination; Arendt, Macpherson and Gandhi show that power can also mean acting
together and developing the capacity to resist. Democratic power must therefore be
lawful, answerable, contestable and enabling rather than merely effective.
