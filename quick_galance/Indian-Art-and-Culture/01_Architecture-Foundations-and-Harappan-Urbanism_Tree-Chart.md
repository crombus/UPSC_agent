                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Harappan architecture is best read as civic urbanism rather than dynastic
monumentality. Its strongest evidence lies in planned street grids,
standardised burnt brick, functional zoning, domestic water access, public
works and maintainable drainage across varied ecological settings. These forms
support inferences about coordinated technical norms and labour organisation,
but the undeciphered script and absence of securely identified palaces or
temples prevent confident claims about named rulers, state religion or rigid
social hierarchy. The most accurate interpretation therefore moves from form
to function cautiously: common planning norms were adapted regionally rather
than copied identically.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Harappan architecture's distinction lies less in spectacular royal monuments
than in repeatable civic systems: gridded streets, standard brick, zoned
settlements, domestic water facilities and maintainable drainage. Dholavira,
Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal and Kalibangan show that common planning norms
were adapted to local needs. These remains strongly support technical
coordination and civic investment, but the undeciphered script and uncertain
building labels prohibit confident reconstruction of kingship, religion or
fixed hierarchy. Harappan urbanism is therefore both architecturally advanced
and methodologically dependent on disciplined limits of archaeological inference.
