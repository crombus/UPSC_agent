                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indian dance is a composite art in which body, gesture, drama, music, poetry
and rasa work together. The Natya Shastra and Abhinaya Darpana provide a shared
technical language, but the eight Sangeet Natak Akademi-recognised classical
forms possess different regional, social, devotional and patronage histories.
Modern "classical" status is an institutional layer placed upon older practices,
not proof of an unchanged line from antiquity. Bharatanatyam's movement from
Sadir and hereditary performers to the modern stage makes this especially
clear. Dance vocabulary can illuminate temple sculpture, but sculpted posture
proves a valued movement idiom, not an unchanged modern choreography.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indian dance combines a shared aesthetic grammar with irreducibly regional
performance histories. The Natya Shastra links movement to drama, music,
sculpture and rasa, while each recognised form embodies a distinct ecology of
temple, court, monastery, troupe, community and modern stage. Twentieth-century
revival preserved important repertoires but also reconfigured ownership and
respectability, especially in Bharatanatyam. Institutional recognition can aid
safeguarding, yet it cannot substitute for performers, teachers and living
contexts. Continuity is real, but it survives through adaptation rather than
unchanged transmission.
