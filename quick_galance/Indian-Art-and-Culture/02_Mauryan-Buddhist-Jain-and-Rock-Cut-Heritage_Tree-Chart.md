                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Early Indian monumental architecture is best understood as a layered record of
patronage, technique, worship and memory rather than a sequence of isolated
dynastic styles. Mauryan pillars joined polished stone, animal capitals and
site-specific inscriptions in an imperial communication system; royally
dedicated Ajivika caves expose the limits of a simple court-versus-popular
binary; stupas grew through donor and dynastic accretion; chaitya and vihara
plans made ritual and residence legible; and Ajanta, Ellora,
Udayagiri-Khandagiri and Elephanta show that sacred landscapes were reused
across centuries. Inscriptions strengthen attribution but remain selective
royal or donor texts, while religious coexistence must not be romanticised as
simultaneous, centrally planned pluralism.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Mauryan and post-Mauryan monuments are valuable because rock, stone and
inscription preserve patronage, technique, ritual movement and institutional
change across centuries. Their strongest lesson is accretion: Ashokan
foundations, Sunga rebuilding, Satavahana donors, Gupta additions and later
sectarian reuse coexist in the same landscapes. Yet durable monuments
over-represent patrons able to commission them, and inscriptions commemorate
rather than neutrally record. Rock-cut heritage therefore illuminates early
Indian art and history most effectively when form, patronage and text are read
together and their silences remain explicit.
