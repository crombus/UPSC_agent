                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Architecture under European rule was programmatic before it was merely
aesthetic: churches and forts projected Portuguese religious-maritime power,
French grids rationalised colonial towns, British revival styles appropriated
Indian forms into imperial public buildings, and New Delhi monumentalised the
Raj after 1911. Political independence did not create an instant architectural
break. State axes and institutional typologies survived while programme and
symbol changed. A more distinct Indian modernism emerged unevenly through
specific solutions: Baker changed client, cost, material and climate logic;
Correa, Kanvinde and Doshi adapted institutional modernism; and later public
projects continued to renegotiate colonial inheritance and modern heritage.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Colonial architecture organised Indian space to display religious,
administrative and imperial power, but it was never one imported style:
Portuguese, French and British programmes differed and adapted to local craft
and climate. Independence changed patron and symbolism more quickly than it
changed monumental planning or institutional typology. The clearest
architectural decolonisation occurred where designers changed whom buildings
served and how they responded to Indian cost and climate, as in Baker's work
and Doshi's housing. The survival of Samvidhan Sadan and loss of the Hall of
Nations show that national identity is still negotiated through both reuse and
protection of the built past.
