                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indian painting changed not only in style but in surface, scale, patronage and
audience. Rock images and murals were fixed to collective spaces; manuscripts
and albums became portable objects for monastic, royal and connoisseurial
viewing; colonial painting served officials and markets; nationalist painters
made technique a political argument; and folk and tribal forms remained tied
to community, ritual and material ecology even as markets expanded. The
mural-to-miniature shift enabled many regional courts and workshops to sustain
distinct schools, but survival is unequal: few surviving murals do not prove
little original production, and shared artists or Persianate resources do not
make Mughal, Deccani, Rajasthani and Pahari painting one style.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indian painting developed through changing supports and patrons rather than a
simple succession of styles. Monumental murals joined architecture and
collective devotion; portable manuscripts and albums allowed court workshops
and regional schools to multiply; colonial markets changed subject and
audience; and the Bengal School turned aesthetic choice into nationalist
argument. Folk and tribal traditions retained distinct material and ritual
ecologies while entering wider markets. Because murals perish, workshops move
and attributions change, the surviving corpus must be read as selected evidence,
not the totality of what painters produced or communities saw.
