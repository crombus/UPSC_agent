                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indian music is a shared theoretical system differentiated through regional
performance grammars, repertoires and patronage ecologies. Swara, raga, tala,
rasa and composition provide the common foundation; Hindustani and Carnatic
music organise parent scales, improvisation and forms differently without
becoming foreign-versus-indigenous opposites. Gharanas are not merely styles
but lineage-based systems for preserving specialised skill through changing
patrons. Temple, court, household, festival, concert, radio, recording and
state recognition successively redirected musical labour. Because oral
tradition produces layered memory, claims that one person invented an
instrument, genre or raga require strong source caution.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indian music's unity lies in swara, raga, tala and rasa, while its diversity
lies in how communities organise melody, improvisation, composition and
transmission. Hindustani gharanas and Carnatic temple-court-sabha lineages
responded differently to patronage without becoming sealed traditions.
Treatises and institutions widened transmission, but oral history makes
single-inventor claims hazardous. The survival of music after courts declined
shows that living traditions preserve substance by changing patronage, moving
from household and shrine to concert, radio, recording, festival and state
recognition.
