                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
India's literary culture is layered rather than sequential. Language family,
spoken language, script, social register, literary genre, constitutional
status, classical recognition and documentary-heritage status are separate
classifications that repeatedly overlap without mapping neatly onto one
another. Sanskrit, Pali, Prakrits, Persian and regional languages coexisted in
different ritual, courtly, dramatic, administrative and popular functions.
Texts are also material histories: oral composition, redaction, inscription,
manuscript copying, commentary, critical edition, digitisation and UNESCO
recognition are distinct events. A rigorous answer therefore separates the age
of a work from the age of its surviving manuscript or modern inscription.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
India's linguistic and literary history is not a march from Sanskrit to
vernaculars or from one script to another. Multiple families, scripts and
registers coexisted, and patronage, religion, theatre, administration and
regional publics assigned them different functions. Texts survive through oral
memory, inscription, copying, commentary and modern conservation, so their
dates must be disaggregated. Documentary recognition can improve visibility
and access, but it neither dates composition nor resolves attribution. The
best account therefore treats language as a social institution and manuscripts
as both texts and material witnesses.
