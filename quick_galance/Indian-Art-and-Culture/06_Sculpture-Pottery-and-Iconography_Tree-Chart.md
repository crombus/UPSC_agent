                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indian sculpture must be read through evidence rather than adjectives. A
six-variable method links material, technique, posture or gesture,
iconographic attribute, patron, site context and cross-site comparison.
Harappan labels remain uncertain because the script is undeciphered; Yaksha
cults show popular forms entering major religions; Gandhara, Mathura and
Amaravati demonstrate parallel regional responses rather than a single source
of iconic Buddha imagery; Gupta sculpture refines form; and South Indian
traditions integrate image, temple and performance. The Nataraja is a model:
its form predates the Cholas, while Chola lost-wax bronzes brought it to a
canonical, portable and ritual peak. Sculpture reveals patron-selected social
visibility, not a complete photograph of society.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indian sculpture is most revealing when medium, body, symbol, patron and site
are analysed together. Harappan objects demand caution because their names
cannot be verified; Gandhara's borrowed surface must be separated from its
Buddhist content; Mathura and Amaravati show parallel indigenous choices; and
Nataraja's Aihole-to-Chola arc distinguishes origin from canonical perfection.
Animal, deity and social imagery changed meaning across periods, so continuity
of motif does not imply continuity of doctrine. Sculpture is a durable witness
to selected belief, skill and social imagination, but never an unmediated
record of the whole society that produced it.
