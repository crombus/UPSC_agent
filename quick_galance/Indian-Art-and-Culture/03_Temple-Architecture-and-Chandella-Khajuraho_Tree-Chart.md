                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indian temple architecture is a grammar of sacred access expressed through
regional materials, patronage and changing emphasis. The worshipper moves from
threshold and hall toward the garbhagriha, while the exterior superstructure
marks the sanctum. Nagara, Dravida and the debated Vesara category are not
rigid boxes but families of solutions. Khajuraho represents mature Nagara
integration: high jagati, axial halls, clustered urushringas and a sculptural
skin that joins divine, courtly, musical, animal, ascetic and erotic registers.
Its "breadth of life" cannot be reduced to maithuna imagery. Across Pallava,
Chola, Chalukya, Hoysala, Vijayanagara and Nayaka traditions, temples record
technical experimentation, political legitimation, ritual movement and social
representation, while inscriptions and sculpture require source criticism.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indian temple architecture developed through shared ritual requirements and
regional solutions rather than a single linear style. Nagara concentrated
visual force around the sanctum spire, Dravida distributed it through axial
enclosures, vimanas and gateways, and Deccan builders tested both grammars in
new materials and plans. Khajuraho's importance lies in the union of political
autonomy, mature Nagara form and a sculpture programme broad enough to include
the sacred, social and sensual. Temple evidence is therefore a powerful record
of patronage and selected social ideals, but its commissioned images and later
heritage status must not be mistaken for complete social documentation.
