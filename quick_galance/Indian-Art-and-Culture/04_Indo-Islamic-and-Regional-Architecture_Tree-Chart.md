                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indo-Islamic architecture was neither a wholesale foreign import nor a simple
continuation of pre-Sultanate building. It emerged when large-scale scientific
arcuate construction, stronger lime mortar and Persian-Central Asian spatial
and ornamental vocabularies entered a building industry staffed by indigenous
craftsmen and conditioned by local materials, trabeate habits, climate and
patronage. The result was not one homogeneous style: Delhi's dynastic forms,
Bengal's rain-responsive roof, Malwa's ventilation and water works, Jaunpur's
screen facade, Bijapur's dome, Mughal material transitions and Rajput, Sikh and
Awadh adaptations remained distinct. Synthesis must therefore specify which
technique, material, motif and workshop practice interacted at a named monument.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indo-Islamic architecture is best understood as a transformation of building
practice rather than a meeting of two fixed civilisational styles. Scientific
arcuate construction and new spatial and ornamental vocabularies expanded what
builders could do, while trabeate methods, indigenous craftsmen, local motifs,
regional materials and climate shaped what they actually built. Qutb's layered
patronage, Bengal's roof, Malwa's water and ventilation, Mughal material
transitions and Rajput, Sikh and Awadh forms demonstrate diversity within
synthesis. The evidence also requires restraint: one spolia inscription cannot
define an era, one architect cannot own a workshop achievement, and reduced
imperial patronage did not extinguish the architectural idiom.
