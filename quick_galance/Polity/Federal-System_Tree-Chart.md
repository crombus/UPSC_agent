                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indian federalism divides authority constitutionally between Union and states,
yet deliberately gives the Union greater territorial, legislative,
administrative, fiscal and emergency capacity. It is neither a treaty-based
American federation nor a unitary delegation of power. It is a holding-together,
Union-weighted and asymmetric federation whose balance changes through
cooperation, competition, confrontation and judicial review. Since S.R. Bommai,
federalism is part of the basic structure: states are not sovereign or
indestructible, but within their allotted constitutional sphere they are not
mere administrative appendages.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indian federalism is constitutionally real but deliberately unequal in favour
of the Union. Its success lies not in removing conflict but in channelling
regional interests through courts, councils, finance, Parliament and elections.
Federalism as basic structure prevents states from becoming mere agencies,
while national integrity prevents them from becoming sovereign enclaves. The
future balance depends on predictable finance, meaningful consultation, neutral
constitutional offices and respect for diversity through workable asymmetry.
