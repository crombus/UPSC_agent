                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Comparative constitutionalism studies how different societies allocate power,
secure rights and manage conflict. A useful comparison does not list foreign
features mechanically: it identifies the common institutional problem, compares
legal rules and political operation, explains the historical context, and asks
why India borrowed, rejected or adapted a design. Constitutional transplants
work only through local party systems, federal bargains, conventions, courts
and administrative capacity.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Comparative design is valuable when it reveals choices rather than rankings.
India's Constitution borrowed widely but reordered every major device within
a republican, parliamentary, federal and transformative framework. Foreign
experience can illuminate reform, yet a constitutional mechanism carries its
party system, conventions, historical anxieties and enforcement capacity with
it. The right lesson is therefore not to copy the most visible institution,
but to adapt the safeguards that make its function legitimate.
