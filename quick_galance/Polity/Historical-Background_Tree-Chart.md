                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The Constitution of 1950 did not arise on a blank institutional field. British
statutes from 1773 to 1947 progressively centralised administration, separated
commercial from political control, widened legislative association, introduced
electoral and provincial machinery, and constructed the federal-administrative
framework later adapted by the Constituent Assembly. The continuity was mainly
structural; the rupture was normative and democratic. Universal adult
franchise, popular sovereignty, justiciable rights, republicanism and social
transformation replaced colonial accountability, restricted representation,
racial rule and communal electorates.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Colonial statutes supplied much of India's administrative vocabulary and the
Government of India Act 1935 supplied the largest institutional quarry. They
did not supply the Constitution's democratic legitimacy. The framers retained
workable structures, rejected communal and authoritarian devices, and placed
the inherited state under popular sovereignty, universal franchise, rights,
responsible government and constitutional review. The Constitution is thus
both culmination and transformation, not a colonial copy.
