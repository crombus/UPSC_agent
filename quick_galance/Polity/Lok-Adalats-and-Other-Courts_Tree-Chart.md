                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Access to justice requires more than a formal court hierarchy. Article 39A,
Article 21 jurisprudence and specialised statutes create legal-aid authorities,
Lok Adalats, Permanent Lok Adalats, Family Courts and Gram Nyayalayas with
different combinations of proximity, conciliation and adjudication. The central
exam rule is to distinguish consent, subject matter, power to decide merits,
legal effect and appeal. Faster disposal is legitimate only with voluntary
settlement, procedural fairness, competent staffing and constitutional review.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
India's plural justice system can make constitutional equality tangible only
when each forum performs its legally assigned role. Ordinary Lok Adalats derive
finality from genuine compromise; Permanent Lok Adalats possess only a bounded
public-utility merits power; Family Courts and Gram Nyayalayas remain statutory
courts. The reform goal should therefore move beyond disposal totals toward
informed consent, competent representation, local access, durable outcomes and
review against illegality.
