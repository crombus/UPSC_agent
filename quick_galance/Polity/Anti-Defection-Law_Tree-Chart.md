                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The Tenth Schedule seeks to protect the electoral mandate and cabinet
stability from legislator switching. It validly treats voluntary abandonment
and whip-defying votes as disqualification, subject to narrow exceptions.
Yet party control over virtually every vote can suppress deliberation, while
delay by partisan Speakers can distort majority tests. Sound reform must retain
anti-corruption protection but confine the whip, ensure independent and prompt
decision, and never convert merger doctrine into an individual-defection route.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The Tenth Schedule is a legitimate response to corrupt and destabilising
switching, but party stability cannot justify extinguishing legislative
judgment. Its core should survive for confidence, supply and clear abandonment
of the electoral mandate. Adjudication must be prompt, reasoned and visibly
independent; merger protection must remain a genuine organisational exception,
not arithmetic laundering of defection. This calibrated model better reconciles
stable government, accountable parties and deliberative representation.
