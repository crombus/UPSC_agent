                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
India entered freedom with two contrasting inheritances: a poor, dependent
economy and an authoritarian administrative apparatus from colonial rule, but
a tested leadership, mass organisation and democratic-secular values from the
national movement. The early republic survived not because its crises were
minor, but because it handled territorial, communal, insurgent, constitutional,
economic and diplomatic challenges simultaneously without abandoning
representative government or civilian authority.

                 QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
The republic neither discarded the colonial state nor simply inherited a
ready-made democracy. It retained administrative capacity, transformed its
source of legitimacy through universal franchise and constitutional government,
and used secular reassurance, force and accommodation to survive simultaneous
crises. Planning, education and non-alignment created a developmental horizon,
but poverty, hierarchy, uneven reform and regional tension persisted. The
founding settlement was therefore a remarkable democratic consolidation, not a
completed social revolution.
