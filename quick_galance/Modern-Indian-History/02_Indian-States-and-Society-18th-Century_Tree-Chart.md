INDIAN STATES & SOCIETY IN THE EIGHTEENTH CENTURY
                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
The eighteenth century was neither a uniform dark age nor an age of
unbroken prosperity. Mughal central power contracted while successor,
rebel and independent states reorganised revenue, military labour,
credit and patronage. Regional vitality coexisted with warfare,
extractive pressure, technological limits and weak collective resistance
to an increasingly well-financed European company.

                  THREE-TYPE CLASSIFICATION

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Eighteenth-century India remained politically creative and economically
connected even as Mughal central power failed. Successor states adapted
imperial institutions, rebel formations captured revenue from below
and southern kingdoms pursued centralisation. Their diversity generated
resilience and cultural vitality, but weak collective command, fiscal
competition and technological-financial asymmetry later allowed the
Company to defeat them serially rather than all at once.
