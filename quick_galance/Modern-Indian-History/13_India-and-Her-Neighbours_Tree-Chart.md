                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
British India treated neighbouring territories as extensions of
imperial defence, commerce and prestige. Where a rival great power was
feared, Britain preferred buffers, missions and control of external
relations; where no equivalent rival intervened, as in Burma, it moved
toward staged annexation. Defensive arguments repeatedly generated new
frontiers, costs and conflicts paid from Indian resources.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
British frontier policy sought to secure India by controlling the
politics beyond its borders. Afghanistan and Nepal exposed the limits
of occupation and favoured buffer arrangements, while Burma's commercial
and strategic value encouraged annexation. The policy purchased imperial
security with Indian revenue and left lines and administrative habits
whose consequences survived the empire that created them.
