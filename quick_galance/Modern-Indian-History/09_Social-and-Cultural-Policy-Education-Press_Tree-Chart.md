                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Colonial education and press policy were double-edged instruments.
The state sought trained intermediaries, ideological control and
administrative utility; Indian reformers, students, writers and editors
appropriated the same schools, languages and print networks to create
social reform, public opinion and nationalism. Expansion remained
elite, urban, gendered and regionally unequal.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Colonial education and press policy sought administratively useful,
culturally manageable subjects, but knowledge and print could not remain
confined to that purpose. Indian actors converted English and vernacular
education into reform and nationalism, while repeated press restrictions
revealed the state's fear of an autonomous public. The legacy was real
institutional modernity built on narrow access and imperial control.
