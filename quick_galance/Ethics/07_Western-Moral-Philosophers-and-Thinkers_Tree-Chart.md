                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Western moral philosophy supplies distinct methods of ethical judgment: Socrates
requires examined self-knowledge; Plato links justice with wise, ordered rule;
Aristotle cultivates virtue and practical wisdom; Kant sets universal duties and
human dignity as non-negotiable constraints; Bentham and Mill evaluate welfare and
liberty; Rawls tests institutions from behind a veil of ignorance. These are not
decorative names. A strong administrative answer states the philosopher's actual
mechanism, applies it to a public decision, compares it with a competing lens and
acknowledges limitations and quotation-provenance uncertainty.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Western thinkers illuminate different ethical objects: Socrates the reflective agent,
Plato the competence of rulers, Aristotle the cultivated character, Kant the duty and
dignity of each person, Mill the welfare and liberty consequences, and Rawls the
fairness of institutions. Public administration should not choose one mechanically.
It should preserve rights as a floor, evaluate consequences, exercise practical wisdom
and test policy design from the position of those most vulnerable to its errors.
