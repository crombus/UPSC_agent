                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Indian moral thought is plural, not one undifferentiated tradition. Kautilya offers
institutional realism about temptation and public finance; Buddha offers self-aware
proportionality; Gandhi joins ethical means with universal welfare and trusteeship;
Vivekananda turns spiritual unity into service; Mahavir combines non-violence,
self-restraint and epistemic humility; Basava dignifies labour and participation;
Guru Nanak joins honest earning with sharing; Ambedkar makes equality, fraternity and
constitutional morality institutional duties. Ethical administration needs both moral
formation and controls, using historical analogy carefully rather than claiming exact
identity with modern constitutional concepts.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Indian moral thought supplies no single formula for administration. Kautilya reminds
the State to design for temptation, Gandhi to preserve ethical means, Buddha to act
proportionately, Mahavir to listen humbly, Vivekananda, Basava and Nanak to serve with
dignity, and Ambedkar to secure equality through constitutional institutions. Their
lasting relevance lies in disciplined, historically cautious translation into modern
rights, procedures and accountability, not ornamental quotation or civilisational
overclaim.
