                     CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
Attitude is a relatively stable evaluative disposition toward a specific object,
person, policy or idea. It combines belief, feeling and action tendency, but it does
not mechanically determine behaviour: social pressure, incentives, law and opportunity
can override a sincerely held stance. Ethical administration therefore uses truthful,
transparent persuasion to change cognition and emotion while using fair institutions
to close the attitude-behaviour gap. The boundary is autonomy: persuasion offers
reasons; manipulation covertly exploits vulnerability; coercion removes meaningful
choice and needs lawful, necessary and proportionate authority.

                  QUALIFIED CONCLUSION
Attitude is malleable but not infinitely controllable, and its influence on behaviour
depends on institutional context. Ethical persuasion respects truth and autonomy while
targeting the cognitive, affective and behavioural dimensions that actually sustain a
problem. Civil-service training can reshape attitudes, but durable conduct also needs
rules, incentives, protection and review. The democratic administrator persuades
citizens and colleagues without covertly engineering consent or suppressing legitimate
dissent.
