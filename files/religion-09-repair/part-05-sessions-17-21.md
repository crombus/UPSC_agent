### SESSION 17 — TOLERATION: THREE COMPONENTS, ONE PARADOX, FOUR CONCEPTIONS

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** Toleration is not being indifferent and it is not approving. It is putting up with something you genuinely think is wrong, when you could stop it, because you have reasons not to.

**Technical definition.** Toleration is a **three-condition** attitude: an **objection component** — one genuinely disapproves of, or believes false, what is tolerated; an **acceptance component** — one has reasons that outweigh the objection without cancelling it; and a **power component** — one is in a position to interfere and deliberately refrains. Remove the objection component and you have indifference or approval; remove the power component and you have mere endurance.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Toleration is indispensable but insufficient: its indispensability lies in the objection component, since it is precisely tolerance *of what one thinks wrong* that a plural society requires; its insufficiency lies in the power component, since a tolerance granted by the strong to the weak is permission rather than equality, and only respect-based toleration is compatible with equal citizenship.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**objection component · acceptance component · power component · paradox of toleration · first-order disapproval / second-order reasons · Forst's four conceptions — permission, coexistence, respect, esteem · Locke (1689) · Mill's harm principle and epistemic argument (1859) · Popper's paradox of tolerance · Rawls's overlapping consensus · Aśoka Rock Edict XII · concord (*samavāya*) · Akbar's *ṣulḥ-i kull* · Gandhi's *sarva-dharma-samabhāva* · Vivekananda's acceptance · Articles 25–28**

**How to use them:** the definition does the work. Define toleration by its three components first, and the "importance" the 2020 question asks about follows from the definition rather than having to be asserted.

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The three components.** Toleration is not indifference. Objection, acceptance and power are all required. Remove objection and you have indifference or approval; remove power and you have mere endurance.

**[FACT] The paradox and its resolution.** To tolerate is to allow what one holds to be wrong — which looks either weak-willed (if the objection is serious) or insincere (if it is not). The resolution is to locate the reasons for acceptance at a **different level** from the objection: I may believe your doctrine false (first-order) while holding that coercing belief is wrong, ineffective, or destructive of the very good at stake (second-order).

**[FACT] Forst's four conceptions.** From *Toleration in Conflict*: **(1) Permission** — the powerful permit a minority to exist on sufferance; **(2) Coexistence** — mutual, pragmatic, a modus vivendi to avoid conflict; **(3) Respect** — reciprocal recognition of others as equal moral-political persons despite deep ethical disagreement; **(4) Esteem** — positively valuing the other's beliefs as containing something worthwhile. **Only (3) and (4) are compatible with equal citizenship; (1) is what most historical "tolerance" actually was.**

**[FACT] Locke, *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) — three arguments.** The magistrate has no commission over souls; **force cannot produce belief**, only profession; and even if it could, coerced conformity would not save.

**[FACT] Mill, *On Liberty* (1859).** The **harm principle**; and the epistemic argument that suppressing an opinion robs us of either the truth or "the clearer perception of truth produced by its collision with error".

**[FACT] Popper's paradox of tolerance.** *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, note to ch. 7: unlimited tolerance leads to the disappearance of tolerance, so a tolerant society must retain the right to refuse to tolerate the intolerant — **though Popper's own formulation reserves this for those who refuse rational argument and answer with violence**.

**[FACT] Rawls.** An **overlapping consensus** on political principles allows citizens with incompatible comprehensive doctrines to share a stable polity.

**[FACT] Aśoka's Rock Edict XII — the strongest verifiable ancient anchor in the clause.** It enjoins *not* honouring one's own sect while disparaging others; holds that whoever does so out of devotion to his own sect **injures his own sect more gravely**; and commends **concord (*samavāya*)** and listening to one another's dharma. The owner records it as **the earliest verifiable state-level argument** for toleration.

**[FACT] Akbar's *ṣulḥ-i kull*** ("universal peace/reconciliation") and the *Ibādat Khāna* debates — a **historical policy, not a philosophical doctrine**, and the owner's discipline table requires you to say so when citing it.

**[FACT] Gandhi and Vivekananda.** Gandhi's *sarva-dharma-samabhāva* — equal respect combined with fallibilism about one's own grasp of truth. Vivekananda at the 1893 Parliament explicitly demands **acceptance, not merely toleration**, arguing that "toleration" still condescends. **[INFERENCE]** Read against Forst, Vivekananda is refusing level (1) and demanding something at level (3) or (4) — which is a genuinely comparative observation and exactly the sort of point the owner's parity bench is built to generate.

**[FACT] Jain many-sidedness as the epistemic ground.** If every judgement is standpoint-relative, dogmatic suppression of another standpoint is an **epistemic** as well as a moral error.

**[FACT] Constitutional expression.** Articles 25–28 and the "freedom of conscience" formula. **[INFERENCE]** Cite as the constitutional expression of the principle; the clause does not own constitutional litigation, and Session 18 marks the boundary explicitly.

**Objection.** Toleration is second-best: respect and esteem are what a pluralist society actually needs, and toleration keeps the tolerator in the position of judge.
**Reply.** **[FACT]** Correct as a critique of Forst's level (1); but the **objection component** is what makes toleration a **moral achievement** rather than mere indifference, and a society of genuine disagreement cannot be built on esteem alone.

**Second objection.** Unlimited toleration is self-destructive (Popper).
**Reply.** **[FACT]** Limits must be drawn by **harm and reciprocity**, not by doctrinal content — otherwise the limit-drawing becomes the intolerance it was meant to prevent.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Religious tolerance matters in a multicultural society because of what it is — the acceptance of what one genuinely believes wrong by one who could interfere and refrains — and not because differences are unimportant.
> **Named evidence:** Its three components are objection, acceptance and power; Forst's ladder grades it from permission through coexistence and respect to esteem; Locke argues in 1689 that force cannot produce belief, Mill in 1859 adds the harm principle and the epistemic cost of suppression, Popper limits tolerance only against those who answer argument with violence, and Rawls locates stability in an overlapping consensus; on the Indian side, Aśoka's Rock Edict XII holds that disparaging another sect injures one's own most and commends concord, Akbar's *ṣulḥ-i kull* is the corresponding policy, Gandhi couples equal respect with fallibilism, and Vivekananda demands acceptance rather than mere toleration.
> **Analysis:** Because the objection component is retained, toleration is a moral achievement rather than indifference, and because the power component is retained, most historical toleration turns out to be permission granted by the strong — which is why the ladder must be applied before any example is praised.
> **Qualification:** Toleration is therefore indispensable but insufficient: only its respect-based forms are compatible with equal citizenship, and its limits must be drawn by harm and reciprocity rather than by doctrinal content, or the limit-drawing becomes the intolerance it was meant to prevent.

50. ⚠️ Do not state Popper's paradox as a general licence to suppress. His formulation targets those who **refuse rational argument and answer with violence**.
51. ⚠️ Do not cite Akbar's *ṣulḥ-i kull* as a philosophical doctrine. It is a historical policy; the owner's discipline table requires the label.

Western stock: Locke, Mill, Popper, Rawls. Indian stock: Aśoka RE XII (earliest verifiable state-level argument), Akbar (policy), Gandhi, Vivekananda, Jain epistemic ground. Verdict: indispensable but insufficient.

### SESSION 18 — RELIGIOUS FREEDOM IN A MULTIRELIGIOUS SOCIETY

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** "Religious freedom" is not one right but a bundle: to believe, to say you believe, to act on it, to persuade others, to change or leave, to be free from imposed observance, and for communities to run their own affairs. Different members of the bundle are protected differently.

**Technical definition.** The bundle comprises **freedom of conscience** (the inner forum — inviolable), **freedom to profess** (declare), **to practise** (act), **to propagate** (persuade), **to change or leave** a religion, **freedom from** compelled religious observance, and the **associational** right of communities to order their internal affairs. The **practice/belief asymmetry** is the structural key: belief is absolutely protected; practice is not, because it can harm.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

> Religious freedom is possible in a multireligious society, and is more nearly *necessary* there than in a homogeneous one; what is not possible is an unlimited or a perfectly neutral version of it, since every regime must limit practice and every limit embodies a judgement.

#### MUST-WRITE KEYWORDS

**freedom of conscience · profess · practise · propagate · change or leave · freedom from compelled observance · associational right · practice/belief asymmetry · public order, morality and health · coercion and fraud, not persuasion · internal minorities · paradox of multicultural vulnerability (Shachar) · wall of separation · principled distance (Bhargava) · essential religious practices**

**How to use them:** the marks are in the **hard problems**, not the list of rights. Name the bundle in one sentence, then spend the answer on the asymmetry, conversion, internal minorities, neutrality and the judicial determination problem.

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The bundle.** Freedom of conscience, profession, practice, propagation, change or exit, freedom from compelled observance, and the associational right. Separating them is the first analytical move.

**[FACT] Why it is philosophically defensible — four arguments.** **(1) Locke's incoercibility argument:** belief is not directly voluntary, so coercion produces hypocrisy, not faith. **(2) Autonomy:** matters of ultimate concern are the paradigm case in which a person must judge for herself. **(3) Epistemic:** given deep disagreement and fallibility, no institution is entitled to the certainty that suppression presupposes. **(4) Social:** in a plural society, enforced uniformity is a cause of conflict rather than its cure.

**[FACT] Hard problem 1 — the practice/belief asymmetry.** Belief is absolutely protected; practice is not, because it can harm. Every constitutional order therefore limits practice by **public order, morality and health** — and the philosophical difficulty is that these limits are stated in terms whose content is **contested**.

**[FACT] Hard problem 2 — propagation and conversion.** If profession is free, propagation follows; but conversion is experienced by communities as loss, and "inducement" is hard to distinguish from "persuasion". The principled line is **coercion and fraud**, not persuasion as such.

**[FACT] Hard problem 3 — internal minorities.** **Ayelet Shachar's "paradox of multicultural vulnerability"**: group rights granted to protect a community from the majority can entrench the power of that community's dominant members over its own weaker members, typically women. Accommodating a group may thus reduce the freedom of persons inside it. The owner records this as **the single most examinable tension in the clause**.

**[FACT] Hard problem 4 — state neutrality.** Two models: the **wall-of-separation** model (the state abstains entirely) and **Rajeev Bhargava's "principled distance"** — the Indian model, in which the state may engage with or abstain from religions **differentially**, guided by the values of freedom and equality rather than by a uniform rule of non-engagement. Principled distance permits reform — temple-entry, abolition of untouchability — that strict separation would forbid.

**[FACT] Hard problem 5 — judicial determination of what counts as religion.** The Indian courts' "essential religious practices" doctrine makes the state an **arbiter of theology**; philosophically this sits uneasily with neutrality, whichever way particular cases go. **Note the tension; do not litigate cases.**

**Objection.** Religious freedom is a liberal value being universalised, and traditions that reject liberal individualism have not consented to it.
**Reply.** **[FACT]** The incoercibility argument is **not distinctively liberal** — it is a claim about the **psychology of belief** — and traditions from Aśoka's edicts to Sufi and Bhakti universalism reach comparable conclusions on their own grounds.

**Second objection.** Unlimited freedom of practice makes governance impossible.
**Reply.** **[FACT]** The limits are real, and the philosophical task is to justify them by **harm and reciprocity** rather than by orthodoxy.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Religious freedom is possible in a multireligious society, but only in a limited and non-neutral form, because the rights it bundles together are protected at different strengths.
> **Named evidence:** Conscience is inviolable while practice is limitable by public order, morality and health; the justification rests on Locke's incoercibility argument, on autonomy, on the epistemic point that deep disagreement denies any institution the certainty suppression presupposes, and on the social point that enforced uniformity causes rather than cures conflict; the hard cases are conversion, Shachar's paradox of multicultural vulnerability, the choice between a wall of separation and Bhargava's principled distance, and the courts' essential-religious-practices doctrine.
> **Analysis:** Each limit embodies a contested judgement, and accommodating a community can reduce the freedom of persons inside it — so neutrality of the strict kind is unavailable, and principled distance is defensible precisely because it permits reforms such as temple-entry that strict separation would forbid.
> **Qualification:** The internal-minorities problem should be treated as a standing cost rather than a solved one, and the limits on practice should be justified by harm and reciprocity rather than by orthodoxy, since limits drawn by doctrinal content reproduce the intolerance they were meant to prevent.

#### UPSC TRAPS

52. ⚠️ Do not equate religious freedom with state indifference. Contrast the wall-of-separation model with **Bhargava's principled distance**, and raise the **internal-minorities** problem. This is trap 11 in the owner's list.
53. ⚠️ Do not answer with a list of constitutional articles. Articles 25–28 are the constitutional expression; the philosophy is the asymmetry, the conversion line, the internal minorities and the neutrality models.
54. ⚠️ Do not litigate particular cases under "essential religious practices". Note the philosophical tension — the state as arbiter of theology — and move on.

#### MINI RECAP

Seven-member bundle; conscience absolute, practice limitable. Four justifications: incoercibility, autonomy, epistemic, social. Five hard problems: asymmetry, conversion, internal minorities (Shachar), neutrality (wall vs principled distance), judicial determination. Verdict: possible, necessary, but never unlimited or perfectly neutral.

### SESSION 19 — "IS THE HISTORY OF RELIGIONS THE HISTORY OF CONFLICTS?"

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** The claim sounds like one claim but is three, of increasing strength: religions have been *involved* in conflict; religion is a *significant cause* of conflict; religion is *the* engine of human conflict. The first is trivial, the third is false, and the argument is about the second.

**Technical definition.** Grade the thesis into (a) **involvement**, (b) **significant causation**, (c) **defining engine**. Then distinguish religion's four possible roles: **cause**, **marker** of an antagonism generated by land, power or economics, **mobiliser** of pre-existing grievance, and **legitimiser** of a decision taken on other grounds. Most historical cases are (ii)–(iv), and a claim of type (i) must be argued case by case.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

That symmetry is itself a strong closing move.

> **Claim:** The thesis that the history of religions is the history of conflicts is unacceptable in its strong form, defensible only in a narrow form, and confused in its central concept.
> **Named evidence:** Kimball's five markers give a usable typology of religion "becoming evil"; against it, Cavanaugh's *The Myth of Religious Violence* (2009) argues that the religious/secular distinction is a modern construction that licenses the secular state's own violence, Armstrong's *Fields of Blood* (2014) argues that pre-modern religion was not a separable sphere, and Sen's *Identity and Violence* (2006) identifies the singular-affiliation fallacy as what makes identity lethal; the counter-evidence includes Aśoka's *dhamma* policy after Kaliṅga, monastic learning, famine relief, abolitionism, the civil-rights movement, *satyāgraha*, Bhakti and Sufi universalism and the interfaith movement.
> **Analysis:** The decisive move is to distinguish religion as cause, as marker of an antagonism generated by land or power, as mobiliser of pre-existing grievance and as legitimiser of a decision taken on other grounds — since most historical cases fall into the last three, a causal claim must be argued case by case rather than assumed from correlation.
> **Qualification:** The narrower truth should nonetheless be conceded: exclusivist certainty joined to singular identity is genuinely dangerous, and religions supply both more readily than most other institutions — while noting that the deconstruction of "religion" as a variable weakens the defence as well as the indictment.

### SESSION 20 — "IS RELIGION A UNITING FORCE IN A GLOBALISING WORLD?"

#### DEFINITION

**Plain-language definition.** The honest answer is "it depends", but only if you say what it depends on. Religion unites when its ethics reach beyond the group, when its epistemology permits dialogue, and when its identity is not politically owned.

**Technical definition.** Religion is a **conditional** unifier. It unites when three conditions hold: its ethics are **universalist** rather than communal in scope; its epistemology is **fallibilist** enough to permit dialogue; and its identity-function is **not** politically monopolised. It divides when any of the three fails. It is therefore a **high-amplitude** social force whose direction is set by those three variables.

#### ANSWER-GRABBING OPENING — WRITE OR ADAPT IN THE EXAM

#### TEACHING

**[FACT] The case for — Durkheim.** Religion's primary social function is integrative: shared rites generate "collective effervescence" and constitute a **moral community**; on his definition, a *church* just is the community united by relation to sacred things.

**[FACT] The case for — universalist ethics.** Every major tradition contains a version of the golden rule and a doctrine of universal moral standing: *vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam*; the Buddhist four abodes (*brahmavihāra*); *ummah*; the image of God.

**[FACT] The case for — institutional evidence.** The **1893 Parliament of the World's Religions** and its **1993 centenary**, which adopted the ***Declaration Toward a Global Ethic***, drafted principally by **Hans Küng**, whose formula — "**no peace among the nations without peace among the religions; no peace among the religions without dialogue between the religions**" — is the standard citation for religion as a resource for global order.

**[FACT] The case for — transnational networks.** Religious networks supply relief, migration support and diaspora solidarity at a scale states often cannot.

**[FACT] The case against — Durkheim cuts both ways.** His integration is **in-group** integration: the same mechanism that unites also demarcates.

**[FACT] The case against — Huntington.** The "clash of civilizations" thesis predicts that in a globalised world the fault-lines become **cultural-religious**. It is **heavily criticised** (by Sen and others) for treating civilisations as internally homogeneous and externally sealed, and **should be cited as a thesis under criticism, not as a finding**.

**[FACT] The case against — Roy.** Globalisation **deterritorialises** religion, detaching it from local culture and producing both cosmopolitan and reactive-fundamentalist forms — Olivier Roy's analysis of deculturated, globalised religiosity.

**[FACT] The case against — instrumentalisation.** Religious identity is easily instrumentalised by electoral and geopolitical actors.

**[FACT] The synthesis.** Religion is a **conditional** unifier: it unites when its ethics are universalist in scope, its epistemology fallibilist enough to permit dialogue, and its identity-function not politically monopolised. It divides when any of the three fails. **That is a determinate, defensible answer to the printed question, and it is more useful than either optimism or cynicism.**

**[INFERENCE] Why the three conditions are the right shape of answer.** Each condition names a variable that can actually vary, and each is independently checkable against a case. A verdict of that form can be applied to an example the examiner has in mind without the candidate having to name a contemporary community — which is also the safest way to answer a question of this kind.

**Objection.** The conditional verdict is evasive: the question asks whether religion unites, and "it depends" is not an answer.
**Reply.** **[INFERENCE]** It is an answer, provided the dependencies are specified and the specification is defensible. An unconditional answer would have to claim that the scope of a tradition's ethics, the humility of its epistemology and the politics of its identity make no difference to its social direction — and that claim is refuted by the two institutional examples on either side, the Parliament of Religions and communal violence, produced by the *same* traditions.

#### HOW TO WRITE THE PARAGRAPH

> **Claim:** Religion is a conditional rather than an intrinsic unifier, and the conditions can be stated precisely.
> **Named evidence:** For unification, Durkheim's integrative function with collective effervescence and the church as moral community, the universalist ethics present in every tradition, the 1893 and 1993 Parliaments of the World's Religions with the *Declaration Toward a Global Ethic* drafted principally by Hans Küng, and transnational relief networks; against it, the in-group character of Durkheimian integration, Huntington's clash thesis cited as a thesis under sustained criticism, Roy's account of deterritorialised religiosity producing both cosmopolitan and reactive forms, and electoral or geopolitical instrumentalisation.
> **Analysis:** The evidence on both sides is drawn from the same traditions, so what varies is not the religion but three conditions: whether its ethics are universalist in scope, whether its epistemology is fallibilist enough to permit dialogue, and whether its identity-function has been politically monopolised.
> **Qualification:** Religion is therefore a high-amplitude social force whose direction is set by those variables, which is why the same traditions produce both the Parliament of Religions and communal violence, and why an unconditional verdict in either direction is refuted by the record.

59. ⚠️ Do not use Durkheim only on one side. His integrative mechanism is **in-group** integration, and saying so converts a one-sided citation into a two-sided one.
60. ⚠️ Do not answer "is religion a uniting force" unconditionally. The directive decoder requires a conditional verdict **with the conditions stated up front**.

#### MINI RECAP

For: Durkheim, universalist ethics, 1893 and 1993 Parliaments, Küng's formula, transnational networks. Against: in-group integration, Huntington (criticised), Roy's deterritorialisation, instrumentalisation. Synthesis: three conditions — universalist ethics, fallibilist epistemology, non-monopolised identity. Verdict: high-amplitude, not intrinsically unifying.

#### TEACHING — THE FOUR CRITICISMS AND THEIR REPLIES

1. **Relativism.** ⚠️ Equal validity erases truth. **Reply:** distinguish equal dignity or salvific efficacy from identical propositional truth.
2. **Self-reference.** ⚠️ Pluralism itself becomes an exclusive meta-truth. **Reply:** hold it as a defeasible hypothesis; **rejoinder:** it still judges first-order traditions.
3. **Conflicting salvation goals.** ⚠️ Communion with God, non-dual knowledge and cessation are not obviously one end. **Reply:** family resemblance or the transformation criterion; critics call this flattening.
4. **Conflict claim.** ⚠️ Pluralism may create, not remove, dispute by relativising commitment. **Reply:** conflict is not caused by plurality alone; power, identity and exclusivist certainty mediate it.

**[FACT]** Primary ownership is **12 of 112 question-parts** in the audited 2018–2025 Paper II corpus, with **two owned parts in each of 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021** and one in each later paper. **[FACT]** The required doctrinal depth across those years is exclusivism–inclusivism–pluralism, "truth is one", religion as unifier, conflict-history, tolerance, multireligious freedom and absolute truth. **[FACT] The trap with an answer consequence:** do not infer that pluralism denies truth or that tolerance establishes equal truth; separate truth, salvation, epistemic humility and political coexistence.
