## PYQS AND ANSWER PRACTICE

### Verified previous-year questions — 12 complete solutions

**Word bands used throughout.** 10 marks ≈ 150 words · 15 marks ≈ 250 words · 20 marks ≈ 350 words. Every question below is reproduced in its **exact printed wording** from the audited Philosophy of Religion previous-year ledger, 2018–2025, in which this clause owns **twelve question-parts out of 112** — two in each of 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021, and one in each of 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025. **The model answers are independent learner practice and are never official UPSC keys.**

**Timing skeleton.** 10 marks — 1 minute to decode, 1 to outline, 6 to write, 1 to check. 15 marks — 1 / 2 / 10 / 2. 20 marks — 2 / 3 / 13 / 2. **Standing penalties in this clause:** defining pluralism as "all religions say the same thing"; merging inclusivism with pluralism; presenting exclusivism only as bigotry; citing *ekaṃ sat* without its context or its bridging argument; quoting *extra ecclesiam nulla salus* as Cyprian's own words; treating "tolerance" as one thing; answering the conflict-history stem with a list of wars; and citing Huntington as a finding.

#### PYQ 1 — 2018, Q6(a), 20 marks

**Exact verified question:** Distinguish between Exclusivism, Inclusivism and Pluralism with regard to the conflicting truth-claims of different religions.

**Demand decoding:** "Distinguish A, B and C with regard to X" buys **three clean differentiae on one axis**. Fix the axis before distinguishing — here the printed axis is *conflicting truth-claims*, so truth is primary and salvation is secondary. A narrative of three thinkers will not score; a three-way differentia will.

**Model solution:**
The three positions differ on a single question: **who sets the terms on which another tradition's claims are judged?**

**Exclusivism** holds that one revelation carries uniquely decisive truth and salvific authority, so where others contradict it they are mistaken. It appeals to determinate revelation and non-contradiction. Its classical soteriological expression is *extra ecclesiam nulla salus* — a later standard phrasing of Cyprian's *salus extra ecclesiam non est* (*Ep.* 73), hardened at Lateran IV (1215), *Unam Sanctam* (1302) and Florence (1442), then decisively qualified by *Lumen Gentium* 16 and *Nostra Aetate* (1965). Its strength is that it preserves determinate content; its weakness is privileged warrant, sharpened by the contingency of birth. It does not entail intolerance: Locke's argument that force cannot produce belief is available to it.

**Inclusivism** grants other traditions **partial truth** and possible salvation, but interprets that success through the home tradition — Rahner's "anonymous Christians". Others are included **on terms they have not set**; the standard does not move, only the boundary. Its cost is asymmetry: the home tradition is never redescribed in the other's categories.

**Pluralism** denies that any finite tradition exhaustively possesses the absolute. Hick's identist version treats the traditions as culturally conditioned responses to the transcategorial Real, assessed by transformation from self-centredness to Reality-centredness; Heim's differential version holds that communion, non-dual realisation and cessation are three destinations rather than three descriptions. It is not relativism, indifferentism, syncretism or "all religions say the same thing".

Plantinga defends exclusivism by parity — the pluralist's own denial is contested too — while D'Costa charges pluralism with covert exclusivism. The triad is therefore a map, not a verdict.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Fixes one axis at the outset, which is exactly what the directive buys.
- States each position by **who sets the terms**, giving three clean differentiae rather than three summaries.
- Carries the full attribution chain on the Latin tag, including the 1965 qualification.
- Distinguishes two varieties of pluralism, which most scripts treat as one.
- Closes with the parity exchange, converting classification into assessment.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** the compressible part is the conciliar chain — at 350 words, keep Cyprian's wording and the 1965 qualification and drop the three council dates. What must never be cut is the "who sets the terms" formula, because it is the differentia the directive is grading.

#### PYQ 2 — 2018, Q6(b), 15 marks

**Exact verified question:** "Truth is one, yet people perceive differently." Critically evaluate by considering the present Indian context.

**Demand decoding:** A quotation **plus** a context clause. The clause carries marks, so an Indian application is compulsory. "Critically evaluate" requires the quotation to be tested, not endorsed.

**Model solution:**
The statement asserts a single truth with plural perception — an absolute **referent** with perspectival **access**. Evaluated carefully it is defensible, and it is more precise than it first appears.

Its strongest Indian warrant is *ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti*, **Ṛgveda 1.164.46**, from the *asya vāmasya* riddle-hymn ascribed to Dīrghatamas. Discipline is required: the verse's own subject is the **naming of Vedic deities** — Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa, Agni, Garutmān, Yama, Mātariśvan as names of the one existent — so its inter-religious extension is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century development, associated with Vivekananda, Radhakrishnan and Gandhi, and must be **argued** rather than assumed. What the verse supplies is a structure: one referent, many namings.

Two Indian resources make the structure rigorous. Advaita distinguishes Brahman **without qualities** (*nirguṇa*), beyond exhaustive predication, from Brahman **with qualities** (*saguṇa*), which structures actual devotional paths. Jain **many-sidedness** goes further: *nayavāda* names the standpoint, *syādvāda* prefixes assertions with *syāt*, and *saptabhaṅgī* formalises the modes — framework-relativity marked *inside* the proposition rather than left implicit.

The critical evaluation has two parts. First, the maxim does not license "all religions say the same thing": Vivekananda himself preserves distinctive forms, and Heim's differential pluralism holds that the ends themselves differ. Second, plural perception cannot dissolve **hard contradiction**: a personal creator either exists or does not.

In the present Indian context the maxim functions as an argument for equal respect grounded in fallibilism — Gandhi's position, summarised as *sarva-dharma-samabhāva* — and against the singular-affiliation fallacy that Sen identifies as what makes identity lethal. It is a genuine philosophical claim, provided its ancient warrant is cited with discipline and its limits are conceded.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Reads the quotation as referent-versus-access rather than paraphrasing it.
- Applies the full citation discipline to RV 1.164.46, which the strong band requires.
- Uses *nirguṇa*/*saguṇa* and Jain many-sidedness as machinery, not as decoration.
- Answers the context clause with a determinate Indian application, including Sen.
- Concedes the hard-contradiction limit, which is what "critically" is buying.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 250 words, compress the Jain apparatus to one sentence naming *syāt* and standpoint-indexing. What must never be cut is the context clause, since omitting the Indian application forfeits marks the directive explicitly allocates.

#### PYQ 3 — 2019, Q5(b), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Is religion a uniting force for humanity in the globalizing world as of today? Discuss.

**Demand decoding:** A "uniting force" stem buys a **conditional verdict with the conditions stated up front**. Structure: for / against / conditions / verdict. Neither optimism nor cynicism scores.

**Model solution:**
Religion is a conditional unifier, and the conditions can be stated precisely.

For unification: Durkheim treats religion's primary social function as integrative, with shared rites generating collective effervescence and constituting a moral community. Every major tradition contains a version of the golden rule and a doctrine of universal moral standing — *vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam*, the Buddhist four abodes, *ummah*, the image of God. Institutionally, the 1893 Parliament of the World's Religions and its 1993 centenary adopted the *Declaration Toward a Global Ethic*, drafted principally by Hans Küng: no peace among nations without peace among religions, and none among religions without dialogue.

Against: Durkheim's integration is **in-group** integration, so the mechanism that unites also demarcates. Huntington's clash thesis predicts cultural-religious fault-lines, but must be cited as a thesis under sustained criticism, not a finding. Globalisation deterritorialises religion, producing cosmopolitan and reactive-fundamentalist forms alike. Religious identity is readily instrumentalised.

Religion therefore unites when three conditions hold: its ethics are universalist rather than communal in scope; its epistemology is fallibilist enough for dialogue; and its identity-function is not politically monopolised. It divides when any fails.

Religion is a high-amplitude social force whose direction is set by the scope of its ethics and the politics of its identity — which is why the same traditions produce both the Parliament of Religions and communal violence.

**Why this earns marks:**
- States the verdict form in the first line, so the structure is visible immediately.
- Uses Durkheim on both sides, which is what makes the citation analytical.
- Handles Huntington with the required qualification.
- Names three checkable conditions rather than gesturing at complexity.
- Ends with the amplitude formula, the clause's signature line for this stem.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 150 words, cut the list of universalist doctrines to one example and compress Roy to a clause. What must never be cut is the three-condition synthesis, because the conditions *are* the answer the directive is buying.

#### PYQ 4 — 2019, Q7(a), 20 marks

**Exact verified question:** Expound and explain the central problem in the discussion between religious pluralists and religious exclusivists.

**Demand decoding:** "The **central** problem" is singular. Name it in one sentence, then unfold it. A survey of both positions without an isolated central issue answers a different question.

**Model solution:**
The central problem is this: **is there any standpoint from which the competing claims of the traditions can be adjudicated without already occupying one of them?** Everything else in the dispute follows from that.

The exclusivist says no neutral standpoint is needed, because one revelation carries decisive authority; determinate truth and non-contradiction do the rest. The pluralist says a second-order account is available which privileges no first-order tradition: Hick relocates the centre to the transcategorial Real by four steps — persistent, birth-linked diversity; transformation produced across traditions; equal divine concern resisting arbitrary exclusivism; therefore one Real experienced through different conceptual schemes.

The problem bites in both directions. **Plantinga** answers the charges of arbitrariness, arrogance and irrationality by parity: the pluralist too holds a contested belief that the exclusivist rejects; believing what others deny is the ordinary condition of every philosopher; and awareness of disagreement may reduce warrant without extinguishing it. That establishes permissibility, not truth, and it leaves the contingency of birth untouched.

**D'Costa** answers from the other side: pluralism makes its own second-order account uniquely true and reinterprets traditions against their self-understanding — a covert exclusivism. Hick's reply, that his is a philosophical hypothesis rather than a rival creed, does not fully escape the charge, since a theory that tells traditions what they are *really* doing exercises the authority it denies them.

Underneath both lies **deep disagreement** in Fogelin's sense: the parties do not share the framework propositions against which evidence would count, so the failure of convergence is predicted rather than scandalous. It neither convicts exclusivism of irrationality nor establishes pluralism.

The central problem is therefore not which side is more generous but whether neutrality is available. It is not; what is available is corrigibility — fallibilism, humility, charity, and making one's framework explicit.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Names the central problem in a single sentence before unfolding it, exactly as the directive requires.
- Reconstructs Hick's argument in numbered steps rather than describing it.
- Gives Plantinga and D'Costa as a symmetrical pair, which is what makes the problem central rather than partisan.
- Adds the deep-disagreement layer, which the verdict ladder places in the strong band.
- Converts the stalemate into a determinate conclusion about corrigibility.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 350 words, compress Hick's four steps to two clauses. What must never be cut is the pairing of Plantinga with D'Costa, since a script with only one of them reads as advocacy rather than as an account of a *problem*.

#### PYQ 5 — 2020, Q5(c), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Is it acceptable that the History of Religions is the History of Conflicts? Discuss.

**Demand decoding:** A sweeping historical claim. **Grade it into versions**, refute the strong one, concede the narrow one. A list of wars answers a different question.

**Model solution:**
The claim has three versions: religions have been *involved* in conflict (trivially true); religion is a *significant cause* (arguable); religion is *the defining engine* (false). The argument concerns the second.

The case for it is structural: absolute truth-claims plus exclusivist soteriology plus strong group identity plus sacralised authority is in principle conflict-generating, since what is at stake is held infinite, dissent is heresy and compromise looks like betrayal. Kimball's markers of religion "becoming evil" give a usable checklist — a typology, not a law.

The case against is conceptual and historical. Cavanaugh's *The Myth of Religious Violence* (2009) argues that the religious/secular distinction is a modern Western construction, so "religion" is not an isolable variable; Armstrong's *Fields of Blood* (2014) argues that pre-modern religion was not a separable sphere; Sen's *Identity and Violence* (2006) locates the danger in the singular-affiliation fallacy.

The decisive move is to distinguish religion as **cause**, **marker**, **mobiliser** and **legitimiser**. Most historical cases are the last three, so a causal claim must be argued case by case. The counter-evidence is substantial: Aśoka's *dhamma* policy after Kaliṅga, monastic learning, famine relief, abolitionism, the civil-rights movement, *satyāgraha*, Bhakti and Sufi universalism, the interfaith movement.

The claim is therefore historically selective and conceptually confused — while conceding the narrower truth that exclusivist certainty joined to singular identity is genuinely dangerous.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Grades the claim in the first sentence, which is the structural consequence the directive demands.
- States the case *for* fairly before refuting it.
- Deploys the four-role distinction, which the owner identifies as the mark-earning move.
- Supplies counter-evidence, making the refutation historical as well as conceptual.
- Concedes a narrow truth, which is what "discuss" is buying.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 150 words, reduce the counter-evidence list to three items. What must never be cut is the four-role distinction, because without it the answer becomes the list of wars the trap list forbids.

#### PYQ 6 — 2020, Q5(e), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** What is the importance of religious tolerance in a multicultural pluralistic society? Justify your answer.

**Demand decoding:** "Importance … justify" means the importance must be **argued**, not asserted. Define tolerance properly first: the definition does the work.

**Model solution:**
Tolerance is not indifference. It has three components: an **objection** — one genuinely believes the tolerated thing false or wrong; an **acceptance** — reasons that outweigh without cancelling the objection; and **power** — one could interfere and deliberately refrains. Remove the first and it becomes indifference; remove the third and it becomes endurance.

That definition generates the paradox — allowing what one holds wrong — and its resolution by levels: first-order disapproval coexists with second-order reasons against coercion.

Its importance follows. Locke (1689) argues that the magistrate has no commission over souls, that force cannot produce belief, and that coerced conformity would not save. Mill (1859) adds the harm principle and the epistemic argument that suppression costs us the truth or its clearer perception through collision with error. Rawls locates stability in an overlapping consensus. Popper's paradox limits tolerance, but only against those who answer argument with violence.

India supplies the earliest verifiable state-level argument: Aśoka's **Rock Edict XII** enjoins not honouring one's own sect by disparaging others, holds that doing so injures one's own sect most gravely, and commends concord (*samavāya*). Akbar's *ṣulḥ-i kull* is the corresponding policy; Gandhi couples equal respect with fallibilism; Vivekananda demands **acceptance, not mere toleration**; Jain many-sidedness supplies the epistemic ground.

Graded on Forst's ladder — permission, coexistence, respect, esteem — most historical tolerance was permission. Tolerance is therefore indispensable but insufficient: indispensable because of the objection component, insufficient because tolerance granted by the strong to the weak is permission, not equality.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Defines by three components, so the importance is derived rather than asserted.
- States the paradox and resolves it by levels, which is the philosophical core.
- Gives both argument-stocks, with Aśoka correctly identified as the earliest verifiable state-level argument.
- Applies Forst's ladder as a grading instrument rather than a list.
- Closes with the indispensable-but-insufficient verdict, naming both reasons.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 150 words, keep the three components, Locke, Aśoka and the ladder; compress Mill, Rawls and Popper into one clause. What must never be cut is the power component, since the closing verdict depends on it.

#### PYQ 7 — 2021, Q5(b), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Discuss the possibility of Absolute Truth in the context of religious pluralism.

**Demand decoding:** A **possibility** claim. The examiner is buying the **compatibility mechanism**: absolute referent with perspectival access. Show the mechanism, do not merely assert the compatibility.

**Model solution:**
Absolute Truth is possible within religious pluralism, because the apparent conflict rests on a suppressed premise.

Separate four things: the **referent** (what the truth is about), the **formulation** (how it is stated), the **access** (who can know it, and how well), and the **salvific** claim (who is liberated by it). Pluralism denies exhaustive possession of the absolute by a finite perspective; it does not deny that there is an absolute.

The mechanism is this. Ontological unity does not entail epistemic monopoly. One may affirm an absolute referent while holding every finite formulation partial, perspectival and corrigible. Three independent traditions already deny that a finite formulation can exhaust the absolute: Advaita's **without-qualities** (*nirguṇa*) Brahman is beyond exhaustive predication while **with-qualities** (*saguṇa*) forms structure real devotional paths; Jain **conditional predication** (*syādvāda*) indexes every assertion to a standpoint; Hick's Real is transcategorial.

Ontological absolutism, epistemic fallibilism and political tolerance are therefore three separate theses, and none entails the others.

Two limits must be conceded. The position presupposes that truth admits degrees of adequacy or non-literal mediation. And where two claims contradict on the same question in the same respect, no relocation helps: pluralism must acknowledge the residue rather than harmonise it.

Absolute Truth is thus possible in a pluralist framework — as a single referent inexhaustibly formulated, not as a finite formulation held absolutely.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Separates referent, formulation, access and salvation, which is the owner's instruction for every absolute-truth stem.
- Exhibits the compatibility **mechanism**, which is what a possibility directive buys.
- Cites three independent denials of the suppressed premise, one Western and two Indian.
- Names the presupposition the position itself makes.
- Concedes the hard-contradiction residue in the same answer.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 150 words, keep the four-way separation and one denial of the premise. What must never be cut is the closing distinction between a single referent inexhaustibly formulated and a finite formulation held absolutely, since that sentence is the possibility claim.

#### PYQ 8 — 2021, Q5(c), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Is religious freedom possible in a multireligious society? Explain.

**Demand decoding:** Feasibility, **with the hard cases faced**. Naming rights without facing the practice/belief asymmetry and the internal-minorities problem will not reach the upper bands.

**Model solution:**
Yes — and it is more nearly necessary in a multireligious society than in a homogeneous one. What is not possible is an unlimited or perfectly neutral version.

Religious freedom is a bundle: freedom of **conscience** (the inner forum, inviolable), and the freedoms to **profess**, **practise**, **propagate**, **change or leave**, to be **free from** compelled observance, and the **associational** right of communities to order their internal affairs.

Four arguments justify it. Locke's incoercibility: belief is not directly voluntary, so coercion yields hypocrisy rather than faith. Autonomy: matters of ultimate concern are the paradigm case for judging oneself. Epistemic: deep disagreement and fallibility deny any institution the certainty suppression presupposes. Social: enforced uniformity causes conflict rather than curing it.

The hard cases decide the answer. Belief is absolutely protected but **practice** is not, since it can harm — hence limits by public order, morality and health, stated in contested terms. Propagation follows from profession, and the principled line is **coercion and fraud**, not persuasion. Shachar's **paradox of multicultural vulnerability** shows that group rights can entrench dominant members over weaker ones, typically women. Neutrality admits two models: the wall of separation, and **Bhargava's principled distance**, which permits reforms such as temple-entry that strict separation forbids. The courts' "essential religious practices" doctrine makes the state an arbiter of theology.

Religious freedom is therefore possible as principled distance justified by freedom and equality, with the internal-minorities problem treated as a standing cost rather than a solved one.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Separates the bundle, so that "possible" can be answered differently for different members.
- Gives four independent justifications rather than one.
- Faces all the hard cases, including the one the owner calls the most examinable tension.
- Contrasts two neutrality models and explains what principled distance permits.
- Ends with a qualified verdict that names the residual cost.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 150 words, compress the four justifications to Locke plus one. What must never be cut is the internal-minorities paradox, since it is the discriminator between an average and a good script on this stem.

#### PYQ 9 — 2022, Q7(a), 20 marks

**Exact verified question:** "An unquestionable acceptance of only one Absolute Truth will inevitably result in religious exclusivism." Discuss.

**Demand decoding:** A quotation whose **inference** is to be tested, not its mood. The word doing the work is "inevitably". Show that ontological absolutism does not entail epistemic exclusivism.

**Model solution:**
The claim asserts an entailment. Reconstructed, it runs: there is exactly one Absolute Truth; a finite formulation can exhaust it; my tradition's formulation is that one; therefore every other tradition is simply mistaken. The argument is valid. It is unsound if either suppressed premise fails.

On one reading the inference does succeed, and that must be conceded first. If "absolute truth" means *my religion's propositions are the sole truth*, exclusivism follows immediately — indeed trivially, because exclusivism has been built into the premise.

But one may hold that there **is** an Absolute Truth — the Real, or Brahman — while recognising all religions as **partial and perspectival** approaches to it: an absolute **referent** with pluralist **access**. That breaks the link. Three traditions deny the suppressed premise independently. Advaita's Brahman **without qualities** (*nirguṇa*) is beyond exhaustive predication, while **with-qualities** (*saguṇa*) forms structure real devotional paths. Jain **many-sidedness** holds reality many-sided and every viewpoint partially true, with conditional predication (*syādvāda*) indexing assertions to a standpoint. Hick's Real is transcategorial, so personal and non-personal predicates attach to manifestations rather than to the Real itself.

Ontological absolutism, epistemic fallibilism and political tolerance are three separate theses. Establishing the first settles neither of the others — which is why an exclusivist may consistently defend religious freedom on Locke's ground that force cannot produce belief, and why tolerance cannot be inferred from metaphysics without an additional premise.

Two concessions keep the answer honest. The reply presupposes that truth admits degrees of adequacy or non-literal mediation. And hard contradictions remain: a personal creator either exists or does not, and pluralism must acknowledge that residue rather than harmonise it.

The entailment therefore fails. Ontological absolutism entails epistemic exclusivism only if one adds the premise that a finite formulation can exhaust the absolute — and that premise is exactly what Advaita's *nirguṇa* and Jain many-sidedness deny.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Reconstructs the argument formally and locates the suppressed premises, which is what "discuss" a claim means.
- Concedes the reading on which the inference succeeds before denying the other.
- Supplies three independent denials of the premise, two of them Indian.
- Separates the three theses explicitly, blocking the tolerance-from-metaphysics slide.
- Concedes both limits, which is what the top band rewards.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 350 words, compress the three denials to Advaita plus Jainism. What must never be cut is the formal reconstruction, since the whole answer depends on exhibiting a premise the quotation leaves unstated.

#### PYQ 10 — 2023, Q8(b), 15 marks

**Exact verified question:** Does religious pluralism invite inter-religious conflicts and destroy the truth of religion? Discuss.

**Demand decoding:** **Two allegations, answered separately.** Conflict-generation and truth-destruction are different charges, and a single undifferentiated reply loses half the marks.

**Model solution:**
The stem makes two charges, and they must be separated.

**Charge one: pluralism invites conflict.** The thought is that relativising commitment provokes dispute rather than settling it. But conflict is not caused by plurality alone; power, identity and exclusivist certainty mediate it. The correct analysis distinguishes religion as **cause**, **marker**, **mobiliser** and **legitimiser** of conflict — most historical cases being the last three — and Sen's singular-affiliation fallacy locates the actual danger in the reduction of persons to a single identity. If anything, the combination pluralism opposes — absolute truth-claims joined to exclusivist soteriology and monopolised identity — is the conflict-generating one. Political coexistence, moreover, does not require agreement about truth; it requires reciprocity and harm-limitation.

**Charge two: pluralism destroys the truth of religion.** This is the more serious charge and it is answered by distinguishing **equal dignity or salvific efficacy** from **identical propositional truth**. Pluralism does not deny truth; it **relocates** absoluteness in the transcendent Real and treats religions as culturally conditioned but genuine responses. It denies exhaustive possession by a finite perspective, not the existence of an absolute. Coexistence does not mean "anything goes".

Two concessions are required. Pluralism cannot dissolve **hard contradictions**: a personal creator either exists or does not, and where reinterpretation by level, symbol, standpoint or end is unavailable, the contradiction must be acknowledged. And traditions may reasonably object that being redescribed as culturally limited appearances erases their self-understanding — the identity and mission concern, which D'Costa sharpens into the charge that pluralism is a covert exclusivism.

Pluralism therefore neither invites conflict nor destroys religious truth; it relocates truth and reassigns the sources of conflict. What it cannot do is harmonise every contradiction, and a pluralism that pretends otherwise is rhetoric rather than philosophy.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Splits the two allegations explicitly, which the directive requires.
- Answers the conflict charge with the four-role analysis rather than with reassurance.
- Answers the truth charge with the dignity-versus-truth distinction, which is the recorded reply.
- Concedes the hard-contradiction residue and the identity objection.
- Ends with the relocation formula and its qualification.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 250 words, compress the four-role analysis to a single clause. What must never be cut is the explicit separation of the two charges, since a merged answer forfeits the structure the directive is grading.

#### PYQ 11 — 2024, Q5(b), 10 marks

**Exact verified question:** Write a note on the notion of absolute truth in the context of religion.

**Demand decoding:** A **notion** question. It asks for the concept to be analysed, not for a verdict on a controversy. The four-way separation *is* the analysis.

**Model solution:**
"Absolute truth" in religion names a truth that is unconditioned — not relative to a person, a period or a perspective. Analysing the notion requires four distinctions that scripts routinely merge.

**Referent.** What the claim is about: the Real, Brahman, God, the unconditioned. Absoluteness here is ontological.

**Formulation.** How the referent is stated. Every formulation is made in a language, a genre and a conceptual scheme, and it does not follow from the absoluteness of the referent that a formulation is exhaustive. Advaita marks this internally by distinguishing Brahman **without qualities** (*nirguṇa*), beyond exhaustive predication, from **with-qualities** (*saguṇa*) forms.

**Access.** Who can know it, and how well. Finite knowers grasp aspects rather than totality — the Jain thesis of many-sidedness, with conditional predication (*syādvāda*) indexing assertions to a standpoint.

**Salvific claim.** Whether knowing the truth liberates, and whether it does so exclusively. This is a further question, not a corollary.

Two consequences follow. First, ontological absolutism does not entail epistemic monopoly: an absolute referent is compatible with plural, corrigible, partially adequate formulations. Second, absoluteness of the referent does not entail political privilege, since coexistence requires reciprocity rather than agreement.

The notion is therefore coherent and religiously indispensable — provided absoluteness is predicated of what is known rather than of anyone's statement of it, and provided contradictions that survive reinterpretation are acknowledged rather than harmonised.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Treats the stem as conceptual analysis, which is what "write a note on the notion" asks for.
- Gives four distinctions, each with a named tradition attached.
- Draws two consequences instead of leaving the analysis inert.
- Uses *nirguṇa*/*saguṇa* as a tradition's own internal marking of the point.
- Closes with the discipline that keeps absoluteness on the referent.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 150 words, drop one of the two consequences. What must never be cut is the four-way separation, since it is the analysis the notion-question is asking for.

#### PYQ 12 — 2025, Q7(a), 20 marks

**Exact verified question:** How does the Vedāntic view of Religious Pluralism address the conflicting truth claims of different faiths? Answer with reference to Swami Vivekananda's view of Universal Religion.

**Demand decoding:** "**How does** X address…? Answer with reference to [thinker]" buys a **mechanism** plus **fidelity to the named thinker**. Generic Vedānta does not score; Vivekananda's own theses do.

**Model solution:**
The Vedāntic response addresses conflicting truth-claims by a single move: it separates the **one referent** from the **many formulations**, and then explains the plurality of formulations by the plurality of human temperaments.

The textual anchor is *ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti*, **Ṛgveda 1.164.46**, from the *asya vāmasya* riddle-hymn ascribed to Dīrghatamas. Discipline is required: its native subject is the naming of Vedic deities, and the inter-religious extension is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century development that must be argued. What the verse supplies is the structure — one existent, many namings.

Vivekananda's **Universal Religion** supplies the mechanism. Human capacities vary; spiritual disciplines must therefore vary; the non-dual ground supplies a common metaphysical reference; and the test is **realisation**, not doctrinal uniformity. The four disciplines — knowledge (*jñāna*), devotion (*bhakti*), action (*karma*) and meditative discipline (*rāja*) — address distinct dispositions. Plurality of path is therefore *expected*, not merely tolerated. At the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893 he demands **acceptance, not mere toleration**, on the ground that toleration still condescends.

Conflicting claims are handled at two levels: Brahman **without qualities** (*nirguṇa*) is beyond exhaustive predication, while **with-qualities** (*saguṇa*) forms structure real devotional paths. Jain many-sidedness adds an epistemic discipline — every judgement is standpoint-indexed — and so dogmatic exclusivism is dissolved without abandoning truth.

Two evaluations are required. Against Hick, the Vedāntic model grounds pluralism metaphysically rather than in a transcategorial Real that is arguably too thin to describe. Against Heim, it owes an argument: it does not follow without proof that traditions seeking personal communion, creator relation or cessation all reach the same end. And the standing objection is assimilation — if Vedānta supplies the final meta-framework, other faiths are included on terms they have not set, which is inclusivism. Vivekananda answers that distinctive forms are preserved and harmony does not require homogenisation; whether the metaphysical hierarchy remains inclusivist is **contested**.

The Vedāntic view therefore addresses conflicting truth-claims by relocating absoluteness in the referent and deriving plurality from temperament — a strong and specifically Indian pluralism whose unresolved question is whether its own framework is neutral.

**Why this earns marks:**
- Names the mechanism in the first sentence, which is what "how does" buys.
- Applies the full citation discipline to RV 1.164.46 rather than quoting it as a slogan.
- Uses Vivekananda's own theses — temperaments, the four disciplines, acceptance over toleration.
- Places the model against Hick and Heim, which converts exposition into assessment.
- Leaves the assimilation objection contested, as the owner requires.

**Upgrade route for this answer:** at 350 words, compress the Jain addition to one clause. What must never be cut is the assimilation objection with its reply and its contested status, since that is the evaluative move the 20-mark band is grading.

### Original solved Mains practice — 6 models

These six questions are **learner-written practice**, not previous-year questions. They are constructed to drill the demands the printed corpus has not yet combined.

#### Original 1 · 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish between alethic and soteriological religious pluralism, and state one consequence of the distinction. *(Answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:**
Alethic pluralism concerns the **truth** of religious claims: it holds that several traditions state genuine truths about ultimate reality, because no finite formulation exhausts what it formulates. Soteriological pluralism concerns **efficacy**: it holds that several traditions really transform, liberate or save their adherents.

The two are separable in both directions. One may hold that another tradition's doctrines are largely mistaken while conceding that its practice transforms; one may hold that its doctrines are substantially true while denying that its path is efficacious for salvation.

The consequence is decisive for the standard argument. Hick's soteriological criterion — transformation from self-centredness to Reality-centredness — assesses traditions for authenticity and, by his own statement, does **not** render contradictory doctrines jointly true. A script that treats the criterion as settling the truth question has conceded the relativism charge without argument, since it has answered an alethic objection with soteriological evidence.

#### Original 2 · 10 marks

**Question:** "Toleration granted by the strong to the weak is permission, not equality." Examine. *(Answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:**
The statement is correct, and its force comes from the **power component** in the concept of toleration itself.

Toleration requires three things: an objection to what is tolerated, reasons that outweigh without cancelling that objection, and the power to interfere combined with deliberate restraint. The third condition means that toleration is always exercised by someone who *could* have done otherwise.

Forst's ladder makes the consequence explicit. **Permission** is the powerful allowing a minority to exist on sufferance; **coexistence** is a pragmatic modus vivendi; **respect** is reciprocal recognition as equal moral-political persons; **esteem** positively values the other's beliefs. Only respect and esteem are compatible with equal citizenship, and most historical toleration was permission.

Vivekananda reaches the same conclusion from the other direction, demanding acceptance rather than toleration because toleration still condescends. Toleration is thus indispensable — the objection component makes it a moral achievement — and insufficient.

#### Original 3 · 15 marks

**Question:** Assess the claim that Hick's pluralistic hypothesis is a covert exclusivism. *(Answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer:**
The claim is D'Costa's, and it is the strongest single objection to Hick.

Hick's hypothesis treats the great traditions as culturally conditioned responses to the transcategorial Real, assessed soteriologically by transformation from self-centredness to Reality-centredness, and it is offered as a **second-order** philosophical hypothesis rather than a first-order creed. D'Costa's charge is that this second-order account is itself held to be uniquely true, and that it reinterprets every tradition against that tradition's self-understanding.

Hick's reply is that a philosophical hypothesis about religions is not a rival religion. The reply is intelligible but incomplete. A theory that tells traditions what they are *really* doing exercises exactly the adjudicating authority it denies to each of them; the distinction between orders relocates the claim to privileged access rather than dissolving it. This is why the objection converges with the identity and mission concern that traditions themselves raise.

Two qualifications are needed. First, the charge is diagnostic rather than constructive: it shows that Hick has not achieved neutrality, not that exclusivism is true — and Plantinga's parity replies establish only permissibility, not truth, leaving the contingency of birth untouched. Second, the charge does not fall equally on every pluralism: Heim's differential pluralism, which holds that communion, non-dual realisation and cessation are three destinations rather than three descriptions, respects the traditions' self-understanding far better, though at the cost of a single ultimate.

The verdict is that Hick's pluralism is not neutral, that no available position is, and that the live question is therefore whose framework is held corrigibly.

#### Original 4 · 15 marks

**Question:** How far does Jain many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*) resolve the problem of conflicting religious truth-claims? *(Answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer:**
It resolves a substantial part of the problem and leaves a residue that must be conceded.

The apparatus is three-tiered. **Many-sidedness** (*anekāntavāda*) holds reality to be many-sided; **standpoint theory** (*nayavāda*) makes explicit the standpoint from which a judgement is made; **conditional predication** (*syādvāda*) prefixes assertions with *syāt*, "in a certain respect", and the **sevenfold scheme** (*saptabhaṅgī*) enumerates the modes. Framework-relativity is thereby marked **inside** the proposition rather than left implicit.

This is not relativism. Relativism denies that there is a fact of the matter; conditional predication states the respect under which a predicate holds, so the assertions remain determinate and checkable. Predication is regulated, not abandoned.

Its power appears at two points. In the absolute-truth debate it denies the premise that a finite formulation can exhaust the absolute, so belief in one absolute Truth no longer entails exclusivism. In the epistemology of disagreement it addresses Fogelin's problem directly: deep disagreement arises because parties do not share the framework against which evidence would count, and the Jain device makes that framework explicit — a genuine Indian contribution to a live Western problem, and worth far more than the parable of the elephant.

Its limit is exact. The apparatus works wherever a difference of aspect, level or soteriological orientation can be specified. Where two claims contradict on the same question in the same respect — a personal creator exists, or does not — no index of standpoint helps. There, pluralism must distinguish literal conflict, symbolic language and different soteriological aims, and acknowledge whatever contradiction survives.

#### Original 5 · 20 marks

**Question:** "Political coexistence between religions neither requires nor follows from agreement about religious truth." Critically discuss with reference to both Western and Indian resources. *(Answer in about 350 words.)*

**Model answer:**
The statement makes two claims, and both are defensible.

**Coexistence does not require agreement about truth.** Locke's *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) supplies the decisive argument: the magistrate has no commission over souls, force cannot produce belief but only profession, and coerced conformity would not save in any case. The argument is about the psychology of belief, so it is available to an exclusivist who thinks every other religion false. Mill (1859) adds the harm principle and the epistemic argument that suppressing an opinion costs us either the truth or its clearer perception through collision with error; Rawls locates stability in an overlapping consensus among incompatible comprehensive doctrines. On the Indian side, Aśoka's **Rock Edict XII** — the earliest verifiable state-level argument — enjoins not honouring one's own sect by disparaging others and commends concord (*samavāya*) without requiring doctrinal agreement.

**Coexistence does not follow from agreement about truth either.** Metaphysical unity does not by itself deliver a political conclusion: an additional premise about coercion, harm or reciprocity is always required, and inferring tolerance directly from a metaphysical thesis is a standard error. The point cuts both ways — an exclusivist may be tolerant, and a pluralist may be coercive.

Three qualifications keep the claim honest. First, Forst's ladder shows that "coexistence" is ambiguous: permission granted by the strong is not equality, and only respect and esteem are compatible with equal citizenship. Second, coexistence has limits — Popper's paradox, restricted to those who answer argument with violence, and the practice/belief asymmetry under which practice may be limited by public order, morality and health. Third, Shachar's paradox of multicultural vulnerability shows that accommodating a community can reduce the freedom of persons inside it, so coexistence between groups is not automatically freedom for individuals.

Vivekananda's demand for **acceptance rather than toleration** and Gandhi's pairing of equal respect with fallibilism show that the Indian tradition reaches the same conclusion by a different route — through the partiality of one's own grasp of truth rather than through the incompetence of the magistrate.

The statement therefore holds, provided coexistence is specified at the level of respect rather than permission, and its limits are justified by harm and reciprocity rather than by doctrinal content.

#### Original 6 · 20 marks

**Question:** Examine whether the epistemology of deep disagreement strengthens or weakens the case for religious pluralism. *(Answer in about 350 words.)*

**Model answer:**
It does both, and the balance is what makes it examinable.

**The apparatus.** Within a shared framework, the dispute is between **conciliationism** or equal weight (Feldman, Christensen), on which discovering peer disagreement should reduce confidence, and **steadfastness** (Kelly, van Inwagen), on which one may retain a belief because one's own reasoning is part of one's evidence; the **independence principle** — assess the disagreement without relying on the reasoning under dispute — is what conciliationism needs and steadfastness denies. **Deep disagreement**, in Fogelin's technical sense (1985), goes further: the parties do not share the **framework propositions** — Wittgenstein's hinge commitments in *On Certainty* — against which evidence would count, so the same evidence is not evidence for the same thing. Lynch extends the analysis to disagreement about epistemic principles themselves, where any defence is circular.

**Why religion is a paradigm case.** The parties differ over the sources of evidence, the criteria of adequacy — salvation, liberation, cessation — and the framework in which any datum would count.

**Where it weakens pluralism.** Two consequences cut against it. Exclusivism cannot be convicted of irrationality merely for persisting, which removes the easiest charge against it and gives Plantinga's third reply its technical backing. And pluralism cannot be established merely by pointing at persistence — the persistence that seemed to support Hick's first premise is now explained without any pluralist assumption.

**Where it strengthens pluralism.** Two further consequences cut in its favour. The failure of convergence is *predicted* rather than scandalous, so it is weak evidence for relativism. And what *can* be rationally required are the **procedural virtues** — fallibilism, humility, epistemic charity, and willingness to make one's framework explicit — which is precisely the ethic pluralism recommends. Jain conditional predication is the most developed attempt to make framework-relativity explicit inside the proposition.

**The verdict.** Deep disagreement weakens the case for pluralism as a *demonstrated metaphysics* and strengthens it as an *ethic of engagement*. Unresolvability is epistemic, not alethic: one party may still be right, and what fails is our capacity to show which. That is a smaller claim than Hick's, and it is more defensible.
