# Anti-Corruption Institutions — Live Session Edition

This session teaches Topic 20 as one continuous institutional map: how the Central Vigilance Commission (CVC), the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI), the Lokpal, State Lokayuktas, State Vigilance Commissions, state Anti-Corruption Bureaus (ACBs) and departmental/CVO vigilance fit onto a single jurisdiction map without being confused for one another; how the CVC moved from a 1964 executive resolution to statutory status under the CVC Act, 2003 following the *Vineet Narain* judgment, and exactly what it can and cannot do; how the CBI's police powers rest on the Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946 rather than on any dedicated CBI statute, and how the State-consent gate for those powers actually works; how the 2nd ARC's lean, three-member 2007 Lok Pal proposal differs — in composition, jurisdiction and organic-linkage design — from the larger, differently composed Lokpal that Parliament actually enacted in 2013; how the Prime Minister's position under the enacted Act is a qualified inclusion rather than the ARC's proposed blanket exclusion; how the ARC's own "multiplicity and lack of coherence" diagnosis survives, in modified form, even after the 2013 Act; how Lokayuktas, State Vigilance Commissions and ACBs vary from state to state with no uniform design; which institution actually operates each procedural gate that Topic 19 names in the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988; and who currently holds each of these offices, with what vacancies and continuity questions, as of October 2026. It does not re-teach, in full doctrinal depth, the Prevention of Corruption Act's offence architecture, Sections 7/7A/8/9-10/13/17A/19/20, the Benami law or the whistleblower-law status (Topic 19's exclusive ground); the honest-official-protection design — the bona fides test, ARC 7.9's screening and secret-verification sequence, preventive vigilance and the POSH Act's parallel harassment due process (Topic 21's exclusive ground); the eight-element case-study method (Topic 22); or named comparative case evidence (Topic 23). Those remain cross-links only, held to their owning topic's boundary.

They fail because, under time pressure, they write "refer it to the CVC" as if that single sentence disposes of the institutional question — when the honest answer requires naming which of several distinct bodies actually has the power being asked for. The first working definition any answer needs is the **CVC**: the apex Union vigilance body, set up by executive resolution in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation, given statutory status only decades later by the CVC Act, 2003. It is **advisory and superintending**, not investigating or prosecuting — a distinction the next lesson develops in full. The second is the **CBI**: the Union's principal investigating agency for corruption, economic and organised (non-terror) crime, whose police powers derive entirely from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946 — it has no separate CBI statute of its own, a fact examiners test directly. The third is the **Lokpal**: a statutory body created by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, with overarching jurisdiction that can direct or refer a matter to the CBI and that also runs its own inquiry and prosecution wings. The fourth is **departmental/CVO vigilance**: every government department and public-sector undertaking has its own Chief Vigilance Officer, who is usually the first point of contact for a complaint about that agency's own staff and who routes matters to the CVC where required.

At the State level the same three functions — advisory/superintending, investigating, and overarching-ombudsman — are supplied by three separate, state-specific bodies: the **State Vigilance Commission** (where a state has constituted one), the **state Anti-Corruption Bureau**, and the **State Lokayukta**. Unlike the Union trio, none of these three is uniform across states — Lesson 9 develops exactly how and why they differ.

| Fact pattern | Which body actually has this power? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Advising the Union government on integrity in administration; superintending CBI's PC Act work | CVC | Statutory advisory/superintending role under the CVC Act, 2003 |
| Investigating a bribery case involving a central government officer, with lawful territorial jurisdiction | CBI | DSPE Act, 1946 gives it police powers, subject to the State-consent gate (Lesson 3) |
| Directing the CBI's investigation in a case referred to it, or running its own inquiry into a Minister | Lokpal | Statutory power under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 |
| Receiving the first internal complaint about a departmental clerk's conduct | Departmental/CVO vigilance | Internal, agency-specific disciplinary machinery; the usual first stop |
| Investigating a bribery case involving a state government officer, under state law | State Anti-Corruption Bureau | State police machinery; distinct legal basis from the Union CBI |
| Acting as an ombudsman over a state Minister or state official under a state Lokayukta Act | State Lokayukta | State-specific statute; composition and powers vary (Lesson 9) |

The ARC's own diagnosis (para 4.2.5) is the reason this map matters at all: it found "a multiplicity of institutions with overlapping functions" that "undermined their coherence" and left anti-corruption work without a "unified focus" — identifying CVC, departmental vigilance units and CBI at the Union level, and a varying mix of Lokayuktas, State Vigilance Commissions and Anti-Corruption Bureaus at the State level, as suffering from exactly this uncoordinated overlap. That diagnosis is why a stronger, overarching institution — which eventually became the Lokpal — was proposed at all, and it is the thread this entire topic follows through Lessons 5-8.

A predictable objection is: "if there is so much overlap, why not simply merge every one of these bodies into one"? The reply is that the functions are deliberately separated for a reason that is itself examinable: an advisory/superintending function (CVC), an investigating function (CBI), and an adjudicatory/ombudsman function (Lokpal) check one another precisely because they are held by different hands — merging them would concentrate advisory, investigative and adjudicatory power in a single body, which the ARC itself never proposed. The ARC's own remedy for multiplicity was **coordination** (the "organic link," Lessons 5 and 7), not **fusion**.

Revision notes:
- Union-level trio: CVC (advisory/superintending), CBI (investigating, DSPE Act basis), Lokpal (overarching, can direct CBI, runs its own inquiry/prosecution wings); plus departmental/CVO vigilance as the internal first stop.
- State-level trio: State Vigilance Commission, state Anti-Corruption Bureau, State Lokayukta — each a separate, state-specific body, not simply a branch office of its Union counterpart.
- ARC 4.2.5's "multiplicity... overlapping functions... undermined coherence... lacking unified focus" is the exact diagnostic language this whole topic traces through to the 2013 Lokpal reform and its unresolved residue.
- The examiner-facing discipline: never write "refer it to the CVC" as an undifferentiated answer; name the specific body whose specific power the fact pattern actually requires.
- The ARC's own remedy for overlap was coordination between separately held powers, not merging those powers into one body.

### Concept check

**Question:** A classmate writes, "CVC, CBI and Lokpal are really just three names for the same anti-corruption function, so naming any one of them is enough in an answer." Using this lesson's map, explain precisely why that claim fails.

**Model answer:** The map in this lesson separates three distinct functions held by three distinct bodies — the CVC's advisory/superintending role, the CBI's investigating role under the DSPE Act, 1946, and the Lokpal's overarching, direction-capable role under the 2013 Act — alongside a separate departmental/CVO first-contact layer and three further state-specific bodies; a fact pattern naming "which body should act" requires identifying the one body whose statutory function actually matches the power being asked for, so collapsing all three (or six) into a single undifferentiated "anti-corruption body" answer loses exactly the institutional precision the ARC's own multiplicity diagnosis was designed to fix.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat "anti-corruption institution" as one generic category; each named body holds a specific, non-interchangeable function, and a complete answer names the correct one for the specific power or action the question actually requires.

A recurring UPSC trap asserts that the CVC can prosecute a corrupt official. It cannot, and the reason is historical before it is legal: the CVC existed for thirty-nine years with no statutory force at all.

```text
1962-64  Santhanam Committee on Prevention of Corruption recommends an apex vigilance body
            |
            v
1964     CVC set up by executive resolution — neither constitutional nor statutory
            |
            v
18 Dec 1997  Vineet Narain v. Union of India, (1998) 1 SCC 226 — Supreme Court finds the
             CBI's investigative independence compromised by executive control; directs
             statutory backing for the CVC and insulation of CBI's functioning
            |
            v
11 Sep 2003  Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003 — CVC finally given statutory status
            |
            v
2026         CVC continues to function under the 2003 Act; see Lesson 11 for current
             officeholders and the 2026 leadership-transition question
```

The CVC's composition is deliberately small: a **Central Vigilance Commissioner** as chairperson, plus **not more than two Vigilance Commissioners** — a multi-member, not single-member, body, which is itself a frequently tested fact. All three are appointed by the President, acting on the recommendation of a three-member committee composed of the Prime Minister (as chairperson), the Union Home Minister, and the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha. Each holds office for **four years or until the age of sixty-five, whichever is earlier**, and may not take further government employment afterward — a design meant to reduce any incentive to decide cases with an eye on post-retirement postings. Removal follows the same high threshold used for constitutional functionaries even though the CVC itself is only statutory: the President may remove a member only on proved misbehaviour or incapacity, established through a Supreme Court inquiry. The Central Vigilance Commissioner draws salary parity with the UPSC Chairman, and a Vigilance Commissioner with a UPSC Member — a small but genuinely examinable detail.

On powers, the single most important precision is this: the CVC's superintendence over the CBI is **not general** — it extends only to the CBI's investigation of offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, not to the CBI's non-corruption work (economic offences, organised crime referred by courts, and so on). Beyond that specific superintendence, the CVC: inquires or causes inquiry into complaints concerning the public-servant categories its governing section and applicable rules/orders specify (a category list, not a single rank-based shorthand); exercises civil-court powers while conducting an inquiry; chairs the committee that recommends the Enforcement Directorate's Director and, since the 2013 Lokpal Act, CBI officers of the rank of Superintendent of Police and above (excluding the Director, whose own committee is separate — Lesson 3); submits an annual report to the President, which is placed before Parliament; and acts as the designated receiving authority for whistleblower complaints under the **Public Interest Disclosure and Protection of Informer (PIDPI) Resolution, 2004** — the operative, non-statutory mechanism that keeps a complainant's identity confidential rather than anonymous (Topic 21 develops this ladder in full; Topic 19 states the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014's own uncommenced status).

| Function | What the CVC does | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|
| Advice | Advises the Union government on integrity in administration | Its advice is not binding; government must record reasons if it disagrees |
| Superintendence | Superintends the CBI's investigation of Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 offences only | Does not superintend the CBI's non-corruption investigative work generally |
| Inquiry | Inquires or causes inquiry into complaints against specified categories of public servants | Does not itself prosecute or try any case |
| Director selection | Chairs the committee recommending the ED Director and CBI officers of SP rank and above (excluding Director) | Does not itself appoint the CBI Director (a separate committee, Lesson 3) |
| Whistleblower route | Receiving authority under the PIDPI Resolution, 2004 | Cannot accept an anonymous or pseudonymous complaint under PIDPI |

A genuine, examinable criticism is that an advisory body whose advice is not binding risks being toothless in practice. The honest reply is not that this criticism is wrong, but that it describes a deliberate trade-off rather than an oversight: pairing non-binding advice with a **reasons-recording requirement** preserves the elected government's final accountability for vigilance decisions while still creating a documented, auditable record whenever that advice is rejected — a weaker safeguard than binding power, but not a meaningless one, and a distinction worth stating explicitly rather than simply conceding the "toothless" label.

Revision notes:
- Lineage: Santhanam Committee (1962-64) -> CVC by executive resolution (1964) -> *Vineet Narain* (18 December 1997) -> CVC Act, 2003 (assent 11 September 2003).
- Composition: Central Vigilance Commissioner (chair) + not more than 2 Vigilance Commissioners; appointed by the President on a PM + Home Minister + LoP (Lok Sabha) committee; term 4 years or age 65, whichever earlier; removal only via a Supreme Court inquiry into proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
- Superintendence over the CBI is limited strictly to Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 offences — the single most tested CVC fact.
- The CVC is advisory/inquiring, never prosecuting; its advice is not binding, but disagreement must be recorded in reasons.
- PIDPI, 2004 (not the still-uncommenced Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014) is the CVC's operative whistleblower-receiving mechanism; it excludes anonymous/pseudonymous complaints.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes, "Since the CVC was created in 1964, it has always had full legal powers to compel compliance with its advice." Identify and correct the two separate errors in this claim.

**Model answer:** The first error conflates the CVC's 1964 origin with legal force: the CVC was created only by executive resolution in 1964 and remained neither a constitutional nor a statutory body until the CVC Act, 2003 gave it statutory status, following the Supreme Court's 1997 *Vineet Narain* directions — so for nearly four decades it operated without the statutory backing the claim assumes; the second error asserts that the CVC can "compel compliance," when in fact its advice has never been binding even after 2003 — the government may decline to follow it, subject only to the discipline of recording its reasons for disagreement, so "full legal powers to compel compliance" overstates what the statutory status actually achieved.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate a body's year of creation with the year it acquired legal force, and do not equate statutory status with binding authority; the CVC gained the former in 2003 without ever acquiring the latter.

The single most commonly mis-stated CBI fact is its legal foundation itself: unlike the CVC, which is at least statutory since 2003, the **CBI is not a statutory body at all**. It was set up in 1963 by a Home Ministry executive resolution, on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation, and its police powers — the power to investigate, arrest and prosecute through designated prosecutors — derive entirely from the **Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946**, a colonial-era statute never replaced by a dedicated CBI Act.

The Director of CBI's appointment, since the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 amended the process, is decided by a three-member committee: the Prime Minister (chair), the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or, where there is no recognised Leader of the Opposition, the leader of the single largest opposition party, under the DSPE (Amendment) Act, 2014), and the Chief Justice of India or a Supreme Court judge the Chief Justice nominates. This is a deliberately **different** committee from the CVC's own appointment committee (which has the Home Minister rather than the CJI/nominated judge) — a distinction examiners use as a trap in its own right. Tenure is a minimum of two years, with annual extensions permissible on the committee's recommendation and recorded reasons, up to a total of five years under the DSPE (Amendment) Act, 2021 — three one-year extensions beyond the initial term. The sitting CBI Director as of 2026, Praveen Sood, illustrates this mechanism directly: appointed on 25 May 2023, he received a first one-year extension in 2025 and a second one-year extension in May 2026, taking his tenure to May 2027, approved by a committee in which the sitting Leader of the Opposition recorded formal dissent about the process's transparency — a live, current illustration of the extension mechanism's own independence debate (Lesson 11 develops this as a current-functioning data point rather than a settled verdict).

The jurisdiction gate is the second heavily tested dimension. Ordinary exercise of DSPE police powers within a State requires that State's **consent**, which may be **general** (a standing, open-ended consent covering all cases of a defined type) or **case-specific** (consent sought and granted for one named case). Several States have, over time, withdrawn their general consent — the most recently traced official roster names ten States: Punjab, Jharkhand, Kerala, Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal, Mizoram, Telangana, Meghalaya and Tamil Nadu — which changes the CBI's **ordinary** route for any fresh case arising in those States from "automatic" to "case-specific consent required." Withdrawal of general consent is **not**, however, an absolute veto on CBI action in that State: the Supreme Court under Article 32 and High Courts under Article 226 retain an **exceptional constitutional power** to direct a CBI investigation in an appropriate case, a power the courts use cautiously rather than as a routine bypass of the consent structure.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| The CBI is a statutory body | False — it is not even statutory; its powers rest on the DSPE Act, 1946 |
| Withdrawal of general consent ends the CBI's power in that State absolutely | False — fresh ordinary-route cases need case-specific consent, but constitutional courts retain the Article 32/226 exception |
| The CBI Director's appointment committee is identical to the CVC's | False — the CBI panel has the CJI/nominated SC judge; the CVC panel has the Home Minister |
| The CBI's tenure-extension mechanism is unlimited | False — capped at a total of five years via up to three one-year extensions under the 2021 DSPE Amendment |

A fair criticism of the CBI's design is captured in the Supreme Court's own 2013 "caged parrot" remark during the Coalgate proceedings — that the CBI's structural dependence on the executive for funding, personnel and (at the time) single-directive approval compromises its functional autonomy. The strongest reply available within this topic's own institutional ground is twofold: first, the fixed, extension-capped Director tenure (post-2013, post-2021) is itself a structural response aimed at reducing one specific lever of executive control over CBI leadership; second, the State-consent gate's constitutional-court exception shows that CBI's ordinary-route dependence on executive/State cooperation is not the only legal route available when a case genuinely warrants independent investigation. Neither reply fully answers the "caged parrot" critique — a complete answer should say so rather than claim the criticism has been resolved.

Revision notes:
- The CBI was set up in 1963 by executive resolution; it is not a statutory body; its police powers rest entirely on the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946.
- CBI Director's appointment committee (post-2013): PM (chair) + LoP, Lok Sabha (or single-largest-opposition-party leader, per the 2014 DSPE Amendment) + CJI/nominated SC judge; distinct from the CVC's own panel.
- Tenure: minimum two years; up to three one-year extensions under the 2021 DSPE Amendment, for a maximum total of five years.
- State consent (general or case-specific) is the ordinary DSPE Act gate; its withdrawal by a State changes the route for fresh cases but does not disable the Supreme Court's Article 32 or a High Court's Article 226 power to direct a CBI investigation.
- "Caged parrot" (2013, Coalgate) remains a genuine, only partially answered structural criticism of CBI independence.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State government announces it has withdrawn "general consent" for the CBI. A classmate concludes the CBI can now never investigate any case in that State again. Using this lesson, state precisely what is right and wrong about that conclusion.

**Model answer:** What is right is that withdrawal of general consent genuinely changes the CBI's ordinary route for fresh cases in that State — it can no longer rely on a standing, open-ended consent and must instead seek case-specific consent for each new matter; what is wrong is the word "never," because constitutional courts retain an exceptional power untouched by any State's consent decision: the Supreme Court under Article 32 and the relevant High Court under Article 226 can still direct a CBI investigation in an appropriate case, a power that exists precisely because the ordinary DSPE Act consent structure governs the executive-led route, not the constitutional-remedy route.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat "withdrawal of general consent" as an absolute jurisdictional bar; it is a change to the ordinary executive-route gate only, leaving the exceptional constitutional-court route fully intact.

Second, **jurisdiction**: the ARC's proposal was deliberately narrow (Ministers and MPs only), while the enacted Act reaches every central government official across Groups A to D and a defined set of government-linked and government-or-foreign-funded bodies — comprehensive coverage the ARC itself had not recommended. Why did the design expand this far beyond the ARC's own proposal? The honest answer is political-economy, not technocratic refinement: the ARC's 2007 report predates the sustained 2011 Anna Hazare-led "India Against Corruption" movement and the parliamentary negotiation that followed it; ten separate Lokpal bills had been introduced in Parliament between 1968 and December 2011 without passage, and it was only in that post-2011 climate of public mobilisation demanding comprehensive coverage — not merely ministerial accountability — that the broader 2013 design was finally enacted.

Is the Prime Minister inside or outside the Lokpal's reach? Neither a flat "yes" nor a flat "no" survives contact with the actual text — the honest answer is a **qualified inclusion**, and the qualification is itself the ARC's own unresolved worry, only partially redesigned.

The enacted Act's actual design has four distinct features, each independently examinable. First, **subject-matter exclusion**: no Lokpal inquiry may touch allegations relating to "international relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space" — a named, closed list, not a general security carve-out. Second, a **procedural threshold**: an inquiry against the PM may be initiated only if the Lokpal's full Bench considers the complaint and **at least two-thirds of the Members** approve — a supermajority, not a simple majority. Third, **secrecy of process**: proceedings touching the PM are held **in camera**. Fourth, a **records restriction**: if the complaint is ultimately dismissed, the records are neither published nor made available to anyone — a protection that operates specifically at the point a complaint fails.

| Feature | ARC's 2007 proposal | Enacted 2013 Act |
|---|---|---|
| Basic stance | Blanket exclusion of a formal Lok Pal enquiry into the PM | Qualified inclusion of the PM |
| Subject-matter limit | Not applicable (PM excluded entirely) | Five named exclusions: international relations, external/internal security, public order, atomic energy, space |
| Threshold to proceed | Not applicable | Full-Bench consideration plus at least two-thirds of Members' approval |
| Process | Not applicable | In camera |
| Outcome if dismissed | Not applicable | Records neither published nor made available to anyone |
| PM's actual check | Parliament, via a no-confidence motion | Both Parliament's ordinary political accountability and this qualified Lokpal route now coexist |

A genuine, balanced evaluation must hold two things at once. On one hand, the enacted compromise can fairly be read as honouring **both** of the ARC's own competing concerns better than a flat exclusion would: it satisfies the "no functionary should be exempt" principle the ARC itself called technically correct, while using a raised threshold — rather than an outright exemption — to address the institutional-continuity worry. On the other hand, the advanced critique the ARC's own reasoning implies is that **any** formal Lokpal enquiry into a sitting Prime Minister, however procedurally hedged, still carries a reputational and governance-disruption risk the moment it becomes public knowledge that two-thirds of the Members approved proceeding — a risk that exists *before* the enquiry's outcome is known, which is precisely the institutional-continuity harm the ARC identified in 2007. The honest verdict, then, is that the 2013 compromise **narrows** the tension the ARC diagnosed through a higher threshold and narrower subject matter; it does not **eliminate** it.

A boundary case sharpens this: an allegation against a sitting Prime Minister concerning a matter that squarely touches national security would trigger the Act's own exclusion outright, redirecting accountability back to Parliament alone — a result consistent with the ARC's own underlying reasoning even though the enacted safeguard's mechanism (a named subject-matter carve-out) differs from the ARC's blanket-exclusion proposal.

Revision notes:
- ARC's own concession: "no functionary should be exempt" is technically correct, but a formal PM enquiry carries an institutional-continuity harm distinct from any claim of personal immunity.
- ARC's resolution: Parliament's no-confidence motion remains the PM's check; Ministers/MPs (whose removal does not itself destabilise government) properly fall under Lok Pal jurisdiction.
- Enacted Act's four-part compromise: five named subject-matter exclusions; a two-thirds-of-Members threshold to initiate inquiry; in-camera proceedings; records neither published nor made available if the complaint is dismissed.
- Balanced verdict: the 2013 compromise narrows, but does not fully eliminate, the ARC's own institutional-continuity worry.
- Boundary case: a national-security-linked allegation against the PM triggers the Act's own exclusion, redirecting accountability to Parliament.

The Selection Committee's own composition is worth naming precisely because it differs from both the CVC's and the CBI Director's panels already taught in Lessons 2-3: it brings together the Prime Minister, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, the Chief Justice of India or a Supreme Court judge he nominates, and one eminent jurist nominated by the President on the first four members' advice — five seats rather than three, with a Search Committee (itself bound by the same social-category representation rule as the Lokpal's own membership) assisting the shortlisting process. ```

The honest, examinable position is neither "the Lokpal solved multiplicity" nor "the Lokpal changed nothing." It is a qualified middle position with two separate halves. At the **Union** level, the 2013 Act's design is best read as an attempt at **coordination rather than fusion**: the Lokpal does not replace CVC or CBI, does not acquire a standing command over all CBI work, and instead holds a bounded referral power plus its own independent inquiry/prosecution machinery — a design that, on paper, is meant to close gaps (a case falling between CVC's advisory role and CBI's investigative one) rather than create a fifth overlapping function. Whether this design succeeds **in practice** is a question about referrals actually made, reasons actually recorded, and timelines actually met — evidence, not assumption, and Lesson 11 supplies the closest available 2026 data point (the Lokpal's own FY2025-26 case-handling figures). At the **State** level, the picture is considerably less resolved: Section 63 of the 2013 Act compelled every State to establish *some* Lokayukta, but prescribed no uniform composition, jurisdiction, tenure or removal design — so the ARC's own 2007 "multiplicity and lack of coherence" diagnosis remains **substantially valid** at the sub-national level even after the Union-level reform, precisely because the 2013 Act's own text targets high Union functionaries and leaves State design to State legislatures (Lesson 9 develops exactly how widely that State-level design actually varies).

| Claim | Verdict | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| "The 2013 Lokpal Act fully resolved the ARC's 2007 multiplicity diagnosis" | False | It addresses central high-functionary coverage; State-level heterogeneity and Union-level operational-coordination evidence remain open questions |
| "The Lokpal simply duplicates the CVC and CBI" | Overstated | Its referral-based CBI-direction power and independent inquiry/prosecution wings are designed as coordination instruments, not parallel duplicates, though evidence of effectiveness is still required |
| "State-level multiplicity is identical to Union-level multiplicity" | False | Union level now has a coordination design (Lokpal) layered atop CVC/CBI; State level has only a bare existence mandate (Section 63) with no uniform design at all |

A balanced answer to "has India's anti-corruption institutional architecture solved the multiplicity problem" must therefore name both halves explicitly: a genuine, if operationally unproven, coordination attempt at the Union level, and a substantially unresolved coherence gap at the State level — never a single-direction verdict in either direction.

Revision notes:
- ARC 4.2.5's core diagnosis: multiplicity with overlapping functions undermining coherence and unified focus, at both Union (CVC, departmental vigilance, CBI) and State (varying Lokayukta/SVC/ACB) levels.
- The Lokpal's 2013 design is best read as an attempt at coordination (referral-based CBI-direction power; its own independent inquiry/prosecution wings; no replacement of CVC/CBI) rather than simple duplication — but operational effectiveness is a separate, evidence-based question (Lesson 11).
- Section 63 mandates a State Lokayukta's bare existence (by 16 January 2015) without a uniform model, so the ARC's 2007 State-level diagnosis remains substantially valid even after 2013.

Alongside the Lokayukta, two further State-specific bodies complete the State-level trio Lesson 1 first named. The **State Vigilance Commission**, where a State has constituted one, is the State-level analogue of the Union CVC — an advisory/superintending body over that State's own vigilance administration, operating under State rules rather than the CVC Act, 2003. The **State Anti-Corruption Bureau (ACB)** is the State-level analogue of the CBI's Anti-Corruption Division — state police machinery investigating corruption offences under State jurisdiction, operating under the relevant State police legislation rather than the Union's Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. The precise boundary matters because a state-cadre officer's case does **not** automatically fall to the Union CBI merely because the offence resembles one the CBI would investigate at the Union level; jurisdiction follows the officer's and the offence's own level of government.

This distinction becomes concretely examinable in exactly the routing scenario the basic owner's mechanism point names directly: a district officer who discovers a tout network operating through subordinate officials has **three separate, non-interchangeable channels**, and naming the wrong one costs marks even where the underlying concern is correctly identified. The department's **CVO** is the internal vigilance route for the officials' own conduct, feeding into the **CVC** (for central-government/PSU staff) or the **State Vigilance Commission** (for State-cadre staff) — this is where a secret preliminary verification (Topic 21's ground) is initiated and where the disciplinary track originates. The **State Anti-Corruption Bureau** is the route for criminal action, including trap or surveillance operations against the touts themselves under the Prevention of Corruption Act's intermediary provision (Topic 19's Section 7A) — a trap is an ACB/police function, not an administrator's. The **PIDPI route to the CVC** is the confidential channel through which a frightened subordinate can report without exposure — but only for **central-government and PSU** employees; it does **not** cover State-government employees, for whom the equivalent State-specific whistleblower or vigilance-commission mechanism must be separately identified by name, not assumed to be the same PIDPI route.

| State Vigilance Commission | CVC | State rules (not the CVC Act, 2003) | Whether constituted at all; its precise advisory/superintending remit |
| State Anti-Corruption Bureau | CBI's Anti-Corruption Division | State police legislation (not the DSPE Act, 1946) | Organisational placement, reporting line, operational capacity |

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1-3

**Question:** A case study describes a district office where a clerk's conduct is under suspicion, a collusive tout network operates alongside him, and the matter may also require cross-border coordination with a neighbouring State whose police have withdrawn general CBI consent. Using Lessons 1-3 together, name every institution that could plausibly be involved and the specific role each would play.

**Model answer:** Lesson 1's map first confirms this fact pattern touches at least four distinct bodies rather than one: the clerk's own conduct is a matter for departmental/CVO vigilance, feeding into the CVC (Lesson 2) if he is a central-government/PSU employee; the tout network is a criminal matter for the relevant Anti-Corruption Bureau, Union or State depending on jurisdiction; and if the investigation needs to cross into the neighbouring State, Lesson 3 confirms the CBI's ordinary route there would require case-specific rather than general consent, though the Supreme Court's Article 32 or the relevant High Court's Article 226 power remains available in an appropriate case regardless of that State's consent position. No single institution disposes of the whole fact pattern; a complete answer names each one against its own specific role.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume a single department or a single institution can resolve a multi-actor corruption fact pattern; map each actor to the specific body whose statutory function actually matches the action required for that actor.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 4-6

**Question:** A classmate argues, "Since the old single-point directive and the Prime Minister's blanket exclusion from Lok Pal scrutiny were both features of an earlier, less accountable era, today's law has simply removed both limitations." Using Lessons 4-6 together, correct this claim precisely.

**Model answer:** The claim is half right and half wrong. It is right that the single-point directive (Lesson 4) no longer operates as a legal safeguard — it was annulled in 1997, re-enacted as Section 6A of the DSPE Act in 2003, and struck down as unconstitutional in 2014 — so that specific rank-based gate has genuinely been removed, replaced by the differently structured, rank-neutral Section 17A. It is wrong, however, to describe the Prime Minister's position the same way: the ARC's 2007 proposal recommended a blanket exclusion of a formal Lok Pal enquiry into the PM (Lesson 6), and the enacted 2013 Act did not simply "remove" that limitation in the direction of full accountability — it instead designed a qualified inclusion, with five subject-matter exclusions, a two-thirds-of-Members threshold, in-camera proceedings and a records restriction on dismissal, which narrows rather than eliminates the ARC's own institutional-continuity concern.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat "an old limitation was replaced" as proof that the new rule removed all constraint in the same direction; the single-point directive was struck down outright, while the PM's Lokpal-jurisdiction qualification was redesigned, not abolished.

### Cumulative check C — Lessons 7-9

**Question:** A Union-government employee and a State-government employee both want to confidentially report suspected corruption within their own departments, and both ask whether "the Lokpal" can help them directly. Using Lessons 7-9 together, explain what is available to each, and where the Lokpal itself does or does not fit in.

**Model answer:** Lesson 7 confirms the Lokpal has no suo motu power and depends on a validly lodged, non-anonymous complaint, so neither employee can rely on the Lokpal to act merely from a confidential tip; the Union-government employee's actual confidential route is the PIDPI Resolution to the CVC (Lesson 2, reaffirmed at Lesson 9), not the Lokpal directly, since PIDPI and the CVC are the designated receiving mechanism for this purpose, while the Lokpal's own jurisdiction under the 2013 Act is better suited to specified categories of complaint (Ministers, MPs, central officials, and so on) than to an anonymous-style confidential tip; the State-government employee has no PIDPI route at all (Lesson 9's own stated limit) and must instead use her own State's equivalent mechanism, most likely a State Vigilance Commission where one is constituted, or her State's own specific whistleblower provision — again not the Union Lokpal directly for an ordinary departmental complaint.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the Lokpal as a universal confidential-reporting channel for any public servant; PIDPI-to-CVC is the actual confidential route for central-government/PSU staff, State-specific mechanisms serve State-cadre staff, and the Lokpal's own jurisdiction is narrower and complaint-form-dependent.

### Cumulative check D — Lessons 10-12

**Question:** A candidate's 20-mark answer on "India's anti-corruption institutional architecture" spends the entire answer on composition and jurisdiction facts, then closes with "hence these institutions are fully adequate and fully coordinated in practice." Using Lessons 10-12 together, explain precisely why this answer underperforms.

**Model answer:** Lesson 10 establishes that a complete institutional answer must also state the specific actor behind each procedural gate (the accused's own employer/appointing authority for Section 19 sanction, not a default "CVC" or "Lokpal" answer), which a composition-and-jurisdiction-only answer omits entirely; Lesson 11's October 2026 checkpoint further shows that, as of this session, the CVC was headed only by an Acting Commissioner pending a permanent successor, the Lokpal's own membership appeared short of its full ceiling, and the Lokpal's own FY2025-26 data showed zero prosecution sanctions against 558 complaints received — directly contradicting any claim of "fully adequate and fully coordinated in practice"; Lesson 12's six-link spine shows the missing final step is a specific, bounded reform recommendation, not an unqualified closing verdict in either direction.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let correct composition and jurisdiction facts substitute for current-functioning evidence and a specific reform recommendation; this topic's own October 2026 checkpoint facts directly forbid an unqualified "fully adequate" conclusion.
# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

### 10-mark model practice

**Question:** Distinguish the Central Vigilance Commission's jurisdiction over the Central Bureau of Investigation from the Lokpal's jurisdiction over the same agency. Why does a single case sometimes engage both relationships simultaneously?

**Model answer:**
The CVC's relationship with the CBI is a **statutory superintendence**, but a narrow one: under the CVC Act, 2003, the CVC superintends the CBI's investigation of offences under the **Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 only** — it has no superintending role over the CBI's non-corruption work, and it does not itself investigate, arrest or prosecute. The Lokpal's relationship with the CBI, by contrast, is a **referral-based direction power** created by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013: the Lokpal can direct or supervise the CBI's investigation specifically in matters **referred** to it, while the CBI continues to operate under its own statute (the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946) for every other matter.

A single corruption case can engage both relationships at once precisely because they operate on different planes: if the case is a Prevention of Corruption Act offence being investigated by the CBI's Anti-Corruption Division, the CVC's limited statutory superintendence applies throughout; if the same case is also referred to the Lokpal because it involves an official within the Lokpal's own jurisdiction (a Minister, an MP, or a specified central official), the Lokpal's own direction power over that specific referred matter applies alongside, not instead of, the CVC's superintendence. The two relationships are therefore cumulative rather than mutually exclusive, and a complete answer should name both rather than treating "the CVC superintends the CBI" and "the Lokpal can direct the CBI" as competing, contradictory claims.

**Why this scores:** It states the precise statutory basis and precise scope limit for each relationship, explicitly rejects the false "either/or" framing, and gives the examiner the specific legal hook (PC Act-only superintendence; referral-based direction) rather than a vague "both oversee the CBI" answer.

### 15-mark model practice

**Question:** Evaluate the case for an "organic link" between the CVC and the Lokpal in India's anti-corruption institutional architecture, comparing the 2nd ARC's 2007 proposal with the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013's actual design.

**Model answer:**
The 2nd ARC's 2007 proposal (Lesson 5) envisaged a direct hierarchical "organic link": the CVC would work under the Lok Pal's "overall guidance and superintendence" while retaining its own functional autonomy — a design meant to solve the ARC's own diagnosed problem of "multiplicity of institutions with overlapping functions" (para 4.2.5) by placing one coordinating body above the others. The enacted 2013 Act chose a materially different mechanism: rather than placing the CVC or CBI under the Lokpal's standing superintendence, it gave the Lokpal a **referral-based direction power** over CBI investigations in cases referred to it, while leaving CVC and CBI to continue operating under their own separate statutes (Lesson 7).

The case *for* this redesigned linkage is that it avoids concentrating advisory, investigative and adjudicatory authority inside a single hierarchy, preserving the functional separation Lesson 1 identified as a deliberate check against power concentration, while still giving the Lokpal a concrete lever — the power to direct CBI action in matters it chooses to pursue — that the ARC's own proposal, limited to CVC only, never extended to CBI at all. The case *against* it is that a referral-based link depends entirely on referrals actually being made, reasons recorded and outcomes tracked; Lesson 11's own FY2025-26 data (eight investigations initiated against 431 registered complaints, and zero prosecution sanctions) shows how thin the pipeline from complaint to concrete institutional outcome can be, which raises a genuine question about whether the "organic link" concept is operating as intended or merely exists on paper.

A balanced evaluation should therefore hold that the 2013 Act's referral-based redesign is a defensible institutional choice distinct from, not a simple weakening of, the ARC's original hierarchical proposal, but that its practical value remains to be demonstrated by published referral and outcome data rather than assumed from the statute's text alone — precisely the kind of qualified verdict a 15-mark evaluative question rewards over either an unqualified endorsement or an unqualified dismissal.

**Why this scores:** It names the precise mechanism difference between the two proposals rather than treating "organic link" as one unchanging idea, supplies current data to test the redesigned mechanism's actual operation, and closes with an explicitly qualified rather than one-sided verdict.

### 20-mark model practice

**Question:** "The 2013 Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act has fully resolved the institutional multiplicity and coherence problem that the 2nd ARC identified in 2007." Critically examine this statement with reference to both Union-level and State-level anti-corruption institutions, current as of October 2026.

**Model answer:**
The statement should be rejected as a one-sided overstatement, though its underlying premise — that the 2013 Act represents a genuine institutional response to the ARC's 2007 diagnosis — is correct and worth crediting before it is qualified.

At the **Union level**, the 2013 Act does introduce a coordination mechanism the ARC's 2007 landscape lacked: the Lokpal's referral-based power to direct CBI investigations, its own independent inquiry and prosecution wings, and its composition rule requiring both judicial expertise and broad social-category representation (Lesson 5), together with the CVC's continuing narrow role in CBI officer selection (Lesson 2) and its unchanged PC Act-specific superintendence. This is a genuine advance on the pre-2013 picture of CVC, departmental vigilance and CBI operating with no overarching, specified-jurisdiction coordinating body at all.

However, "fully resolved" fails on at least three distinct grounds this topic has independently traced. First, the **Prime Minister's jurisdiction** (Lesson 6) remains a qualified compromise, not a clean resolution of the ARC's own institutional-continuity worry — the enacted five-exclusion, two-thirds-threshold, in-camera design narrows but does not eliminate the risk a formal enquiry, however hedged, poses to governance continuity. Second, **current functioning evidence** (Lesson 11) shows real institutional-continuity strain even in 2026: the CVC was headed by an Acting Commissioner pending a permanent successor, the Lokpal's own membership appeared short of its statutory ceiling, and its FY2025-26 data recorded zero prosecution sanctions against 558 complaints received — figures that complicate any claim of a smoothly functioning, fully resolved architecture. Third, and most decisively, the **State level** (Lessons 8-9) remains substantially unresolved: Section 63 of the very same 2013 Act mandates only that every State establish some Lokayukta, prescribing no uniform appointment panel, composition, jurisdiction, tenure or removal design, so the ARC's own 2007 diagnosis of State-level "multiplicity... overlapping functions... lack of coherence" survives largely intact at the sub-national level even after the Union-level reform.

A complete, examiner-rewarded answer should therefore state the statement is **substantially overstated**: the 2013 Act is a genuine, partial response to the ARC's 2007 diagnosis at the Union level, with real but incompletely demonstrated coordination value, while leaving the Prime Minister's-jurisdiction trade-off only narrowed, current institutional-continuity gaps visible even in the latest data, and the State-level coherence problem essentially where the ARC left it in 2007 — not "fully resolved" in any of these three respects.

**Why this scores:** It credits the genuine institutional advance before qualifying it, names three independent, specific grounds for rejecting "fully resolved" rather than one generic objection, anchors the critique in named October 2026 evidence rather than abstract doubt, and closes with a precisely qualified thesis rather than a one-sided verdict in either direction.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Institutional-jurisdiction map and definitions
- Union trio: CVC (advisory/superintending), CBI (investigating, DSPE Act basis), Lokpal (overarching, can direct CBI, own inquiry/prosecution wings); plus departmental/CVO vigilance as the internal first stop.
- State trio: State Vigilance Commission, state Anti-Corruption Bureau, State Lokayukta — each separately constituted, not a branch office of its Union counterpart.
- ARC 4.2.5's diagnosis: "multiplicity of institutions with overlapping functions" undermining "coherence" and "unified focus" is the thread this entire topic traces.
- Never write "refer it to the CVC" as an undifferentiated answer; name the specific body whose specific power the fact pattern requires.

## 2. The CVC
- Lineage: Santhanam Committee (1962-64) -> executive resolution (1964) -> *Vineet Narain* (18 December 1997) -> CVC Act, 2003 (assent 11 September 2003).
- Composition: Central Vigilance Commissioner (chair) + up to 2 Vigilance Commissioners; appointed by the President on a PM + Home Minister + LoP (Lok Sabha) committee; term 4 years or age 65; removal only via Supreme Court inquiry into proved misbehaviour/incapacity.
- Superintendence over CBI is limited strictly to Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 offences.
- Advisory only (not binding); government must record reasons to differ; PIDPI, 2004 is its operative whistleblower-receiving mechanism, excluding anonymous/pseudonymous complaints.

## 3. The CBI
- Set up 1963 by executive resolution; not a statutory body; police powers rest entirely on the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946.
- Director's appointment committee (post-2013): PM (chair) + LoP, Lok Sabha (or single-largest-opposition-party leader, 2014 DSPE Amendment) + CJI/nominated SC judge — distinct from the CVC's own panel.
- Tenure: minimum 2 years; up to 3 one-year extensions under the 2021 DSPE Amendment, maximum total 5 years.
- State-consent gate (general or case-specific) is the ordinary route; its withdrawal changes the route for fresh cases but does not disable the Article 32/226 constitutional-court exception.

## 4. CVC-CBI organic linkage and the single-directive history
- Single-point directive: executive instruction -> annulled (*Vineet Narain*, 1997) -> re-enacted as DSPE s.6A by the CVC Act, 2003 -> struck down (*Dr. Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI*, (2014) 8 SCC 682, 6 May 2014).
- Replacement safeguard, Section 17A PC Act (2018), is conduct-relatable and rank-neutral, not rank-based.
- CVC's one surviving concrete organic link: chairs the committee selecting CBI officers of SP rank and above, excluding the Director.

## 5. ARC's 2007 proposal versus the enacted 2013 Lokpal
- ARC: 3-member body (SC-judge chair, eminent jurist, CVC ex officio); jurisdiction limited to Ministers/MPs; PM explicitly excluded; CVC under Lok Pal's "overall guidance."
- Enacted Act: Chairperson + up to 8 Members (>=50% judicial; >=50% SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women); jurisdiction covers PM (qualified), Ministers, MPs, all central officials, specified funded bodies; CVC/CBI retain own statutes, Lokpal gets referral-based CBI-direction power.
- Expansion driven by the post-2011 Anna Hazare movement and ten failed bills (1968-2011), not a simple enactment of the ARC's design.

## 6. The Prime Minister's jurisdiction qualification
- ARC's argument: "no functionary should be exempt" is technically correct, but a formal PM enquiry carries an institutional-continuity harm distinct from personal immunity; resolution — Parliament's no-confidence motion remains the PM's check.
- Enacted Act's compromise: five named subject-matter exclusions (international relations, external/internal security, public order, atomic energy, space); two-thirds-of-Members threshold to initiate; in-camera proceedings; records neither published nor made available if dismissed.
- Balanced verdict: narrows, does not fully eliminate, the ARC's own institutional-continuity worry.

## 7. The Lokpal's own architecture
- Selection Committee: PM (chair) + Speaker (LS) + LoP (LS) + CJI/nominated SC judge + one eminent jurist; assisted by a Search Committee bound by the same representation rule.
- Powers: referral-based CBI-direction; own inquiry and prosecution wings; can attach/confiscate property; grants its own prosecution sanction for matters within its jurisdiction.
- Timelines: preliminary enquiry 3(+3) months; investigation 6(+6) months; trial 1(+1) year via special courts.
- Drawbacks: no suo motu power; form-over-substance risk; no anonymous complaints; heavy (double-edged) deterrent for false complaints; 7-year limitation; non-transparent PM-complaint procedure.
- Section 63: every State must establish a Lokayukta by 16 January 2015, with no uniform model.

## 8. Multiplicity, coherence and overlap/gaps
- Does a fourth body (Lokpal) solve ARC 4.2.5's diagnosis or add to it? At Union level, a qualified coordination attempt (referral-based, not a parallel command); at State level, Section 63 mandates existence only, leaving ARC's diagnosis substantially valid.
- Operational effectiveness (referrals made, reasons recorded, timelines met) is an evidence question, not a design-on-paper one (Lesson 11's data).

## 9. State-level variations
- Maharashtra (1971): India's first Lokayukta; Odisha enacted 1970, enforced only 1983 — enactment and enforcement are separate facts.
- Section 63 mandates existence by 16 January 2015 without prescribing appointment panel, composition, jurisdiction, tenure, age limit or removal design.
- Three-channel routing rule: CVO/CVC or State Vigilance Commission for disciplinary action; State ACB for criminal/trap action; PIDPI-to-CVC only for central-government/PSU whistleblowers, never State-government employees.

## 10. The investigation-prosecution-sanction interface
- Section 17A's competent authority and Section 19's sanctioning authority both depend on the accused's specific service category and governing rules — never a CVC/CBI/Lokpal default.
- CVC's role in an ordinary Section 19 case is advisory, not the sanctioning signature.
- Lokpal supplies its own, separate prosecution-sanction route for matters within its own jurisdiction.
- Justice Viswanathan's 2026 Lokpal/Lokayukta-screening proposal for Section 17A is an institutional-capacity question this topic owns; its constitutional fate remains Topic 19's ground.

## 11. Institutional reforms, trade-offs and current functioning (October 2026)
- Lokpal: Chairperson Justice A.M. Khanwilkar (since March 2024, after a near-two-year vacancy); six named Members; apparently short of the full Chairperson-plus-8 ceiling.
- CVC: Praveen Kumar Srivastava's tenure completed 2 August 2026; A.S. Rajeev officiating as Acting CVC from 3 August 2026, pending a permanent successor.
- CBI: Director Praveen Sood's tenure extended a second time in May 2026, to May 2027, with the Leader of the Opposition's dissent recorded on the process.
- Lokpal Circular No. 01/2026 (24 July 2026) and FY2025-26 data (558 received; 431 registered; 407 disposed; 81 preliminary inquiries; 8 investigations; 0 prosecution sanctions; 2 disciplinary directions; 99 pending) are the closest available current-functioning evidence; preserve category labels exactly.
- A complete current-functioning assessment names evidence in both directions, never a single celebratory or condemnatory verdict.

## 12. Applied mastery
- Topic-20 institutional-routing spine: name the institution -> state its power/limit -> state its appointment/removal/independence fact -> state its current-functioning caveat -> state the procedural gate it operates or feeds -> give a bounded, specific reform recommendation.
- Applied to the Surinder Nagar tout-network case: three channel assignments (CVO/CVC or State Vigilance Commission; State ACB; PIDPI-to-CVC with its two limits), explicitly excluding Topic 19's legal architecture, Topic 21's screening sequence, Topic 17's continuity track and Topic 22's full case-study method.
- Final thesis: India's anti-corruption institutional architecture in October 2026 is a precisely mapped, only partially coordinated system with named current leadership gaps and an unresolved State-level design question — not a finished, fully coherent, or a wholly failed one.
