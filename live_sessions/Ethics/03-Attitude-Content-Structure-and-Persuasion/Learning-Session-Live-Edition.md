# Attitude: Content, Structure and Persuasion — Live Session Edition

> **Syllabus clause:** “Attitude: content, structure, function; its influence and relation with thought and behaviour; moral and political attitudes; social influence and persuasion.”

Rules can prohibit misconduct, but they cannot by themselves make an official respect a citizen, interpret a difficulty constructively or resist a prejudiced group norm. Those outcomes depend partly on attitude. Yet attitude is not magic: a sincere belief can collapse under authority pressure, and a successful campaign can become manipulation if it bypasses autonomy. This session therefore moves from the inner structure of attitude to social influence, persuasion, ethical limits and institutional safeguards.

### Visual: four questions, four different concepts

```text
VALUE       What is worth pursuing across situations?       “Equality matters.”
BELIEF      What do I hold to be true?                       “This scheme reaches villages.”
ATTITUDE    How do I evaluate this specific object?          “I support this scheme.”
APTITUDE    What am I capable of learning or performing?     “I can analyse delivery data.”
```

The visual prevents the first major error: attitude is not a synonym for every inner quality. It is a relatively enduring **evaluative disposition toward a specific object, person, group, policy, event or idea**. Evaluation gives it direction—favourable, unfavourable or mixed—and object-specificity gives it a target.

### Start with the administrative problem

Two officers may know the same rule and possess similar analytical ability. One sees a poor applicant as a citizen entitled to assistance; the other sees the same applicant as a nuisance. The difference is not primarily knowledge or aptitude. It is an evaluative stance toward the citizen and the work.

An attitude does not need to be fully conscious. A person may sincerely endorse equality in the abstract while displaying discomfort toward a particular caste, gender or disability group. This is why public administration cannot infer fair treatment from a declared value alone.

### The decisive distinctions

| Concept | Scope | Main content | Example | Reform lever |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Value | broad and cross-situational | standard of desirability | honesty | constitutional/professional education |
| Belief | a claim held true | cognitive | “cash transfers reduce leakage” | evidence and correction |
| Attitude | object-specific evaluation | cognition, feeling and action tendency | approval of a welfare reform | experience, reflection and persuasion |
| Aptitude | capacity or potential | ability/disposition to perform | numerical analysis | training and practice |

A value can shape many attitudes. Equality may support favourable attitudes toward reservations, accessible offices and non-discriminatory policing. A belief can support an attitude but is not itself evaluative: “the office opens at ten” becomes attitudinal only when joined to approval, resentment or a behavioural tendency.

### India-centric example and its limit

Suppose an officer supports digital delivery because she believes it reduces discretion and feels optimistic about technology. That is a favourable attitude. If biometric authentication repeatedly excludes elderly beneficiaries, evidence should revise the belief and perhaps the attitude.

**Limit:** A negative view of one flawed authentication design is not necessarily an anti-technology value. Always identify the exact attitude object.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** If attitudes are internal and difficult to observe, the concept is too vague for administration.

**Reply:** Attitudes are inferred cautiously from repeated choices, language, attention, avoidance and treatment patterns, not from one act. The concept helps diagnose why the same rule produces different conduct.

**Residual:** Behaviour remains the proper object of accountability. An institution should not punish an unexpressed private attitude; it should regulate discriminatory conduct and design fair processes.

### UPSC application

The definition is the opening move for every direct attitude PYQ. In 2014 Q6, object-specificity helps separate a general value such as equality from contrasting attitudes toward caste. In 2021 Q4(a), the distinction from aptitude prevents a “suitable attitude” answer from becoming a list of skills.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** Distinguish attitude from value, belief and aptitude. Why does the distinction matter in public administration?

**Suggested model:** An attitude is a relatively stable evaluative stance toward a specific object; a value is a broad cross-situational standard, a belief is a proposition held true, and aptitude is the capacity to perform or learn a task. The distinctions identify different administrative failures. If an officer wrongly believes that a community misuses welfare, verified evidence may correct the belief. If the officer knows the facts but dislikes the community, contact, reflection and accountability must address attitude. If she values equality but lacks data skill, training must build aptitude. A positive attitude still does not prove fair conduct, so transparent criteria and review remain necessary. Effective reform therefore diagnoses whether the problem is factual, evaluative, normative or capacity-related before selecting the intervention.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** four accurate meanings (4); administrative diagnostic (2); example (2); institutional qualification (1); direct conclusion (1).

### Revision notes

- Attitude is an evaluative disposition toward a specific object.
- Valence may be favourable, unfavourable or ambivalent.
- Value is broader; belief is cognitive; aptitude concerns capacity.
- A declared value can coexist with a prejudiced object-specific attitude.
- Reform must match the diagnosed problem.
- Accountability attaches to conduct, not mere unexpressed opinion.

### Concept check

**Question:** An officer values equality but repeatedly avoids interacting with sanitation workers. Which concept best identifies the immediate gap?

**Model answer:** The immediate gap is an object-specific negative attitude toward the group, even though the officer endorses the broader value of equality.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not call every inner disposition a value; the target-specific evaluation is the attitude.

The next lesson opens that evaluation and examines its three interacting components.

### Visual: the same policy seen through three components

```text
ATTITUDE OBJECT: “doorstep pension verification”

COGNITIVE                 AFFECTIVE                  BEHAVIOURAL / CONATIVE
beliefs and information   feeling and emotional     intention or readiness
“It reduces exclusion.”   “I feel reassured.”       “I will support and implement it.”
           \                    |                    /
            \___________________|___________________/
                         OVERALL EVALUATION
```

The **ABC model** treats attitude content as cognitive, affective and behavioural or conative. “Behavioural” here primarily means a predisposition or intention, not proof that the act has occurred.

### Component interaction

1. **Cognitive component:** what the person believes or knows about the object.
2. **Affective component:** the feeling attached to it—fear, pride, dislike, trust or empathy.
3. **Behavioural component:** the tendency or intention to approach, avoid, support, resist or act.

The components often reinforce one another. Believing that vaccination is safe can reduce fear and increase willingness. But they can also conflict. An officer may know that manual scavenging is unlawful and degrading, feel socially uncomfortable challenging local elites, yet still intend to enforce the law. Such ambivalence matters because a weakly integrated attitude is less stable under pressure.

### Valence, strength and consistency

| Dimension | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Valence | positive, negative or mixed? | predicts direction of evaluation |
| Strength | how certain, important and rehearsed? | stronger attitudes resist change and often predict conduct better |
| Accessibility | how quickly does it come to mind? | accessible attitudes guide rapid judgment |
| Consistency | do ABC components align? | inconsistency creates instability or dissonance |
| Specificity | does the attitude match the behaviour measured? | specific attitudes predict specific acts better than general ones |

### An example that carries the reasoning

A district team may hold a cognitively positive attitude toward women’s employment because it improves household welfare, an affectively negative reaction because it disrupts familiar gender roles, and a publicly supportive behavioural intention because policy requires it.

This explains why information campaigns alone may fail. The missing component is affective and social, not factual.

**Where the example stops:** The three components are an analytical model, not three sealed boxes. Feelings contain appraisals, intentions include beliefs, and actual conduct is filtered by opportunity.

### Comparison: content is not function

ABC tells us **what an attitude contains**. It does not tell us **why the person needs or holds it**. Two people may express the same anti-corruption content: one because it organises knowledge, another because it expresses moral identity. Lesson 3 therefore adds function.

### Objection and qualified reply

**Objection:** The ABC model overstates internal consistency; people are contradictory.

**Reply:** The model does not require perfect consistency. Its value lies precisely in locating mismatch and deciding which component needs intervention.

**Residual:** Surveys that measure only stated belief can miss emotion and behaviour. Triangulate language with observed treatment, situational tests and institutional data.

### UPSC application

Use ABC as an answer-building diagnostic: define the attitude object, identify the weak or conflicting component, select an intervention, then add the attitude–behaviour limitation. This architecture supports 2014 Q6, 2016 Q6, 2020 Q4(b), 2021 Q4(a) and 2022 Q2(a).

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** Explain how inconsistency among the components of attitude can affect public-service delivery.

**Suggested model:** The ABC model separates beliefs, feelings and action tendencies. An officer may cognitively accept that disability accommodation is a right, affectively regard requests as troublesome, and behaviourally delay approval. The inconsistency makes service dependent on supervision rather than conviction. Reform should therefore not repeat legal information alone. Direct interaction can humanise the affected citizen, simulations can expose emotional bias, and reasoned service standards can convert the intended conduct into a reviewable duty. Yet component alignment is not sufficient: workload, superior pressure and incentives may still block action. Hence administrative reform should join cognitive correction, affective learning and behavioural rehearsal with accessible grievance and audit mechanisms.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** ABC explanation (3); inconsistency mechanism (2); public-service example (2); matched reform (2); limit (1).

### Revision notes

- Cognitive = beliefs; affective = feelings; behavioural/conative = action tendency.
- Behavioural component is not identical to completed behaviour.
- Valence, strength, accessibility, consistency and specificity affect prediction.
- Mixed components produce ambivalence.
- Information alone cannot repair an affective or identity-protective attitude.
- ABC describes content; Katz explains function.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why might accurate information about a marginalised group fail to change discriminatory service behaviour?

**Model answer:** Because the cognitive component may improve while affective dislike, identity pressure or the behavioural habit remains unchanged.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume cognitive correction automatically aligns feeling and conduct.

### Visual: one statement, four possible psychological jobs

```text
“This department’s old method is best.”
        |
        +-- KNOWLEDGE: simplifies a confusing environment
        +-- ADJUSTMENT / UTILITARIAN / INSTRUMENTAL: wins reward or avoids cost
        +-- EGO-DEFENSIVE: protects self-esteem from admitting error
        +-- VALUE-EXPRESSIVE: signals loyalty, professionalism or identity
```

Daniel Katz’s functional approach asks not merely **what** an attitude says but **what work it performs for the holder**.

### The four functions

| Function | Plain-language intuition | Administrative illustration | Function-matched response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | “Help me organise a complex world.” | a stereotype supplies a quick but crude category | better categories, evidence and repeated counterexamples |
| Adjustment / utilitarian / instrumental | “Help me gain reward or avoid cost.” | public agreement with a superior’s preference | change incentives, protection and accountability |
| Ego-defensive | “Protect me from an uncomfortable truth.” | blaming citizens to avoid admitting poor design | psychologically safe feedback plus responsibility |
| Value-expressive | “Let me express who I am and what I stand for.” | pride in impartial public service—or partisan identity | connect reform to a defensible identity and constitutional values |

The adjustment, utilitarian and instrumental labels refer to the same broad reward–cost function. Function is distinct from ABC content. “I oppose biometric exceptions” may contain beliefs, anxiety and resistance; its function may be knowledge simplification, status protection or value expression.

### Why matching matters

Suppose an officer resists citizen charters:

- If the function is **knowledge**, show how standards clarify rather than oversimplify.
- If it is **instrumental**, alter performance incentives and complaint review.
- If it is **ego-defensive**, avoid humiliation while making data undeniable.
- If it is **value-expressive**, present responsiveness as professional excellence rather than surrender.

An intervention can fail while being factually excellent because it attacks the wrong function.

### India-centric example and limit

A village elder may oppose girls’ schooling partly because of inaccurate beliefs about safety, partly because patriarchal status is threatened, and partly because the attitude expresses inherited identity. A factual pamphlet addresses only the first.

**Limit:** Function is inferred, not read directly from speech. Multiple functions may coexist; diagnosis should remain tentative and testable.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Functional explanation excuses prejudice by treating it as psychologically useful.

**Reply:** Explanation is not justification. Knowing the function improves correction while law and rights define the non-negotiable boundary.

**Residual:** Some conduct requires immediate prohibition even while longer-term attitude change proceeds.

### UPSC application

Function-matched diagnosis deepens 2014 Q6 on caste, 2015 Q14(b) on patriarchal elders and 2016 Q6 on undesirable attitudes. It also prevents a generic answer that recommends “awareness” for every attitude problem.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** “Attitude change fails when administration targets content but ignores function.” Discuss with an Indian example.

**Suggested model:** Attitude content answers what a person believes, feels and intends; function answers why the stance is psychologically useful. Katz identifies knowledge, adjustment or instrumental, ego-defensive and value-expressive functions. This distinction changes intervention design.

Consider resistance to girls’ education. False claims about employability require evidence; fear of social sanction requires visible support from peers and local leaders; threatened patriarchal status requires alternative forms of respected community leadership; identity-based opposition requires connecting education to dignity and constitutional citizenship. Safety measures and lawful protection are also necessary because persuasion cannot ask girls to bear preventable risk.

The functional approach is explanatory, not exculpatory. A discriminatory practice remains unlawful or unethical even if it protects status or identity. Moreover, functions are inferred and may overlap, so administrators should test hypotheses through dialogue and behaviour rather than label communities. Effective reform therefore combines a rights floor with function-matched communication, incentives, role models and institutional safeguards.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** content/function distinction (3); four functions (4); worked Indian application (4); rights and inference limits (2); reasoned conclusion (2).

### Revision notes

- Katz explains why attitudes are held; ABC explains what they contain.
- Knowledge function organises complexity.
- Adjustment/utilitarian/instrumental function serves reward and cost.
- Ego-defensive function protects self-image.
- Value-expressive function communicates identity and principle.
- One attitude may perform several functions.
- Explanation never removes ethical or legal responsibility.

### Concept check

**Question:** An officer publicly endorses a minister’s proposal mainly to avoid transfer. Which attitude function is dominant?

**Model answer:** The adjustment or instrumental function is dominant because the stance helps avoid a personal cost.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not classify the function from the statement’s content alone; ask what benefit the stance gives the holder.

### Visual: formation and repair loop

```text
DIRECT EXPERIENCE ─┐
FAMILY / PEERS ────┼→ beliefs + feelings + habits → attitude → selective attention
MEDIA / MODELS ────┤                                  ↑            |
REWARD / PUNISHMENT┘                                  └── feedback ┘

attitude conflicts with belief or conduct
                    ↓
            COGNITIVE DISSONANCE
        ┌───────────┼────────────┐

                                  “everyone does it”
```

Attitudes form through direct encounters, observation of others, communication and reinforcement. Once formed, they influence which information is noticed and believed, creating a feedback loop rather than a one-way chain from thought to attitude.

### Four formation pathways

1. **Direct experience:** repeated respectful or humiliating encounters attach evaluation to an object.
2. **Social learning:** family, peers, leaders and media model approval or disapproval.
3. **Reinforcement:** rewarded conduct becomes attractive; punished dissent becomes aversive.
4. **Belief–attitude feedback:** attitudes guide interpretation, which then appears to confirm the attitude.

This is why prejudice can survive contrary evidence: disconfirming cases are treated as exceptions, while confirming cases are remembered.

### Cognitive dissonance

Festinger described discomfort arising from inconsistent cognitions or from acting against one’s attitude. An officer who values transparency but conceals a routine record faces dissonance.

Three broad resolution routes follow:

- **Change attitude:** “Secrecy is actually responsible administration.”
- **Change behaviour:** disclose or record a principled objection.
- **Add a justifying cognition:** “No one is harmed,” “this is temporary,” or “everyone follows orders.”

The third route is ethically dangerous because rationalisation can convert an isolated compromise into normal conduct.

### Example, limit and remediation

A clerk accepts a small facilitation payment while believing himself honest. The discomfort may lead to refusal next time—or to the thought that low pay makes the payment deserved.

**Limit:** Dissonance theory does not predict which route will be chosen. Power, peer norms, incentives and available reporting channels shape the direction.

Useful countermeasures include contemporaneous written reasons, peer discussion, rotation, audit trails and safe challenge. These make self-serving justifications harder to sustain.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** People often act inconsistently without visible discomfort.

**Reply:** Dissonance varies with importance, commitment, perceived choice and self-concept. The theory is a conditional mechanism, not a universal claim that every inconsistency causes change.

**Residual:** Institutional reform should never rely on discomfort to self-correct wrongdoing.

### UPSC application

The formation chain is central to 2014 Q6 and 2016 Q6. Dissonance adds depth to 2020 Q4(b): stress can intensify either principled consistency or rationalisation. In answers, distinguish formation from later maintenance and change.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** How can cognitive dissonance become an ethical risk in public administration?

**Suggested model:** Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort produced when conduct conflicts with belief or self-image. An officer who values transparency but withholds a routine record may restore consistency by correcting the conduct, changing the attitude or adding a justification. The last route creates ethical risk: “everyone does it” or “the public cannot understand” converts a compromise into a self-protective norm. Repetition then weakens the original attitude and normalises concealment. Contemporaneous reasons, written instructions, peer review, audit trails and safe escalation interrupt this process by exposing justifications to external scrutiny. Yet dissonance does not guarantee reform; pressure and incentives influence the chosen route. Ethical administration must therefore support principled resolution institutionally rather than expect conscience alone to win.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** definition (2); three routes (3); administrative risk (2); safeguards (2); qualification (1).

### Revision notes

- Formation sources: experience, modelling, communication and reinforcement.
- Attitudes influence later attention and interpretation.
- Dissonance is discomfort from inconsistency.
- Resolution may change attitude, change behaviour or add justification.
- Rationalisation is the main administrative danger.
- Importance, choice and self-concept affect dissonance strength.
- Written reasons and review interrupt self-serving stories.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “everyone in the office does it” a dissonance response rather than evidence that the conduct is ethical?

**Model answer:** It adds a justifying cognition that reduces personal discomfort without changing the wrong conduct or establishing a defensible moral reason.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat psychological comfort after rationalisation as ethical resolution.

### Visual: attitude reaches behaviour through gates

```text
ATTITUDE
   ↓
INTENTION
   ↓
Is it strong? accessible? specific to the act?
   ↓
SITUATION: time, authority, peers, incentives, fear, opportunity
   ↓
INSTITUTION: rule, monitoring, protection, resources, review
   ↓
```

Attitude influences behaviour; it does not mechanically determine it. Prediction improves when the attitude is strong, accessible and measured at the same level of specificity as the behaviour.

### The LaPiere warning

In Richard LaPiere’s 1934 study, a Chinese couple was generally served during travel, while many establishments later reported that they would not serve Chinese guests. The classic lesson is a gap between stated general attitude and specific behaviour.

The study does **not** prove that attitudes never matter. It shows that correspondence, context and measurement matter. The mailed response and the face-to-face encounter were different situations, possibly involving different respondents.

### Five moderators

| Moderator | Better prediction when… | Administrative illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Intention | a concrete plan exists | officer schedules a camp, not merely “supports outreach” |
| Strength | certainty and personal relevance are high | deeply held anti-corruption stance |
| Accessibility | evaluation comes readily to mind | rehearsed service norm |
| Specificity | attitude and act match | attitude toward disclosing this class of record |
| Situation | pressure and cost are manageable | protection exists against retaliation |

### India-centric example and limit

An officer may express a positive attitude toward RTI during training but delay disclosure when a superior signals displeasure. Calling this hypocrisy may be premature; the attitude–behaviour gap identifies situational pressure. Still, pressure cannot become an excuse. The officer remains responsible for using lawful channels.

**Limit:** Behaviour can also reveal that the stated attitude was shallow or socially desirable. The gap has multiple explanations.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** If behaviour is what matters, attitude training wastes resources.

**Reply:** Attitude affects interpretation, initiative and conduct where rules are incomplete. But training must be joined to incentives and safeguards.

**Residual:** Measure field behaviour and citizen experience, not course completion or declarations alone.

### UPSC application

**2020 GS-IV Q4(b), 10 marks, 150 words:** “A positive attitude is considered to be an essential characteristic of a civil servant who is often required to function under extreme stress. What contributes to a positive attitude in a person?”

**Demand:** Explain contributors and connect them to stress performance.

**Approach:** realistic beliefs, emotional regulation, purpose, prior mastery, social support and institutional protection; then qualify that a positive attitude is necessary but not sufficient.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** A favourable attitude toward ethical conduct does not guarantee ethical behaviour. Analyse.

**Suggested model:** A favourable attitude supplies direction, but conduct passes through intention, attitude strength, accessibility, specificity and situation. A general claim—“I support transparency”—predicts a specific disclosure poorly unless the official has formed an intention, the attitude is salient and the situation permits action. LaPiere’s 1934 study classically showed that stated general attitudes can diverge from face-to-face behaviour, though its different respondents and settings limit broad inference.

In administration, superior pressure, peer norms, transfer fear, workload and weak reporting protection can override a genuine stance. Conversely, observed compliance may reflect surveillance rather than internal conviction. Reform must therefore combine reflective and experiential learning with written directions, audit, safe dissent, incentives and citizen feedback.

This does not absolve the officer. Situational explanation is not moral exoneration; lawful channels must still be used. The sound conclusion is that attitudes matter most when strong and specific, while institutions determine whether they can survive pressure.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** qualified thesis (2); five moderators (5); LaPiere with limit (3); administrative application (3); responsibility/institution synthesis (2).

### Revision notes

- Attitude affects intention, not conduct by automatic command.
- Strength, accessibility and specificity improve prediction.
- Situation and institution can override or support intention.
- LaPiere shows a stated-attitude/action gap, not irrelevance of attitude.
- Social desirability can inflate declared attitudes.
- Field outcomes are stronger evidence than training completion.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a strong general attitude toward honesty still predict a specific procurement decision imperfectly?

**Model answer:** Because the attitude may not be specific to the act, and authority pressure, incentives, opportunity and institutional safeguards intervene between intention and conduct.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use the attitude–behaviour gap to erase personal responsibility.


Deutsch and Gerard distinguished **informational influence**, accepting others as evidence about reality, from **normative influence**, conforming to gain approval or avoid rejection.

### Asch and group judgment

In Asch’s line-judgment experiments, participants sometimes gave an obviously wrong answer after unanimous confederates did so. The enduring lesson is not that people are mindless. It is that unanimity and social isolation can distort public judgment even when the task appears clear.

An ally or a break in unanimity can reduce pressure. This suggests an institutional design principle: dissent becomes easier when it is procedurally normal and socially supported.

### Public agreement and private acceptance

| Outcome | Public response | Private belief | Likely durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public compliance | agrees | privately disagrees | low if surveillance ends |
| Private acceptance | agrees | changes belief | higher, though revisable |
| Independence | disagrees | retains judgment | depends on evidence and protection |

Informational influence can produce genuine learning. Normative influence can also transmit good norms—such as punctuality or non-discrimination—but may produce mere appearance.

### Indian administrative example and limit

In a district review meeting, junior officers may support an unrealistic target because every senior appears confident. A structured “independent estimate first” procedure reduces conformity before discussion.

**Limit:** Asch’s artificial visual task, historical setting and restricted samples do not measure Indian administration directly. Use the experiment as a mechanism illustration, not a numerical forecast.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Conformity is always cowardice.

**Reply:** Informational reliance is often rational under uncertainty, and legitimate professional norms coordinate action. The ethical problem is uncritical conformity when evidence or rights demand dissent.

**Residual:** Independence can also become contrarianism. Dissent must give reasons and remain reviewable.

### UPSC application

Conformity explains how undesirable attitudes named in 2016 Q6 persist through family and social surroundings. It also supports 2017 Q8(a) as a bounded cross-link: discipline can secure coordination yet become counter-productive if public agreement suppresses private warning.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** Distinguish informational and normative social influence. How can public institutions reduce harmful conformity?

**Suggested model:** Informational influence occurs when others are treated as evidence about an uncertain reality; normative influence occurs when a person agrees to gain approval or avoid rejection. The first can produce genuine private acceptance, while the second often produces public compliance without changed belief. In administration, a junior may accept a technical estimate because experts know more, or endorse an impossible target because seniors appear unanimous. Harmful conformity can be reduced through independent written assessment before meetings, protected dissent notes, diverse teams, rotating chairpersons and explicit review of minority reasons. Yet conformity is not always wrong: shared professional norms enable coordination. The aim is therefore not permanent contrarianism but reasoned independence where evidence, legality or citizen rights are at stake.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** two definitions (3); acceptance/compliance distinction (2); institutional example (2); safeguards (2); qualification (1).

### Revision notes

- Informational influence uses others as evidence.
- Normative influence seeks belonging or avoids rejection.
- Public agreement need not mean private acceptance.
- Unanimity intensifies pressure; supported dissent can weaken it.
- Good norms also spread socially.
- Asch illustrates a mechanism, not a universal rate or Indian forecast.

### Concept check

**Question:** A junior officer privately rejects a target but endorses it in the meeting to avoid isolation. What has occurred?

**Model answer:** Normative influence has produced public compliance without private acceptance.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not call all reliance on group judgment irrational; under genuine uncertainty informational influence can be reasonable.

Kelman distinguished three processes by the motive sustaining change.

### Process-by-process

| Process | Motivational base | Source dependence | Durability | Administrative example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Compliance | reward or avoidance of punishment | high | often low | filing disclosure because inspection is imminent |

### Values and institutional design

Internalisation connects attitude with value, but they remain distinct. The value is broad—impartiality; the attitude is toward a specific practice—recusal from this tender. Institutions should make the ethical act understandable, practicable and socially credible:

```text
clear rule → fair enforcement → respected examples → reflection → repeated practice → internal commitment
```

### India-centric example and limit

Mission Karmayogi and civil-service training may provide courses, scenarios and behavioural competency language. These can support compliance, identification and reflection.

**Limit:** course enrolment or completion proves exposure, not internalisation. Evidence of internal commitment requires conduct under pressure, quality of reasons, citizen treatment and response to review.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Internalisation is impossible to verify and therefore administratively irrelevant.

**Reply:** It should not be a disciplinary finding, but it remains a training objective because unsupervised discretion requires more than fear of sanction.

**Residual:** Institutions must still design for the possibility that internalisation fails.

### UPSC application

Kelman sharpens 2021 Q4(a): “build a suitable attitude” should not mean lecture-only compliance. It also explains why value-based and compliance-based work cultures are complementary rather than substitutes.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** Compare compliance, identification and internalisation as methods of cultivating ethical civil-service conduct.

**Suggested model:** Compliance secures behaviour through reward, punishment or monitoring. It is indispensable for minimum standards but may disappear when scrutiny ends. Identification occurs when an official adopts conduct associated with a respected leader or professional group. It can build aspiration quickly, but remains vulnerable to leadership change and hero worship. Internalisation occurs when the conduct is accepted because it coheres with values such as impartiality and public service; it is the most durable and useful under discretion.

An ethics system should combine them. Conduct rules and fair discipline establish the floor; credible leaders model the norm; dilemma-based learning, citizen exposure, written-reason exercises and feedback allow reflection and internal commitment. Mission Karmayogi can illustrate structured competency development, but platform participation cannot establish field-level internalisation.

Internalisation should not become a licence to inspect private belief or excuse weak controls. Public institutions must judge conduct through evidence and due process. The aim is a layered system in which rules secure minimum behaviour and values sustain action where rules cannot anticipate every case.

- Identification rests on a valued relationship or identity.
- Internalisation rests on value congruence.
- Internalisation is generally more durable and less source-dependent.
- Identification can transmit harmful as well as good norms.
- Training exposure is not proof of internal commitment.
- Rules remain necessary even in a value-based institution.

**Question:** An officer follows impartial criteria after the admired mentor retires because the criteria are independently judged right. Which process now dominates?

### Visual: three influence situations that must not be merged

Here, **compliance** means responding to a request without formal command authority. That source-of-pressure usage differs from Kelman’s Lesson 7 use, where compliance describes shallow attitude change sustained by reward, punishment or monitoring.

```text
CONFORMITY                 COMPLIANCE                  OBEDIENCE
peer/group judgment        request without formal     command from recognised
                           command authority           authority
“Everyone agrees.”         “Please do this.”           “You are directed to do this.”
```

Obedience is behaviour in response to a command from perceived legitimate authority. It differs from peer conformity and from compliance with a request.

### Why authority is powerful

Authority can supply expertise, coordination and lawful hierarchy. It can also diffuse responsibility: “I only followed orders.” Milgram’s experiments showed that ordinary participants could continue administering what they believed were painful shocks under an experimenter’s direction.

The ethical lesson is not that everyone will obey every authority. It is that legitimacy cues, stepwise escalation, proximity and responsibility framing can weaken independent judgment.

### Limits of the experimental inference

Milgram’s setting involved deception, an artificial laboratory, a particular historical context and restricted samples. Debate continues over participant belief and interpretation. It does not yield a numerical prediction for Indian bureaucracy. Its durable use is conceptual: systems should not assume personal character alone will defeat authoritative pressure.

### Responsible obedience and lawful dissent

```text
clarify facts and authority
        ↓
test legality, rights, public purpose and foreseeable harm
        ↓
state concern respectfully
        ↓
seek written direction where applicable
        ↓
record dissent / escalate / seek review
        ↓
refuse an unlawful act; preserve lawful service continuity
```

Civil service requires disciplined implementation of lawful policy. It does not require moral blankness or obedience to illegality. Written directions, dispersed approval, protected reporting, independent review and rotation reduce both arbitrary disobedience and blind compliance.

### India-centric example and limit

A superior orally asks an officer to exclude eligible households from a list to meet a target. The officer should verify the rule, record the concern, seek written confirmation and escalate.

**Limit:** Not every unwelcome instruction is unlawful, and not every oral operational direction is invalid. Emergencies require coordination; material directions should be regularised and reviewable.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Encouraging dissent will paralyse administration.

**Reply:** Channelled dissent improves reliability. It asks for reasons before compliance, not endless veto. Clear escalation timelines preserve decision.

**Residual:** Institutions can punish dissent informally; protection and leadership culture matter.

### UPSC application

**Bounded cross-link — 2017 GS-IV Q8(a), 10 marks, 150 words — provisional transcription:** “Discipline generally implies following the order and subordination. However, it may be counter-productive for the organisation. Discuss.”

The wording is cross-checked against reproduced papers, but no locally readable official pre-2018 paper or controlling ledger was available; retain the provisional-transcription label.

This is not a direct Topic 03 attitude question. It is useful here for authority, conformity and constructive dissent.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** Distinguish ethical obedience from blind obedience in public administration.

**Suggested model:** Ethical obedience implements a lawful command after clarifying authority, purpose, facts and foreseeable rights impact. Blind obedience suspends personal and professional judgment merely because a superior directed the act. Hierarchy is necessary for coordination, but it can diffuse responsibility and produce incremental wrongdoing. A civil servant should respectfully state concern, seek a written direction where applicable, record dissent, use authorised escalation and refuse an unlawful act while preserving lawful service continuity. Milgram’s research illustrates vulnerability to authority but cannot be mechanically generalised to Indian administration. The institutional answer is written reasons, dispersed approval, review and protection against retaliation. Discipline is therefore fidelity to lawful public purpose, not personal submission to a superior.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** distinction (3); authority-risk mechanism (2); lawful-dissent sequence (3); research limit (1); conclusion (1).

### Revision notes

- Conformity follows peers; compliance follows a request; obedience follows authority.
- Legitimate authority is necessary but can diffuse responsibility.
- Milgram illustrates vulnerability, not universal or India-specific rates.
- Ethical obedience tests law, purpose, rights and harm.
- Written directions and review preserve both discipline and accountability.
- Refusal is required for unlawful acts, not every disagreement.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does obtaining a written order not automatically make compliance ethical?

**Model answer:** A written order preserves accountability and evidence, but it cannot legalise an unlawful act or remove the subordinate’s duty to use review and refusal where required.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat hierarchy as a transfer of moral responsibility.

### Source, message and audience

### Cognitive and affective routes

A cognitive route engages reasons, evidence and comparison. An affective route uses emotion, imagery and identification. Ethical public communication may use both. A disaster warning must be understandable and emotionally salient, but fear must not be exaggerated beyond evidence.

## Lesson 10 — Ethical Boundaries of Influence

Progress: 10 / 13 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Persuasion, manipulation, propaganda, coercion and undue influence; autonomy, transparency, truthfulness and vulnerability

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Not available — Topic 03, coercion/undue-influence and 2026 consent boundaries were queried

CA found: No separate current-affairs item is needed
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: an influence ladder

```text
PERSUASION        MANIPULATION        PROPAGANDA          UNDUE INFLUENCE       COERCION
reasons offered   judgment bypassed   repeated selective   dependency/authority  threat removes
choice retained   or intent hidden    mass narrative       bends the will        meaningful choice
```

The ethical issue is not whether behaviour changed. It is **how** the change was produced and whether the person remained a reason-responsive agent.

### Five modes compared

| Mode | Truth/intent | Choice quality | Typical mechanism | Ethical status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Persuasion | relevant reasons; purpose disclosed | meaningful | argument, evidence, proportionate emotion | normally legitimate |
| Manipulation | selective, concealed or bias-exploiting | formally present but degraded | dark patterns, fabricated urgency | presumptively wrong |
| Propaganda | selective narrative, repetition and identity mobilisation at scale | critical evaluation weakened | one-sided mass communication | ethically suspect; assess content, control and contestability |
| Undue influence | may contain true claims | dependency bends voluntariness | family, professional or hierarchical pressure | consent suspect |
| Coercion | may be truthful | meaningful choice removed | credible threat, penalty or physical duress | legitimate only under lawful, necessary and proportionate authority |

### Four tests for ethical public influence

1. **Truthfulness:** Are claims accurate, complete enough and correctable?
2. **Transparency:** Is sponsorship and purpose visible?
3. **Autonomy:** Can the person question, refuse or choose an alternative?
4. **Vulnerability safeguards:** Are literacy, dependence, fear and unequal power addressed?

Accessibility and distribution also matter. A technically truthful message can be unethical if vulnerable citizens cannot understand or contest it.

### 2026 bounded cross-link

**2026 GS-IV Q4(a), 10 marks:** a doctor convinces a female patient’s family for surgery, after which the patient reluctantly consents despite originally preferring medication; the question asks candidates to explain paternalism and beneficence by analysing the action.

The main substantive treatment belongs under moral theories (Topic 08). This lesson contributes only the persuasion–manipulation–coercion–undue-influence ladder. Persuading the competent patient with reasons may be beneficent; persuading around her through a dependency relationship can compromise voluntariness.

### India-centric example and limit

A district campaign may use fear-provoking images to promote road safety. Salient harm information can be legitimate. Fabricated crash probability, hidden sponsorship or humiliation of victims would be manipulative.

**Limit:** Emotional communication is not automatically unethical; emotion often makes real risk understandable.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The state always possesses power, so no state persuasion can be voluntary.

**Reply:** Power asymmetry raises the standard; it does not erase the category. Disclosure, alternatives, consultation, appeal and proportionality can preserve meaningful agency.

**Residual:** Where access to an essential benefit depends on agreement, “choice” may remain nominal. Independent review is required.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** Distinguish persuasion from manipulation, propaganda, undue influence and coercion in public administration.

**Suggested model:** Persuasion offers truthful reasons and proportionate emotion while preserving meaningful choice. Manipulation conceals intent, withholds material information or exploits bias to bypass judgment. Propaganda uses selective narrative, repetition and identity mobilisation at scale, often weakening contestability. Undue influence operates through trust, dependency or authority so that apparent consent is not fully autonomous; coercion uses a credible threat that removes reasonable choice. Public influence should be tested for truthfulness, transparent sponsorship, refusal or appeal, and safeguards for vulnerable audiences. State power makes these tests stricter, not impossible. Emotional communication may still be legitimate when it accurately conveys risk. A desirable policy goal never cures a deceptive or autonomy-destroying method.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** five distinctions (5); four ethical tests (2); public-power qualification (1); example/limit (1); conclusion (1).

### Revision notes

- Ethical evaluation concerns method, not goal alone.
- Persuasion preserves reason-responsive choice.
- Manipulation bypasses judgment or hides intent.
- Propaganda is scaled, selective narrative with weakened contestability.
- Undue influence exploits relationship or dependency.
- Coercion uses threat and removes meaningful choice.
- Vulnerability raises the duty of disclosure and support.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can factually correct advice still amount to undue influence?

**Model answer:** Because voluntariness can be compromised by dependency or authority even when the content is true; consent quality depends on the relationship and freedom to refuse.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not make truthfulness the only ethical test of influence.


A **moral attitude** evaluates right and wrong, justice and injustice. A **political attitude** evaluates a party, ideology, leader, programme or contest for power. A civil servant needs moral judgment but must prevent personal partisan preference from steering official action.

### The official rule position

The Central Civil Services (Conduct) Rules, 1964 provide the correct provenance:

- **Rule 5 — “Taking part in politics and elections”:** restricts political-party association and assistance to political movements or activity; sub-rule (4) bars canvassing, interference, use of influence or taking part in elections, while preserving the right to vote without indicating how one voted and permitting election duty imposed by law.
- **Rule 7 — “Demonstration and strikes”:** restricts specified prejudicial demonstrations and any strike, coercion or physical duress connected with service matters.

Rule 7 does **not** cover election canvassing. That belongs to Rule 5. This correction matters because legal precision is part of ethical credibility.

### Non-partisanship without political blankness

| Required | Not required |
|---|---|
| equal criteria and reasons | absence of every private opinion |
| candid, evidence-based advice | passive agreement with ministers |
| lawful implementation of elected policy | partisan promotion of the government |
| objection to illegality or impropriety through channels | sabotage of a lawful policy one dislikes |
| voting privately as permitted | canvassing or using office influence |

Policy awareness is not partisanship. The permanent executive must understand priorities and consequences. Moral objection is not automatically political hostility; its language should be law, evidence, rights and public interest.

### India-centric example and limit

A returning officer may privately prefer a party but must apply identical scrutiny and avoid public signals of preference. Conversely, non-partisanship does not require ignoring targeted intimidation merely to produce equal-looking outcomes.

**Limit:** Formal symmetry can conceal unequal harm. Neutrality is consistent criteria, not mechanical equidistance.

### 2026 bounded cross-link

The 2026 DM Rajan communal/ethnic-tension case is a cross-link only. Its full case analysis belongs in Topic 22. This lesson contributes the distinction that non-partisanship concerns official conduct and auditable criteria; it does not require moral indifference or identical outcomes where facts differ.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Moral attitudes are also subjective, so they threaten neutrality.

**Reply:** Public moral judgment must be disciplined by constitutional values, law, evidence, reasons and review—not private intuition alone.

**Residual:** Disagreement persists; recorded reasons and appeal manage it.

### UPSC application

This lesson answers the syllabus phrase “moral and political attitudes.” It also provides a precise legal anchor while leaving the broader treatment of foundational civil-service values to Topic 04.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250-word ceiling

**Practice prompt:** A civil servant must retain moral judgment while bracketing partisan political preference. Examine.

**Suggested model:** Moral attitude concerns right and wrong, justice and dignity; political attitude concerns parties, ideologies, leaders and contests for power. Administration needs the first because discretion requires judgment about fairness, harm and propriety. Personal partisan preference, however, must not determine advice, benefit allocation, enforcement or communication.

The CCS (Conduct) Rules give a precise anchor. Rule 5 regulates participation in politics and elections, including canvassing and influence, while preserving a private vote subject to non-disclosure. Rule 7 concerns specified demonstrations and strikes; it is not the election rule. Non-partisanship therefore means candid evidence-based advice, lawful implementation and equal public criteria—not ignorance of policy or silent obedience.

Moral judgment must itself be disciplined by constitutional values, law, facts, recorded reasons and review. In conflict administration, identical outcomes may be unjust when conduct and need differ; symmetry should attach to process and criteria. The proper synthesis is morally reasoned, legally bounded and visibly non-partisan official conduct.

- Moral attitude evaluates right/wrong; political attitude evaluates party/power objects.
- Moral judgment is necessary in office but must be publicly reasoned.
- Private political belief is not political blankness.
- Rule 5 covers politics, movements and elections, including canvassing.

- Policy awareness and candid advice are not partisanship.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question — 150-word ceiling

**Question:** Distinguish attitude content from attitude function. How does the distinction improve administrative reform?

**Model answer:** Attitude content describes what the stance contains: beliefs, feelings and behavioural tendencies under the ABC model. Attitude function explains why the person holds it. Katz identifies knowledge, adjustment or instrumental, ego-defensive and value-expressive functions. The distinction changes intervention. An officer’s resistance to citizen feedback may reflect false beliefs, fear of adverse appraisal, defence of self-image or an identity built around authority. Evidence addresses the first; incentive and protection reform addresses the second; psychologically safe accountability addresses the third; professional identity and constitutional values address the fourth. Function is inferred and may be multiple, so diagnosis must remain testable. Rights and conduct rules also remain non-negotiable. Administrative reform is strongest when it targets the psychological function without excusing the resulting misconduct.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** content definition (2); function definition (2); four-function application (4); inference/rights qualification (1); conclusion (1).

## Original 15-mark question — 250-word ceiling

**Question:** Analyse how social influence can both improve and corrupt public administration.

**Model answer:** Social influence coordinates conduct by transmitting information, norms and professional identity. Informational influence helps officials learn from genuine expertise; normative influence spreads punctuality, service orientation and anti-discrimination expectations. Identification with credible leaders can inspire integrity, and internalisation can sustain conduct beyond monitoring.

The same mechanisms create risk. Group unanimity may suppress warnings; reward-dependent compliance may disappear when inspection ends; identification may become personality cult; authority can diffuse responsibility; partisan norms can displace constitutional duty. Asch and Milgram illustrate these mechanisms but their experimental settings cannot be mechanically generalised to India.

Institutional design should therefore preserve influence while preventing capture: independent assessment before meetings, recorded minority reasons, diverse teams, written directions, protected escalation, rotation, review and fair enforcement. Leaders should model reasons, not demand personal loyalty. Training should aim at internalisation but field conduct, citizen treatment and appeal outcomes must remain the evidence.

Social influence is ethically valuable when it improves knowledge and shared public standards while retaining reasoned independence, accountability and lawful dissent.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** beneficial mechanisms (4); corrupting mechanisms (4); research qualification (2); safeguards (3); balanced conclusion (2).

## Original 20-mark question — 250-word ceiling

**Question:** Evaluate persuasion as an instrument of democratic governance. When does it become ethically illegitimate?

**Model answer:** Democratic governance cannot rely only on command. Persuasion explains policy, invites cooperation and helps citizens act on shared risks. The Hovland–Yale framework highlights source credibility, message design and audience characteristics; channel is a later addition. Katz adds that intervention should match knowledge, instrumental, ego-defensive or value-expressive functions. Participatory SBC in disaster communication illustrates how affected communities can contribute rather than remain passive recipients.

Persuasion becomes illegitimate when truth is withheld, sponsorship or purpose is concealed, bias is exploited, alternatives are unavailable or vulnerability and dependency destroy meaningful refusal. Manipulation bypasses judgment; propaganda scales selective narrative; undue influence bends the will through relationship; coercion removes choice through threat. Even lawful coercion requires necessity and proportionality.

The strongest objection is that state power makes all persuasion suspect. The reply is stricter democratic safeguards: evidence, transparent authorship, accessible language, consultation, correction, opt-out or appeal where possible, independent review and outcome monitoring. Persuasion must also accompany material capacity; sanitation messaging without services shifts blame to citizens.

Democratic persuasion is legitimate when it treats people as reason-responsive participants, not behavioural targets.

**Rubric — 20 marks:** democratic role (3); persuasion framework/function (4); illegitimacy ladder (5); state-power objection/reply (3); material/participatory safeguards (3); conclusion (2).

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Core definition and distinctions

- **Attitude:** relatively stable evaluative disposition toward a specific object, person, group, policy, event or idea.
- **Value:** broad, enduring, cross-situational standard.
- **Belief:** proposition held true.
- **Aptitude:** capacity or potential to perform.
- Always identify the **attitude object** and valence.

## ABC structure

- Cognitive: belief/information.
- Affective: emotional charge.
- Behavioural/conative: intention or readiness to act, not the completed act.
- Key dimensions: valence, strength, accessibility, consistency and specificity.
- Component mismatch produces ambivalence and weak prediction.

## Katz functions

- Knowledge: organises complexity.
- Adjustment/utilitarian/instrumental: gains reward or avoids cost.
- Ego-defensive: protects self-image.
- Value-expressive: communicates identity or principle.
- Content asks “what?”; function asks “why held?”

## Formation, thought and dissonance

- Direct experience, family/peers/media, modelling and reinforcement form attitudes.
- Attitude also filters later attention and belief.
- Dissonance routes: change attitude, change behaviour, add justification.
- Rationalisation is the chief ethical risk.

## Attitude–behaviour relation

- Attitude → intention → situational/institutional filters → behaviour.
- Strong, accessible and specific attitudes predict better.
- LaPiere: stated general attitude and specific conduct may diverge.
- Explanation of pressure does not remove responsibility.

## Social influence taxonomy

- Informational: others are evidence.
- Normative: approval/belonging.
- Public compliance ≠ private acceptance.
- Kelman: compliance → reward/punishment; identification → relationship/identity; internalisation → value congruence.
- Obedience follows authority; conformity follows peers; compliance may follow a request.

## Authority safeguards

- Clarify authority and facts.
- Test law, rights, public purpose and harm.
- State concern; seek written direction where applicable.
- Record dissent; escalate and seek review.
- Refuse unlawful action while preserving lawful service.

## Persuasion

- Original Hovland–Yale variables: source, message, audience.
- Channel is a later addition.
- Cognitive route: reasons/evidence; affective route: emotion/identification.
- Match the intervention to attitude function.
- Communication cannot substitute for infrastructure.

## Ethical influence ladder

- Persuasion: reasons, transparency, meaningful choice.
- Manipulation: hidden intent or bias exploitation.
- Propaganda: selective repeated narrative at scale.
- Undue influence: dependency or authority bends voluntariness.
- Coercion: threat removes meaningful choice.
- Tests: truthfulness, transparency, autonomy, vulnerability, accessibility and contestability.

## Moral and political attitudes

- Retain moral judgment; bracket personal partisan preference in office.
- Policy awareness and candid advice are not partisanship.
- CCS Rule 5: politics and elections, including canvassing.
- CCS Rule 7: demonstrations and strikes.
- Non-partisanship = symmetry of process/criteria, not automatic symmetry of outcome.

## Administrative applications

- Caste: rights floor + education + cooperation + representation + grievance safeguards.
- Patriarchy: safety + education continuity + affected-person voice + norm change + law.
- Bureaucratic attitude: legality/expertise/continuity; risk of rigidity.
- Democratic attitude: participation/responsiveness/legitimacy; risk of populism.
- Swachh Bharat: norms and persuasion plus sanitation infrastructure and dignity.
- Civil-service cultivation: exposure, mentoring, cases, feedback, incentives, safe dissent, codes and audit.
- Mission Karmayogi may illustrate structured competency building; completions do not prove internalisation.

## One-line conclusion

> Attitude shapes how a public servant sees a specific citizen, rule or reform; ethical administration makes that orientation informed and humane, then supports and checks it through lawful institutions.
