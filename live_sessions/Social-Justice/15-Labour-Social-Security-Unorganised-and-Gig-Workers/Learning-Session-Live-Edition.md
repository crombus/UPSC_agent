## Lesson 1 — Labour welfare as constitutional social justice

Progress: 1 / 11 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Rights, directives and legislative competence

Recent context: No separate dated development is required for this constitutional foundation.
Focus: Distinguish constitutional direction, enforceable right and statutory benefit.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
PREAMBLE: social and economic justice
                 ↓
FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS
equality · dignity · freedom of association · protection from forced labour
                 +
DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES
livelihood · assistance · humane work · maternity relief · living wage · worker participation
                 ↓
CONCURRENT LEGISLATIVE POWER
industrial disputes · social security · labour welfare
                 ↓
STATUTES + SCHEMES + INSTITUTIONS
                 ↓
claimable and delivered protection?
```

*The Constitution supplies a normative and legislative foundation; the worker still needs an applicable law, scheme or institution to obtain a benefit.*

A worker who loses income after an accident faces more than private misfortune. Labour converts physical effort, time and skill into livelihood, while illness, maternity, disability, unemployment, old age or a breadwinner’s death can abruptly remove that livelihood. **Social security** pools or redistributes these risks so that one contingency does not destroy a household’s minimum capabilities.

### 1. The constitutional map

- **Articles 14 and 16** support equality and equal public opportunity; apparently neutral delivery can still exclude workers who lack stable documents or digital access.
- **Article 19(1)(c)** protects the freedom to form associations or unions, subject to constitutional restrictions. Worker voice is therefore not merely an administrative convenience.
- **Article 21** protects life and personal liberty; dignity and livelihood inform labour-welfare interpretation, although a specific cash benefit normally requires a statutory or scheme basis.
- **Article 23** prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. Labour extracted through coercion or prohibited economic compulsion is a rights issue, not only a wage dispute.
- **Article 24** prohibits employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines and other hazardous employment.

Part IV supplies the positive welfare direction. **Article 38** asks the State to promote a social order informed by justice and reduce inequalities. **Article 39(a)** concerns adequate means of livelihood; **39(d)** equal pay for equal work; **39(e)** protection of workers’ health and strength. **Article 41**, within the State’s economic capacity and development, addresses work, education and public assistance in unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement. **Article 42** requires just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief. **Article 43** seeks a living wage and decent life; **Article 43A** promotes worker participation in management.

### 2. Who may legislate?

Labour is substantially shared through the **Concurrent List**. Entry 22 covers trade unions and industrial and labour disputes; Entry 23 covers social security, social insurance, employment and unemployment; Entry 24 covers labour welfare, including conditions of work, provident funds, employer liability, compensation, pensions and maternity benefits. Parliament can create national architecture, while States remain crucial rule-makers and delivery authorities.

### 3. Strongest objection and reply

The objection is that Directive Principles are non-justiciable and resources are finite. That is correct but incomplete. Article 37 makes them fundamental in governance; legislation can convert a direction into a defined claim. Resource limits justify transparent priority-setting, not describing every portal or policy objective as an enforceable entitlement.

**UPSC use:** Start with the constitutional purpose, then move to the exact statute, eligible worker, financing channel and remedy. **Trap:** “DPSP recognises social security” does not by itself prove that a particular worker can compel payment.

### Revision points

1. Labour welfare protects income and health against specified contingencies.
2. The Preamble connects labour protection to social and economic justice.
3. Articles 14 and 16 supply equality principles; Article 21 supplies dignity.
4. Article 19(1)(c) supports worker association and collective voice.
5. Articles 23 and 24 create direct prohibitions concerning coercive and child labour.
6. Article 39 links livelihood, equal pay and protection of workers’ health.
7. Article 41 is expressly conditioned by economic capacity and development.
8. Article 42 concerns humane work and maternity relief.
9. Articles 43 and 43A concern living wage and worker participation.
10. Concurrent List Entries 22–24 explain Centre–State responsibility.
11. A constitutional directive becomes a payable benefit only through an applicable legal route.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a worker invoke the Constitution’s welfare vision yet still fail to establish a claim for a particular pension?

**Model answer:** The Constitution directs the State toward livelihood security, public assistance and humane work, but most Directive Principles are not directly enforceable benefit schedules. A pension claim needs an applicable statute or notified scheme, eligibility, enrolment, financing and a deciding institution.

**Misconception to avoid:** Non-justiciability does not make labour welfare optional; it means the answer must distinguish constitutional direction from a specific enforceable claim.

**Original Mains practice (10 marks; answer in 150 words):** Explain the constitutional foundations of labour welfare and social security in India.

**Mains model response:** Labour welfare rests on both rights and governance directives. Articles 14 and 16 support equality; Article 19(1)(c) protects association; Articles 21, 23 and 24 connect dignity with protection from coercive and hazardous child labour. Part IV gives the positive programme: Articles 38 and 39 seek social justice, livelihood, equal pay and protection of workers’ health; Article 41 addresses public assistance within economic capacity; Articles 42, 43 and 43A concern humane work, maternity relief, living wage and worker participation. Concurrent List Entries 22–24 permit both Parliament and States to legislate on industrial disputes, social security and labour welfare. These provisions do not automatically create every pension or insurance payment. Legislation, finance, eligibility, institutions and remedies convert the constitutional promise into protection. Thus, the Constitution supplies the direction and competence; effective administration determines whether a worker can actually claim and receive support.

**Scoring guide (10):** rights foundation 2; DPSP labour map 3; federal competence 2; promise-to-claim qualification 3.

## Lesson 2 — Worker categories without false binaries

Focus: Classify the enterprise, job relationship and coverage separately.

*“Informal” is not one legal identity. Several dimensions can coexist in the same worker’s life.*

### 1. Organised and unorganised are not identical to formal and informal

The Code defines an **unorganised sector** enterprise as one owned by individuals or self-employed workers and, where it employs workers, employing fewer than ten. An **unorganised worker** includes a home-based worker, self-employed worker or wage worker in the unorganised sector, and also specified workers in the organised sector who are outside named coverage.

“Formal” and “informal” are broader analytical descriptions. A registered company may use a worker through an insecure or uncovered arrangement; a small enterprise may maintain a stable written employment relationship. Therefore, firm registration, employment contract, statutory coverage and contribution history must be checked separately.

### 2. The Code’s principal worker definitions

- An **employee** is broadly a person employed on wages by an establishment, directly or through a contractor, subject to chapter-specific qualifications.
- A **home-based worker** produces goods or services for an employer from home or another chosen premise for remuneration.
- A **self-employed worker** is not employed by an employer and works in an unorganised occupation, subject to notified conditions.
- A **wage worker** in the unorganised sector may be temporary, casual, migrant, home-based or employed by households, including domestic workers.
- A **gig worker** earns from work or a work arrangement outside the traditional employer–employee relationship.
- **Platform work** is paid work outside that traditional relationship in which organisations or individuals use an online platform to access others for specified services.
- A **platform worker** undertakes platform work.
- An **aggregator** is a digital intermediary or marketplace connecting a service user with a provider.

Every platform worker is located within the Code’s gig architecture, but not every gig worker necessarily obtains work through an online platform. Nor does statutory social-security recognition finally decide whether a particular relationship should be treated as employment for every wage, dismissal or collective-bargaining question.

### 3. A person can cross categories

A woman may sew garments at home, undertake domestic work during part of the year and accept platform-based beauty-service assignments. A mason may be a migrant wage worker at one site and self-employed between contracts. Classification should follow the **specific transaction and entitlement**, not freeze a person permanently into one box.

**Strongest objection:** multiple categories make administration complex. **Reply:** one universal label is simpler only on paper; it can assign the wrong payer and benefit. A common minimum floor can coexist with relationship-specific protections.

**UPSC use:** Define the category, then explain its consequence. **Trap:** “organised enterprise” does not guarantee that every person connected with it receives employee social security.

### Revision points

1. Enterprise form, job formality, employment relationship and social-security coverage are distinct.
2. The Code’s unorganised-sector threshold is fewer than ten workers where workers are employed.
3. An unorganised worker may also be located in an organised sector but outside specified coverage.
4. Home-based work can be remunerated work for an employer.
5. Self-employment lacks an employer relationship but does not imply absence of economic dependence.
6. Wage work may be casual, migrant, domestic or home-based.
7. Gig work is outside the traditional employer–employee relationship under the Code’s definition.
8. Platform work uses an online intermediary for paid services.
9. Aggregator, platform worker and employee are not interchangeable labels.
10. One person may change category across time or hold several work arrangements.
11. Classification should be tied to the disputed benefit, duty or labour standard.
12. A common protection floor can coexist with category-specific obligations.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a rider’s app registration insufficient to establish either employee status or actual social-security coverage?

**Model answer:** The app proves a platform-mediated work arrangement, not the complete legal facts of control, wages or statutory coverage. Social-security receipt additionally needs an applicable scheme, eligibility, funding, registration, a covered contingency and a claim route.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Platform worker” is a statutory social-security category, not a universal answer to every labour-law classification dispute.

**Original Mains practice (15 marks; answer in 250 words):** Distinguish formal, informal, organised, unorganised, gig and platform work. Why do these distinctions matter for social security?

**Mains model response:** Labour categories answer different questions. Organised and unorganised principally describe enterprise structure and statutory thresholds; formal and informal describe the security, documentation and coverage of a work arrangement. The Code defines the unorganised sector around individually or self-employed enterprises and, where workers are employed, fewer than ten workers. An unorganised worker includes home-based, self-employed and wage workers, with specified uncovered workers in organised settings. Gig work occurs outside the traditional employer–employee relationship. Platform work is a subset mediated through an online platform, while an aggregator connects users and providers.

The distinctions matter because responsibility differs. An eligible employee may enter EPFO or ESIC architecture; a construction worker may depend on a State BOCW board; an unorganised worker may use a separately notified or contributory scheme; a platform worker enters the Code’s gig architecture. A registered enterprise can still contain uncovered work, and one worker may move between construction, self-employment and apps. Policy should therefore combine a common minimum floor with relationship-specific contributions, claims and remedies. The aim is not taxonomic neatness but identifying who owes which protection when a defined risk occurs.

**Scoring guide (15):** four-dimensional taxonomy 3; exact Code distinctions 4; responsibility consequences 4; mobility and qualified design 4.

## Lesson 3 — Institutions and the risk they actually cover

*The correct institution follows from the legal relationship and contingency, not from the existence of one identity number.*

### 1. Employee-linked organisations

The Code identifies the **Central Board of Trustees of the Employees’ Provident Fund** and the **Employees’ State Insurance Corporation** as social-security organisations. EPFO concerns provident-fund, pension and deposit-linked insurance architecture for applicable establishments and workers. ESIC concerns contributory social insurance and specified medical and cash benefits for covered insured persons. Provident savings are not the same as medical insurance, and neither route automatically covers every worker earning through an app or small household enterprise.

### 2. Unorganised-worker boards

The **National Social Security Board for Unorganised Workers** is chaired by the Union Labour Minister, with the Labour Secretary as Vice-Chairperson and representation from workers, employers, civil society, legislatures, governments and Union Territories. Its functions include recommending schemes, advising the Union, monitoring Central schemes, reviewing State record-keeping and reviewing fund expenditure.

Every State is to constitute a **State Unorganised Workers’ Social Security Board**. State boards connect national design to local scheme formulation and implementation. The Code requires adequate representation for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, minorities and women in the relevant nomination architecture; representation is valuable only if it influences scheme design and monitoring.

For gig/platform welfare, section 114 adapts the National Board’s composition to include aggregator and gig/platform-worker representatives, alongside EPFO/ESIC leadership, experts and States.

### 3. Construction-worker welfare

State **Building and Other Construction Workers’ Welfare Boards** remain an occupation-specific route supported by the construction cess. Registration and State-notified conditions determine actual benefits. A cess balance is not a paid injury, education or pension benefit; board variation and claim accessibility matter.

### 4. Administrative actors

The Ministry of Labour and Employment frames central policy and rules. State labour departments make or apply State rules and administer local institutions. Employers, contractors and aggregators supply records and contributions where law requires. Facilitation centres and assisted channels matter because the worker may not know which institution owns the decision.

**Objection and reply:** Multiple institutions create fragmentation. A single agency might simplify contact, but abolishing specialist risk pools can erase occupation-specific expertise. A common front door with interoperable records and institution-specific liability is more realistic than pretending every scheme is identical.

**UPSC use:** Compare institution, finance, contingency and remedy. **Trap:** calling e-Shram itself a social-security insurer.

### Revision points

1. EPFO and ESIC are distinct social-security organisations with different functions.
2. Provident-fund protection is not the same as health or employment-injury insurance.
3. The National Social Security Board recommends, advises, monitors and reviews.
4. State Unorganised Workers’ Boards connect scheme design to State delivery.
5. Section 114 modifies Board representation for gig/platform-worker welfare.
6. Aggregator and worker representation does not itself create a payable benefit.
7. BOCW boards are State-level occupation-specific institutions.
8. Construction cess must be distinguished from benefit disbursement.
9. Ministry and State labour departments have complementary roles.
10. A common interface can coexist with distinct legal liabilities.
11. Institutional success is measured by accessible claims and remedies, not organisational existence.

### Concept check

**Question:** A construction worker has an e-Shram UAN and suffers an injury. Why must the answer still identify a State board or another applicable institution?

**Model answer:** The UAN identifies the worker but does not decide BOCW registration, the relevant benefit, eligibility, financing or the authority that settles the claim. The applicable occupational or employee-linked institution must accept legal responsibility.

**Misconception to avoid:** Institutional convergence means connected access, not the disappearance of different funds, eligibility rules and decision-makers.

**Original Mains practice (10 marks; answer in 150 words):** Map the principal institutions in India’s labour social-security architecture.

**Mains model response:** India uses differentiated institutions. EPFO administers applicable provident-fund, pension and deposit-linked insurance arrangements; ESIC administers employee social insurance for covered establishments and insured persons. The National Social Security Board recommends and monitors schemes for unorganised, gig and platform workers, while State Unorganised Workers’ Boards connect design to State implementation. State BOCW Welfare Boards use construction-linked cess architecture for registered construction workers. The Ministry of Labour and Employment frames central policy and rules; State labour departments regulate and deliver within their competence. Employers, contractors and aggregators provide records and contributions where applicable. This plurality allows risk-specific expertise but fragments access. Reform should therefore provide a common worker-facing interface, accurate interoperable records and clear referral while preserving the institution legally responsible for financing, deciding and reviewing each claim.

**Scoring guide (10):** named organisations 3; risk-function matching 3; Centre–State allocation 2; integration-with-liability solution 2.

### 1. Commencement is Code-specific and provision-specific

The four commencement notifications dated **21 November 2025** do not use identical formulas:

| Code | Gazette control | Exact status |
|---|---|---|
| **Industrial Relations Code, 2020** | **S.O. 5320(E)** | Appoints 21 November 2025 for “the provisions” of the Code: the whole Code commenced. |
| **OSHWC Code, 2020** | **S.O. 5321(E)** | Appoints 21 November 2025 for “the provisions” of the Code: the whole Code commenced. |
| **Code on Wages, 2019** | **S.O. 5322(E)** read with **S.O. 4604(E), 18 December 2020** | Most provisions commenced, but not every sub-provision. S.O. 4604(E) commenced section 42(1)–(3), (10)–(11) and section 67(2)(s)–(t) only **to the extent they relate to the Central Advisory Board**. S.O. 5322(E) later commenced section 42(4)–(9) and omitted section 42(10)–(11) and section 67(2)(s)–(t); their State Advisory Board-related portions therefore remain outside these two notifications. |
| **Code on Social Security, 2020** | **S.O. 1730(E), 30 April 2021**; **S.O. 2060(E), 3 May 2023**; **S.O. 5319(E), 21 November 2025**, read as corrected by **S.O. 5936(E), 19 December 2025** | Section 142 commenced from 3 May 2021. Specified Employees’ Pension Scheme-related section 15/16, section 143 and section 164 portions commenced in 2023. The corrected 2025 notification commenced the remaining provisions, including the balance of sections 15, 16 and 164. Read together, the Code’s provisions are commenced. |

The corrigendum matters. The original text of S.O. 5319(E) printed narrower entries for sections 15, 16 and 164. **S.O. 5936(E)** corrected them to “section 15” and “section 16” and to the broader section 164 fields, excluding only the portions already commenced by S.O. 2060(E). It did not leave those residual provisions uncommenced. Separately, **S.O. 1730(E)** had already commenced section 142 from **3 May 2021**. The accurate formulation is therefore: **all Social Security Code provisions have commencement support across the 2021, 2023 and corrected 2025 notifications, although their commencement dates differ**.

The **Social Security (Central) Rules, 2026**, published as **G.S.R. 344(E) on 8 May 2026**, came into force on publication. Rules can operationalise matters delegated to them; they cannot commence a statutory provision omitted from the controlling commencement notifications.

### 2. Code on Wages: a wage floor needs a payer and a forum

The **Code on Wages, 2019** addresses four connected questions:

1. **Non-discrimination:** an employer cannot discriminate on the ground of gender in wages for the same or similar work, recruitment or conditions of employment, subject to lawful restrictions.
2. **Minimum and floor wages:** the appropriate Government fixes minimum wages; section 9 requires the Union to fix a floor wage, below which State minimum wages cannot fall. A statutory power is not the same as assuming one unsupported rate for every place and occupation.
3. **Payment discipline:** the Code regulates wage periods, timely payment and authorised deductions; the employer remains responsible for dues.
4. **Remedy and institutions:** Central and State Advisory Boards advise on minimum wages and related matters; a notified claims authority may determine dues and compensation; the Inspector-cum-Facilitator combines compliance advice with inspection and enforcement powers.

```text
floor wage by Union
       ↓ minimum cannot fall below floor
minimum wage by appropriate Government
       ↓ employer pays on time with lawful deductions
shortfall / delay
       ↓ claims authority → compensation / recovery → appeal
```

The merit is wider wage-standard coverage and a common wage definition. The implementation question is whether the appropriate Government has fixed the applicable rate, the worker can prove the employment and hours, and the claims machinery produces recovery.

### 3. Industrial Relations Code: collective bargaining is institutional, not rhetorical

The **Industrial Relations Code, 2020** places workplace voice and dispute settlement on several levels:

- a **Works Committee** may be required in an industrial establishment with 100 or more workers to promote amity;
- every industrial establishment with 20 or more workers requires a **Grievance Redressal Committee** for individual grievances;
- the **Registrar of Trade Unions** registers unions, subject to statutory membership criteria;
- where more than one registered union exists, a union supported by **51% or more** of workers is recognised as the sole negotiating union; if none reaches 51%, unions with at least **20% support** enter a negotiating council;
- conciliation officers, **Industrial Tribunals** and the **National Industrial Tribunal** handle different dispute stages;
- section 62 regulates strikes and lock-outs through notice and pendency restrictions, while the worker re-skilling fund addresses retrenchment transition.

Collective bargaining therefore requires a representative body, a defined bargaining counterpart, information, negotiation and an enforceable settlement or adjudicatory route. The 51% rule may simplify representation; the counter-concern is whether smaller unions and dispersed or platform workers retain effective voice. Social-security recognition of gig work does not by itself confer Industrial Relations Code bargaining status.

### 4. OSHWC Code: safety is an employer duty backed by worker rights

The **Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020** is not merely a licensing statute. Its core chain is:

| Actor or institution | Statutory role |
|---|---|
| Employer | Keep the workplace free from hazards; comply with safety standards; provide prescribed free health examinations; maintain a safe working environment; issue appointment letters; charge workers nothing for safety measures. |
| Employee | Take reasonable care, follow safety requirements, cooperate and report unsafe conditions without misusing protective equipment. |
| Worker facing imminent danger | Seek information, represent safety concerns to the employer or Safety Committee and approach the Inspector-cum-Facilitator. |
| National and State OSH Advisory Boards | Advise on standards, implementation, policy and administration. |
| Safety Committee / safety officers | Provide worker-employer participation and specialist safety capacity where required by order or statutory threshold. |
| Inspector-cum-Facilitator | Inspect, advise, investigate and exercise statutory enforcement powers; sector-specific authorities retain specialised roles. |

The Code also places prescribed health, sanitation and welfare duties on employers, makes principal employers responsible for specified welfare facilities for contract labour, and provides inter-State migrant-worker duties including suitable working conditions, accident reporting, extension of establishment benefits and prescribed annual journey allowance. Women may work in all establishments and at night with consent, subject to prescribed safety and working-condition safeguards.

Many details—health-examination classes, safety standards, committee procedure, welfare facilities, journey allowance and night-work safeguards—depend on rules, standards, orders or prescriptions by the appropriate Government. Commencement makes the statutory architecture operative; it does not justify inventing an unnotified standard or claiming uniform enforcement capacity.

### 5. What the 2026 Social Security Rules make concrete

The Rules make several gig/unorganised-worker procedures concrete:

- registration generally begins at age sixteen;
- gig/platform workers register on the designated portal on self-declaration with prescribed identity material;
- aggregators must share existing worker details within forty-five days and provide new engagement and exit information through electronic modes;
- eligibility for a gig/platform scheme may require at least **ninety days with one aggregator**, or **one hundred and twenty cumulative days across multiple aggregators**, in the previous financial year;
- the Rules specify how engagement days are counted;
- Rule 49 provides contribution assessment, return, interest and refund machinery.

These are operative procedural provisions. They should not be described as a future draft.

### 6. What remains scheme- or notification-dependent?

Section 114 permits Central schemes covering life and disability, accident insurance, health and maternity, old age, crèche and other benefits. It also permits multiple funding combinations. The aggregator contribution must fall within **1–2% of annual turnover**, capped at **5% of the amount paid or payable to gig and platform workers**.

But the Code itself says the exact rate and the date on which aggregator contribution commences are to be notified. Forms XX and XXI in the Rules retain a blank percentage “as notified” under section 114(4). Therefore, the statutory range and contribution machinery must not be converted into an unsupported assertion that one fixed nationwide rate has been separately notified, collected and used to pay a universal gig benefit.

### 7. The current-affairs linkage

The **8 May 2026 Rules** and the **1 June 2026 aggregator-onboarding direction** mark a real movement from enabling legislation toward registration and contribution administration. The direction required onboarding and API integration with e-Shram. It proves a data and compliance obligation; it does not alone prove that a particular section 114 benefit scheme has paid a claim.

### 8. Objection, reply and residual

One may argue that distinguishing every legal rung understates reform. The reply is the opposite: precision credits the reform actually achieved and identifies the next accountable act. As of **3 October 2026**, the defensible formulation is: the Industrial Relations and OSHWC Codes are commenced as whole Codes; the Social Security Code’s provisions are commenced across the 2021, 2023 and corrected 2025 notifications; and the Code on Wages is substantially commenced but retains the specific State Advisory Board-related exclusions in section 42(10)–(11) and section 67(2)(s)–(t). The Social Security Central Rules specify registration, eligibility and contribution procedures, but no unsupported claim is made here of a separately fixed nationwide section 114 rate, contribution-start notification or universal paid gig-worker benefit.

**UPSC use:** Name all four Codes, match each to its duties and institutions, and then state the notification-specific status. **Trap:** neither “all Codes are still pending” nor “every provision and every delegated detail operates uniformly” is accurate.

### Revision points

1. Industrial Relations Code: whole Code commenced by S.O. 5320(E) on 21 November 2025.
2. OSHWC Code: whole Code commenced by S.O. 5321(E) on the same date.
3. Code on Wages is substantially commenced, but State-board portions of section 42(10)–(11) and section 67(2)(s)–(t) remain outside S.O. 4604(E)/5322(E).
4. S.O. 5936(E) corrects S.O. 5319(E): the remaining sections 15, 16 and 164 fields commenced in 2025, excluding only portions already operative under S.O. 2060(E).
5. S.O. 1730(E) commenced Social Security section 142 from 3 May 2021; do not describe it as uncommenced.
6. Wage protection links minimum/floor wages and timely payment to Advisory Boards, claims authorities and inspectors.
7. IR voice links unions to a 51% negotiating-union rule or a 20%-support negotiating council.
8. OSHWC links employer hazard duties to worker safety rights, Boards, Safety Committees and inspectors.
9. G.S.R. 344(E) notified the Social Security (Central) Rules on 8 May 2026, operative on publication.
10. Worker registration and aggregator data duties are operative procedural rules.
11. Rule 48 contains the 90-day single-aggregator and 120-day multi-aggregator eligibility thresholds.
12. The thresholds concern applicable scheme eligibility, not a benefit amount.
13. Section 114 fixes a 1–2% statutory range and 5% payout-linked cap.
14. The exact contribution rate and contribution-start date require separate notification.
15. Commencement, delegated rules, State readiness, scheme operation, enforcement and outcomes must be tested separately.

### Concept check

**Question:** Which statements are justified after the 8 May Rules: “gig workers are recognised and registration duties operate” or “every aggregator already finances the same paid national benefit”?

**Model answer:** The first is justified because the relevant Social Security provisions and operative Rules establish recognition, registration, eligibility and administrative machinery. The second additionally requires the relevant rate/start notification, funded scheme, collection and paid-claim evidence.

**Misconception to avoid:** An enacted percentage range is not the same thing as a verified rate, remittance or benefit receipt.

**Original Mains practice (15 marks; answer in 250 words):** Explain the present legal stage of the Code on Social Security, 2020, with special reference to gig and platform workers.

**Mains model response:** The Code on Social Security, 2020 is operative, but its notification history must be stated correctly. S.O. 1730(E) commenced section 142 from 3 May 2021. S.O. 2060(E) commenced specified Employees’ Pension Scheme-related provisions in 2023. S.O. 5319(E), read with its S.O. 5936(E) corrigendum, commenced the remaining provisions from 21 November 2025, including the balance of sections 15, 16 and 164. The Social Security (Central) Rules, 2026 were published on 8 May and commenced on publication. They operationalise registration from age sixteen, aggregator sharing of existing worker records within forty-five days, continuing engagement/exit updates and scheme-eligibility thresholds of ninety days with one aggregator or one hundred and twenty cumulative days across multiple aggregators in the previous financial year. Rule 49 also creates assessment and return machinery.

Yet legal precision remains essential. Section 114 authorises gig/platform schemes and an aggregator contribution within 1–2% of annual turnover, capped at 5% of payments to workers. The Code separately requires notification of the exact rate and contribution-start date, while the Rules’ forms refer to the rate “as notified”. Hence operative registration and contribution machinery do not by themselves prove a universal collected levy or paid benefit. *The UAN can connect stages; it does not erase the legal tests between them.*

e-Shram was launched in 2021 as the national database for unorganised workers. Registration creates a **Universal Account Number**. Its one-stop architecture can connect a worker to welfare, employment, skill and pension services and can reduce repeated identification when a worker changes occupation or State.

### 1. Four functions, four limits

1. **Identification:** the same person can be recognised across transactions. It does not prove scheme membership.
2. **Discovery and convergence:** the portal can display or connect relevant schemes. A link does not establish eligibility.
3. **Updating and portability:** occupation, address and engagement records can follow the worker. The destination institution must still accept liability.
4. **Evidence for planning:** aggregate records can reveal categories and location. Registration totals are not beneficiary or claim-settlement totals.

### 2. Why correction and assisted access matter

A wrong occupation, duplicate record, failed Aadhaar authentication or outdated mobile number can block a benefit. Workers may need Common Service Centres, facilitation centres, labour offices or offline support. A digital-only system shifts administrative cost onto people with the weakest connectivity.

### 3. Data protection is a delivery issue

Cross-platform records may reveal earnings, location and work patterns. Share only what the lawful scheme needs; provide notice, access and correction; record which institution used the data; and offer a remedy when an error causes exclusion. Privacy and portability are not opposites: accurate, purpose-limited exchange can improve both.

**Strongest defence:** a national database makes previously invisible work legible. **Residual objection:** visibility can become surveillance or exclusion when data are excessive, inaccurate or treated as entitlement. The solution is worker-controlled correction and claims evidence, not abandonment of registration.

**UPSC use:** State which transaction digitisation improves and which legal decision remains. **Trap:** e-Shram is not EPFO membership, BOCW registration, PM-SYM subscription or a universal insurance policy.

### Revision points

1. e-Shram is an unorganised-worker database and service-convergence platform.
2. Registration creates a UAN, not an automatic cash or insurance benefit.
3. Identification, eligibility, enrolment and receipt are four different stages.
4. A scheme link on a portal is not proof that its legal conditions are met.
5. Updating occupation and location can support mobile workers.
6. Benefit portability requires a responsible paying institution.
7. Registration counts must not be reported as settled-claim counts.
8. Assisted and offline access reduce digital exclusion.
9. Data accuracy and correction are social-security safeguards.
10. Purpose limitation matters for earnings and location information.
11. A registry should be evaluated through successful claims, rejection reasons and appeals.

### Concept check

**Question:** A migrant worker’s UAN remains valid after she changes State, but a welfare-board claim is rejected. Which part of the chain has failed?

**Model answer:** Identity portability has worked, but entitlement portability may have failed at board registration, State eligibility, contribution recognition, claim acceptance or review. The reasoned rejection must identify the responsible institution and correctable defect.

**Misconception to avoid:** A nationally portable number does not automatically make every State-administered benefit portable.

**Original Mains practice (10 marks; answer in 150 words):** “e-Shram is necessary infrastructure, not social security by itself.” Explain.

**Mains model response:** e-Shram identifies an unorganised worker through a UAN and can connect records, schemes, employment and assisted services. A worker must still satisfy scheme eligibility, complete any separate enrolment or contribution, experience a covered risk, file a claim and obtain payment or review. A construction worker’s UAN is not State BOCW-board membership; a street vendor’s record is not PM-SYM subscription; a rider’s platform data do not prove a funded gig benefit. *The instruments complement rather than replace one another because they cover different risks and relationships.*

```text

work injury → employment-injury / board / scheme route
maternity → applicable statutory or scheme route
```

*Start with the contingency; a scheme name should follow rather than lead the analysis.*

### 1. PM-SYM after the PYQ

Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan-dhan is a **voluntary and contributory** pension scheme for eligible unorganised workers. Entry age is **18–40 years**, subject to scheme conditions including the income and exclusion criteria. The worker pays an age-specific contribution and the Union Government contributes equally. A qualifying subscriber receives a minimum **₹3,000 monthly pension after age sixty**. After the subscriber’s death in the pension phase, the spouse may receive fifty per cent as family pension; the scheme describes family pension as applicable only to the spouse.

The scheme is administered by the Labour Ministry with LIC as pension-fund manager and CSC support. e-Shram registration can facilitate discovery, but PM-SYM requires its own enrolment and contribution.

### 2. Why pension is not a complete floor

A casual worker may value old-age income yet be unable to contribute during lean months. Pension at sixty does not pay today’s hospital bill or replace income after an accident. Contribution continuity, exits, defaults and survivor conditions matter. A tax-financed minimum floor may therefore be necessary alongside voluntary contributory depth.

### 3. BOCW welfare as occupation-specific pooling

Construction welfare uses a sector-linked cess and State boards. It can address accident, education, maternity, pension or other State-notified needs, but registration and benefit rules vary. Migrants can face site, duration, renewal or document friction. Publishing cess collected without claims paid hides the worker outcome.

**Objection and reply:** multiple schemes cause overlap. Yet combining every risk into one product may make finance opaque and benefits inadequate. A common worker account should show distinct memberships, contributions and claim rights without pretending they are one scheme.

**UPSC use:** For the 2024 objective question, keep entry age, beneficiary contribution, pension timing and survivor coverage as separate propositions. **Trap:** similar names do not merge e-Shram, PM-SYM and any other pension product.

### Revision points

1. PM-SYM is voluntary and contributory.
2. Entry age is 18–40 years under the scheme conditions.
3. Worker contributions are age-specific.
4. The Union Government matches the worker contribution equally.
5. The minimum pension is ₹3,000 per month after age sixty for qualifying subscribers.
6. Family pension in the pension phase is for the spouse at fifty per cent.
7. e-Shram registration is not PM-SYM subscription.
8. A pension does not replace accident, maternity or current health protection.
9. Irregular income can interrupt contribution continuity.
10. BOCW welfare is financed principally through the construction cess architecture.
11. State-board eligibility and benefits require State-specific examination.
12. Scheme quality requires contribution persistence, timely claims and adequate benefits.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can two e-Shram registrants—one contributing to PM-SYM and one registered with a BOCW board—possess different enforceable claims?

**Model answer:** PM-SYM creates a voluntary contributory old-age promise under its conditions, whereas BOCW registration links a construction worker to State-notified occupation-specific benefits financed through cess architecture. Their contingencies, funds and deciding bodies differ.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equal government matching in PM-SYM does not mean pension without worker contribution or coverage of every lifecycle risk.

**Original Mains practice (15 marks; answer in 250 words):** Assess the role and limits of contributory and occupation-specific schemes in protecting unorganised workers.

**Mains model response:** Contributory and occupation-specific schemes add depth that a general registry cannot provide. PM-SYM offers eligible unorganised workers a voluntary old-age route: age-specific worker contributions receive equal Union matching and qualifying subscribers receive a minimum ₹3,000 monthly pension after sixty. BOCW welfare instead pools a construction-linked cess through State boards for occupation-specific benefits. EPFO and ESIC provide still different employee-linked provident and insurance routes.

Their strength is defined finance and institutional responsibility. Their limit is exclusion. A worker with volatile income may interrupt pension contributions; a migrant construction worker may struggle with State-board registration; pension cannot replace immediate accident or maternity protection. Fragmentation also makes it difficult to know which record or office controls a claim. Reform should provide assisted enrolment, transparent contributions, visible accrued rights, reasoned rejections and a common interface while preserving the correct fund. A publicly financed basic floor is still needed for people unable to sustain contributions. The balanced objective is layered protection: minimum security against severe risks, with contributory and occupation-specific additions where liability and payment are clear.

*Migration is protected only when rights and remedies, not merely records, survive movement.*

A seasonal worker may move from agriculture to construction, from one contractor to another and from one State to another. The risks remain continuous; institutions often do not. ### 1. Four kinds of portability

1. **Identity portability:** the same UAN identifies the person.
2. **Contribution portability:** prior payments or accrued balances remain visible and usable.
3. **Benefit portability:** a destination institution recognises the right and pays.

### 2. Federal responsibility

The Union can define national standards and shared digital architecture. States administer labour departments, boards and many schemes. Origin States hold prior records; destination States encounter the worker and contingency. Clear rules must allocate the cost of accrued benefits and specify which office decides an appeal.

### 3. Multi-platform work is a digital migration problem

A rider may move between platforms within one day. Rule 48 provides cumulative engagement-day counting across aggregators, but a benefit scheme must still prevent both exclusion and double counting. Platforms must transmit accurate engagement and exit records; workers need access to correct errors.

**Strongest objection:** uniform national rules would simplify everything. **Reply:** national minimums and interoperable claims help, but State welfare choices and occupation-specific funds cannot be wished away. Uniformity without a local paying office is only centralised paperwork.

**UPSC use:** Link migration to social security without reducing the answer to ONORC. **Trap:** “one nation” identity does not mean one undifferentiated benefit.

1. Migrants often change State, employer, contractor and occupation together.
2. Identity portability is the first, not final, portability test.
3. Contributions and accrued rights must remain traceable.
4. Benefit portability requires a destination paying institution.
5. Remedy portability allows correction and appeal without costly return migration.
6. ONORC addresses food entitlement, not every labour contingency.
7. Origin and destination States hold different responsibilities and information.
8. BOCW-board variation can interrupt construction-worker protection.
9. Multi-platform work creates parallel contribution and attribution problems.
10. Rule 48 counts engagement across multiple aggregators cumulatively.
11. National standards need local assisted delivery and accountable appeals.
12. Portability success is a completed out-of-State claim, not a portable card.

### Concept check

**Question:** What evidence would establish that a migrant construction worker has portable social security rather than only portable identity?

**Model answer:** Evidence should show recognised prior registration or accrual, accepted destination eligibility, an identified paying board, a filed and settled claim, and access to correction or appeal without returning to the origin State.

**Misconception to avoid:** Cross-State data exchange can support portability but cannot itself allocate legal and financial liability.

**Original Mains practice (15 marks; answer in 250 words):** Analyse the social-security problems of inter-State migrant workers and suggest a workable portability design.

**Mains model response:** Inter-State migrants face continuity failure because work, residence, contractor and institution change together. A national UAN can preserve identity, but construction-board membership, contribution history, scheme eligibility and the paying office may remain State-specific. Food portability through ONORC supports household consumption but cannot substitute for injury compensation, maternity support, pension or provident rights. Multi-platform workers face a similar problem when engagements are split among aggregators.

Second, make accrued contributions and valid board membership visible through interoperable records. Third, establish rules allocating financial responsibility between origin and destination institutions and across aggregators. Fourth, provide assisted destination claims, reasoned rejection, correction and appeal. The Union should set minimum data and service standards; States should retain accountable delivery offices and disclose out-of-State claims. Privacy requires purpose-limited sharing rather than unlimited tracking.

The objection that this is administratively costly is serious. Yet making workers repeatedly restart registration merely externalises the cost onto the poorest. Portability should be measured by timely destination-State payment and review, not by the number of cards issued.

*Legal remittance identifies who pays the government; economic incidence asks whose income or price ultimately changes.*

### 1. Classification is entitlement-specific

The Code recognises gig and platform workers outside the traditional employer–employee relationship for its social-security architecture. That inclusion is valuable because it does not force a worker to win an employee-status dispute before entering every welfare discussion. Yet it does not settle minimum wage, working time, dismissal or collective bargaining under all other laws.

Control remains analytically relevant. Who sets price? Can the worker reject tasks without penalty? Who owns customer data? Can a rating remove access? Is substitution real? These facts reveal dependence and bargaining power, even when the statute uses the gig category.

### 2. Financing design

Section 114 allows government, State, beneficiary, aggregator, CSR and other funding combinations. An aggregator contribution links sector activity to sector risk. Rule 49 creates self-assessment, returns, a separate gig/platform account and interest for delayed contribution. But an answer must identify the applicable notified percentage and start date before converting the statutory range into a realised fiscal flow.

### 3. Multi-homing and attribution

A worker may use three apps. Rule 48 can count engagements cumulatively, but a scheme must decide how exposure, contribution and claims are attributed. A worker should not disappear because no single platform crosses a threshold, nor should identical exposure be counted three times without justification.

### 4. Incidence and objection

Platforms may pass contribution costs through lower payouts, higher consumer prices or reduced incentives. That possibility does not invalidate a levy; it requires evidence. Publish remittances, worker earnings, prices, platform margins and paid claims. The counterfactual also matters: without pooling, workers and public services bear accident and old-age costs.

**UPSC use:** Distinguish legal classification, social-security inclusion and economic incidence. **Trap:** “aggregator contributes” does not prove the platform alone bears the cost.

### Revision points

1. Gig recognition avoids making every welfare claim depend on employee classification.
2. The gig definition does not decide all wage, dismissal or bargaining rights.
3. Price-setting, allocation, substitution and deactivation reveal practical control.
4. Section 114 permits several public, private and mixed funding combinations.
5. The statutory contribution range is 1–2% with a 5% worker-payment cap.
6. Rule 49 creates assessment, return, account and refund machinery.
7. A separately notified rate and start date remain legally significant.
8. Multi-homing complicates engagement counting and responsibility.
9. Legal incidence identifies the remitter; economic incidence identifies the ultimate bearer.
10. Cost shifting is an empirical question, not an automatic conclusion.
11. Fund receipts must be paired with eligible-to-paid claim evidence.
12. Classification should be tied to the exact entitlement under dispute.

**Mains model response:** The Code’s gig/platform category is a major inclusion device because social security need not await a final employee-status ruling. However, it does not settle every wage, working-time, dismissal or bargaining claim. Practical control—price-setting, task allocation, ratings, substitution and deactivation—still reveals dependence and should inform labour standards.

Financing is equally layered. Section 114 permits public, aggregator, beneficiary and mixed funding and creates a 1–2% turnover range capped at 5% of worker payments. The 2026 Rules establish registration, eligibility and contribution-administration machinery. Yet the exact notified rate, commencement of contribution, scheme, receipts and claims must be identified before asserting realised protection. Multi-platform work complicates engagement counting and attribution.

Aggregators object that levies may reduce flexibility or raise prices. The concern is credible because legal liability can be shifted through lower payouts or consumer charges. Transparent fund accounts and incidence studies should therefore compare platform margins, worker net earnings, prices and paid benefits. Still, leaving accidents and old age entirely with workers also shifts costs to families and public services. A sound model combines a publicly supported floor, sector contributions, worker representation, multi-platform records and accessible claims while preserving separate adjudication of employment status.

**Scoring guide (20):** classification distinction 5; financing law and status 5; multi-platform/incidence analysis 5; counterargument and qualified design 5.

## Lesson 10 — Algorithmic management, voice and remedy

Progress: 10 / 11 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Ratings, deactivation, bargaining and procedural fairness

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Social insurance covers contingencies; it does not automatically regulate every platform decision.
Recent context: No additional dated event is required for the algorithmic-management analysis.
Focus: Separate insured risk from platform power.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
data + ratings + demand forecast
            ↓
task allocation · dynamic price · incentive · surveillance
            ↓
income volatility / unsafe pressure / exclusion from app
            ↓
notice → intelligible reason → human review → independent remedy
            +
worker association and collective voice
```

*An accident policy can pay for an accident; it cannot decide whether an opaque deactivation was fair.*

### 1. What algorithmic management changes

Platforms can coordinate millions of transactions efficiently. The same system may vary prices, rank workers, allocate tasks, impose incentives or deactivate access. A worker may not know which data produced an adverse score. Volatile income also makes regular pension or insurance contributions harder.

### 2. Different disputes need different remedies

- A **benefit dispute** asks whether a covered contingency and scheme conditions are satisfied.
- A **data dispute** asks whether the record is accurate and lawfully used.
- A **deactivation dispute** asks whether loss of access followed fair standards and procedure.
- A **classification dispute** asks which labour rights follow from the actual relationship.
- A **collective dispute** concerns bargaining power and sector-wide terms.

An in-app ticket may resolve a spelling error but is structurally weak when the platform itself made and reviews a livelihood-ending decision.

### 3. Proportionate safeguards

Consequential decisions should receive advance or prompt notice, an intelligible reason, access to relevant records, human reconsideration and an independent forum. Worker associations can identify repeated errors that isolated complaints conceal. Routine matching decisions need not become litigation; safeguards can be calibrated to seriousness.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

Platforms argue that rapid matching and flexibility would be harmed by employment-like procedure. The reply is proportionality: focus external review on deactivation, withheld earnings, safety and serious discrimination. Genuine flexibility means worker choice, not unilateral opacity.

**UPSC use:** A complete gig-worker answer includes social insurance, fair working conditions and voice as separate pillars. **Trap:** do not promise that the Social Security Code itself has already enacted every algorithmic safeguard proposed here.

### Revision points

1. Algorithmic management includes allocation, pricing, rating, incentives and deactivation.
2. Efficient matching and opaque power can coexist.
3. Income volatility undermines contribution continuity.
4. Benefit, data, deactivation, classification and collective disputes are distinct.
5. An internal helpdesk may not be independent.
6. Consequential decisions merit reasons and human reconsideration.
7. Independent review should be proportionate to the harm.
8. Worker associations reveal recurring systemic failures.
9. Accident insurance does not regulate wrongful exclusion from work.
10. Data access is necessary to contest an adverse score.
11. Flexibility should be tested from the worker’s as well as the platform’s perspective.
12. Proposed safeguards must not be misreported as already operative statutory guarantees.

### Concept check

**Question:** An insured rider is deactivated after a disputed rating. Which parts of the loss fall outside the insurance claim?

**Model answer:** Insurance may cover only the specified accident, health or disability contingency. Lost future access, opaque rating, withheld earnings, discrimination and absence of fair review require separate labour, data or grievance remedies.

**Misconception to avoid:** Social-security inclusion is important but does not exhaust justice at work.

**Original Mains practice (15 marks; answer in 250 words):** “Registration and insurance cannot by themselves secure justice for platform workers.” Discuss.

**Mains model response:** Registration and insurance solve only parts of platform vulnerability. A UAN can identify the worker; a funded scheme can cover a specified accident, health or old-age risk. Neither determines how tasks and prices are set, why incentives change, whether customer bias affects ratings, or whether deactivation is justified. These decisions can destabilise income and make contributions harder to sustain.

Remedies must match disputes. Benefit denial needs a claims appeal; inaccurate records need correction; deactivation requires notice, intelligible reasons and human reconsideration; sector-wide unilateral terms require worker association and collective voice. An internal app ticket may be useful for clerical problems but lacks independence when the platform reviews its own livelihood-ending decision.

Platforms reasonably warn that heavy procedure can slow flexible matching. Safeguards should therefore be proportionate: routine allocations need not be litigated, while deactivation, withheld earnings, serious safety decisions and discrimination merit stronger review. Social security should be integrated with, not substituted for, fair working conditions. The balanced test is whether workers receive funded protection against contingencies and can contest consequential exercises of platform power.

**Scoring guide (15):** protection limits 4; dispute-remedy taxonomy 3; procedural and collective safeguards 5; proportional counterargument 3.

## Lesson 11 — Reform design from recognition to realised protection

Progress: 11 / 11 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Universal floor, contributory layers, governance and outcomes

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Reform must connect constitutional purpose, financing, portability, institutions and worker power.
Recent context: The legal status remains dated to 3 October 2026 as stated in Lesson 4.
Focus: Build a feasible design and specify how failure would be measured.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
MINIMUM FLOOR
essential health + accident/disability + maternity + old-age income security
                         ↓
CONTRIBUTORY / OCCUPATION-SPECIFIC LAYERS
EPFO · ESIC · PM-SYM · BOCW · gig/platform schemes
                         ↓
COMMON ACCESS SPINE
UAN · assisted enrolment · portable records · claims · appeal
                         ↓
ACCOUNTABLE GOVERNANCE
clear payer · fund audit · State coordination · worker voice · data safeguards
                         ↓
OUTCOMES
coverage breadth · benefit adequacy · timeliness · continuity · remedy
```

*Good reform is layered: a floor prevents destitution, while additional schemes deepen protection without hiding liability.*

### 1. A social-protection floor

Workers with the least stable earnings are least able to sustain contributions. A publicly financed floor should address severe lifecycle and work-related risks without treating regular payment as the only proof of deservingness. Its fiscal cost must be transparent, targeted where necessary and periodically evaluated.

### 2. Contributory and sector-specific depth

Employee-linked contributions, PM-SYM, BOCW cess and aggregator funding can add protection. The worker should see which fund holds contributions, which contingency is covered, and which office must pay. Avoid double contribution without double protection.

### 3. A common access spine

Use e-Shram/UAN for identification and scheme discovery, not as a substitute for entitlement. Permit assisted and offline enrolment, simple updating, purpose-limited data exchange, portable contribution histories and destination-State claims. A refusal should provide reasons and an appeal path.

### 4. Governance and evidence

Publish:

- eligible, enrolled and contributing workers separately;
- assessed and collected employer/aggregator/cess amounts;
- claims filed, accepted, rejected and pending;
- benefit amount and settlement time;
- interstate and multi-platform outcomes;
- disaggregation by gender, caste, occupation and disability where lawful and safe;
- appeal outcomes and repeated system errors.

### 5. Objections and balanced design

A universal floor may strain budgets; contribution mandates may reduce work or shift costs; interoperability may threaten privacy; specialised boards may fragment delivery. The answer is not maximalism. Cost the floor, phase benefits by severity, audit incidence, minimise data, retain specialised competence and create a common worker-facing remedy.

**UPSC use:** End with a measurable institutional design rather than a list of schemes. **Trap:** “universal” should identify the minimum risk floor, not promise identical benefits regardless of relationship or contribution.

### Revision points

1. A floor protects workers unable to contribute regularly.
2. Fiscal transparency is necessary for a credible universal minimum.
3. Contributory schemes add depth but can exclude irregular earners.
4. Occupation-specific finance should remain linked to accountable benefits.
5. A common access spine need not merge every fund.
6. Worker-visible contribution histories reduce uncertainty.
7. Assisted and offline access are part of universality.
8. Portability must include claims and appeals.
9. Data minimisation and correction protect both privacy and access.
10. Measure eligible, enrolled, contributing, claiming and paid populations separately.
11. Benefit adequacy and settlement time matter alongside breadth.
12. Incidence analysis tests whether costs are shifted to workers.
13. Worker voice should shape scheme and platform governance.
14. Reform succeeds when a contingency produces timely, adequate and contestable support.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a universal floor compatible with distinct EPFO, ESIC, BOCW, PM-SYM and gig-worker schemes?

**Model answer:** The floor supplies minimum protection against severe risks for everyone, while distinct schemes provide contribution- or occupation-linked depth. Compatibility requires clear coordination, no unjustified duplication and a common access and remedy spine.

**Misconception to avoid:** Universal minimum protection does not require one identical institution, contribution or benefit for every worker.

**Original Mains practice (20 marks; answer in 250 words):** Design a social-security architecture for India’s informal, migrant and platform workers that is inclusive, portable and fiscally credible.

**Mains model response:** India needs a layered architecture. First, a costed public floor should protect severe health, accident, disability, maternity and old-age risks for workers unable to sustain regular contributions. Second, contributory and occupation-specific layers—EPFO, ESIC, PM-SYM, BOCW welfare and notified gig schemes—should deepen protection with clear payer and benefit rules. Third, e-Shram/UAN should provide a common access spine for identification, scheme discovery, portable records and assisted claims without being mistaken for entitlement.

Portability requires contribution continuity, destination-State payment, multi-platform attribution, correction and appeal. Governance should publish assessed and collected contributions, eligible workers, claims, rejections, settlement time and benefit adequacy. Purpose-limited data exchange and offline access reduce surveillance and digital exclusion. National and State boards should include meaningful worker representation; consequential platform decisions need a separate fair-review route.

Fiscal and employment objections are real. The floor should be phased by severity and capacity, while contribution incidence is reviewed for cost shifting. Specialised institutions should remain where they possess risk expertise, but workers need one understandable interface. The outcome test is whether an injured, older, pregnant or mobile worker can identify the payer, obtain timely adequate support and challenge refusal.

**Scoring guide (20):** layered finance 4; portability and access architecture 5; governance/outcome metrics 4; power and data safeguards 4; fiscal qualification 3.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

**Original 10-marker — Distinguish recognition, registration and protection in labour social security (150 words).**

**Model answer:** Recognition identifies a worker category in law: the Social Security Code, for example, defines gig and platform workers. Registration records the person or engagement; e-Shram supplies a UAN and the 2026 Rules require platform-worker data. Protection is the final substantive outcome: an applicable scheme must define eligibility and contingency, receive finance, accept a claim and provide payment or review. A construction worker may be recognised and registered yet lack valid State-board membership; a rider may cross an engagement threshold while no asserted scheme covers the accident; a PM-SYM subscriber receives old-age protection only after contributions and qualifying age. Good administration connects the three stages but does not collapse them. Report category, record, contribution, claim and receipt separately.

**Scoring guide (10):** three concepts 3; named illustrations 2; causal delivery chain 3; precise conclusion 2.

**Original 15-marker — Examine whether digital convergence can overcome fragmentation in labour welfare (250 words).**

**Model answer:** Digital convergence can reduce repeated identification and make fragmented institutions easier to navigate. An e-Shram UAN can persist across occupations and States, connect workers to scheme information and support aggregator or board data exchange. It can also expose duplicate or missing records.

But fragmentation is not only informational. EPFO, ESIC, BOCW boards, PM-SYM and gig-worker schemes use different finance, eligibility and contingencies. A common portal cannot decide which State board bears a migrant claim, turn a UAN into PM-SYM subscription or substitute for a notified aggregator contribution and benefit scheme. Incorrect records can spread across systems, while device, language, authentication and bank barriers exclude workers.

Convergence should therefore provide worker-controlled correction, assisted/offline access, purpose-limited exchange, visible contributions, scheme-specific reasons for rejection and a common referral/appeal interface. Legal and financial liability must remain explicit. Publish successful cross-State claims, settlement times and appeals rather than only registration totals. Digital architecture can become the spine of protection, but only institutions, finance and remedies supply the body.

**Scoring guide (15):** convergence advantages 3; institutional limits 4; data/access safeguards 5; measured qualified verdict 3.

**Original 20-marker — “India’s labour social-security challenge is less absence of categories than failure to connect risk, finance, institution and remedy.” Critically evaluate (250 words).**

**Model answer:** India now possesses multiple legal categories and institutions: employees, unorganised workers, gig/platform workers, EPFO, ESIC, BOCW boards, e-Shram and pension schemes. The commenced Social Security provisions and 2026 Rules further specify registration, engagement thresholds and contribution administration. The central weakness is often the chain between category and protection.

An accident requires a covered worker, funded scheme, responsible institution, accessible claim and review. A migrant may retain identity but lose State-board continuity. A PM-SYM subscriber has old-age protection, not present wage replacement. Section 114’s statutory range and Rule 49 machinery do not alone establish a fixed collected rate or paid universal benefit. Women, caste-disadvantaged and home-based workers may face documentation, care, subcontracting and digital barriers after formal inclusion.

Yet categories still matter because they allocate responsibility and can exclude borderline workers. Reform should combine a publicly financed minimum floor with employee-, occupation- and sector-linked depth; interoperable worker-controlled records; destination-State claims; audited contributions and outcomes; and remedies for both benefit denial and platform power. Cost and flexibility objections require phased benefits and incidence analysis. The decisive test is whether a worker facing a specified risk can identify who owes support, receive it adequately and challenge refusal.

**Scoring guide (20):** thesis and architecture 5; category-to-delivery failures 4; intersectional and power analysis 4; reform design 4; counterargument and verdict 3.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional and conceptual base

- Labour social security protects health care and income against old age, unemployment, sickness, invalidity, work injury, maternity and breadwinner loss.
- Rights spine: Articles 14/16 equality; 19(1)(c) association; 21 dignity; 23 forced-labour prohibition; 24 hazardous child-labour prohibition.
- DPSP spine: Articles 38, 39(a)/(d)/(e), 41, 42, 43 and 43A. Article 41 is conditioned by economic capacity and development.
- Concurrent List: Entry 22 industrial disputes/unions; Entry 23 social security, insurance, employment/unemployment; Entry 24 labour welfare and related protection.
- Constitution → statute/scheme → finance → institution → claim → remedy. Do not jump from the first to the last.

## Worker taxonomy

- Organised/unorganised describes enterprise structure and legal thresholds; formal/informal describes the security and coverage of work. They overlap but are not synonyms.
- Unorganised sector under the Code: individually/self-employed enterprise and, where workers are employed, fewer than ten.
- Unorganised worker includes home-based, self-employed and wage workers plus specified uncovered workers in organised settings.
- Gig worker: earns through work outside the traditional employer–employee relationship.
- Platform work: paid service matching through an online platform; platform worker undertakes it; aggregator connects user and provider.
- Social-security gig recognition does not finally determine every minimum-wage, dismissal or collective-bargaining issue.

## Four-Code core: duty, institution and remedy

- **Code on Wages, 2019:** non-discrimination, minimum wages, Union floor wage, payment discipline and lawful deductions; institutions include Central/State Advisory Boards, notified claims authorities, appellate authority and Inspector-cum-Facilitator.
- **Industrial Relations Code, 2020:** a Works Committee may be required at 100 or more workers; a Grievance Redressal Committee is required from 20 workers; the architecture also includes the Trade Union Registrar, 51% negotiating union or 20%-support negotiating council, conciliation, Industrial Tribunal/National Industrial Tribunal, strike/lock-out rules and worker re-skilling fund.
- **Collective-bargaining test:** legal recognition of a union is not enough; identify representativeness, bargaining counterpart, information, settlement and enforcement.
- **OSHWC Code, 2020:** employer must control hazards, follow standards, maintain safe conditions, provide prescribed free health examinations and appointment letters, and charge workers nothing for safety measures.
- **OSHWC worker rights/institutions:** employees may raise safety concerns and imminent danger; National/State OSH Advisory Boards, Safety Committees/safety officers and Inspector-cum-Facilitators support standards, participation and enforcement.
- **Migrant/contract labour:** principal-employer welfare duties, suitable inter-State working conditions, accident reporting, establishment benefits and prescribed journey allowance require the exact statutory and delegated route.
- Commencement activates the statutory architecture; a detail expressly left to rules, standards, orders or notification must not be invented.

## Institutions and established routes

- EPFO: applicable provident fund, pension and deposit-linked insurance architecture.
- ESIC: contributory employee insurance and specified medical/cash benefits for covered insured persons.
- National Social Security Board: recommends, advises, monitors, reviews records and fund expenditure.
- State Unorganised Workers’ Boards: State-level scheme and delivery architecture.
- BOCW Welfare Boards: construction-worker route supported principally through cess; benefits and eligibility are State-specific.
- PM-SYM: voluntary contributory pension; 18–40 entry age, age-specific worker contribution, equal Union contribution, minimum ₹3,000 monthly pension after sixty, spouse-only family pension at fifty per cent under the stated condition.
- e-Shram: UAN/database/convergence route, not automatic EPFO, ESIC, BOCW or PM-SYM membership.

## Precise legal status at 3 October 2026

- **S.O. 5320(E):** the Industrial Relations Code commenced as a whole on **21 November 2025**.
- **S.O. 5321(E):** the OSHWC Code commenced as a whole on the same date.
- **S.O. 5322(E) + S.O. 4604(E):** most Code on Wages provisions are commenced, but section 42(10)–(11) and section 67(2)(s)–(t) remain uncommenced to the extent they relate to State Advisory Boards.
- **S.O. 1730(E):** Social Security section 142 commenced from **3 May 2021**.
- **S.O. 2060(E):** specified Employees’ Pension Scheme-related section 15/16, section 143 and section 164 portions commenced in 2023.
- **S.O. 5319(E), corrected by S.O. 5936(E):** the remaining Social Security provisions commenced from 21 November 2025, including the corrected balance of sections 15, 16 and 164. Together the three notification stages cover the Code’s provisions.
- Social Security (Central) Rules, 2026: **G.S.R. 344(E), 8 May 2026**, operative on publication.
- Rule 48: age-sixteen registration; aggregator data-sharing; 90 days with one aggregator or 120 cumulative days across aggregators in the prior financial year for relevant scheme eligibility; prescribed day-counting.
- Rule 49: collection authority, separate account, assessment, returns, interest and refund procedures.
- Section 114: possible gig/platform benefits; funding combinations; contribution range **1–2% annual turnover**, capped at **5% of amounts paid/payable to workers**.
- Exact rate and contribution-start date require notification. Forms XX/XXI retain the percentage “as notified”. Do not state an unverified fixed nationwide rate, universal collection or paid benefit.
- Central Rules do not prove uniform State readiness or individual benefit receipt.

## Portability, intersection and advanced gaps

- Portability has four layers: identity, contribution/accrual, benefit and remedy.
- ONORC supports food portability but is not labour insurance or pension.
- Migrants need destination-State claims and appeals; multi-platform workers need reliable attribution.
- Women may face unpaid-care, maternity, safety and contribution-continuity barriers.
- Caste-linked occupational segregation and subcontracting can combine high exposure with low bargaining and weak proof.
- Assisted/offline access, data correction and purpose limitation are social-security safeguards.
- Algorithmic management affects allocation, pricing, ratings, incentives and deactivation.
- Benefit, data, deactivation, classification and collective disputes need different remedies.

## Reform and answer spine

- Minimum publicly financed floor + contributory and occupation-specific depth.
- Common UAN/access spine, not one undifferentiated fund.
- Clear payer, eligible worker, contingency, claim, decision and appeal.
- Publish contribution receipts, claims, rejections, settlement times, benefit adequacy and disaggregated outcomes.
- Examine both statutory and economic incidence of levies.
- Preserve worker voice in boards and consequential platform decisions.
- **10 marks:** define + distinguish three links + one qualification.
- **15 marks:** institutions + finance + portability + exclusion + remedy.
- **20 marks:** constitutional frame + legal status + causal gaps + objections + feasible layered design.
