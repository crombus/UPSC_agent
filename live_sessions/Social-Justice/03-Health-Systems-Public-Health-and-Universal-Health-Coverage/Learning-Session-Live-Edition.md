# Health Systems, Public Health and Universal Health Coverage — Live Session Edition

## Roadmap

Imagine a pregnant woman with a fever, an older relative with diabetes and a child requiring emergency surgery in the same village. A hospital card alone will not deliver antenatal checks, medicines or timely referral. This session follows their care journeys before asking how India pays for, regulates and measures a health system.

## Lesson 1 — A hospital card is not universal health coverage

Progress: 1/9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Public health, care continuum and UHC cube

**2023 Prelims GS-I Q75:**

> Consider the following statements:
>
> **Statement-I:** India's public sector health care system largely focuses on curative care with limited preventive, promotive and rehabilitative care.
>
> **Statement-II:** Under India's decentralized approach to health care delivery, the States are primarily responsible for organizing health services.

> (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
>
> (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
>
> (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

**Demand:** assess each statement and the claimed explanatory relation independently.

```text
                    NEED CARE WITHOUT FINANCIAL HARDSHIP
                            /       |       \
                     WHO?          WHAT?       HOW PAID?
                  population       services     financial protection
                  excluded?       missing?      household burden?
                         \          |          /
                quality + equitable real access test all three

Population prevention → first contact → referral → recovery / palliation
```

*The three UHC dimensions interact: widening the insured list cannot compensate for unavailable services or unaffordable medicines.*

A household needs more than protection against one dramatic admission. **Public health** addresses risks across a population: immunisation, safe water, surveillance and health promotion reduce the chance of illness; **clinical care** diagnoses and treats an individual. They complement rather than replace one another. **Universal health coverage (UHC)** means people receive needed **promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative and palliative** services of adequate quality without financial hardship. WHO's familiar coverage cube asks **who**, **which services** and **how much of their cost is protected**. It is a conceptual instrument, not proof that a country has reached UHC. Equity asks which groups and districts remain outside each face; quality asks whether a recorded visit actually helped.

Consider an ASHA worker who notices repeated diarrhoea in a settlement. Individual rehydration treats cases, but safe water, sanitation, risk communication and surveillance act on the shared cause. If a district protects only hospital bills, families may still buy oral rehydration supplies, tests or transport. Conversely, an accessible preventive centre does not replace an emergency referral hospital. The example shows why a continuum is necessary; it does not prove a particular intervention's observed impact without local evidence.

**Reasoning chain:** disease risks are unevenly distributed → waiting for severe illness raises human and household costs → population prevention and first-contact care avert or detect problems → referral and recovery services handle needs that primary care cannot → pooled financing prevents illness from converting into debt. The strongest objection is that universal aspiration is too expensive in a resource-constrained state. A defensible reply is to prioritise effective primary care, essential medicines, functioning referrals and protection against catastrophic bills, then test quality and inclusion; calling an entitlement universal before delivering it would defeat the argument. Article 47 directs the state to improve public health, while the detailed constitutional-rights doctrine belongs elsewhere.

**UPSC application:** The GS-II social-sector-services clause invites a three-dimensional definition followed by an actual patient journey. **2023 Prelims GS-I Q75** tests the scope of India's public-sector care and state responsibility under decentralised delivery. Approach: distinguish curative from preventive/promotive/rehabilitative functions, then identify the state delivery role. Trap: **universal ≠ only free hospitalisation**.

**Revision:**

1. Public health acts on shared population risks; clinical care diagnoses and treats an individual.
2. UHC asks **who** can use care, **which services** are available and **how much financial hardship** remains.
3. Quality and equitable real access qualify all three UHC dimensions; nominal coverage is insufficient.
4. Promotive action supports healthier behaviour and conditions; preventive action averts disease or detects it early.
5. Curative care treats illness; rehabilitation restores function; palliative care relieves serious suffering.
6. Population prevention and individual treatment are complements, not substitutes.
7. A hospital card protects only a defined financing segment and cannot establish comprehensive service access.
8. Geography, discrimination, medicine availability and referral capacity can block formally covered care.
9. Article 47 supplies a public-health direction but does not itself prove that UHC has been delivered.
10. Target, registration, encounter, quality and outcome are distinct evidence stages.
11. The next learning problem is how a person moves through community, primary, secondary and tertiary care.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does issuing every household a hospital card not, by itself, establish UHC for a village?

**Model answer:** Hospital cover addresses only some treatment costs; access may still fail for prevention, outpatient medicines, rehabilitation, geography or quality. UHC also requires needed services for the population without financial hardship.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the size of the beneficiary list as a measure of usable, comprehensive care erases the service and quality dimensions.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain why population-level prevention and financial protection must be examined together in assessing universal health coverage in India.

**Model (approximately 113 words):** Universal health coverage combines access to needed services with protection from financial hardship; neither a hospital entitlement nor a prevention campaign alone meets both tests. In a district facing repeated diarrhoeal illness, safe water and surveillance address a shared risk, while first-contact treatment, referral and medicines support affected people. A household that pays repeatedly for transport or drugs can face hardship even if eligible for a hospital package. Equally, free drugs cannot substitute for a working referral in severe illness. India therefore needs a continuum through local care, district facilities and appropriate pooled financing. Assessment should track service availability, quality, excluded groups and household spending rather than infer success from a card count.

**Scoring guide (10):** UHC definition and three dimensions 3; population-versus-individual mechanism 3; concrete Indian care journey 2; qualified measurement conclusion 2.

**2018 GS-II Q7:**

> “Appropriate local community-level healthcare intervention is a prerequisite to achieve ‘Health for All’ in India. Explain.”
> **10 marks · 150 words**

**2021 GS-II Q6:**

> “Besides being a moral imperative of Welfare State, primary health structure is a necessary pre-condition for sustainable development. Analyze.”
> **10 marks · 150 words**

Both questions require a causal account of how accessible primary care supports health, welfare and sustainable development.

| Need or tier | Typical public node and task | When to refer |
|---|---|---|
| Community outreach | ASHA links households to screening, antenatal care and follow-up; local committees can identify barriers | Symptoms or risks needing an examination |
| Primary | Sub-centre/AAM and PHC: first contact, prevention, basic treatment, medicines and continuity | Complication or specialist assessment |
| Secondary | CHC, sub-district/district hospital: specialist and inpatient care | Advanced procedure beyond capacity |
| Tertiary | Medical college/referral hospital: super-specialist and complex intervention | Return to local follow-up after treatment |

*The rows describe functions, not a guarantee that each individual facility is staffed or offers every listed service.*

```text
Household → AAM/PHC → CHC/district → tertiary intervention
    ↑          ↑             ↑                |
    +----------+--- return plan / medicines --+
```

*A return referral reconnects specialised treatment with affordable continuing care near home.*

Suppose a rural patient first notices high blood pressure at a village screening. If the local centre can confirm the reading, provide medicine and review the patient, many visits need not become hospital admissions. A severe complication requires an equipped referral; after discharge, the same patient needs continuing local treatment. The transfer back is as important as transfer upward. A CHC is not simply a larger PHC: staff, diagnostics and inpatient capacity change the kinds of problems it can resolve. A district hospital may perform both secondary and some advanced services; the functional classification is more useful than rigidly assigning every procedure to a building.

**Community health** starts where families live: an Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA) mobilises and connects, an Auxiliary Nurse Midwife (ANM) supports essential community and facility services, and a trained clinician or community health officer provides services within the facility's scope. The **Alma-Ata primary-health-care approach** asks for more than a closer clinic: **equity** means actively reaching households deterred by distance, cost or discrimination; **community participation** means residents can identify barriers and help shape a response; **inter-sectoral action** joins health care to safe water, sanitation and nutrition; and **appropriate technology** uses methods suited to local need, skills and resources without compromising clinical safety. These are operating principles, not a claim that every clinic already implements them.

Consider a village with repeated childhood diarrhoea. An ASHA notices missed consultations; the **Village Health, Sanitation and Nutrition Committee (VHSNC)** brings residents and local services together to identify unsafe water and inaccessible clinic hours and to feed these concerns into village-level health planning and monitoring. The ANM and AAM/PHC team assess dehydration, provide suitable care and arrange referral for a severely ill child; water/sanitation authorities address the shared exposure, while food and nutrition support helps households facing repeated illness. Simple risk communication and usable first-contact treatments are appropriate technology when backed by trained judgement; neither a mobile app nor a cheap remedy substitutes for safe referral. A VHSNC can expose needs and follow up, but cannot order a hospital admission or independently repair a water system. Check which responsible department acted, whether the child received care and whether repeated cases declined before attributing success.

**Participation challenge:** a meeting may be dominated by well-connected residents or consume time without changing the service. **Reply:** seek voices of excluded households, publish the identified gap and assign named health and water-service duty-holders with follow-up dates and an accessible complaint route. This does not abolish power inequalities; it makes participation testable rather than ceremonial. Outreach cannot replace a specialist, and a referral slip cannot cure the absence of transport or a receiving team. Effective community-to-care linkage therefore needs staffing, stock, transport, communication, feedback and continuity, not simply a chain on paper. Urban informal settlements need analogous first contact and resident voice, though their service and mobility barriers differ.

**Objection:** Putting public resources into primary centres diverts funds from life-saving hospitals. **Reply:** Hospitals and prevention are complements: effective first contact handles manageable conditions earlier, while reliable secondary and tertiary capacity prevents avoidable deaths. The residual is the opportunity cost of staffing and maintaining all tiers; budget decisions must be tested against local burden and waiting times, not a slogan about prioritising either pillar.

**UPSC application:** **2018 GS-II Q7**: community health intervention for “Health for All,” directive *Explain*, 10 marks, 150 words. Answer route: show why equity, participation, coordinated water/sanitation/nutrition action and suitable local technology lead to outreach, usable first contact and referral; identify what a VHSNC can and cannot do. Do not substitute hospital insurance for community action. **2021 GS-II Q6**: primary health structure as a precondition for sustainable development, directive *Analyze*, 10 marks, 150 words. Route: prevention and continuity → fewer avoidable severe episodes → human capability/productivity, qualified by workforce and referral gaps. The 2023 Prelims Q75 linkage from Lesson 1 tests the decentralised state-delivery aspect.

**Revision:**

1. Community outreach identifies barriers and connects households; it does not replace clinical examination.
2. Sub-centres, AAMs and PHCs provide nearby first contact, prevention, basic treatment and continuity.
3. CHCs and district hospitals add specialist, diagnostic and inpatient capacity.
4. Tertiary facilities manage complex intervention; patients still need return follow-up near home.
5. Forward referral and return referral form one care loop rather than a one-way ladder.
6. Alma-Ata equity requires active reach to people excluded by distance, cost or discrimination.
7. Participation means affected residents influence priorities and follow-up, not merely attend a meeting.
8. Inter-sectoral action connects health services with responsible water, sanitation and nutrition agencies.
9. Appropriate technology must fit local resources while preserving clinical safety and referral.
10. ASHA connects, ANM supplies community-linked services and VHSNC identifies and monitors local gaps; none can perform every other's function.
11. A building count does not establish staffing, medicine stocks, transport, referral acceptance or clinical quality.
12. The 2018 and 2021 PYQs require a causal route from usable local care to Health for All and sustainable development, qualified by capacity gaps.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a district have a hospital and still fail a diabetes patient's need for continuing care?

**Model answer:** Diagnosis and an admission do not ensure routine medicines, monitoring, accessible primary visits and a return-referral plan. The continuum needs both acute capability and longitudinal first-contact care.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming specialist capacity substitutes for affordable everyday care mistakes an episodic service for a system.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse the role of referral and return follow-up in making India's tiered public-health infrastructure useful to a chronic-disease patient.

**Model (approximately 113 words):** The public-health hierarchy works only as a connected care path. An Ayushman Arogya Mandir can screen a patient for hypertension, arrange basic treatment and recognise warning signs; a CHC or district hospital can assess complications, and a tertiary hospital may undertake complex intervention. Referral must convey records and secure timely transport and reception. Once discharged, the patient needs affordable medicines and monitoring near home, otherwise the same emergency can recur. In a rural block, an ASHA may support follow-up but cannot replace stocked facilities or trained clinicians. The answer therefore measures treatment continuity and outcomes across tiers, not simply the number of centres and hospital beds. Staffing and geographic differences qualify expected gains.

**Scoring guide (10):** distinct tier functions 3; bidirectional mechanism 3; named patient example and ASHA's bounded role 2; capacity qualification 2.

```text
Article 47 public-health direction
      ↓
NHP 2017: policy goal, primary-care emphasis, public-spending aspiration
      ↓
Union support / standards + State budgets, facilities and personnel
      ↓
NHM = rural (NRHM) + urban (NUHM) platforms
      ↓
ASHA + ANM + trained clinical teams + free drugs/diagnostics
      ↓
actual access? → stock, staff, functioning care and grievance correction
```

*Each arrow has an implementation test; a policy target is neither a funded facility nor an outcome.*

**National Health Policy (NHP) 2017** is a policy, not a newly enacted statutory guarantee. Its goal of public health expenditure reaching **2.5% of GDP** is an aspiration: never write it as the achieved level or confuse an annual appropriation with GDP-wide actual spending. It stresses primary care, financial protection and wider access. The **Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW)** is the nodal Union ministry for NHP, NHM and both Ayushman Bharat pillars; the National Health Authority's PM-JAY role does not replace that wider policy responsibility. **National Health Mission (NHM)** combines the National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) and National Urban Health Mission (NUHM). It is a Centre-supported, state-executed system-strengthening umbrella: flexible funding supports personnel, infrastructure, community outreach and free medicine/diagnostic initiatives. The Pradhan Mantri Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission (PM-ABHIM) additionally addresses preparedness, public-health infrastructure, laboratories and critical-care needs; it is not a replacement name for NHM or for PM-JAY hospital cover.

Work backwards from a missed antenatal check. A national policy sets direction; a state must budget, recruit and supervise; a district must run the PHC; the ANM and ASHA must connect the woman with usable appointments; supplies and transport must exist. **Finance chain:** approved allocation → funds released → expenditure on staff/supplies → functioning service → use by the intended person → safer outcome. Every arrow can fail. Centre–state cost sharing and flexible funds allow local adaptation, but uneven state fiscal and administrative capacity produces differing results. Local monitoring and grievance correction help identify failure; neither a complaint portal nor an audit alone is a treatment.

**Objection:** More public money automatically improves health. **Reply:** adequate financing is necessary, but a vacancy, drug stock-out or inaccessible clinic blocks conversion to treatment. The reverse claim, that efficient management obviates public funding, also fails where there is no clinic or worker to manage. The defensible answer combines sufficient, traceable finance with workforce, free essential services, supervision and outcome measurement. A private provider can complement public supply, but cannot cancel the state's financing and regulatory responsibilities.

**UPSC application:** Use Article 47 briefly, without turning a health answer into constitutional-case doctrine. For a GS-II “discuss implementation” question distinguish policy, scheme, district facility and observed health outcome. The 2021 GS-II Q6 link applies directly: trace primary-care readiness to sustainable development, not simply to an NHM budget headline. Trap: NHM is **not rural-only**; NHP's 2.5% is **a target**, not a proved realised ratio.

**Revision:**

1. NHP 2017 is policy direction, not a newly enacted statutory health guarantee.
2. Its 2.5%-of-GDP public-health-expenditure objective is an aspiration, not verified attained spending.
3. MoHFW is the nodal Union ministry for NHP, NHM and both Ayushman Bharat pillars; NHM combines NRHM and NUHM and is not rural-only.
4. NHM supports personnel, outreach, infrastructure, free medicines and diagnostics through Centre-supported, state-executed arrangements.
5. PM-ABHIM strengthens preparedness, laboratories and health infrastructure; it does not purchase PM-JAY admissions.
6. Union finance and standards require state budgets, recruitment, procurement and district management to become services.
7. Allocation, release, expenditure, facility readiness, utilisation and outcome are six different stages.
8. A sanctioned post is not a worker present; a purchased diagnostic machine is not a completed test.
9. More money is necessary where capacity is absent, but conversion also depends on supervision, access and supplies.
10. Administrative efficiency cannot substitute for adequate funding where no workforce or facility exists.
11. Federal variation permits adaptation but can also reproduce unequal capacity across states and districts.
12. The next lesson tests the actual service menu and limits of the first-contact facility.

### Concept check

**Question:** If NHM funding rises but antenatal visits fall in a district, what would you investigate before declaring success or failure?

**Model answer:** Check whether funds were released and spent, posts filled, appointments and transport available, drugs/diagnostics stocked and women's actual visits and outcomes measured. A headline allocation cannot settle delivery performance.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating “funded” with “treated” skips multiple distinct operational steps.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Discuss how the National Health Policy and National Health Mission translate a public-health commitment into state-level service delivery. Why may the translation fail?

**Model (approximately 171 words):** National Health Policy 2017 sets a primary-care-led, financially protective direction, including an aspiration to raise public health expenditure to 2.5% of GDP; it is not proof that the aspiration has been met. The National Health Mission brings rural and urban public-system support together. Centre-supported flexible funding and state execution can finance ASHA and ANM outreach, staff, drugs and diagnostics. An antenatal-care journey makes the chain concrete: a district needs a staffed facility, a scheduled examination, usable tests and referral when risks are detected. Even a sanctioned post or a released instalment does not ensure that the woman is seen. State vacancies, poor transport, late procurement and inconvenient opening hours may interrupt care; local monitoring must reveal and correct these barriers. PM-ABHIM's infrastructure and laboratory investments may improve capacity but do not by themselves guarantee trained operators. Thus the state should track release and spending separately from availability, utilisation and maternal outcomes. The proper test of policy is reliable access for households across both rural and urban settings, not a policy-announcement count.

**Scoring guide (15):** policy and aspiration distinction 3; NHM rural/urban and division of duties 3; financed-to-delivered chain with example 5; bottlenecks and conditional outcome test 4.

## Lesson 4 — What a local wellness centre can and cannot do

| Familiar first-contact work | Added comprehensive-primary-care need | Limit requiring coordination |
|---|---|---|
Pregnancy, birth/child care and immunisation | Adolescent health and family planning | Emergency obstetric referral |
| Infectious illness and routine treatment | NCD screening and continuing diabetes/hypertension care | Complications needing specialist |
| Health promotion and free essential supplies | Mental, eye/ENT, oral, elderly/palliative and basic emergency services | Severe illness or advanced procedure |

*The groups illustrate the wider **12-package** comprehensive primary health care menu; the local facility's actual staff, drugs, diagnostics and service availability still have to be checked.*

```text
NCD risk → screen → confirm → supply medicines → review control
                   ↘ urgent danger sign → district referral
```

*A counted screening is the first step in a care cascade, not the final clinical outcome.*

Earlier first-contact work was often described mainly through reproductive and child services. **Comprehensive primary health care (CPHC)** expands that front door over a person's life: screening, prevention, basic curative care, follow-up and appropriate referral. Ayushman Bharat originally set a programme target of transforming **1.5 lakh** eligible sub-centres and PHCs into **Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs)**, now widely called **Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (AAMs)**. That historical scale target is not a current ceiling or a service-readiness measure: later operational counts can exceed it while package availability still varies. A Community Health Officer (CHO), also described as a mid-level health provider, works with the team at the appropriate centre; their scope does not magically become that of a district specialist. Free essential drugs, diagnostics and teleconsultation can make local care usable when stock, connectivity and clinical follow-through actually exist.

For example, an older person screens positive for hypertension. The useful sequence is repeated assessment → counselling and medicines → follow-up → referral if danger signs or complex illness arise. A counted screening is not a confirmed diagnosis, successful control or prevented stroke. Nor can a teleconsultation compensate for absent medication. The **2025–26 Economic Survey** table reports **1,82,944 AAMs operational as of 31 December 2025** and **1,51,116 with expanded services, excluding AAM-AYUSH, as of that reporting basis**. The **PIB factsheet dated 12 August 2026** reports **over 1.86 lakh functional AAMs**; these are differently dated operational measures, not proof of uniform service readiness.

**Objection:** Expanding the nominal service menu spreads thin staff even thinner. **Reply:** recruit and train teams, phase service availability honestly, verify drug stocks and referral connections, and measure continuity rather than merely centres renamed. Residual question: the adequacy of staffing and service availability varies by location; the national count does not settle it.

**UPSC application:** In a 10-mark answer contrasting Ayushman Bharat's pillars, AAM = primarily **provision of comprehensive first-contact services**, PM-JAY = **hospitalisation financial protection**. For the 2018 GS-II Q7 community-health demand, connect outreach, local screening, actual services and referral; do not treat an AAM announcement as a completed population-health outcome.

**Revision:**

1. HWC/AAM is a comprehensive first-contact service platform, not an insurance office.
2. CPHC extends primary care beyond reproductive and child health across the life course.
3. Its wider package includes NCD, mental, eye/ENT, oral, elderly, palliative and basic emergency needs.
4. Screening begins a care cascade; confirmation, treatment, medicine supply and follow-up complete it.
5. A CHO or mid-level health provider works within a defined team and scope; the role is not equivalent to a specialist.
6. Teleconsultation can extend advice but cannot replace physical examination, medicines or emergency referral where these are needed.
7. AAM provision and district-hospital capability are complementary.
8. The original 1.5-lakh HWC transformation target provides historical scale context; later operational counts are neither capped by it nor proof that all 12 packages are ready.
9. Expanded-service reporting must retain its denominator, exclusions and reference date.
10. Useful performance measures include staffing, drug stocks, completed referrals, continuity and disease control—not screenings alone.
11. The next lesson separates this provision pillar from PM-JAY's hospital-financing pillar.

### Concept check

**Question:** An AAM reports many hypertension screenings. What extra evidence would establish useful primary-care delivery?

**Model answer:** Check confirmation, medicine availability, regular follow-up, referral for complications and patient outcomes, with denominators and access by underserved groups. Screening volume is an activity count, not disease control.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling every screened patient successfully treated collapses the care cascade.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine how Ayushman Arogya Mandirs can expand the reach of primary health care without becoming miniature hospitals.

**Model (approximately 106 words):** Ayushman Arogya Mandirs widen the first-contact offer beyond reproductive and child care to include non-communicable disease screening, mental and geriatric care, medicines, diagnostics and follow-up. An older patient with high blood pressure benefits when a local team confirms the condition, maintains drug supply and arranges timely review; a specialist referral handles complex complications. The community health officer and outreach workers coordinate this chain but cannot perform every hospital intervention. National operational-centre counts measure reach only provisionally: an unstaffed centre or one without drugs cannot deliver its menu. A state should report expanded-service readiness, continuity and equitable utilisation alongside centre counts, retaining district hospitals for advanced treatment.

**Scoring guide (10):** service expansion 3; clear clinical limit and referral 2; named patient mechanism 3; readiness-versus-count qualification 2.

## Lesson 5 — Protect the admission, but identify the missing bills

Progress: 5/9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: PM-JAY eligibility, hospitalisation and 70-plus expansion

*Eligibility, a generated card, a hospitalisation and a paid claim are four different events.*

A family may need an expensive operation it cannot finance immediately. **Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB-PMJAY)** provides eligible families **up to ₹5 lakh per family per year** of publicly financed, cashless *secondary and tertiary hospitalisation* cover through empanelled public or private hospitals, subject to scheme packages and rules. Its original core eligibility follows notified deprivation and occupational criteria derived from the Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC); this is not general employee-contribution insurance. The separate **70-plus age route** allows every citizen aged 70 or above to enrol irrespective of income. Do not imply that every younger person automatically gains this age-based entitlement or that all older people's non-hospital bills disappear. The National Health Authority (NHA) steers PM-JAY nationally; State Health Agencies implement it and states may add their own cover.

Walk through a household's cancer treatment: confirm eligibility, obtain usable credentials, locate an empanelled facility that actually offers the package, obtain care without improper extra billing, then return for outpatient medicines and follow-up. The cover helps with eligible admissions; it is **not** a universal outpatient drug, transport or all-illness benefit. If the nearest empanelled hospital is far away, formal financial protection does not ensure spatial access. A private hospital's empanelment expands a network only if it treats patients fairly and claims are verified. A card issued is not a visit; authorisation/admission is not automatically a good outcome.

The Economic Survey 2025–26 reports **42.78 crore cards generated and 10.98 crore hospital admissions as of 12 January 2026**. A later PIB factsheet reports **over 45.5 crore cards issued as of 12 August 2026** and **12.69 crore hospitalisations as of 30 June 2026**. These are cumulative portal/service figures with different dates, not a matched control group proving impact; the hospitalisation amount reported is a claim-scale measure, not evidence that every bill was fully covered. The estimated eligible population, enrolled people, issued cards, admissions and successfully delivered quality services require distinct denominators.

**Objection:** A cashless card encourages unnecessary procedures and benefits only hospital towns. **Reply:** audited packages, rational rates, quality standards, grievance channels and stronger public capacity can curb perverse incentives; IT-based fraud detection can flag unusual or duplicate claim patterns for investigation, but cannot itself prove wrongdoing or replace clinical audit. Primary care handles conditions before admission. The remaining geographical inequality cannot be solved by claims software alone.

In marketisation answers, PM-JAY is only the financial-protection arm; NHM and public provision are indispensable. Trap: **₹5 lakh = annual family cover ceiling under scheme conditions**, not a guaranteed cash transfer to every Indian.

**Revision:**

1. PM-JAY finances covered secondary and tertiary hospitalisation for eligible people; it is not comprehensive primary care.
2. The annual ₹5 lakh figure is a family cover ceiling under scheme rules, not a cash transfer.
3. The core eligibility route uses notified SECC-derived deprivation and occupational categories.
4. The separate age route permits all citizens aged 70 or above to enrol irrespective of income.
5. Age-based eligibility does not make every outpatient medicine, journey or provider available free of cost.
6. The National Health Authority steers PM-JAY nationally; State Health Agencies implement it.
7. National Health Authority and National Health Accounts are unrelated meanings of “NHA” and must not be confused.

*Transition changes the dominant pattern of illness and death; it does not mean that every place moves through identical stages or that infections disappear when NCDs rise.*

An **epidemiological transition** is a long-run shift in the relative pattern of disease and mortality as survival, age structure, living conditions and exposures change. Falling mortality from some infections and maternal-child causes can increase the share of people living long enough to develop chronic conditions; urbanisation, diet, inactivity, tobacco, pollution and occupational risks can also change exposure. India has not completed a neat replacement of one burden by another. It faces an **overlap or double burden**: communicable diseases including tuberculosis persist while non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease and cancers grow. The transition can differ by state, district, income, gender and age, so a national cause-of-death share cannot be assigned to every locality.

The system consequence is explicit. Infection control needs surveillance, vaccination where applicable, diagnosis, treatment and outbreak capacity; chronic disease needs risk reduction, repeated screening linked to confirmation, regular medicines and long-term follow-up. Maternal and child health remain central because the transition is neither uniform nor irreversible. A hospital-centred response misses prevention and continuity, while an infection-only system misses ageing and chronic disability. The Economic Survey 2025–26, citing cause-of-death evidence, describes this coexistence; do not mistake an undated single percentage for every district's disease mix. A maternal-care visit, childhood immunisation, TB detection, mental-health follow-up and elderly chronic treatment therefore test different service capabilities.

Child undernutrition also tests this care continuum. Inadequate diet can impair growth; repeated diarrhoeal disease linked to unsafe water can worsen nutrient loss and appetite; antenatal anaemia can increase maternal risk. An anganwadi worker under **Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0** may monitor growth and link a child to nutrition support, while the AAM/PHC assesses repeated illness and arranges appropriate treatment or referral. Neither can alone repair unsafe drinking water or a food-access failure; the responsible agencies must act together. ⚠️ **Inference:** a registered nutrition beneficiary or distributed supplement alone does not prove a child's intake, disease exposure or growth improved. Equity asks which girls, informal-settlement households or remote communities miss both food and clinical care. Inter-sector coordination works only if named agencies correct real delivery failures; do not treat malnutrition as a hospital-only condition.

For a pregnant woman, an **ASHA** may mobilise early contact and an **ANM** supports appropriate maternal services. **Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY)** supports institutional delivery among eligible pregnant women; it is not a general sick-infant facility construction programme. **Pradhan Mantri Surakshit Matritva Abhiyan (PMSMA)** offers a minimum package of free **antenatal** checks in the second or third trimester at designated government facilities on the ninth day of the month, including identifying high-risk pregnancies and planning further care. Private obstetric, radiology and physician specialists may **volunteer at public facilities** to supplement government clinicians; this does not privatise the entitlement or mean all participating providers are compulsory volunteers. Follow-up and safe **post-delivery care** are necessary in the broader maternal-care continuum, but PMSMA's minimum-package promise concerns antenatal services; do not merge it with JSY's delivery-linked purpose or represent it as a blanket post-delivery package. A safe institutional delivery still depends on skilled personnel and emergency referral. For an older person, recurrent medicines, physical access and community follow-up matter even where PM-JAY's separate 70-plus hospitalisation route applies.

Mental-health needs are not solved by screening alone. The **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017** establishes a rights-oriented legal framework; **Tele-MANAS** expands remote access to mental-health support. Neither replaces trained district teams, continuity and accessible escalation when crisis care is required. For TB, surveillance and diagnosis must connect to treatment adherence; for an NCD, screening must connect to control. Community participation, clean water, nutrition and sanitation influence risks, but their generic delivery machinery is not the entire health-sector answer.

*The financing problem feeds back into care-seeking; a reimbursed admission does not erase the outpatient loop.*

**Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE)** is money households pay directly for health care. The **National Health Accounts** estimate its *share of total health expenditure*, which is not the fraction of individual families who pay or their average rupee bill. The Basic series is **47.1% in 2019–20**, **44.4% in 2020–21** and **39.4% in 2021–22**. The fall is welcome, but the last dated estimate still shows substantial household financing. **Catastrophic spending** means a sufficiently large share of a household's resources is consumed by health costs, under a stated study-specific threshold; do not assert a national catastrophic-incidence figure without defining its measure and year. Direct spending can cause borrowing or forgone care even if hospital benefits exist.

**Marketisation** here means increased dependence on privately sold care where price, information and access are uneven. Private participation is not inherently harmful: it can add capacity and specialised treatment. The problem is dependence without affordable public alternatives, accountable rates or clinical standards. A patient offered an unnecessary procedure cannot reliably judge its necessity. Supplier incentives and poor information can raise cost, but one should not assert that all private treatment is unnecessary or that insurance invariably inflates claims.

**Core financing architecture:** **revenue collection** raises money through taxes, contributions or direct household payment → **risk pooling** spreads uncertain treatment costs across people and time → **strategic purchasing** decides which services and providers pooled funds will pay, at what rates and conditions → **service provision** determines whether the patient actually receives care. Insurance without staffed primary care can pool hospital risk while leaving prevention, outpatient medicines and repeated OOPE unresolved.

**Core regulatory architecture:** the **Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010**, where applicable, or equivalent state law can support registration and standards; **standard treatment guidelines (STGs)** help test clinical necessity; and **National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA)** controls apply to relevant notified medicines or devices, not every hospital bill. Claim audit, transparent rates and an accessible grievance route must make these tools enforceable; quality accreditation can test facility processes, while social audit or structured community review can expose access and billing failures. Neither accreditation nor social audit replaces clinical investigation, and uneven state adoption and implementation remain a limit.

**State response, in causal order:** (1) invest in staffed AAMs/PHCs and free essentials to reduce preventable and recurrent bills; (2) strengthen CHCs, district hospitals and public-health preparedness through NHM and relevant infrastructure support; (3) use pooled PM-JAY funds to purchase eligible admissions from accountable public or empanelled private providers; (4) apply relevant Clinical Establishments Act/state standards, STGs, NPPA controls, claim audit, quality accreditation, social audit and grievance remedies to quality, necessity, prices and wrongful billing; (5) compare measured household burden with service quality and inequities. These are complementary, not a choice between “all hospitals” and “all insurance.” Article 47 and NHP 2017 support the public mandate without establishing that any target is already met.

**Objection:** Insurance protects the poor more quickly than slow public construction. **Reply:** it may protect against eligible admission costs, but outpatient medicines, absent hospitals and prevention still need accessible public provision. The counter-objection that private capacity must never be used also ignores places where accountable empanelment helps; regulate and test it. **Residual:** district supply gaps can keep both strategies from reaching remote households.

*The core financing chain from Lesson 7 is recapped here before the optional analysis of pool fragmentation and provider-payment incentives.*

Lesson 7 established **revenue collection → risk pooling → strategic purchasing → service provision** and the named regulatory safeguards as core answer architecture. The optional depth begins with the design choices inside that chain. Tax-financed NHM and PM-JAY differ from contribution-linked **social health insurance**. India does not have a single comprehensive pool: PM-JAY, state schemes, the contribution-linked **Employees' State Insurance Scheme (ESIS)** and the **Central Government Health Scheme (CGHS)** coexist with different eligible populations and financing arrangements. Fragmentation can complicate portability, benefit design and household understanding, although plural arrangements can allow state adaptation.

Payment matters. **Fee-for-service** pays each billed activity and can reward volume; **capitation** pays per person registered and can encourage preventive management but also skimping; a **case-based package/diagnosis-related grouping** fixes a rate for a treated episode and can restrain unit costs but may prompt selection or upcoding. PM-JAY uses defined packages: describing *all* payments as identical across providers would be misleading. Consider a common scheduled procedure: a predictable package rate may shield a patient from an open-ended charge, but the provider may select easier cases or add prohibited fees. Audits, standard treatment guidelines (STGs), transparent packages and patient grievances are safeguards. IT-based fraud detection can flag improbable combinations, duplicate claims or unusual coding for review; it is a screening tool, not proof of fraud, and still requires clinical verification and fair procedure. Each safeguard also requires enforcement and may introduce administrative costs.

**Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010** provides a framework for registering/regulating clinical establishments where applicable; adoption and implementation vary across states/UTs, and equivalent state law may operate. It is not uniformly enforced across India. Price regulation may include specific devices under the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA); this is not a claim that NPPA sets every hospital bill. Standard treatment guidelines help assess necessity and quality, quality accreditation can assess institutional processes, and social audit can reveal patient-facing access or billing failures; none enforces itself or substitutes for an independent clinical or financial audit and appeal. The strongest objection to stringent rates is that poorly calibrated payments may drive providers from underserved areas. The reply is to assess costs, geography and quality transparently while building public capacity; unresolved rural supply can require a different delivery response rather than another portal.

10. Clinical-establishment regulation varies with state adoption and implementation; it is not uniform nationwide.
11. NPPA controls apply to relevant notified products, not every component of a hospital bill.
12. Fair rates must consider cost, geography and provider participation without sacrificing clinical necessity or patient protection.
13. Private participation is not identical to unregulated privatisation, and purchasing never removes the state's equity duty.
14. The next question is whether reported numbers prove that the financed service improved health or reduced hardship.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why might a fixed hospital-package price protect a patient yet still require an audit?

**Model answer:** A fixed rate limits an admissible episode's open-ended price, but providers could select easier patients, code a costlier package or demand extra payment. Audit tests necessity, coding, quality and patient bills.

**Misconception to avoid:** A pre-set price solves neither inappropriate care nor the geographic absence of a provider.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine how public purchasing can enlarge access to private hospital capacity without abandoning the state's quality and equity duties.

**Model (approximately 171 words):** Strategic purchasing uses publicly pooled funds to pay providers for defined services while specifying rates and accountability conditions. Under AB-PMJAY, an empanelled private hospital can extend covered admission capacity where a public facility lacks a service. A clearly specified case package can restrain an open-ended bill, but may encourage avoidance of complex patients, upcoding or extra charging. The state should verify clinical necessity with standard treatment guidance, audit claims, use proportionate IT-based detection to flag suspicious patterns, assess quality and offer an accessible patient grievance route. Accreditation and social audit can add institutional and patient-facing evidence but do not by themselves establish clinical correctness or fraud. Rates need review against actual costs and regional capacity; if a poorly priced package causes withdrawal from remote districts, nominal empanelment will not mean accessible treatment. The Clinical Establishments Act, where adopted and implemented, or equivalent state rules can support registration and standards, but legal coverage is not uniform. Parallel investment in district hospitals, NHM staff and local medicines remains necessary. The state remains guarantor of equitable, safe access even when it purchases an episode rather than directly performing it.

### 10 marks — 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish a universal health-coverage commitment from universal health-insurance registration, using an Indian care example.

**Model (approximately 117 words):** Insurance registration is evidence of a potential financing route, not universal health coverage. UHC asks whether all people obtain needed promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative and palliative care of adequate quality without financial hardship. In an Indian block, a household may possess a PM-JAY card and obtain cover for an eligible hospital admission, yet still travel far to a specialist and purchase monthly hypertension medicines because its nearby centre has stock-outs. A staffed Ayushman Arogya Mandir, referral to a functioning hospital and free essential supplies complement hospital protection. Monitor who is excluded, which services are actually delivered and household spending; count enrolled people, completed admissions and clinical outcomes separately. A scheme can contribute to UHC without constituting it.

**Scoring guide (10):** definition and dimensions 3; insurance-specific limit 2; named linked example 3; measurement and qualified conclusion 2.

### 15 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Analyse how primary-care investment and hospital purchasing could jointly reduce health-related deprivation in a state with high travel costs.

**Model (approximately 178 words):** Primary-care provision and hospital purchasing protect different points of the same pathway. Under the National Health Mission a state can staff urban and rural first-contact teams, maintain free medicines and diagnostics and improve timely referral. An Ayushman Arogya Mandir can screen and manage hypertension near home, reducing recurring visits to a distant hospital, but it cannot handle a complex emergency. PM-JAY can pay for an eligible secondary or tertiary admission at an empanelled facility; effective purchasing requires a fair package, clinical audit and an accessible grievance route so patients are not charged outside the rules. In remote blocks, however, a hospital card is of limited value if no accessible provider exists or if transport and wages remain a household burden. The state should strengthen district hospitals, referral transport and return follow-up and disaggregate results by geography and disadvantage. Distinguish funds allocated and released from drug availability, enrolment from admission and a paid claim from clinical improvement. Assess household hardship using a dated, clearly defined measure; do not infer that either intervention alone caused a fall in state poverty.

**Scoring guide (15):** differentiated primary/hospital mechanisms 4; geography and patient journey 4; enforceable purchasing safeguards 3; measurement, attribution and qualified conclusion 4.

### 20 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Critically evaluate an integrated strategy to improve equitable health access, service quality and financial protection in India's federal system.

**Model (approximately 230 words):** Universal health coverage requires population reach, needed services of sufficient quality and protection against financial hardship. Policy intent alone does not supply these conditions. National Health Policy 2017's public-expenditure aspiration guides action, while states must translate NHM support into staffed rural and urban facilities, free essentials and a referral network. Ayushman Arogya Mandirs can provide preventive and continuing care; CHCs and district hospitals manage more complex needs; PM-JAY shields eligible hospital episodes, with a separate age-based route for citizens aged 70 and over. For a person with chronic disease, medicines and follow-up near home matter alongside protection for an expensive complication. The state must also address public-health risks such as infectious disease and the growing NCD burden. Private empanelment can add capacity, but weak oversight risks extra charging, unnecessary procedures and unequal geographic access. Apply clinically defensible packages, enforceable quality standards and remedies while strengthening public provision. Federal diversity permits local adaptation yet leaves unequal staffing and finances; allocations and portal registrations cannot be read as delivered care. National Health Accounts record a 39.4% household OOPE share for 2021–22, a dated national aggregate rather than proof of a scheme's effect on every household. Measure staffing, stocks, completed referrals, treatment quality, equity and hardship by district and population; test alternative explanations before crediting one intervention. The practical goal is not more certificates but reliable, affordable care across the entire patient journey.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### UHC: reconstruct the answer

- UHC = **who** has access, **which** promotive/preventive/curative/rehabilitative/palliative services of sufficient quality, **how much** financial hardship remains. Add distribution by population and region; insurance enrolment alone proves none of the remaining conditions.
- Public-health intervention prevents or controls population risk; clinical care addresses an individual. Epidemiological transition means the relative disease-and-mortality pattern changes with survival, age structure, living conditions and exposure. India's transition is overlapping rather than a completed replacement: communicable and maternal-child burdens persist while NCDs, ageing and chronic disability rise, so use infection control, prevention and continuing treatment together.
- A complete 10-mark answer uses the UHC cube, one patient journey, two Ayushman Bharat pillars and a measured limit. A 15/20-mark answer adds NHM, federal delivery, finance/OOPE, regulation and outcome testing.

### The patient journey and the public system

- Alma-Ata community-primary-care route: **equity** finds the excluded; **participation** lets affected residents shape services; **inter-sectoral action** joins safe water, sanitation and nutrition to care; **appropriate technology** fits local capacity without abandoning safety. An ASHA notices barriers, an ANM supplies community-linked services and a VHSNC identifies and monitors village health/sanitation/nutrition gaps; named agencies and clinicians must actually correct them. A meeting ≠ inclusive influence or treatment.
- Sub-centre/AAM and PHC deliver first contact; CHC/district facilities provide specialist/inpatient service; tertiary centres handle complex intervention. Require referral **and** return follow-up, skilled teams, medicine, diagnostics and transport.
- Article 47 directs improvement of public health. **NHP 2017 is policy**, not a statute; its **2.5% of GDP** public-expenditure objective is aspirational, not a statement of attained spending.
- **MoHFW** is the nodal Union ministry for NHP, NHM and both Ayushman Bharat pillars. **NHM = NRHM + NUHM**: Centre-supported, state-executed support for rural/urban personnel, outreach, free essentials and infrastructure. **PM-ABHIM** supports preparedness, laboratories and broader public-health capacity; neither is PM-JAY hospital insurance.
- Centre allocation → release → expenditure → facility readiness → actual service → health/financial outcome. Never promote an earlier step into a later one.

### Two Ayushman Bharat pillars and life-course care

- **HWC/AAM** began with a **1.5-lakh transformation target** and upgrades first-contact sub-centres/PHCs toward **12 comprehensive primary health care service packages**. The historical target is not a present ceiling or readiness measure. CHO/MLHP is not a specialist. NCD, mental, eye/ENT, oral, elderly/palliative and basic emergency needs extend beyond maternal/child services; verify local implementation.
- **AB-PMJAY** provides publicly financed cashless covered **secondary/tertiary hospitalisation**, annual **₹5 lakh/family** ceiling for eligible families. Core notified SECC-derived categories ≠ all younger citizens. Distinct 70-plus route: **all citizens aged at least 70**, irrespective of income, can enrol; eligibility ≠ a delivered episode. NHA administers nationally; State Health Agencies implement.
- JSY supports eligible institutional delivery. PMSMA's minimum free antenatal package applies in the second/third trimester at designated public facilities; private specialists can volunteer there. Necessary post-delivery care belongs to the wider continuum, not an invented PMSMA minimum-package guarantee. Maternal safety also requires skilled attendance and emergency referral. Older-person, mental-health, TB and chronic care require repeated local contacts; Mental Healthcare Act 2017 and Tele-MANAS support rights/access but not a replacement for local teams.

### Financing, regulation and honest measurement

- National Health Accounts: household OOPE as a **share of total health expenditure** = **47.1% (2019–20), 44.4% (2020–21), 39.4% (2021–22)**. Not household prevalence, an undated current-year measure or proof of causal PM-JAY impact.
- Marketisation risk = inaccessible/weak public alternatives plus price and information imbalance; private empanelment may help when packages, clinical quality, audit and grievance routes work. Catastrophic spending needs a named study, year and threshold before citing incidence.
- Core financing and regulation: taxes/contributions/OOPE raise revenue → pools share risk → purchasers pay public/empanelled providers → services must reach patients. Applicable Clinical Establishments Act/state rules, STGs, relevant NPPA controls, quality accreditation, social audit, claim audit and grievance remedies are complementary safeguards. Optional depth adds named fragmentation across PM-JAY, state schemes, ESIS and CGHS; fee-for-service can reward volume, capitation can encourage under-service, case packages can encourage selection/upcoding, and IT-based fraud detection only flags claims for verification.
- Dated illustration: Economic Survey 2025–26 table XI.5 reports **1,82,944 operational AAMs as of 31 December 2025**, **1,51,116 expanded-service AAMs excluding AAM-AYUSH**, **42.78 crore PM-JAY cards and 10.98 crore admissions as of 12 January 2026**. PIB 12 August 2026 reports **over 1.86 lakh functional AAMs and over 45.5 crore cards**, while its **12.69 crore hospitalisations** figure is **as of 30 June 2026**. Different dated denominators: avoid claiming uniform quality or unique beneficiaries.
- NFHS-5 reference period **2019–21**: a result must retain survey round, indicator, denominator and geographic scope. No NFHS figure in this session is represented as a fresh 2026 survey result. ABHA digital identifier ≠ PM-JAY entitlement; e-Sanjeevani consultations ≠ completed physical care. Kerala's stronger health indicators and decentralised investment coexist with an officially recognised OOPE challenge, so the case supports neither a uniformly low-OOPE claim nor single-factor causation.
- **Answer spine:** public failure → patient-level causal chain → provision + protection + regulation → strongest implementation objection and reply → measurable, equity-sensitive conclusion. Relevant PYQs: 2018 community/Alma-Ata/VHSNC; 2020 maternal–geriatric; 2021 primary–development; 2024 state response to marketisation; 2026 Q7 malnutrition (integrate food access, nutrition programmes, equity and welfare delivery with the disease and care pathway). The 2023 and 2024 objective questions test public-sector scope, JSY and PMSMA.
