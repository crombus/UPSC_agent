# Social Justice, Inclusion and the Welfare State — Live Session Edition

## Roadmap

An equal rule does not necessarily create equal freedom. This session follows the journey from an equal legal promise to an actual, dignified opportunity to live well. Complete the core before reading the optional extensions.

Each lesson has one concept check and a distinct original Mains question. Five relevant Mains PYQs are approached but not solved here.

## Lesson 1 — Equality of rules is not equality of life chances

Progress: 1/8 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Formal equality and three dimensions of justice

Book context: queried — *Indian Polity by M Laxmikant*, PDF p. 215, on the welfare-state aim of Directive Principles
CA search: "official India social justice equality inclusion welfare state April September 2026"
CA found: None directly pertinent in the last six months
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Starting problem | Justice question | Suitable intervention | What must still be checked? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A child cannot afford school materials | **Distributive:** who gets resources and opportunity? | Service or scholarship | Could the child actually learn? |
| A worker is forced into a degrading occupation | **Corrective:** how is a specific harm remedied? | Prohibition, accountability, rehabilitation | Has the wrong stopped and livelihood changed? |
| That worker is treated as socially inferior | **Recognitive:** whose dignity, identity and voice are denied? | Anti-discrimination and meaningful participation | Can the person participate without stigma? |

*The same life can require all three kinds of justice; the columns are tests, not mutually exclusive populations.*

Imagine two students offered the same open school seat. One can reach a functioning classroom; the other faces disability-related access barriers and caste discrimination. **Formal equality** offers an identical rule; **substantive equality** asks whether differing starting conditions require support. The social-justice question is whether people can use an opportunity on dignified terms, not merely whether it exists on paper. This illustration does not establish how common either barrier is.

✅ **Grounded distinctions:** Distributive justice concerns allocation of resources, opportunities and services; corrective justice responds to a particular wrong through such means as compensation, penalty, restoration or rehabilitation; recognitive justice addresses denied standing through anti-discrimination, representation and respect. They are analytically separate: providing a rehabilitation grant does not by itself punish a perpetrator or end stigma. Conversely, recognition without schooling, assets or livelihood can leave material deprivation untouched. A group-specific measure can advance substantive equality without implying that members of that group lack equal citizenship.

**A core answer needs more than these three labels.** ✅ Rawls's **fair equality of opportunity** asks whether positions are genuinely accessible to people with similar abilities and willingness despite differences in social background; formally open school or employment entry is not enough when prior disadvantage controls preparation. It is distinct from his **difference principle**, which evaluates whether social and economic inequalities benefit the least advantaged; that further test is developed in Lesson 7. ✅ Ambedkar warned of the contradiction between political equality and continuing social and economic inequality: equal voting status cannot by itself undo inherited deprivation. ✅ Nancy Fraser's **redistribution–recognition–representation** lens makes the third issue explicit: those facing stigma need an effective voice in deciding the remedy. ⚠️ In an Indian school example, a scholarship can reduce cost (redistribution), respectful admission can combat stigma (recognition), and affected families' participation can expose inaccessible teaching (representation). Participation alone does not prove learning; the learner's effective opportunity remains the test.

⚠️ **Causal argument:** (1) Unequal starting conditions make identical rules unequally usable. (2) Redistribution lowers a material barrier. (3) Remedy addresses an inflicted harm; recognition tackles stigma and voice. (4) Evaluate whether all three jointly change lived opportunity. **Objection:** differentiated help violates equality. **Reply:** treating substantively unequal positions as if they were alike can preserve inherited barriers; nevertheless, a proposed correction must be justified, lawful and assessed for who actually benefits. Polity treats the detailed constitutional equality and reservation doctrine; here the question is whether a correction reaches people.

**UPSC use:** The GS-II vulnerable-sections and welfare-performance clause asks not only *what was allocated* but *whose access and dignity changed*. The 2024 GS-I Q18 affirmative-action demand tests this promise–benefit gap. Do not write “social justice = redistribution” or treat reservation as its sole instrument. A 10-marker can define the three tests, apply them to one Indian example and distinguish outcome from allocation.

**Revision:** Same rule ≠ same opportunity; Rawls's fair equality of opportunity asks whether social background blocks genuinely open chances. Ambedkar contrasts political equality with unequal social/economic conditions. Distributive = allocation; corrective = remedy for wrong; recognitive = dignity and voice; Fraser adds political representation to redistribution and recognition. A prohibition alone may not create alternative livelihood. Measure access, remedy and standing separately. Better treatment must be justified, not presumed effective merely because it is differentiated.

### Concept check

**Question:** A former manual scavenger receives a one-time grant, but unsafe assignments and social stigma continue. Has a three-dimensional justice response succeeded?

**Model answer:** No. A grant may address distribution; continuing unsafe work defeats corrective protection and continuing stigma defeats recognition. Check enforcement, sustained alternative livelihood and dignified participation rather than equating payment with justice.

**Misconception to avoid:** A transfer is neither evidence that the prohibited harm stopped nor proof that social status changed.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish distributive, corrective and recognitive justice. Examine why all three are needed to address manual scavenging.

**Model (approximately 114 words):** Distributive justice asks who receives resources; corrective justice remedies a wrong; recognitive justice secures equal dignity and voice. In the case of manual scavenging, alternative livelihood and rehabilitation support redistribute opportunity, but cannot alone stop unsafe employment. The statutory prohibition must be enforced and violations addressed: that is the corrective task. Ending stigma and enabling affected workers to participate in decisions is recognitive work; otherwise new jobs may remain inaccessible. Article 46's concern for weaker sections offers a constitutional direction, not proof of a delivered outcome. The approaches reinforce each other, but a grant, a prosecution or a public statement alone is insufficient. Evaluate cessation of harmful work, sustained livelihood and dignified access together.

**Scoring guide (10):** precise three-way definitions 3; named Indian example with distinct mechanisms 3; causal explanation and implementation qualification 2; integrated verdict 2.

## Lesson 2 — Inclusion is not assimilation; welfare is not alms

Progress: 2/8 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Inclusive citizenship and the welfare-state idea

Book context: queried — *Indian Polity by M Laxmikant*, PDF p. 215, contrasts welfare and police-state purposes; p. 96 reproduces Preamble aims
CA search: "official India inclusive citizenship welfare state social inclusion April September 2026"
CA found: None directly pertinent in the last six months
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
Equal membership
       ├─ Not assimilation: a tribal child's language and identity need not vanish
       ├─ Not charity: the rights-holder may demand what law actually guarantees
       └─ Not just enrolment: participation, quality and voice must be usable
                    ↓
WELFARE STATE: public responsibility for minimum living conditions
       ├─ health and education services
       ├─ social security, housing and poverty relief
       └─ recognition and correction alongside material provision
```

*Inclusion is membership on equal, usable terms; the welfare state is a vehicle, not the final measure of success.*

A person invited into a school that cannot accommodate her disability has been counted, not fully included. **Inclusion** means effective access and participation without having to surrender identity; **assimilation** instead demands fitting the dominant norm. In India a person in a remote hamlet may hold the same legal entitlement as a city resident yet face a very different journey to a clinic. The analogy isolates the access problem; it cannot prove that every remote clinic fails.

| Idea | Decisive question | School example |
|---|---|---|
| **Equality** | Are people recognised as equal citizens under the same rights and rules? | The admission rule does not exclude a child because of caste, sex, religion or disability |
| **Equity** | What proportionate support is justified to overcome a relevant barrier? | Accessible material, language support or a scholarship addresses a demonstrated obstacle |
| **Inclusion** | Can the learner participate, influence decisions and learn with dignity? | Admission, usable teaching, voice and freedom from stigma operate together |

*Equality establishes equal status; equity calibrates support; inclusion tests actual participation. They are connected, not synonyms.*

✅ **Grounded foundation:** The Preamble commits India to social, economic and political justice; its “socialist” description and Part IV's Directive Principles give a welfare-state orientation. A **welfare state** accepts public responsibility for a minimum standard of living through services and protection, rather than merely maintaining order. A Directive Principle directs state policy; its text does not automatically grant an individual a directly enforceable claim. ✅ The Preamble's “socialist” term was added by the Forty-second Amendment (1976): do not imply it appeared in the original wording.

**Agency completes the inclusion test.** A person has agency when she can make meaningful choices, communicate priorities, refuse degrading conditions and act on decisions rather than appearing only as a passive recipient. Consultation without accessible information or any influence over the result may record attendance while leaving agency unchanged. Conversely, agency does not mean withdrawing public support and telling disadvantaged people to solve structural barriers alone.

⚠️ **Argument:** (1) Unregulated family or market provision may leave people without basic services or protection. (2) Public duties, finance and institutions can establish a minimum floor. (3) Equal civic standing requires people to receive it without humiliation. **Objection:** public provision crowds out local initiative or is costly. **Reply:** the welfare state need not deliver every service directly: it must still define duty, fund responsibly, supervise quality and secure remedy. The residual challenge is that broad promise can outrun capacity; the existence of a department never establishes functioning provision.

**UPSC use:** For GS-II welfare schemes, separate **value** (justice), **vehicle** (state responsibility) and **test** (effective access). An inclusion answer should consider the barriers faced by women, SCs, STs, persons with disabilities and migrants without treating those groups as interchangeable. Avoid “welfare is charity” and “a scheme announced is inclusion achieved”.

**Revision:**

1. Equality secures equal civic status, rights and non-discriminatory rules.
2. Equity supplies justified, proportionate support where a relevant barrier makes identical treatment ineffective.
3. Inclusion asks whether access becomes participation, voice, learning and dignity.
4. Inclusion is not assimilation: equal membership does not require surrender of language, identity or difference.
5. Enrolment or registration is an entry point, not proof of meaningful participation.
6. Agency means the practical power to choose, speak, refuse degrading conditions and influence decisions.
7. Welfare is a public responsibility for a minimum living floor, not benevolence bestowed on passive recipients.
8. The state may use public, private or community delivery, but remains responsible for standards, finance and correction.
9. The Preamble and Part IV orient policy; enforceability depends on the particular constitutional or statutory basis.
10. Coverage, usability, quality, voice and dignity must be tested separately.

### Concept check

**Question:** A tribal student is admitted to school but is unable to understand instruction and is told not to use her home language. What is missing beyond formal access?

**Model answer:** She needs usable learning and respectful participation. Enrolment provides an entry point, while assimilation pressure and language barriers prevent equal capability and recognitive inclusion; examine teaching arrangements before claiming any particular intervention will work.

**Misconception to avoid:** Admission alone proves neither meaningful inclusion nor learning.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Discuss why a welfare state requires inclusive public services rather than merely declaring equal eligibility.

**Model (approximately 210 words):** Equal eligibility is a necessary floor but not the whole welfare state. India's Preamble pledges social and economic justice, while Part IV directs public action; neither statement alone delivers a classroom or health visit. Equality recognises the person as an equal citizen under a common rule; equity asks what proportionate support removes a relevant barrier; inclusion tests whether the person can participate with voice and dignity. A remote tribal household may face travel and language barriers even when formally eligible. An accessible school for a child with a disability must support actual learning, not merely count admission. A service provided amid stigma also fails equal standing. The RTE's statutory schooling claim shows how public responsibility can move beyond goodwill, but law needs trained staff, financing and a usable complaint route. Agency matters because recipients should be able to understand choices, contest denial and influence how a service is delivered rather than be treated as passive objects. Private or community partners can help with provision; the state remains responsible for standards and correction. Fiscal limits are real, so prioritise an enforceable minimum, monitor who is missing and verify outcomes rather than treating spending or registration as sufficient. Inclusion means people can use institutions as equal citizens without surrendering identity or agency.

**Scoring guide (15):** legal–policy distinction 3; reach, quality and dignity mechanisms 6; named Indian anchors and qualification 4; reasoned conclusion 2.

## Lesson 3 — From constitutional purpose to lived outcome

Book context: queried — *Indian Polity by M Laxmikant*, PDF p. 215: Directive Principles seek economic and social democracy and are non-justiciable
CA search: "official India constitutional welfare rights social justice implementation April September 2026"

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
PREAMBLE: justice — social / economic / political
              │
PART III: Arts 14–17 — equality, non-discrimination,
          enabling special provisions, equal public opportunity,
          abolition of untouchability
              │
PART IV: Arts 38, 39, 41–43, 46–47: state policy directions
              │ legal translation where applicable
              ▼
Law / protective duty / entitlement → eligibility and remedy
              │
Budget + Union/state policy + institutional responsibilities
              │
Frontline: anganwadi | school | primary health centre
              │
Access → quality → actual functionings / capabilities
              │
Complaint + survey + social audit → correction of failure
```

*The chain is only as strong as its weakest link; an official act and a lived result occupy different rungs.*

If a school exists but lacks accessible instruction, the constitutional purpose is clear and perhaps a statute applies; neither fact establishes that a learner can study. Ask **who holds the claim**, **who bears the duty**, **which institution can act**, **what has been funded**, **how the service is delivered**, **where a complaint goes**, and **whether the intended outcome occurs**. Those questions explain a law–life gap without assuming that law itself is useless.

✅ **Fundamental-rights equality framework:** Article 14 protects equality before law and equal protection of the laws. Article 15 prohibits specified discrimination and also contains enabling clauses for special provisions, including for women and children, socially and educationally backward classes, SCs, STs and EWS within the constitutional text. Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment and contains enabling reservation clauses; it is not a general guarantee of a job. Article 17 abolishes untouchability and makes its practice punishable. Together, Articles 14–17 show that constitutional equality combines equal status, non-discrimination and carefully authorised corrective measures rather than demanding identical treatment in every circumstance.

✅ **Welfare-direction map:** Part IV includes Articles 38 and 39 on social order and policy principles; Article 41 on assistance in specified circumstances subject to the state's economic capacity and development; Articles 42–43 on humane work conditions and living wage; Article 46 on educational and economic interests of SCs, STs and other weaker sections; and Article 47 on nutrition and public health. These are policy directives, not all identical enforceable claims. Legislation can make particular duties actionable: the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013, and Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act (RTE), 2009, are statutory examples. The enforceable age scope of the RTE is **6–14 years**; it does not itself create the same entitlement for all younger children.

⚠️ **Affirmative-action caution:** Special provisions and reservation are constitutionally enabled forms of substantive equality, not automatic commands to adopt every proposed classification or proof that benefits reached the intended group. The exact clause, eligible class, field of operation and applicable judicial limits matter. Social-justice analysis then asks whether the measure overcame the identified barrier without reproducing exclusion or stigma.

⚠️ **Reasoning and objection:** An enforceable norm without financing, accessible institutions or an avenue for redress may yield a paper right. But the objection “laws never help” is too strong: a stated duty and a remedy change what a claimant can demand. Commissions such as the NCW, NCM and NCPCR may receive concerns, investigate and recommend within their mandates; they are not substitutes for school staff, officials or courts and must not be collectively described as possessing uniform judicial enforcement powers. Administrative tools such as transfers, monitoring and grievance systems should be judged by how they serve the particular entitlement.

**UPSC use:** The 2024 GS-I Q18 demand about why affirmative-action benefits do not fully reach underprivileged groups is approached by following promise → instrument → identification/access → enforcement → outcome. The 2026 GS-II Q17 rights-based-welfare demand adds fragmented institutions and inadequate public investment: distinguish an enforceable duty from the financing and coordination needed to realise it, then test outcomes. Do not claim a Directive Principle is directly enforceable like every Fundamental Right or treat a portal registration as completed rehabilitation. For GS-II, identify duty-holder, finance, delivery and review.

**Revision:**

1. The Preamble states the constitutional destination of social, economic and political justice.
2. Article 14 combines equality before law with equal protection of the laws.
3. Article 15 joins non-discrimination with enabling special-provision clauses; Article 16 does the same for equality of opportunity and authorised reservation in public employment.
4. Article 17 abolishes untouchability and reaches the prohibited practice itself, including conduct by private persons.
5. Affirmative action is a constitutional method of substantive equality, not a departure from equality.
6. An enabling clause permits a measure; it does not prove that every classification is valid or every intended beneficiary received the benefit.
7. Part IV supplies policy directions through Articles 38, 39, 41–43, 46–47; these provisions do not all create identical individual claims.
8. A statute specifies a claim and duty where enacted and applicable; a scheme supplies implementation design.
9. Budget, responsible institution, frontline access, grievance and outcome are separate links in the welfare chain.
10. A survey or administrative count can reveal a gap but requires a dated indicator and contextual interpretation.

### Concept check

**Question:** A department cites Article 47 and launches a nutrition portal. Does that establish an enforceable food benefit and improved nutrition?

**Model answer:** No. Article 47 is a policy directive. A specified statutory entitlement, such as an applicable NFSA claim, has a different legal status. Portal creation is an administrative step; verify eligible access, service quality, a remedy and nutrition outcomes separately.

**Misconception to avoid:** Constitutional aspiration, statutory right, executive launch and achieved health outcome are not interchangeable.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse how India's welfare-state commitments can fail between constitutional direction and a household's experience. Suggest a defensible test of implementation.

**Model (approximately 220 words):** India's Preamble sets the goal of social and economic justice. Articles 14–17 supply an equality framework: equal protection and non-arbitrariness, non-discrimination with enabling special provisions, equality of opportunity with authorised reservation, and abolition of untouchability. Part IV, including Articles 38, 39 and 47, directs action on social order, resources and nutrition. These provisions have different legal functions; none by itself proves household access. The NFSA demonstrates the statutory route to a food claim, but an eligible family may still face identification or delivery failures. Likewise, an RTE-age child may be admitted yet not learn in an inaccessible classroom: the legal right and the educational functioning differ. A responsible test follows the chain: identify the claimant and duty-holder; check the exact legal basis, eligibility decisions and funding; observe delivery and quality; provide review of refusal; and test outcomes using appropriately dated evidence. Feedback from a social audit can expose missed people but does not automatically cure the problem; officials must act on findings. Commissions can draw attention to marginalised voices, not replace frontline provision. Affirmative action should therefore be assessed both as constitutionally authorised correction and as an implementation process: who was identified, heard, reached and enabled to participate? The solution is to correct the specific failing link rather than announce another scheme or mistake registration counts for capability gains.

**Scoring guide (15):** differentiated constitutional/statutory status 3; two linked Indian service examples 4; complete causal chain and measurable tests 6; qualification and correction 2.

## Lesson 4 — A claim needs a duty and an effective remedy

Progress: 4/8 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Rights-based welfare and protective instruments

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — *Indian Polity by M Laxmikant*, PDF p. 215, for non-justiciable Directive Principles and the legal-status distinction
CA search: "official India procedural justice welfare grievance rights social sector April September 2026"
CA found: None directly pertinent in the last six months
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Level | What a person can ask | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| Constitutional purpose / Directive Principle | Why should the state act? | A personal claim enforceable solely from every DPSP |
| Statutory entitlement | What must a defined duty-holder provide under this law? | That every eligible person already receives it |
| Protective statute | How will a specified harm be prohibited, investigated or remedied? | That a prohibition automatically ends the harm |
| Executive programme | Which services are offered, funded and administered? | That a policy announcement has the legal force of an Act |
| Verified lived result | Was the service received and useful without indignity? | That an average captures every person's experience |

*Move downward only with evidence: authority, scope, commencement, delivery and outcome each require their own check.*

Imagine two households told they will receive food support. One can point to the law's defined entitlement and a grievance route; the other has heard only a speech. Both may still be hungry, but the first can formulate a legal claim under applicable provisions. **Rights-based welfare** regards a person as a claim-holder and identifies a corresponding public duty and remedy where provided. **Charity-based welfare** rests on benevolence and discretion. Neither label guarantees a well-stocked shop or a responsive school.

✅ **Indian anchors:** NFSA 2013 is an entitlement statute, while RTE 2009 creates a schooling right for ages 6–14; the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, addresses disability rights. Protective legislation includes the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act and POCSO. Their coverage, procedures and remedies differ; do not assert that all confer the same individual benefit or that all mechanisms are self-executing. The manual-scavenging prohibition and rehabilitation response makes the distinction vivid: a ban needs enforcement; rehabilitation needs viable alternatives; dignity needs non-discriminatory work and voice.

✅ **Procedural justice:** A just outcome also requires a fair route to it. In welfare administration this ordinarily means accessible notice of the rule, a real opportunity to present one's case, reasons for exclusion or denial, decision by the competent authority, and timely review or correction. The exact procedure depends on the governing law; these elements are an evaluative framework, not a claim that every statute uses an identical appeal.

⚠️ **Strongest challenge:** declaring an entitlement can create a “paper right” when public finance, staff, proof of eligibility or review are inadequate. **Reply:** specify the accountable authority, make reasons for denial reviewable under the applicable rules, and test correction; abandoning the entitlement merely removes the claimant's lever. The fiscal constraint affects implementation choices but does not make a enacted legal duty identical to a discretionary gift. No particular remedy should be asserted without checking the operative law.

**UPSC use:** An examiner may ask whether rights-based welfare improves justice. Show *what it changes legally*, then test identification, enforcement, delivery, quality and lived benefit. The 2026 GS-II Q17 question on rights-based welfare, non-integrated governance and minimal public investment can be approached without answering it here: separate rights, fragmented duty chains, adequacy of investment and a way to test whether delivery changed. Avoid inventing judicial remedies for a mere scheme and avoid conflating enactment with commencement. The 2024 GS-I Q18 demand can be illustrated through the difference between affirmative-action law and real access, without reproducing reservation doctrine.

**Revision:**

1. Rights-based welfare identifies a claimant, a duty-holder and the content of the legal claim.
2. Charity or discretion depends on benevolence; an entitlement changes what a person may demand.
3. A Directive Principle alone is not equivalent to a specified statutory entitlement.
4. NFSA and RTE illustrate statutory concretisation, but eligibility and scope remain law-specific.
5. Protective statutes prohibit or remedy harms; they are not interchangeable transfer programmes.
6. Procedural justice asks whether notice, hearing, reasons, competent decision and review are genuinely usable.
7. A fair procedure is valuable even before the final outcome because it treats the claimant as an agent rather than an administrative object.
8. Procedure without timely correction can become ritual; outcome without fair procedure can conceal arbitrary exclusion.
9. No judicial, administrative or commission remedy should be asserted without checking the operative legal provision.
10. Announced ≠ enacted ≠ commenced ≠ funded ≠ delivered ≠ achieved.

### Concept check

**Question:** A state says it has “ensured the right” to a service after publishing a scheme circular. What would you verify before accepting either the legal or outcome claim?

**Model answer:** Check whether a competent legal instrument creates a specified claim, its operative status, eligibility, duty-holder and review route; then inspect finance, actual access, quality and outcomes. A circular may administer a programme without itself establishing a statutory right.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling a measure “rights-based” cannot by itself convert an executive offer into legislation.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine the difference between rights-based and discretionary welfare with reference to two Indian social-sector measures.

**Model (approximately 140 words):** Rights-based welfare identifies a claimant, duty-holder and legal entitlement; discretionary welfare may offer support without the same statutory claim. NFSA 2013 specifies food-security entitlements, moving an eligible claimant beyond a purely benevolent offer, but exclusion at identification or delivery can still defeat the right in practice. RTE 2009 specifies free and compulsory schooling for children aged 6–14, yet enrolment without usable teaching cannot deliver educational capability. Unlike these statutes, a newly announced programme is not automatically enforceable or operational. Procedural justice requires that a claimant can know the rule, present relevant facts, receive reasons from the competent authority and obtain timely review under the applicable framework. Such procedure treats the person as an agent, but it is hollow without staff, finance and correction. Thus legal status improves the ability to demand provision, while just welfare requires a fair process, accessible service and observed outcome.

**Scoring guide (10):** accurate legal distinction 3; two named examples with scope and analytical use 4; delivery limitation 2; precise verdict 1.

## Lesson 5 — What can a person actually do and be?

Progress: 5/8 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Functionings, capabilities and the growth–development gap

```text
Economic growth / income / budget
          │    (resources, not a complete life)
          ▼
Food | clinic | school | income security
          │    conversion factors:
          ├── disability and health ───┐
          ├── caste / gender exclusion ─┼─► real opportunities
          └── distance and service quality ┘    = CAPABILITIES
                                                    │ choice exercised
                                                    ▼
                                          achieved states = FUNCTIONINGS
                                          (nourished, healthy, literate)
```

*The same inputs can yield different freedoms, and a particular achieved outcome is not identical to the freedom to choose it.*

Two children receive a textbook. If one has an accessible teacher and the other has neither accessible material nor a functioning classroom, counting distributed books misses the difference. **Functionings** are achieved doings and beings: being educated or adequately nourished. **Capabilities** are the real opportunities to achieve such states, not just goods or income. A child who cannot attend a usable school has a constrained capability even if a seat technically exists. Do not infer the full freedom set from a single observed outcome: a person might choose differently when offered a real alternative.

✅ **Theory and application:** Amartya Sen's approach asks what people are actually able to do and be, rather than using income or reported utility alone as the test of advantage. Health, education and income feature in HDI; a multidimensional-poverty index detects several kinds of deprivation. These indices are *proxies*, not complete measures of every person's freedom. The 2019 GS-II Q6 growth-versus-human-development demand and 2023 GS-I Q16 lagging-human-development demand both require a causal explanation, not a denunciation of growth.

✅ **Do not collapse distribution into deprivation:** A **Gini coefficient** summarises inequality in the distribution of a stated variable such as income or consumption; it does not, by itself, measure all poverty, who owns assets or the dimensions of deprivation. A **multidimensional-poverty index** counts selected simultaneous deprivations using its stated indicators and cutoff; it cannot by itself tell you how income or productive assets are distributed among everyone. **HDI** combines average health, education and income dimensions but masks within-country differences. ⚠️ If growth raises average income while some groups still lack clinics or school access, compare dated and appropriately disaggregated deprivation, distribution and service evidence before assigning a cause. An asset-ownership claim requires separate asset evidence, not a generic Gini figure.

⚠️ **Causal sequence:** (1) Growth can enlarge tax resources and jobs. (2) Distribution, public spending and implementation determine whose school or clinic improves. (3) Social barriers and poor quality limit conversion into health and learning. (4) Group-sensitive, dated evidence tests whether gains were shared. **Objection:** incomes and observed results are easier to measure than counterfactual freedoms. **Reply:** use both observed health/learning outcomes and evidence of access and choice, disclose the gap between a proxy and a capability. Income matters; it is not sufficient. Aggregate HDI can conceal caste, gender or regional gaps, and no precise ranking is needed to explain the mechanism.

**UPSC use:** For a “why has development lagged?” prompt, acknowledge growth as an enabling resource, identify at least two conversion failures, test distribution and quality, then qualify the verdict. A ration transfer may increase food access, but nutrition also depends on health, diet and household dynamics.

**Revision:** Resource ≠ service ≠ functioning ≠ capability. HDI = health, education, income dimensions; average ≠ inclusive distribution. MPI tracks selected deprivations; Gini describes distribution of a specified variable, not poverty or asset ownership by itself. Trace income → public provision → conversion conditions → achieved outcomes and freedom. Growth is useful but not sufficient; observed functionings alone do not enumerate choices.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two villages have identical education expenditure per child, but one has inaccessible classrooms. What would the capability lens ask beyond an expenditure comparison?

**Model answer:** Whether children can reach, use and learn in the classroom, including those facing disability barriers. Compare meaningful opportunity and observed learning, not just equal funds; account for other influences on learning before assigning all causation to expenditure.

**Misconception to avoid:** A rupee spent is an input, not an observed functioning or a real choice.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse why economic growth need not produce commensurate human development in India. Use the capability approach and qualify your conclusion.

**Model (approximately 163 words):** Growth increases the resources available to households and the state, but does not automatically expand people's real freedoms. Sen's capability approach distinguishes an input such as income or a classroom from the opportunity to be nourished or educated. The RTE illustrates the gap: an eligible child may enter school yet struggle to learn when teaching or accessibility is inadequate. NFSA creates a food claim, but delivery and health conditions affect whether food access becomes adequate nutrition. Article 47 directs state attention to nutrition and public health; it is a policy direction, not proof of an achieved health gain. HDI brings health and education into development assessment beyond income, although its average can hide caste, gender and spatial disparities. Growth may finance stronger services and jobs; it is therefore enabling rather than irrelevant. The defensible test combines dated distributional and outcome evidence with service quality, remedy for exclusion and people's effective choices, rather than treating either GDP or programme spending as the final verdict.

| Design | Advantage | Failure mode | Corrective safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal basic service | Avoids means-test exclusion and stigma | Expensive; unequal use can persist | Ensure reach and quality, especially in underserved areas |

*Neither design wins by definition: choose a defensible floor, then diagnose which errors and barriers remain.*

A public clinic open to everyone may reduce stigma but still be unreachable for a disabled resident; a targeted travel support can close that specific gap if identification and delivery work. **Universalism** supplies an entitlement to a broad population; **targeting** limits additional resources to defined beneficiaries. Inclusion error means someone ineligible receives the targeted benefit; exclusion error means someone eligible misses it. Universal access avoids means-test exclusion but can still have practical exclusion, and “universal = no inclusion error” is meaningful only after stating the eligibility rule.

✅ **Protection floor:** ILO Recommendation No. 202 (2012) is a **non-binding Recommendation**, not an ILO Convention ratified into Indian law. It calls for access to essential health care and basic income security for children, persons of working age unable to earn sufficient income and older persons; national guarantees can be delivered through different arrangements. It is a benchmark for a floor, not proof of universal Indian receipt. ✅ **Dated anchor:** The ILO's **25 May 2026** account reports **64.3%** of India's population protected by *at least one* social-protection benefit under SDG indicator 1.3.1 methodology. It describes a workshop on data mapping, metadata and remaining gaps; the percentage neither proves comprehensive protection nor establishes the full R202 floor for each person. Do not compare it to an older figure without matching methods, population and reporting dates.

⚠️ **Decision chain:** identify who is missing → specify the common service floor → add focused support for unequal conversion conditions → give applicants a review route → measure access, quality and outcomes. NFSA is a statutory food entitlement with defined coverage rather than a universal identical benefit for every resident; RTE supplies an age-defined educational entitlement, not universal schooling for all ages. A universal health service plus scholarships is a possible hybrid design illustration, not a claim that all residents presently obtain uniform care.

**Objection:** targeting saves resources. **Reply:** only if eligibility is identified accurately and administration does not make the poorest pay higher documentation costs. **Counter-objection:** universalism wastes scarce funds. **Reply:** broad access can reduce exclusion and stigma but must still be financed and its actual use disaggregated. The trade-off is conditional, not a moral ranking. **UPSC use:** For GS-II scheme performance, write both errors, one India-grounded hybrid and a measurable safeguard. For 2026 GS-II Q17, trace what happens when food, health and education duties sit in disconnected institutions and investment is insufficient: map a responsible authority, finance, coordination and review, without assuming that a unified portal alone solves the problem. The 2025 GS-II Q16 resource-ownership/poverty demand also requires attention to underlying assets, not simply cleaner beneficiary lists.

**Revision:** Eligibility defines both errors; universalism does not guarantee equal use. Targeting can focus resources and generate proof burdens; hybrid designs combine a floor with top-ups. R202 ≠ binding Convention; its health and life-cycle income guarantees are a normative reference. “At least one benefit” ≠ full security. Verify coverage, quality, dignity and capability rather than relying on counts.

**Core answer-writing bridge:** You can answer without the optional theories that follow. For **10 marks**, define the tested kind of justice or right, state a qualified thesis, then connect two or three anchors: Rawls's *fair equality of opportunity* identifies background barriers, Article 46 supplies India's policy direction, and a specified entitlement such as RTE tests whether access can be claimed. For **15 marks**, explain why growth or nominal coverage has not converted into learning or health: Sen's capabilities and HDI's health–education–income dimensions illuminate the input–outcome gap, while Ambedkar's political-versus-social/economic equality tension identifies structural barriers; concede that growth can finance better provision. For **20 marks**, triangulate Rawls's fair opportunity, Sen's real freedom, Fraser's redistribution–recognition–representation and Ambedkar's equality warning. Contrast HDI averages and MPI deprivation with a Gini measure of a specified variable's **inequality**, never using Gini as a direct count of poor households or proof of who owns assets. Link these lenses to NFSA, RTE and a group-specific dignity/remedy case; finish with a conditional investment, review and outcome test rather than a list of schemes. For **2026 GS-II Q17**, this means mapping the rights-holder and duty-holder, fragmented delivery, public financing and capability outcome before suggesting coordination. These are exam-building routes, not a solved PYQ.

### Concept check

**Question:** A programme removes duplicate beneficiary names and announces that poverty exclusion is solved. What two separate tests refute that inference?

**Model answer:** Check whether eligible people were wrongly removed or never listed (exclusion error), and whether retained benefits actually improved access and capability. Fewer duplicates addresses possible inclusion error, not the separate entitlement and outcome questions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Cleaning an administrative register does not by itself establish either universal coverage or reduced poverty.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Critically examine universalism and targeting in India's welfare architecture. Propose a hybrid that protects both scarce resources and equal citizenship.

**Model (approximately 199 words):** Universalism offers a common floor, lowers the stigma of being screened and avoids exclusion caused by an inaccurate means test; it can nevertheless cost more and may be used disproportionately by those already well served. Targeting concentrates additional help on disadvantage but requires credible eligibility rules, accessible documents and appeal: an eligible person omitted is an exclusion error, while an ineligible recipient creates an inclusion error. These are distinct failures. India's RTE provides a legal age-defined schooling claim; a disabled pupil may still need extra accessibility support to turn enrolment into learning. NFSA provides a defined food entitlement, but receipt cannot be equated automatically with nutritional capability. ILO Recommendation 202's essential-health and life-cycle income floor supplies an international non-binding benchmark, not an existing domestic entitlement. A defensible hybrid funds usable basic services broadly, adds justified support for conversion barriers, assigns an accountable duty-holder and makes mistaken exclusions reviewable. Compare dated access and outcome evidence by group, and investigate the quality of services alongside beneficiary counts. This approach is not costless: finance and administrative capacity constrain the level of support. The right choice depends on error costs and people's actual freedoms, not on declaring either universalism or targeting always superior.

| Lens | What is being judged? | One valuable question | Blind spot if used alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rawls's fair equality of opportunity (core) | Social-background barriers to genuinely open opportunities | Can equally able and motivated people compete fairly? | Fair entry does not settle the distribution of subsequent gains |

*These lenses explain different failures; this optional comparison does not replace the three core justice tests.*

Suppose a scholarship is fairly distributed but a student cannot safely travel to school. Rawls highlights the condition of the least advantaged; Sen asks what freedom the student really has; Nussbaum asks whether central capabilities including bodily integrity and education-related thought reach a threshold; Fraser asks whether the student has voice and status in deciding policy. The questions complement rather than collapse into one score.

✅ **Precise enrichment:** Rawls's fair-opportunity test asks whether chances are genuinely open; his further **difference principle** permits social and economic inequalities only when arranged to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. Do not reduce either to a licence for any unequal subsidy. Sen leaves the list of valuable capabilities relatively open to public reasoning. Nussbaum specifies **ten central capabilities**: life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one's environment. This is a threshold proposal, not a list of Indian statutory benefits. Fraser's representation criterion can be deepened here by asking whether affected people can *contest the rule itself*, beyond merely being present at a consultation.

⚠️ **Horizontal equity** treats comparably placed people alike; **vertical equity** recognises that unequal starting conditions can justify differentiated support. Their application needs a defensible criterion and measured effect: not every targeting rule is fair merely because it classifies. Ambedkar's warning about political equality coexisting with social and economic inequality underscores the need to link voting equality to lived access; avoid attributing an invented quotation. **Objection:** prescriptive lists impose disputed values, while open-ended freedoms evade measurement. **Reply:** publicly defend thresholds, retain people's voice and triangulate observed services with outcomes; neither a list nor one index exhausts justice.

⚠️ **Intersectionality as a diagnostic:** A person's barriers may arise through interacting locations such as caste, gender, disability, class, tribe or migration status. The point is not to add labels mechanically; it is to identify how one rule or institution produces a distinct combined disadvantage. A disabled woman from a remote tribal community may face mobility, language, gendered safety and administrative barriers that cannot be diagnosed by examining only one category. Policy still needs evidence and a workable remedy; intersectionality does not justify assuming that every member has the same experience.

**UPSC use:** In a 20-marker, use Rawls for distributive fairness, Sen for conversion, Nussbaum for a minimum threshold and Fraser for status/voice only when each advances a different paragraph. The 2023 GS-I Q16 growth–human-development demand can draw on capability and distribution without requiring this optional theory.

**Revision:**

1. Rawls's fair equality of opportunity tests whether social background blocks genuinely open chances.
2. Rawls's difference principle separately asks whether inequalities benefit the least advantaged.
3. Sen tests real freedom to achieve valued functionings, not resources alone.
4. Nussbaum proposes ten central capabilities as threshold claims rather than an Indian benefit list.
5. Fraser separates redistribution, recognition and representation; voice must be able to affect the rule.
6. Horizontal equity treats comparably placed people alike on the relevant criterion.
7. Vertical equity permits justified differentiation where unequal barriers require proportionate support.
8. Intersectionality studies how social locations and institutions interact to create a distinct disadvantage.
9. Intersectionality is not mechanical label addition and does not erase differences within a group.
10. Agency asks whether affected people can choose, contest and shape the response, not merely receive it.
11. Open-ended capability lists and fixed thresholds each face measurement or value-choice objections.
12. These theories diagnose different failures; none by itself proves that a programme succeeded.

## 10 marks — Diagnose the missing link

**Question (150 words):** Distinguish a constitutional welfare commitment from an effective entitlement. Illustrate with a social-sector example.

## 15 marks — Account for a paradox

**Question (250 words):** Analyse how expansion of welfare coverage may coexist with persistent social exclusion. Explain what an outcome-oriented welfare state should do.

**Model (approximately 202 words):** Coverage can rise even when particular people remain unable to use a benefit. The ILO's 25 May 2026 account reports 64.3 per cent of India's population receiving at least one social-protection benefit under SDG 1.3.1. It is a dated one-benefit measure, not proof of comprehensive or adequate protection. An eligible NFSA household may be omitted by an identification rule; an RTE-age child may enter school yet face inaccessible teaching. Sen's distinction explains why receipt is not the same as the freedom to be healthy or educated. Stigma and lack of voice may also persist: rehabilitation of manual scavengers requires cessation of unsafe work and dignified livelihood, not just payment. A welfare state should preserve a usable common floor, offer justified additional help for unequal conversion barriers, provide accessible review of exclusion and assess service quality and group-wise functionings with dated evidence. Distinguish MPI's measured deprivations from a Gini measure of inequality in a specified variable; neither alone proves who owns assets. Broadening coverage is valuable; scarce finance and limited state capacity require priorities. Neither a rising headline nor an isolated anecdote proves that all households are better or worse off. Social justice is judged by the chain from claim to dignified capability.

**Why this earns marks (15):** clear paradox and dated ILO scope 3; four distinct linked examples/anchors 5; identification, quality and status mechanisms 4; feasible qualified response 3.

## 20 marks — Synthesis and critical judgment

**Question (250 words):** “A welfare state must distribute resources, correct harms and secure recognition, but each ambition is incomplete unless it expands real capabilities.” Critically examine with Indian examples and an account of fiscal and measurement limits.

**Model (approximately 233 words):** Social justice combines fair distribution, remedy for specific harms and equal dignity; Sen's capability approach tests whether people gain real freedom to do and be, not merely goods. Article 46 directs the state towards the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, providing a constitutional direction but not proof of delivered benefit. The RTE's 6–14 entitlement may increase access; inaccessible classrooms or weak learning would prevent its conversion into educational capability. NFSA's food claim strengthens a person's position relative to a discretionary offer, yet identification and delivery failures can still exclude eligible families. In responding to manual scavenging, a prohibition and enforcement address harm, rehabilitation creates alternative livelihood, and freedom from stigma secures status: no one dimension can substitute for the others. Rawls's fair equality of opportunity asks whether social background obstructs genuinely open chances; Ambedkar's political-versus-social equality warning highlights the same structural tension in India. Fraser adds representation to redistribution and recognition: those affected need voice. These are diagnostics, not programme evaluations by themselves. ILO Recommendation 202 specifies a non-binding health and income-security floor, while its May 2026 one-benefit coverage statistic cannot measure full fulfilment. A sound policy combines usable minimum services, targeted correction and review, and group-wise outcome evidence. Funding and administrative capacity constrain pace; surveys and administrative proxies cannot observe every genuine option. A qualified verdict compares effective access, choice and dignity, not just a budget or a rights declaration.

**Why this earns marks (20):** thesis with three justice dimensions and capability 4; at least five India/ILO/theory anchors linked to analysis 8; fiscal/measurement counterarguments 4; credible hybrid and conditional verdict 4.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Justice's three separate jobs

- **Formal equality**: identical rule; **substantive equality**: usable opportunity despite different starting barriers. A correction requires justification and evaluation.
- **Equality–equity–inclusion:** equality secures equal status and rights; equity supplies proportionate support for a demonstrated barrier; inclusion tests participation, voice, dignity and usable access.
- **Core thinker anchors:** Rawls's *fair equality of opportunity* asks whether background blocks genuinely open chances (not his separate difference principle). Ambedkar's political equality versus persisting social/economic inequality is an India-specific institutional challenge. Fraser's redistribution–recognition–representation asks who receives, whose standing counts and who helps set the rule.
- **Distributive**: fair allocation of services, resources and opportunity; **corrective**: stop and remedy specific harm; **recognitive**: equal identity, dignity and voice. None substitutes for the others.
- **Procedural justice**: accessible notice, opportunity to present relevant facts, reasons from the competent authority and timely review under the applicable law. Fair procedure must lead to correction rather than become a ritual.
- Manual scavenging stress test: prohibition and effective action against wrongs, viable rehabilitation and livelihood, dignity and non-stigmatised participation. The three dimensions must converge; do not assume either a ban or a transfer alone worked.
- Inclusion is not assimilation; enrolment is not participation. **Agency** is the practical ability to choose, contest, refuse degrading conditions and influence decisions.
- **Intersectionality** tests how barriers such as caste, gender, disability, tribe, class or migration status interact through institutions; it is not mechanical label addition or proof that every group member shares one experience.

## The Indian welfare-state chain

- Preamble: justice — social, economic and political; “socialist” was added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**.
- **Articles 14–17:** equality before law and equal protection; non-discrimination with enabling special provisions; equality of opportunity with enabling reservation clauses in public employment; abolition of untouchability. Affirmative action is a constitutionally authorised route to substantive equality, subject to the exact clause and applicable limits.
- Part IV's DPSP direct the state; they are not individually enforceable in the same way as every Fundamental Right.
- **Articles 38, 39, 41–43, 46–47**: social order, distributive policy, assistance (with Article 41 capacity qualification), work conditions, weaker-section education/economic interests, nutrition and public health.
- Trace constitutional goal → law/protection → eligibility and duty → finance → delivery institution → grievance → access and quality → functioning/capability. A budget, circular, portal, registration or disposal statistic cannot jump rungs.
- NFSA 2013 and RTE 2009 illustrate statutory concretisation; RTE's compulsory-education claim covers **ages 6–14**. NEP 2020's early-childhood focus includes **3–6**, a policy focus rather than an automatic extension of the same statutory age right.
- Protective-law examples: SC/ST atrocities law, RPwD 2016, POCSO and manual-scavenging prohibition. They have different coverage and remedies: read their applicable provisions before asserting a specific legal route.
- NCW, NCM, NCPCR and NHRC have distinct mandates to receive concerns or investigate and advise; a recommendation or recorded disposal does not itself equal enforced redress.

## Capability and development: the causal spine

- Sen: **functionings** = achieved doings/beings (nourished, literate); **capabilities** = real freedoms to achieve them. Goods, income, public spending, access, quality, achieved state and freedom are distinct.
- GDP growth can enlarge resources; distribution, public provision, social access and service quality determine capability conversion. Growth is enabling, not sufficient or irrelevant.
- HDI combines health, education and income dimensions; MPI captures specified overlapping deprivations; a Gini measure concerns inequality in a named variable, often income or consumption, not automatically asset ownership or a poverty headcount. A national average or an observed functioning cannot enumerate every freedom or group difference.
- Answer growth–human-development questions with a *why* chain: resources → unequal conversion conditions → health/learning outcomes → dated group-sensitive evidence → qualified judgment.

## Policy choice, error and evidence

- Universalism lowers means-test stigma and exclusion but needs finance and equal physical access; targeting focuses resources but can impose documentation burdens and misclassification. **Inclusion error** = ineligible receives a targeted benefit; **exclusion error** = eligible misses it.
- Hybrid: usable common service floor plus justified supplements and review. NFSA's defined coverage and RTE's age scope are not identical universal promises.
- ILO **Recommendation 202 (2012)**: non-binding social-protection-floor benchmark: essential health care; income security for children, working-age persons unable to earn sufficient income and older persons. Not a ratified Convention or automatic Indian statutory right.
- ILO **25 May 2026** reports India's **64.3%** coverage by *at least one benefit* under **SDG 1.3.1** methodology and continuing data integration; this is neither every guarantee nor verified adequacy/capability.
- Announced → enacted → commenced → financed → delivered → achieved are different stages. Attach indicator, date, source and eligibility to each volatile claim; use NFHS/PLFS with survey round rather than an undated number.
- **2026 GS-II Q17:** examine whether rights-based welfare can work amid non-integrated governance and minimal public investment; map claimant, law, each institutional duty, actual finance, coordination and remedy, then test capability outcomes. Integration without public capacity does not prove delivery; this is an answer-neutral route, not a solved PYQ.

## Optional refinements and exam routes

- Beyond Rawls's core fair-opportunity test, his **difference principle** judges inequalities by advantage to the least advantaged. Sen leaves the real capability set comparatively open; Nussbaum proposes ten central capabilities as a threshold. Fraser's core triad can be extended by asking who can contest the policy rule itself.
- Nussbaum's ten: life, bodily health, bodily integrity, senses/imagination/thought, emotions, practical reason, affiliation, other species, play, control over environment.
- Horizontal equity = comparably placed treated alike; vertical equity = justified differentiation for disadvantage. Residual welfare is a last-resort backstop; institutional welfare is broadly citizenship-based. India mixes models.
- PYQ quick route: **2019 GS-II Q6 (Examine)** and **2023 GS-I Q16 (Why did … fail)** → growth versus human-capability conversion (L5); **2024 GS-I Q18 (Comment)** → affirmative-action promise versus access and remedy (L1/L3–4); **2025 GS-II Q16 (Discuss)** → unequal ownership, persistent poverty and limits of consumption support (L6/L8); **2026 GS-II Q17 (Examine)** → rights-based promise versus fragmentation, finance and realised access (L3–4/L6). These are demand routes, not solved answers.
- Universal answer spine: define → identify legal status and group → name a relevant Indian example → explain causal barrier and evidence → offer a counterpoint → propose a specific safeguarded correction → conclude conditionally.
