# Social Justice 13 — Transgender Persons and Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Communities

Two histories of exclusion meet in one question: what changes when the state recognises a person or community, but a school, hospital, employer or welfare office still cannot serve them? The routes to recognition are **not** interchangeable. Gender identity is not a community-list entry; denotification is not gender certification.

### 2026 GS-II Q1 — 10 marks, 150 words

Right to privacy relating to self-identity is very dear to every human being and well protected under Article 21 of the Constitution. In this context, examine the effect of the amendment in 2026, to the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019.

| Experience | Transgender person | Denotified / nomadic family |
|---|---|---|

| State interface | Legal gender recognition and civil documents | Community classification, residence documents and service portability |

*The common concern is dignified inclusion; the documents and legal tests differ.*

Imagine two eligible applicants turned away: one has a gender marker that does not match their life; the other has no fixed-address document. Issuing a card is not the same as finding an employer, a school place or safe accommodation. **Recognition** acknowledges standing and identity; **redistribution** supplies resources; **representation** gives affected people a say; **remedy** corrects exclusion when a gatekeeper refuses access.

The 2019 transgender law provides an identity-certificate route and prohibits specified discrimination. Denotified, nomadic and semi-nomadic communities instead encounter variable community recognition and mobility-sensitive eligibility rules. Neither all transgender people nor all DNT people have the same circumstances. A transgender person can also belong to a DNT community; the two labels are not substitutes.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** An identity document may be necessary for a particular administrative benefit, but it cannot alone cause social acceptance. Conversely, the absence of one standard nationwide DNT *statutory* list does not imply that no State recognises any community or that no family has documents. This is why a sensible answer tests the entire chain: recognition → documentation → eligibility → service → grievance → outcome.

**UPSC use:** In GS-II, establish which group and authority are at issue before proposing solutions. For GS-IV, ask whether an official demands unnecessary disclosure or treats mobility as evidence of wrongdoing. **Trap:** describing gender self-identification and a State community certificate as the same legal process.

### Revision points

1. Recognitive justice affirms denied identity and dignity.
2. Distributive justice supplies resources, opportunities and services.
3. Representation gives affected communities a role in decisions that shape them.
4. Corrective remedy addresses a specific denial after it occurs.
5. A transgender person's gender identity is not a community-list classification.
6. DNT status refers to a historical community experience; NT/SNT also describe mobility patterns.
7. Documentation may open an administrative gate without securing a school place, job or safe home.
8. Absence of one central statutory DNT list does not mean that every State ignores every community.
9. Intersection is possible: a transgender person may also belong to a DNT/NT/SNT community.
10. Evaluate the full chain: recognition → documentation → eligibility → service → grievance → outcome.

### Concept check

**Question:** A district issues identity documents but excludes an applicant from a training programme. What was accomplished, and what remains to be established?

**Model answer:** Documentation addresses one recognition barrier; programme eligibility, accessible enrolment, actual participation and an effective remedy still need examination.

**Misconception to avoid:** Counting certificates as jobs confuses an administrative input with a capability outcome.

**Original Mains practice (10 marks; answer in 150 words):** Analyse why recognitive and distributive justice must be considered together without treating transgender identity and denotified-community status as equivalent.

**Mains model response:** Recognition responds to different wrongs. A transgender person's gender can be denied by a binary record or discriminatory employer; a denotified family's community may carry the stigma of the Criminal Tribes Act and face inconsistent documentation across places. Both require dignity, but they do not use the same legal identity test. *NALSA* established constitutional protection for self-identified gender, whereas DNT policy relies on community identification and welfare arrangements. A certificate or listing creates an entry point, not a guaranteed seat, job or safe home. Where a nomadic pupil cannot prove residence, school access fails despite policy inclusion; where an employer discriminates against a qualified transgender applicant, formal recognition has not secured fair employment. A credible response connects documents to service access, complaint routes and measurable educational or livelihood outcomes, with affected people involved in design. Verification is legitimate only if it does not reproduce exclusion.

**Scoring guide (10):** distinct recognition mechanisms 3; named evidence for both groups 3; delivery-chain analysis 2; qualified conclusion 2.

## Lesson 2 — Constitutional recognition before statutory certification

Book context: *Courseware on Indian Polity by M Laxmikanth*, PDF pp. 1282–1283, links *NALSA* to Part III, self-determination and Article 21.
CA search: "transgender self-identification constitutional rights India 2026 official"

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
Person's experienced gender ── not reducible to anatomy
                 ↓
NALSA v Union of India (2014)
                 ↓
Equality (Arts 14–15) + expression (Art 19) + dignity/autonomy (Art 21)
                 ↓
State must recognise gender identity and address exclusion
                 ↓
Parliament's 2019 framework → later 2026 statutory change
                 ≠ reversal of the constitutional judgment by itself
```

*A later statute is assessed against, not automatically substituted for, constitutional rights.*

Why can a government form become a rights question? A binary-only record may make daily participation contingent on accepting somebody else's account of one's identity. In *National Legal Services Authority v Union of India* (*NALSA*, 2014), the Supreme Court recognised transgender persons' constitutional protections, including a right to self-identified gender. It rejected conditioning recognition solely on surgery or a biological test. The Court directed measures for social and educational advancement; do not turn that direction into an already enacted, uniform nationwide reservation quota.

The book's discussion on PDF pp. 1282–1283 traces equal protection and Article 21 self-determination. **Constitutional right** answers whether identity and dignity are protected; **statutory certificate** answers how an administrative record is produced. They interact but are not identical. For example, a qualified applicant whose gender entry differs from an employer's expectations has an equality concern even if a separate administrative dispute over a certificate remains unresolved.

⚠️ **Objection:** Some official records need verifiable consistency to prevent duplication or fraud. **Reply:** A legitimate records objective does not itself prove that a medical expert can determine experienced gender, nor that any disclosure burden is necessary and proportionate. The court, rather than an answer writer, resolves the pending 2026 challenge.

**UPSC use:** Start a privacy answer with *NALSA*'s constitutional baseline; only then examine the Act as amended. **Trap:** claiming *NALSA* and the 2019 Act have identical legal force or identical certificate provisions.

### Revision points

1. *NALSA v Union of India* was decided in 2014.
2. It recognised transgender persons as rights-bearing constitutional subjects.
3. Article 14 supplies equality before law and equal protection.
4. Articles 15 and 16 address discrimination and equality in public opportunity.
5. Article 19 protects expression relevant to gender presentation.
6. Article 21 protects dignity, autonomy and self-determination.
7. The judgment rejected making identity depend solely on surgery or anatomy.
8. Its backward-class direction called for governmental consideration and measures; it did not itself enact one uniform national quota.
9. A constitutional right and a statutory certificate procedure have different legal functions.
10. Parliament may alter administration, but the alteration remains open to constitutional review.

### Concept check

**Question:** If Parliament changes the certificate procedure, has it thereby overruled the constitutional holding in *NALSA*?

**Model answer:** No. A statute can change an administrative route, but its compatibility with *NALSA* and fundamental rights remains open to constitutional review.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating statutory deletion as automatic cancellation of a Supreme Court constitutional interpretation.

**Original Mains practice (15 marks; answer in 250 words):** Examine the difference between constitutional recognition of gender identity and administrative certification, with reference to equality and dignity.

**Mains model response:** The constitutional question is who has standing to define a person's gender; the administrative question is how public documents record it. *NALSA v Union of India* (2014) placed self-identified gender in the framework of equal protection, expression and Article 21 dignity. Its rejection of compulsory surgery as a condition for identity matters because anatomical scrutiny can turn recognition into permission granted by another person. The original 2019 Act created a District Magistrate certificate route and the 2020 Rules specified the application process. A document can make admission, employment and record correction easier, but it does not constitute the underlying constitutional right. Conversely, a practical route is needed for departments to align records. Officials may therefore defend verification as a means to reduce errors and ensure consistent benefits; the response must ask whether that specific verification is necessary, proportionate, accessible and compatible with self-determination. A transgender applicant confronting both a mismatched certificate and refusal of a job needs more than a formal recognition statement: a fair correction route and enforcement against discrimination. As the 2026 statutory route now involves a medical board, the difference between recording identity and independently judging it becomes acute. The amendment's ultimate constitutionality is a pending question, not an inference from the mere existence of litigation. Constitutional recognition and workable documents should reinforce each other; neither should be offered as a substitute for the other.

**Scoring guide (15):** constitutional baseline 4; certification distinction and example 3; verification counterargument 3; proportionality analysis 3; qualified verdict 2.

| Original-law route | Trigger | Effect |

*These describe the **2019 Act before amendment**; the current recognition route changes in Lesson 4.*

Consider a student whose admission form will not accept the gender on their identity document. Recognition helps correct a record; Section 3 also addresses exclusion from education. The original Act's definition included trans-men, trans-women, genderqueer people, intersex variations and specified socio-cultural identities. Do not equate intersex variation (sex characteristics) with every transgender person's lived identity, or mistake sexual orientation for gender identity.

Under the original Act an applicant sought a District Magistrate (DM) certificate based on self-perceived transgender identity; the 2020 Rules operationalised application and documentation, without a surgical precondition for this basic certificate. Section 7's revised certificate was a *separate* route after gender-change surgery. The Act also addressed residence, welfare, education, healthcare and complaint arrangements; Section 18 penalised specified harms including forced labour, denial of passage or public access, forced eviction and injury/abuse. The National Council for Transgender Persons is chaired by the Union Minister for Social Justice and includes government, State/UT, expert and transgender-community representation. Its statutory role is advisory and monitoring-oriented, not quasi-judicial enforcement; an establishment complaint officer does not replace independent judicial relief.

⚠️ **Limit:** A prohibition on discrimination does not show how often complaints succeed. An institution must publicise its officer, receive grievances safely and actually remedy exclusion. Nor should an argument that the 2020 Rules supplied a procedure be misread as an assertion that Rules may override an Act. An Act creates legal powers and rights; subordinate Rules implement within that authority.

**UPSC use:** In a question about the 2026 change, explicitly write “under the original 2019 Act” before describing self-perceived certification. **Trap:** asserting that surgery was mandatory for the original basic certificate or that the Council exercises quasi-judicial powers.

### Revision points

1. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act was enacted in 2019.
2. The 2020 Rules operationalised the original statutory route.
3. The original definition expressly included trans-men, trans-women and genderqueer persons.
4. It also included persons with intersex variations and named socio-cultural identities.
5. Original Section 4(2) affirmed a right to self-perceived gender identity.
6. The original basic certificate required a DM application, not surgery.
7. Original Section 7 created a separate optional revised certificate after gender-change surgery.
8. Section 3 prohibited discrimination in named fields including education, employment and healthcare.
9. Section 18 addressed specified harms; it was not a general code for every act of discrimination.
10. The National Council is representative and advisory, not a court or constitutional commission.
11. A complaint officer is useful only when the route is known, safe and capable of securing correction.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why was the original basic certificate not interchangeable with the original Section 7 revised certificate?

**Model answer:** Basic recognition followed an application on self-perceived transgender identity without surgery; the revised certificate was a distinct, optional route following gender-change surgery.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading the later medical route backward into every applicant's original eligibility.

**Original Mains practice (10 marks; answer in 150 words):** Discuss the strengths and limits of the original 2019 Act as an instrument against discrimination.

**Mains model response:** The original Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 translated recognition into a certificate route and a statutory discrimination prohibition. Section 3 addressed exclusion in education, employment and healthcare; Sections 4–6 allowed application to a District Magistrate for a basic certificate without surgery, while Section 7 offered a separate revised route after gender-change surgery. The National Council offered advice. These provisions mattered to a qualified applicant denied a job because of gender identity: the problem was not merely a mismatched form but an unlawful decision. Yet certificate issuance does not prove changed recruitment practices. A complaint officer needs safe access and effective redress; shelter, health and livelihood support need implementing institutions. A certificate gate can impose delay despite formal rights. The 2026 amending Act has since altered recognition, so this is a historical baseline, not present procedural advice. Measure success through secure participation, not certificates alone.

**Scoring guide (10):** original recognition route 3; anti-discrimination and institutional provisions 3; enforcement limitation 2; present-law qualification 2.

```text
13 March: Bill introduced → 24 March: Lok Sabha passage
                       → 25 March: Rajya Sabha passage
                       → 30 March 2026: assent (Act No. 3 of 2026)

                       → 25 May 2026: amending Act IN FORCE
                 litigation may continue; no merits decision follows merely from passage
```

*A Bill, an assented Act, commencement and a judicial merits ruling are four different events.*

Imagine opening a pre-amendment guide after 25 May and following its “self-perceived declaration” procedure. That would be a serious mistake: a legal right can be constitutionally claimed even while the *current statutory procedure* has changed. The 2026 **Amendment Act** changes the continuing 2019 Act; it does not repeal the entire 2019 Act, and a future implementing Rule is not the source of the enacted change.

| Question | Original 2019 Act | Operative 2026 amendment |
|---|---|---|
| Who is included? | Broad identity-based definition including trans-men, trans-women and genderqueer persons, alongside intersex and socio-cultural categories | Deletes the broad opening formula and those express trans-man/trans-woman and genderqueer inclusions; retains specified socio-cultural and congenital/intersex categories, adds eunuch and persons forced into a transgender identity by specified acts; contains an exclusion concerning different sexual orientations and self-perceived sexual identities |
| Self-perceived identity | Express Section 4(2) statutory right | Section 4(2) omitted |
| Basic identity certificate | DM on application; no board prerequisite | DM examines recommendation of a designated medical board headed by Chief or Deputy Chief Medical Officer; DM may seek other experts |
| Name and revised certificate | Section 7 revised certificate could follow surgery | First-name correction on documents after certificate; revised certificate following gender-change surgery is mandatory and treating institution must report details |
| Criminal protection | Section 18 specified existing harms | Substituted Section 18 retains earlier offences in clauses (a)–(d); adds kidnapping plus grievous/severe injury to force identity (e)–(f), **and** compelled transgender presentation coupled with exploitation in begging, solicitation, servitude or forced/bonded labour (g)–(h) |

The new offences have **different elements**. Under **Section 18(e)**, kidnapping or abducting an adult plus grievous hurt or permanent/severe injury while forcing a transgender identity attracts rigorous imprisonment of **10 years to life** and a fine of **at least ₹2 lakh**. **Section 18(f)** applies the child counterpart with rigorous imprisonment **for life** and a fine of **at least ₹5 lakh**. Under **Section 18(g)**, coercing any person (whether transgender or not) to present outwardly as transgender **and** employing or using that person for begging, solicitation, servitude or other forced/bonded labour is punishable with rigorous imprisonment of **5–10 years** and a fine of **at least ₹1 lakh**. **Section 18(h)** addresses the corresponding exploitation of **a child**, with rigorous imprisonment of **10–14 years** and a fine of **at least ₹3 lakh**. These are statutory offence elements, not a ban on transgender identity or begging as such.

**Precision:** The exclusion's statutory wording refers to “self-perceived sexual identities”; do not silently rewrite that quotation as an exhaustive definition of gender identity. ⚠️ It is a reasoned concern, not a proven adjudicated fact, that narrowing may deprive people outside the amended definition of protection under this *specific Act*. Neither the coercion-and-exploitation provisions nor the kidnapping-and-injury provisions criminalise consensual self-identification merely by their existence. Compare legal text and conduct carefully.

**UPSC use:** Write both the date of assent and the later commencement date; explain definition, certificate and reporting separately. **Trap:** calling the amendment a mere proposal, attributing its changes to the 2020 Rules or dating operation from 30 March.

### Revision points

1. The Bill was introduced on 13 March 2026.
2. Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha passed it on 24 and 25 March respectively.
3. Presidential assent on 30 March 2026 created Act No. 3 of 2026.
4. S.O. 2620(E), dated 22 May 2026, fixed 25 May 2026 as commencement.
5. The 2019 Act continues in force as amended; it was not wholly repealed.
6. Section 4(2)'s express self-perceived-identity right was omitted.
7. The amended Section 6 route requires the DM to examine the appointed medical authority's recommendation.
8. Certification now enables first-name change in birth and other identity documents.
9. Post-surgery revised certification became mandatory, with institutional reporting to the DM and authority.
10. Definition, certification, reporting and offences must be analysed separately.
11. Sections 18(e)–(f) concern kidnapping/abduction plus specified serious injury.
12. Sections 18(g)–(h) require compelled transgender presentation plus exploitative use; they do not criminalise identity or begging alone.

### Concept check

**Question:** A journalist calls the 2026 amendment “only a Bill” on 26 May 2026. What legal stages have they conflated?

**Model answer:** Both Houses passed it, assent made it an Act on 30 March, and the 22 May notification brought it into force on 25 May. A pending constitutional challenge does not restore Bill status.

**Misconception to avoid:** Mistaking the date of an interim court hearing for the commencement date.

**Original Mains practice (15 marks; answer in 250 words):** Analyse how the enacted 2026 amendment changes recognition and protection compared with the original 2019 Act, distinguishing benefits from risks.

**Mains model response:** The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026 received assent on 30 March and commenced on 25 May under S.O. 2620(E). It changes the continuing 2019 Act, rather than replacing it. Previously, the definition expressly included trans-men, trans-women and genderqueer persons and Section 4(2) affirmed self-perceived identity. A District Magistrate issued the basic certificate without a medical-board prerequisite. The amendment removes that express provision, narrows the enumerated categories and makes certification conditional on an appointed medical authority's recommendation. It permits first-name correction after certification. A revised certificate after gender-change surgery, formerly optional, is now mandatory, with institutional reporting. Substituted Section 18 adds kidnapping-and-injury offences and offences coupling compelled transgender presentation with exploitative begging, solicitation, servitude or forced labour; the latter do not criminalise begging alone. Protection against coercion and reliable records are legitimate aims. Yet narrowing may jeopardise remedies; medical review and reporting raise distinct autonomy and privacy concerns. The Court issued notice on 4 May, stayed parallel High Court proceedings on 15 June and later recorded continuity for benefits attached to cards issued earlier, while leaving pending/new applications and constitutional merits unresolved. These effects require assessment against *NALSA*, not automatic approval or invalidation.

**Scoring guide (15):** legislative stage and commencement 3; exact recognition changes 4; offence/protection changes 3; autonomy and privacy risks 3; balanced legal qualification 2.

| Privacy facet | State action to test | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Decisional autonomy | Removing Section 4(2), adding board recommendation | Who determines a person's gender? |
| Informational privacy | Hospital transmits surgery details to authorities | Who receives sensitive medical information, for what purpose, with what safeguards? |
| Bodily autonomy | Mandatory revised certificate after surgery | Does a medical choice trigger compulsory legal disclosure? |

*These are different claims; an excellent answer does not collapse them into the word “privacy.”*

Suppose a person independently decides how to describe their gender but must now present medical material for a certificate. That is an autonomy issue even before any hospital shares data. Suppose another person has undergone surgery and a hospital must report information to a DM. That is an **additional** data-flow question, even if the person's gender is undisputed. *NALSA* supplies the identity baseline; *K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India* (2017) makes privacy, dignity and autonomy central to Article 21 analysis.

⚠️ **Petitioners' case:** Medical gatekeeping may reintroduce the biological test rejected in *NALSA*, and mandatory reporting may burden informational privacy without an express dedicated safeguard in the amendment. The statutory narrowing could exclude people from the Act's protections. **Government's stated case:** Precise and verifiable certification can protect limited welfare access and prevent forced transition; the new coercion offences serve a real protective purpose. **Assessment:** A protective aim does not dispense with asking whether the chosen means are necessary, proportionate, minimally intrusive and accompanied by data safeguards. Neither side's submissions are a judicial finding.

**Procedure and dates:** Article 32 challenges were brought in April 2026. On **4 May**, before the **25 May** commencement, the Supreme Court issued notice to the Union, States and Union Territories and directed placement before a three-judge Bench. On **15 June**, in **T.P.(C) Nos. 1686–1692/2026**, it stayed further proceedings before the High Courts while the transfer request was pending. At the **3 August** hearing, the batch was listed for interim relief on 17 August; the hearing addressed the claimed protection of rights already accrued through identity cards, but the written order did not grant blanket protection to pending or new applications. On **17 August**, the Court recorded the Union's statement that consequential benefits attached to Transgender Identity Cards **issued earlier** would continue to operate as valid, subject to the outcome of the proceedings. This protects existing/issued cards and their attached benefits on the recorded assurance; it does not preserve every pending application under the former route or govern new applications after commencement. None of these proceedings decided the amendment's constitutional validity.

**2026 GS-II application:** The opening Q1 asks for the effect on Article 21 privacy relating to self-identity. Its *Examine* directive requires more than a clause list: identify the *NALSA* baseline, separate decisional and informational effects, weigh the coercion/verification rationale and close without inventing a constitutional verdict.

### Revision points

1. Article 21 privacy is wider than secrecy.
2. Decisional autonomy asks who controls an identity-defining choice.
3. Informational privacy asks who receives sensitive information and for what purpose.
4. Bodily autonomy concerns the legal consequences attached to medical choices.
5. *NALSA* supplies the self-identification and dignity baseline.
6. *Puttaswamy* supplies privacy, autonomy and proportionality principles.
7. Omission of Section 4(2) and medical-authority review raise a decisional-autonomy issue.
8. Hospital-to-DM/authority reporting raises a separate informational-privacy issue.
9. Preventing coercion and protecting scheme integrity are legitimate objectives, not automatic proof of proportionality.
10. A strong answer tests suitability, necessity, less intrusive means, safeguards and review.
11. The 4 May order issued notice and referred the matter; it did not decide constitutional merits.
12. The 15 June order in T.P.(C) Nos. 1686–1692/2026 stayed parallel High Court proceedings, not the Amendment Act.
13. On 3 August the Court heard accrued-rights concerns and listed interim relief for 17 August.
14. The 17 August order recorded that benefits attached to cards issued earlier would continue, subject to the case outcome.
15. Existing/issued cards are distinct from pending or new applications; no blanket former-route protection was recorded for the latter.
16. Assent, commencement, interim procedure and final adjudication are distinct legal stages.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why would an answer about medical-board review alone fail to address every privacy issue raised by the amendment?

**Model answer:** It addresses control over identity decisions, but compulsory institutional reporting after surgery separately concerns sensitive health-data disclosure; mandatory revision also engages bodily-choice consequences.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that a justified verification objective automatically answers a distinct data-minimisation objection.

**Original Mains practice (20 marks; answer in 250 words):** Critically evaluate whether verification and protection from coerced transition can be reconciled with self-identified gender and medical privacy after the 2026 amendment.

**Mains model response:** Verification and protection from violence are valid governmental interests, but must be assessed separately from the method chosen. Under *NALSA* (2014), self-identified gender has an equality, expression and dignity foundation. The 2019 Act reflected this in Section 4(2); the operative 2026 amendment omits it and requires the District Magistrate to examine an appointed medical authority's recommendation. An expert's view may displace the person's identity. Substituted Section 18 separately addresses kidnapping with serious injury **and** compelled transgender presentation coupled with exploitative begging, solicitation or forced labour; these offences target coercers, not consensual identity. Preventing such harm does not logically require invasive proof from every voluntary applicant. Post-surgery certificate revision and hospital reporting expose sensitive health facts to the state. *Puttaswamy* requires attention to privacy and autonomy; proportionality asks whether less intrusive evidence, restricted data access and review suffice. The Supreme Court issued notice on 4 May; stayed parallel High Court proceedings on 15 June; and on 17 August recorded the Union's assurance that benefits attached to cards issued earlier would continue, subject to the case outcome. That continuity does not automatically cover pending or new applications, and no final validity ruling exists. 1. **Question:** A complaint officer receives a hiring complaint from an applicant with a certificate. Does this prove that the statutory prohibition is enforced? **Model answer:** No. The test is accessible hearing, reasoned decision, correction and non-retaliation, not merely receipt.
2. **Question:** Is a proposed all-India DNT classification equivalent to an already operative statutory reservation category? **Model answer:** No. State and other-category classifications may overlap; a recommendation neither enacts a list nor automatically grants uniform reservation.
3. **Question:** Why must an answer separate the 4 May, 15 June and 17 August developments? **Model answer:** The 4 May notice preceded commencement; the 15 June order stayed parallel High Court proceedings rather than the Act; and the 17 August recorded assurance preserved benefits attached to cards issued earlier, not every pending or new application. None was a final merits ruling.
4. **Question:** When is sheltered accommodation a worthwhile targeted response rather than proof of segregation? **Model answer:** When it offers voluntary immediate safety and a route to mainstream housing, health and livelihoods; count lasting outcomes as well as access.
5. **Question:** Does a notice issued in a constitutional challenge establish that the challenged law is valid or invalid? **Model answer:** No. Notice initiates adjudication; validity requires a merits decision, while the enacted law's operative status depends on commencement and any later binding order.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

**Original 10-marker — Examine how a document can both enable and restrict access (150 words).**

**Model answer:** Documents help a school or welfare office link people to benefits, but can exclude those whom policy intends to serve. The original 2019 transgender law used a District Magistrate certificate on self-perceived identity; the operative 2026 amendment requires an appointed medical authority's recommendation. A certificate can enable first-name correction, while invasive or delayed processing may deter applicants. For a mobile DNT/NT/SNT family, SEED's State/UT certification can align benefits to eligible communities; fixed-address demands may interrupt schooling and care. The DWBDNC implements the scheme, not every State classification. Use minimal necessary information, assisted applications, reasoned refusals, review and portability. Test actual services and remedies, not document counts alone.

**Scoring guide (10):** document-enablement mechanism 2; exclusion risks for both groups 3; institutional accuracy 2; implementable correction 2; qualified close 1.

**Original 15-marker — Critically analyse whether a dedicated scheme can compensate for an unenforced right (250 words).**

**Model answer:** A dedicated scheme can reach needs that a general right does not immediately finance, but cannot substitute for enforcement of that right. Section 3 of the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 prohibits discrimination in settings including education and employment. SMILE adds rehabilitation, care, skills and economic linkages through its transgender sub-scheme, including Garima Greh shelters. A person evicted from home may need immediate refuge even when a complaint is possible. But training a qualified worker does not remedy an employer's discriminatory rejection unless complaints are heard and corrective action follows. DNT/NT/SNT communities present a different configuration: repeal of colonial criminal-tribe legislation in 1952 did not end stigma, and mobile families may confront eligibility barriers. SEED addresses education, health-insurance facilitation, livelihoods and housing, while State/UT identification determines access. If documentation fails or policing remains biased, another scheme component cannot alone cure the injustice. Conversely, an anti-discrimination declaration without accessible housing or training is equally insufficient. Audit referral, enrolment, grievance resolution and long-term outcomes; design documents for mobility and privacy, and allow affected communities to evaluate delivery. The strongest defence of specialised schemes is that they can focus resources; their limit is fragmentation unless mainstream services remain available. Effective inclusion requires both enforceable rights and usable programmes.

**Scoring guide (15):** clear claim and distinction 3; named law and schemes 4; causal failure chain 3; strongest counterargument and reply 3; balanced conclusion 2.

**Original 20-marker — Evaluate the proposition: “Recognition without access, access without autonomy and autonomy without remedy are each incomplete justice” (250 words).**

**Model answer:** The proposition connects three non-identical dimensions of social justice. *NALSA* recognises self-identified gender as constitutional dignity; the 2019 Act translated protections into certification and anti-discrimination duties. The operative 2026 amendment replaces the original statutory route with medical-authority recommendation and reporting following gender-change surgery, raising decisional and informational privacy costs. Preventing coercion and ensuring reliable records are legitimate aims: substituted Section 18 punishes both kidnapping with grave injury and compelled transgender presentation coupled with exploitative begging or forced labour, not identity or begging alone. Interim Supreme Court proceedings have not decided merits: parallel High Court cases are stayed, while benefits attached to cards issued earlier continue under a recorded Union assurance; pending/new applications remain distinct. For DNT/NT/SNT communities, colonial stigma outlived the 1952 repeal. SEED and DWBDNC create support routes, but State/UT identification, variable classifications and mobility can obstruct access. The groups are not identical: lived gender is not community-list status, nor movement medical evidence. Remedy requires reasons, appeal, fair policing, privacy safeguards and participation. Test education, health, housing and work alongside administrative counts. Recognition, access, autonomy and remedies must reinforce one another.

**Scoring guide (20):** recognition-access-autonomy framework 5; accurate transgender mechanism 4; accurate DNT mechanism 4; objections and remedies 4; legally qualified synthesis 3.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Gender recognition: constitutional baseline and legal changes

- *NALSA* (2014): self-identified gender, equality/dignity/expression; no surgery-only test. Its constitutional holding is distinct from a later statute.
- Original 2019 Act: broad definition; Section 4(2) self-perceived identity; basic DM certificate without surgery; Section 7 *optional* revised certificate after surgery. 2020 Rules implement the original statute rather than independently rewriting the 2026 Act.
- Section 3: discrimination prohibition; National Council: advisory, not a court; complaint officer: a route whose effective remedy must be tested.
- 2026: Bill 13 March → Lok Sabha 24 March → Rajya Sabha 25 March → assent 30 March (Act No. 3) → S.O. 2620(E) dated 22 May → **in force 25 May**. The 2019 Act continues *as amended*.
- Amended definition removes express trans-man/trans-woman and genderqueer inclusions, specifies other categories and an exclusion for different sexual orientations and “self-perceived sexual identities”; avoid asserting that all self-identification is a criminal offence.
- Section 4(2) omitted; designated medical board recommendation considered by DM; first-name correction after certification; revised certificate post-surgery mandatory; institution reports surgery details.
- Substituted Section 18(a)–(d) retains core existing offences. **(e)** requires adult kidnapping/abduction plus specified grave injury while forcing identity (10 years to life; minimum ₹2 lakh fine); **(f)** is the child counterpart (rigorous imprisonment for life; minimum ₹5 lakh fine). **(g)** covers *any person* compelled to present as transgender **and** used for begging/solicitation/servitude/forced labour (5–10 years' rigorous imprisonment; minimum ₹1 lakh fine); **(h)** is the child coercion-and-exploitation counterpart (10–14 years' rigorous imprisonment; minimum ₹3 lakh fine). No standalone ban on identity or begging is created by those clauses.

## Privacy, competing rationales and procedural posture

- *NALSA*: who identifies gender? *Puttaswamy*: decisional/bodily autonomy and informational privacy; separate the two objections.
- Ask: legitimate end? suitable measure? necessity/less intrusive route? proportionality and data safeguards? feasible appeal?
- Article 32 challenge: the official **4 May** order issued notice and directed three-judge-Bench placement before commencement. In **T.P.(C) Nos. 1686–1692/2026**, the **15 June** order stayed further High Court proceedings, not the Act. The **3 August** hearing addressed accrued identity-card rights and listed interim relief for 17 August. The **17 August** order recorded the Union's statement that benefits attached to cards **issued earlier** would continue as valid, subject to the case outcome; it did not extend that assurance automatically to pending/new applications or decide merits.
- 2026 GS-II Q1, 10 marks/150 words, examines privacy relating to self-identity; route: baseline → actual statutory change → autonomy and information → justification → qualified sub judice close.

## SMILE and real-world transgender access

- Ministry SMILE = transgender rehabilitation **and** separate rehabilitation of persons engaged in begging. Garima Greh helps shelter and immediate care; skill and economic linkages address the later transition.
- Section 18(g)–(h) punishes coercive transgender presentation **combined with** use for begging/other exploitative labour. SMILE's voluntary begging-rehabilitation route must not be mistaken for criminalisation of service users.
- Section 3 enforcement, safe complaint access, confidential medical data and jobs/housing outcomes must be measured separately from certificate or shelter counts.
- Do not claim a universal reservation, assured district shelter or dated nationwide coverage without supporting evidence.

## Denotification, mobility and classification

- 1871 Criminal Tribes Act imposed group stigma; repeal in 1952 removed designation, not prejudice.
- DNT describes a history; NT/SNT describe patterns of mobility/settlement. DNT is not itself an automatic constitutional reservation status; State SC/ST/OBC classifications can vary or overlap.
- Renke Commission (2008) and the Idate Commission (constituted 2015; report submitted January 2018) identify policy needs; Idate's proposed statutory commission and comprehensive list are proposals, not the Board's present legal powers.
- Mobility can complicate residence proof, school continuity and portable health access; it does not prove that every household is homeless or undocumented.

## DWBDNC, SEED and an answer spine

- DWBDNC constituted **21 February 2019** as a registered society/Board; actual terms include programmes, gap assessment, coordination and monitoring, **not** quasi-judicial constitutional-commission powers.
- SEED, launched **16 February 2022**, covers coaching, health-insurance facilitation, livelihoods and housing-related support; Board implements, State/UT identifies and certifies beneficiaries. A scheme component is not a guarantee of receipt.
- PM-YASASVI includes notified educational-support routes for DNT students within a wider OBC/EBC/DNT umbrella; it is distinct from SEED and from the constitutional EWS category.
- Answer spine: identify distinct historic wrong → constitutional/statutory/policy authority → documentary gate → actual service and remedy → strongest justification → proportionate, group-specific reform → outcome test.
- Fast traps: assent ≠ commencement; High Court-proceeding stay ≠ stay of Act; issued card ≠ pending/new application; interim assurance ≠ merits judgment; forced presentation *and* exploitation ≠ begging alone; original basic certificate ≠ revised certificate; DNT denotification ≠ completed rehabilitation; SMILE ≠ SEED; proposed commission ≠ current Board.
