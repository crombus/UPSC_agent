Caption: the box each thinker occupies is not a variation on the box beside it. Kierkegaard needs God for the structure to work; Sartre's whole argument starts from God's absence; Heidegger refuses to say whether God is even the right kind of question. Read the three columns as three answers to three different questions, all provoked by the same discomfort with abstract, timeless system-building.

**Plain-language start.** Imagine three people, each unhappy with the same thing — philosophy that talks about "Man" in general, "Reason" in general, "the Absolute" in general — while never once addressing the fact that *you*, this particular person, at this particular moment, must decide what to do with your one life. All three think that general theories quietly lose the individual who is supposed to be using them. But their diagnoses and remedies are not interchangeable: one turns to God, one turns to freedom, one turns to Being itself. "Existentialism" is the name historians later gave to this loose family, not a club any of the three joined by that name in their own lifetime (only Sartre ever accepted the label for himself).

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** "Existentialism" is a retrospective family-label, not a unified school.

### Why the label is dangerous to use carelessly

| Careless habit | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "The existentialists believed X" | Kierkegaard is a committed Christian; Heidegger explicitly denied being an existentialist; only Sartre used the word approvingly. A shared-belief sentence is almost always false for at least one of the three. |
| Treating "existence precedes essence" as the movement's official slogan | It is **Sartre's** formula (from the lecture delivered in 1945 and published in 1946). Heidegger repudiates it in print. Kierkegaard never uses it. |
| Treating anxiety/dread/anguish as "the same mood, three languages" | Kierkegaard's anxiety is about *possibility*; Sartre's anguish is about *my own freedom*; Heidegger's anxiety is about *Being-in-the-world as such*. Same family of moods, three distinct objects of disclosure — see Lesson 3 (Part B) and the fear-vs-angst table repeated across lessons. |
| Assuming they read and endorse one another as a single tradition | Heidegger was Husserl's student and dedicated *Being and Time* to him, then broke with him; Sartre absorbed Husserl and Heidegger both, then was rejected by Heidegger in the *Letter on Humanism*. The relations are personal, textual and often adversarial, not a smooth relay race. |

### 1. The printed core scope

- **Printed thinkers:** Kierkegaard, Sartre and Heidegger. **Nietzsche, Marcel, Jaspers, Camus and de Beauvoir are not independent core thinkers** here; each appears as a bounded root, comparison or criticism voice.
- **Marks-essential source map:**

| Thinker/source | What it is the central source of |
|---|---|
| Kierkegaard's pseudonymous and signed works | single individual, subjective appropriation, the three spheres, anxiety/despair, faith and the anti-system critique |
| Heidegger, *Being and Time* (1927, unfinished) | Dasein, the Being-question, being-in-the-world, Care, authenticity and temporality |
| Heidegger, "Letter on Humanism" (written 1946, published 1947) | the rejection of Sartrean humanism and of slogan attribution |
| Sartre, *Being and Nothingness* (1943) | in-itself/for-itself, nothingness, facticity/transcendence, bad faith, freedom and the Look |
| Sartre, *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (lecture delivered in October 1945; published in 1946) | existence-precedes-essence, anguish/abandonment/despair, universal responsibility |

- Husserlian reduction and Hegelian system are **indispensable contrasts** (Kierkegaard's target is Hegel; Heidegger's method breaks from Husserl); their positive systems are introduced only to the bounded extent needed for the present comparisons.

### 2. The shared floor, precisely stated

All three share a discomfort with philosophy that treats the human being as one more instance of a general kind, fully knowable from an external, timeless standpoint. Concretely, this discomfort has three recurring symptoms across the three thinkers:

1. **Anti-system/anti-essentialism.** Kierkegaard opposes Hegel's claim that reality is a completed rational System; Sartre denies that a fixed human essence precedes existence; Heidegger denies that "Being" can be captured as one more entity among entities (the *ontological difference*, taught fully in Lesson 10).
2. **Priority of the concrete, situated individual.** Kierkegaard's *den Enkelte* (the single individual); Sartre's *pour-soi* always situated in a factical position; Heidegger's Dasein always already thrown into a world.
3. **Choice/decision as philosophically central, not peripheral.** Kierkegaard's qualitative leap; Sartre's inescapable choosing; Heidegger's resoluteness (*Entschlossenheit*) as the mode of authentically taking up one's thrown possibilities.

### 3. The quick-reference table (learn this before anything else)

| Concept | Kierkegaard | Sartre | Heidegger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core term for human being | The single individual (*den Enkelte*) | The for-itself (*pour-soi*) | Dasein (the entity for whom Being is at issue) |
| "Existence precedes essence" | Not his formula — the existing individual resists systematic closure | Explicit — the founding slogan of atheistic existentialism | Rejected as a *humanistic* misreading, though "the essence of Dasein lies in its existence" |
| Route to authenticity | Religious faith, in pseudonymous and signed contexts | Bad-faith critique; positive authenticity remains underdeveloped | Anticipatory resoluteness toward death, not a moral programme |
| God | Central to religious existence | Atheism is a premise, not a conclusion, of the argument presented in the lecture delivered in 1945 and published in 1946 | Not decided by the fundamental ontology of *Being and Time* |
| Chief enemy | Hegel's objective spirit; the "crowd" | Determinism; bad faith; the "serious" spirit | Cartesian subject–object dualism; *das Man* |
| Key mood | Anxiety/dread (*Angest*), despair | Anguish (*angoisse*), nausea | Anxiety (*Angst*) |
| Freedom | Qualitative choice within a finite God-relation | Radical, but situated and factical | Thrown projection of possibilities, not voluntarism |

> 🔑 **Mnemonic — "K-S-H = Faith / Freedom / Finitude":** **K**ierkegaard = leap of **F**aith; **S**artre = radical **F**reedom; **H**eidegger = **F**initude (being-toward-death). Three thinkers, three F's.

### The one recurring examiner trap this lesson exists to prevent

The single most frequent scoring failure across all sixteen Existentialism PYQs (2018–2026) is writing "the existentialists held that…" and then attributing one thinker's doctrine to all three. Every subsequent lesson in this session will re-apply the discipline established here: **name the thinker, name the specific pseudonym/text where relevant, and never silently generalise.**

### First orientation: Hegel and Husserl as contrasts

- Kierkegaard's entire project is legible only against Hegel's System — the claim that reality is Absolute Spirit's self-development and that "the truth is the whole." The present comparison introduces the System only to the extent required to understand Kierkegaard's critique.
- Heidegger's method is legible only against Husserl's transcendental phenomenology — especially the epoché and the transcendental ego. The contrast is developed only to the extent required to understand Heidegger's and Sartre's transformations of phenomenology.

No primary PYQ is linked directly to Lesson 1; it provides the distinctions every subsequent PYQ approach must respect. Two verified questions test exactly the risk this lesson corrects:

- **2024 Q4(a), 20 marks:** "What do the existentialist thinkers *mean* by the slogan 'existence precedes essence'?" — the plural "thinkers" is a trap; a correct answer states that this is Sartre's formula, then separately states Kierkegaard's and Heidegger's actual positions (concise approach at Lesson 4/Lesson 7).
- **2022 Q4(a)** and **2023 Q1(d)** both require the Kierkegaard-vs-Hegel opposition the doctrine requires.

### Roots and boundary figures: a genealogy, not a roll-call

```text
Kierkegaard: individual, faith, anxiety ────────┐
Nietzsche: death of God, nihilism, revaluation ├─► crisis of inherited meaning
Dostoevsky: freedom, guilt, rebellion ──────────┘
Husserl: intentionality and phenomenology ─► Heidegger ─► Sartre
```

Nietzsche’s **death of God** names the collapse of a transcendent guarantor of inherited value. Its danger is **nihilism**: inherited rankings lose authority before new values have been achieved. The constructive movement is therefore from collapse through **revaluation** to **value creation**—the disciplined formation and testing of life-affirming rankings, not the arbitrary declaration that any private preference is valid. A strong criticism is that self-legislation without an independent standard can make domination masquerade as creativity; the Nietzschean reply is that creation is constrained by intellectual honesty, self-overcoming and the capacity to affirm life, though those constraints do not fully settle the political objection. Nietzsche is thus a bounded precursor who diagnoses the crisis of value; he is not one of the three printed core existentialists. Sartre inherits the no-guarantor situation but centres universal responsibility. Dostoevsky is a literary-theological precursor: freedom, guilt and rebellion are dramatised rather than organised into systematic ontology. Husserl supplies intentionality; Heidegger transforms phenomenology into fundamental ontology, and Sartre turns intentional consciousness into nihilating freedom.

Camus, Marcel, Jaspers and Beauvoir remain bounded comparisons: Camus—absurd and revolt; Marcel—mystery and fidelity; Jaspers—boundary situations and communication; Beauvoir—ambiguity, situated freedom and oppression.

**Objection → reply → residual:** The family label may be too loose. Use it only for a shared problem-field and then name each distinct solution. Its historical boundary nevertheless remains contestable.

### The single individual outside the System

```text
HEGEL'S SYSTEM                          KIERKEGAARD'S COUNTER
════════════════════                    ══════════════════════════
"The truth is the whole."               "The existing individual
Reality = Absolute Spirit's              cannot be a moment inside
self-development.                        a finished system —
The individual is one                    because the individual

process.                                  must still exist,
                                           choose and die."
        │                                          │
        ▼                                          ▼
 Everything is explained            One thing is left unexplained:
 from an impersonal,                 the particular, temporal,

```

Caption: Kierkegaard does not claim Hegel's logic is internally inconsistent. He claims Hegel has answered a question ("what is reality, viewed from nowhere in particular, as a completed whole?") while quietly forgetting the person who is doing the viewing — a person who is never "nowhere in particular," who is never "completed," and who cannot suspend existing long enough to occupy Hegel's God's-eye standpoint.

**Plain-language start.** Think of a textbook that perfectly explains how digestion works — every enzyme, every stage — but never once mentions that *you* have to actually eat, chew and swallow, today, hungry, in your own body. Hegel's System, for Kierkegaard, is like that textbook: comprehensive about "Spirit" in general, silent about the fact that *you*, this individual, must choose what to do with your life *right now*, without the comfort of first seeing the whole picture.

### 1. The single individual (*den Enkelte*)

- Kierkegaard's target is **Hegel's System**: the claim that reality is the self-development of Absolute Spirit (*Geist*), that the individual is a moment in a total rational process, and that "the truth is the whole."
- Kierkegaard's counter: the System explains everything **except** the existing individual who is supposed to understand it. To exist is to be *particular*, *temporal*, *passionate* and *deciding* — none of which is capturable by Hegel's abstract, mediating categories.
- The single individual (*den Enkelte*) is Kierkegaard's positive category: each person stands alone before the ethical demand and, ultimately, before God — a standing no "System," no "crowd" and no "age" can perform on one's behalf.
- **"The crowd is untruth":** authentic existence requires standing alone; the crowd (*Mængden*) is precisely the mechanism by which individuals evade the responsibility of deciding for themselves, hiding inside collective opinion.

### 2. "Truth is subjectivity" (PYQ 2020 Q3(a), 20 marks)

- The claim is argued by the pseudonym **Johannes Climacus** in the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846). Per Kierkegaard's own instruction — the "First and Last Declaration" appended to the *Postscript* — cite the pseudonym, not Kierkegaard directly: write "Climacus argues…", not "Kierkegaard says in the *Postscript*…"; the pseudonymous voice matters.
- **What it does NOT mean:** it is *not* the claim that "anything I believe is true" — that would be relativism, and it is the single most common misreading examiners are trained to penalise.
- **What it means:** for ethical and religious truth, the **mode of appropriation** matters more than the bare objective content. *How* one holds a belief — with infinite passionate inwardness, personal commitment, risk — is the locus of truth for the *existing* subject.
- **Objective uncertainty + inward passion — the technical formula:** Climacus's fullest statement is that truth, for the existing individual in matters of the ethical-religious, is "an objective uncertainty held fast in the most passionate inwardness." This has two halves that must both be kept:
  - **Objective uncertainty:** one cannot *prove*, from a neutral, disinterested standpoint, that God exists or that the Incarnation occurred; the evidence available to detached reason underdetermines the question.
  - **Inward passion:** precisely because objective certainty is unavailable, the individual's own risk-taking, committed appropriation is what makes the relation a *truth-relation* at all — not indifferent assent to a probable proposition, but a wager staked with one's whole existence.
- **Worked example:** to "know" that God exists as an entry in a philosophical system is not the same as *existing before God* in faith, trembling and passionate commitment. The philosophy student who can state Anselm's ontological argument accurately has not thereby related to God as Kierkegaard's religious individual must.
- **The dialectical complement — "subjectivity is untruth":** the existing subject is also *in untruth* (sin, finitude, non-identity with the eternal) and needs the leap to overcome this. Truth-as-subjectivity and untruth-as-subjectivity are two faces of the same existential situation, not a contradiction to be embarrassed about.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** "Truth is subjectivity" concerns the mode of appropriation for ethical and religious truth — not a general theory that factual truth varies with passion.

### 3. The anti-Hegel argument, step by step (PYQ 2023 Q1(d), 10 marks)

1. Hegel's System claims that Absolute Spirit is the true subject of history, and that individuals are its moments/vehicles — the "universal spirit" is more real, more final, than any one person.
2. But a **system** — a completed set of logically mediated categories — can only ever be *about* existence; it cannot itself *exist*, choose, suffer or die.
3. The philosopher who *builds* the System is himself an existing individual: he must still eat, decide, age and die while writing the book that claims to have superseded individuality.
4. Therefore the System cannot, without self-contradiction, claim to have absorbed the individual who constructs and understands it.
5. **Conclusion, in Kierkegaard's own compressed form:** "A logical system is possible; an existential system is impossible." Logic can be closed and completed because it deals with timeless relations between concepts; existence cannot be closed and completed because the existing individual is always still *becoming*, still deciding, never finished until death.
6. Consequently, the individual — not universal Spirit — is "the essence of spirit" in the sense that matters for how one is actually to live: spirit is realised only in the concrete self-relation of a particular existing person, not abstractly in a world-historical process that runs over any given individual's head.

### 4. What this critique does and does not prove

- It is a **critique of systematic closure**, not proof that Hegel simply "erases" every individual from his philosophy. Kierkegaard's target is specifically the *claim to completeness* — that philosophy could, in principle, view existence "sub specie aeterni" (under the aspect of eternity) as a finished whole.
- Kierkegaard does not offer a rival comprehensive metaphysics to replace Hegel's; his method is deliberately indirect and non-systematic, which is itself part of the argument, not a mere stylistic choice.

### Focused objection, reply and residual

| Objection | Kierkegaard's reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| **Hegelian reply:** the "existing individual" whose truth cannot be systematised is simply an unreflective, immediate stage of consciousness that dialectical philosophy legitimately *sublates* (preserves-and-cancels) into a higher, more adequate standpoint. | No system can contain the one who *builds* it; the systematiser must still exist, choose and die while writing. "A logical system is possible; an existential system is impossible." | ⚠️ This is the sharpest single sentence to deploy in any Kierkegaard-vs-Hegel answer, but it does not by itself refute Hegel's claim that individuality is preserved-and-transformed rather than erased; it shifts the burden rather than closing the debate. |
| **"Truth is subjectivity" collapses into relativism.** | Kierkegaard/Climacus distinguishes *what* is believed from *how* it is believed; he does not deny objective truth for logic, mathematics or empirical science — he denies its *sufficiency* for existential/religious truth specifically. | ⚠️ Whether "sufficiency" can be denied for one domain without licensing it everywhere is a live methodological question critics keep pressing. |
| **Elitism of the individual** — elevating the single individual above the community undermines ethics and social responsibility. | "The crowd is untruth" precisely *because* it enables ethical evasion; genuine responsibility requires the individual to stand alone before the ethical/religious demand rather than diffusing it into anonymous collective opinion. | The limited positive account of institutions and community remains a genuine, acknowledged weakness of the position. |

### Argument ledger: assumption, inference and cost

1. A completed System claims a standpoint from which the whole is intelligible.
2. An existing person is temporal, unfinished and must decide without occupying that standpoint.
3. Therefore a correct account of the whole cannot perform first-person appropriation.
4. **Cost:** inwardness can appear insulated from public criticism.

The strongest Hegelian reply invokes recognition and ethical life: individuality is socially mediated, not erased. Kierkegaard’s residual survives—no social theory can make my decision for me. The unresolved issue is how inward appropriation remains publicly criticisable.

2. *Den Enkelte* (the single individual) is Kierkegaard's positive category — standing alone before the ethical/religious demand.
3. "The crowd is untruth" — collective opinion is a mechanism of evasion.
4. "Truth is subjectivity" is Climacus's claim in the *Postscript* (1846) — always attribute to the pseudonym.
5. Technical formula: "an objective uncertainty held fast in the most passionate inwardness."
6. It concerns the *mode of appropriation* for ethical-religious truth — NOT relativism about facts.
7. Dialectical complement: "subjectivity is untruth" — sin, finitude, need for the leap.
8. Anti-Hegel argument: the systematiser is an existing, unfinished individual — "a logical system is possible; an existential system is impossible."
9. This is a critique of closure, not an erasure claim; no rival comprehensive metaphysics is offered.
10. Common trap: "truth is subjectivity = relativism" — always pre-empt this misreading in an answer.

### Three spheres with no automatic escalator

```text
THE THREE SPHERES OF EXISTENCE (qualitative, not chronological)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
   AESTHETIC  ──leap──▶  ETHICAL  ──leap──▶  RELIGIOUS
   (the moment,          (universal          (the absurd,
    enjoyment,            duty,               faith,
    irony,                 marriage,           the single
    detachment)            vocation)           individual
                                                before God)
        │                      │                     │
   limit: BOREDOM,        limit: GUILT           limit: THE
   FRAGMENTATION,       (universal demands       PARADOX (not
   DESPAIR             impossible to           rational, not
                        fulfil perfectly)        mediated)
```

Caption: the arrows are **leaps**, not staircases. Nothing about living well inside the aesthetic sphere automatically produces the ethical; nothing about moral effort automatically produces faith. Each transition is a qualitative, discontinuous decision — and regression is always possible; Kierkegaard's psychology has no automatic escalator.

**Plain-language start.** Picture three ways of organising a life. One person lives for the next interesting experience and structures nothing — pure appetite for novelty. Another builds a life of duties: a marriage, a career, promises kept — structured, but eventually crushed by the sheer weight of never quite living up to universal moral demands. A third stands, without the comfort of either pure pleasure-seeking or a completed moral resume, directly before the Absolute — risking everything on something reason cannot certify. Kierkegaard's three stages are portraits of these three ways of being a self, each with its own characteristic form of unravelling.

### PART A — The three stages / spheres of existence (PYQ 2022 Q4(a), 20m; PYQ 2024 Q1(e), 10m)

| Stage | Core | Mode | Limit / despair |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aesthetic** | Living for the moment — the interesting, enjoyment, avoidance of binding commitment | Immediacy, detachment, irony (pseudonymous portrayals in *Either/Or*, 1843) | Boredom, fragmentation and despair |
| **Ethical** | Living by universal duty — commitment, marriage, vocation, transparency | The "judge" (Judge William, in *Either/Or*); the self as a responsible agent choosing under universal categories | Guilt — the ethical demands are impossible to fulfil perfectly; the universal cannot accommodate the individual's anguished singularity |
| **Religious** | Standing before God as a singular individual — faith as "the absurd," infinite passion | Abraham in *Fear and Trembling* (1843): the knight of faith; "infinite resignation" plus the double movement of faith | The paradox — faith is not rational, not universal, not mediated by the crowd or the church |

- **Movement between spheres** is not a compulsory psychological chronology and not Hegelian dialectical deduction. Transition requires a *qualitative choice/leap* — a decisive, discontinuous act of will, not a gradual maturation.
- **Teleological suspension of the ethical.** The pseudonym **Johannes de Silentio** analyses Abraham (commanded to sacrifice Isaac) in *Fear and Trembling*. Abraham suspends the *universal* ethical law (thou shalt not kill one's child) for the sake of an *absolute relation to the Absolute* — a private, unprovable relation to God that overrides the public, universal moral order. The text **stages the paradox of faith**; it is not Kierkegaard's straightforward licence for private revelation to override morality in general, and using it that way is a serious misreading.
- **The knight of faith vs the knight of infinite resignation:** the knight of infinite resignation visibly renounces the finite world (gives up Isaac, gives up happiness, in resigned grief); the knight of faith, "by virtue of the absurd," receives the finite back — Abraham gets Isaac back — while *simultaneously* having made the total inward renunciation. Outwardly, the knight of faith is indistinguishable from an ordinary bourgeois citizen; the difference is entirely in the mode of inward relation.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** The three stages are qualitative spheres, not temporal stages — one does not "grow out of" the aesthetic by maturation; one leaps, and relapse is always possible.

### PART B — Anxiety, dread and the presupposition of the leap

- **Anxiety/dread (*Angest*)**, presented by the pseudonym **Vigilius Haufniensis** ("watchman of Copenhagen") in *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), is the "**dizziness of freedom**": possibility discloses that one *can* choose and fail. It is not merely fear with greater intensity — see the fear-vs-anxiety table below.
- **Canonical example (innocence and the abyss):** a man standing at the edge of an abyss looks down and grows dizzy — but the dizziness is as much *in his own eye* as in the abyss itself. In innocence there is no knowledge of good and evil, yet anxiety is already present, because freedom's possibility is already dimly sensed. Anxiety is the psychological presupposition of the "leap" by which sin — and thereby full, responsible selfhood — enters.
- **Anxiety is "a sympathetic antipathy and an antipathetic sympathy"** — we are simultaneously drawn to and repelled by the very possibility that makes us anxious; this double structure is what distinguishes anxiety from ordinary fear or aversion.

**FEAR vs ANXIETY — a distinction repeated with each thinker's own vocabulary across this session:**

| | Fear (*Frygt*) | Anxiety/dread (*Angest*) |
|---|---|---|
| Object | A determinate entity in the world — a dog, an exam, a fall | No determinate object — Kierkegaard: "the object of anxiety is a *nothing*" |
| What it is really about | The threatening thing itself | My own **possibility** — the "possibility of being able" |
| Direction | I flee toward the world, seeking shelter in the familiar | It individualises — ordinary meanings recede and I confront my own freedom |
| Disclosive value | Discloses a threat | Discloses freedom and the groundlessness of existence — a privileged mood |

### PART C — Despair and its structure

- **Despair**, analysed by the pseudonym **Anti-Climacus** in *The Sickness unto Death* (1849), is a **misrelation in the self's relation to itself** and to the power that established it (God). The self, for Kierkegaard, is not a substance but a *relation that relates itself to itself* — and despair is what happens when this reflexive relation goes wrong.
- **Forms of despair (the standard taxonomy):**
  - **Not willing to be oneself** — despairingly refusing one's own concrete, particular self, wishing to be someone/something else (weakness).
  - **Willing, defiantly, to be oneself** — without the grounding relation to the power (God) that established the self, asserting a self-sufficient, self-authored identity (defiance).
- **These are pseudonymous analyses with distinct voices** and must not be silently merged with each other or with Sartre's use of the word "despair" in the lecture delivered in 1945 and published in 1946, where the term concerns acting without relying on outcomes beyond one's control. Kierkegaard's despair is a theological-psychological diagnosis of the self's misrelation; Sartre's despair is a practical stance toward uncertain consequences. Same word, different structure, different thinker — never conflate them.

### PART D — Indirect communication and pseudonymity (the leap's method)

**Why this is examinable.** Kierkegaard's use of pseudonyms is not incidental biography; it is the *method* by which the doctrine of subjective truth is delivered, and PYQs on subjectivity/authenticity reward showing you understand this connection.

**The argument, numbered:**
1. Objective truth (historical, scientific, metaphysical) can be transmitted **directly**: state a result, the recipient receives it, and nothing about their existence need change.
2. Ethical and religious truth is not a result; it is a **mode of existing**, an appropriation in inwardness.
3. If such truth were communicated directly, the recipient would take it over as a *result* — information, doctrine, something one can know without becoming — and the communication would thereby **falsify** exactly what it communicates.
4. Therefore ethical-religious truth requires **indirect communication** (*indirekte Meddelelse*): a form that withholds the result, refuses authority, and forces the reader to relate to the possibility *for himself*.
5. The model is **Socratic maieutics** — the midwife who brings to birth what the other must produce, who "**deceives into the truth**." The author becomes a *vanishing occasion*, not a teacher with a doctrine.
6. Therefore, **pseudonymity**: the pseudonyms are not disguises for a shy author but **personified existence-possibilities**, each writing from *within* a sphere of existence, unable to see beyond it.

**The pseudonyms and their standpoints (reproduce this table exactly):**

| Pseudonym | Work (year) | Standpoint written from |
|---|---|---|
| **Victor Eremita** (editor) | *Either/Or* (1843) | Presents the aesthetic ("A") and ethical (Judge William) papers side by side without deciding between them — the reader must choose |
| **Johannes de Silentio** | *Fear and Trembling* (1843) | One who admires faith but does **not** have it — hence the "silence": he can describe Abraham but not understand him |
| **Constantin Constantius** | *Repetition* (1843) | The aesthetic-experimental observer |
| **Vigilius Haufniensis** | *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844) | Psychological observer of anxiety, deliberately not a dogmatician |
| **Johannes Climacus** | *Philosophical Fragments* (1844); *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846) | A humorist who is explicitly **not** a Christian — which is why he can pose the problem of *becoming* one |
| **Anti-Climacus** | *The Sickness unto Death* (1849); *Practice in Christianity* (1850) | A Christian at an extraordinarily high level — above Kierkegaard himself, the deliberate mirror image of Climacus |

- **Double authorship.** Kierkegaard also published, *simultaneously and under his own name*, the *Upbuilding/Edifying Discourses*. This "double authorship" is deliberate: the signed religious writings stand beside the pseudonymous works as the direct pole of a single strategy — pseudonymity is not evasion but method.
- **Kierkegaard's own instruction.** The "First and Last Declaration," appended to the *Postscript* (1846), acknowledges authorship of the pseudonymous books while insisting he is only their "author's author," a **prompter** (*souffleur*) who has produced characters — and asks that citations name the pseudonym, not himself. Write "Johannes de Silentio argues…" or "Climacus holds…"; writing "Kierkegaard says in *Fear and Trembling*…" ignores an instruction the author printed.
- **The leap ("leap of faith"), with provenance discipline.** The passage from the ethical to the religious sphere cannot be *mediated* by reason (contra Hegel, for whom everything is dialectically mediated). Faith is a paradox — that the eternal has entered time, that the universal ethical law can be suspended — and it is *absurd* by rational standards, yet the highest form of authentic existence. **Provenance caution:** Kierkegaard writes of "the leap" (*Springet*) and of faith; the fused phrase "leap of faith" is the **English tradition's** formulation, not a literal translation of a single Kierkegaardian term — write "the leap — what the English tradition calls the leap of faith."

**Strongest objections → replies:**

| Objection | Reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect communication makes Kierkegaard's own position unattributable — one can never pin him down, insulating him from criticism. | The signed discourses and *The Point of View for My Work as an Author* (written 1848, published posthumously 1859) supply the direct pole and an explicit account of the strategy. | *The Point of View* is itself a retrospective self-interpretation and is disputed as evidence. |
| If subjective truth cannot be said directly, the *thesis* "truth is subjectivity" cannot be said directly either — the method is self-undermining. | Climacus concedes this: the *Postscript* ends by **revoking itself**, and Kierkegaard's texts, like the early Wittgenstein's ladder (*Tractatus* 6.54), are meant to be discarded once climbed. | A first-class exam observation: Kierkegaard, the early Wittgenstein and Hegel's Unhappy Consciousness all end with a text that consumes itself. |

**Executable verdict:** pseudonymity is the formal counterpart of "truth is subjectivity" — because ethical-religious truth is a mode of existing rather than a result, it can be communicated only in a form that refuses to deliver a result. The method's cost is that it is self-consuming; its power is that the cost is intended.

### The missing blueprint and self-making

```text
FOR A MANUFACTURED OBJECT (the paper-knife / coupe-papier):
   Artisan conceives DESIGN first  ──▶  THEN produces the object
        ESSENCE                              EXISTENCE
        (concept, function)                  (the physical knife)

TRADITIONAL THEISM extends this to MAN:
   God conceives "human nature"    ──▶  THEN creates individuals
        ESSENCE                              EXISTENCE

SARTRE: there is no God — no divine artisan — so for MAN:
   Man EXISTS first, encounters himself, surges up in the world
        EXISTENCE
                          ──▶  and DEFINES himself afterwards
                                  ESSENCE (self-made, never finished)
```

Caption: the whole slogan is a *denial* riding on an *analogy*. Sartre first grants that the artisan/artefact picture is correct for paper-knives and (on the theistic view) would be correct for humans too, if a divine artisan existed. He then removes the artisan for humans specifically — and the order of essence/existence flips.

**Plain-language start.** A paper-knife is manufactured to cut paper: its *purpose* (essence) exists in the maker's mind before the *object* (existence) is produced. Traditional religious thought pictured human beings the same way — God as the divine craftsman with a "human nature" blueprint in mind, then creating individuals to that specification. Sartre's move is simple to state and enormous in consequence: remove the craftsman, and there is no blueprint left to precede us. We simply turn up in the world — and only then, through what we do, decide what we are.

### 1. Sartre: "existence precedes essence" (PYQ 2024 Q4(a), partial — essence/existence half; freedom half linked in Lesson 7)

- **The slogan and its exact meaning:** for a manufactured object (Sartre's paper-knife/*coupe-papier*), the artisan first conceives its design (essence/function), then produces it (existence) — essence precedes existence. Traditional philosophy and theism treated *man* analogously: God conceives "human nature" and then creates individuals to that blueprint.
- **Sartre denies this.** There is no God — atheism is a *premise*, not a conclusion, of Sartre's existentialism. Therefore there is no prior design. "Man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world — and defines himself afterwards."
- **Consequence for the for-itself (*pour-soi*):** no predetermined blueprint exhausts human reality. Biology, history and society form facticity and constraint, but they do not by themselves settle the meaning or project one takes up. "Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself" belongs to Sartre's lecture delivered in 1945 and published in 1946 and must be balanced by *situation* — it is not a claim that circumstances are irrelevant.
- **Why it entails atheism for Sartre's version:** if God existed, essence *would* precede existence (God would be the divine artisan); so the slogan, in this precise Sartrean form, is incompatible with theism. **Note carefully:** this is *Sartre's* reading. Kierkegaard is an existentialist *and* a theist — so the slogan in its Sartrean form is not definitional of all existentialism.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** Sartre's atheistic argument denies a predetermined human blueprint; it does not erase biological or social facticity.

### 2. Heidegger's rejection of the slogan

- In the "**Letter on Humanism**" (written 1946, published 1947), Heidegger explicitly **disowns** Sartre's reading of him.
- "**The essence of Dasein lies in its existence**" (*Being and Time* §9) does **not** mean "existence precedes essence" in Sartre's voluntarist sense. For Heidegger, this is an **ontological** claim: Dasein's "what" (essence) is not a fixed *quidditas* (a "what-it-is") but its very *manner of being* — its being-open-to-Being, its *Existenz* (standing-out into the clearing of Being).
- **The charge against Sartre:** Sartre, Heidegger argues, remains trapped in the "**metaphysics of subjectivity**" — he merely *reverses* the essence/existence priority while keeping the humanistic framework (man at the centre of the inquiry). Heidegger insists the real question is not *man's self-creation* but **Being** (*Sein*) itself — man is the "**shepherd of Being**," not the lord of beings.
- **Exam implication:** never attribute "existence precedes essence" to Heidegger without immediately noting his repudiation; this precise distinction is directly tested (see the PYQ application below and Lesson 1's trap table).

### 3. The exact scope discipline — avoiding the homogenisation trap

| Thinker | Position on "existence precedes essence" |
|---|---|
| **Sartre** | Explicit source of the familiar public formula in the lecture delivered in 1945 and published in 1946 |
| **Kierkegaard** | Never uses the phrase; his critique of Hegel's System works at a different level — the existing individual resisting closure, not a doctrine about manufactured-object analogies |
| **Heidegger** | Explicitly and publicly repudiates the Sartrean reading as a humanistic misreading of his own, textually similar-sounding, but ontologically different sentence |

- A correct answer on "what do existentialist thinkers mean by 'existence precedes essence'" must therefore present **one** source (Sartre), **one** explicit repudiation (Heidegger), and **one** non-participant whose critique runs on separate tracks (Kierkegaard) — never a single shared doctrine.

### Focused objection, reply and residual

| Objection | Reply | Residual |

| If there is no fixed human essence and no *a priori* values, on what basis does anyone choose anything? ### Nietzsche, Camus and the humanist dispute

Nietzsche’s “death of God” supplies the cultural crisis: inherited values lose transcendent warrant, opening nihilism and the task of value-creation. Sartre radicalises the predicament—there is no divinely fixed human nature—yet makes every project responsible for an image of humanity.

The residual is whether Sartrean humanism escapes anthropocentrism or merely gives it a freedom-based form.

### Consciousness as directed openness

```text
THE "STOMACH OF CONSCIOUSNESS" PICTURE (what both Husserl and Sartre REJECT)
   Mind = a container  ──▶  holding inner ideas/representations
                             which stand BETWEEN us and the world

SARTRE'S PICTURE (radicalised from Husserl)
   Consciousness = "a great emptiness, a wind blowing toward objects"
   It has NO thing-like interior. It is always consciousness OF
   something OUT THERE — never of itself as a first object.
        │
        ▼
   No ego inside. No contents inside. Nothing to inspect except
   the WORLD it is directed at, plus a silent, non-positional
   awareness that it is doing the directing.
```

Caption: both Husserl and Sartre reject the "stomach" model in which ideas are contents digested inside the mind. Sartre's radical step is to say that if you take this rejection seriously enough, there is *nothing left inside consciousness at all* — not even an inner "I."

**Plain-language start.** When you are absorbed in reading this sentence, are you also, at the same moment, watching yourself read it, the way you might watch someone else? Ordinarily, no — you are simply *directed at* the sentence. Sartre's claim is that this directedness is *all* consciousness is: remove the object it is aimed at, and there is no separate "self-stuff" left behind to inspect. Consciousness is not a thing with an inside; it is the very activity of being-aimed-at-something.

### PART A — The shared phenomenological floor (state this first, or the contrast is unearned)

1. **Intentionality.** Sartre takes over Husserl's thesis that consciousness is always **consciousness *of* something** and is directed beyond itself; the intentional object is not an inner picture standing between us and the world.
2. **Anti-representationalism.** Both refuse the "stomach of consciousness" model in which ideas are contents digested inside the mind.
3. **Pre-reflective self-awareness.** Both accept that consciousness is tacitly aware of itself without first turning itself into an object.
4. **Method.** Both describe *how* things are given rather than inferring hidden causes behind appearances.

### PART B — Sartre's radicalisation, six steps

1. **No contents.** If intentionality is taken seriously, consciousness has **no thing-like interior**: it is "a great emptiness, a wind blowing toward objects."
2. **No inhabitant.** The Ego is **not** a transcendental subject *inside* consciousness; it is a **transcendent object constituted in reflection** (*The Transcendence of the Ego*, 1936–37) — the "I" is met *in the world*, alongside other objects, not behind the looking.
3. **Pre-reflective cogito.** Consciousness is **non-positionally** aware of itself while **positionally** directed at the world; reflection is a later, second-order act that *produces* the Ego as its object.
4. **Why "nothing."** Consciousness is *néant* (nothing) — not because it does not exist, but because it **lacks the self-identity and plenitude of being-in-itself**: it has no fixed essence, no content, no self-coincidence.
5. **Nihilating activity.** Consciousness introduces nothingness into being by standing back from what is given — questioning it, imagining alternatives, negating it — a capacity Sartre calls **nihilation** (*néantisation*).
6. **Freedom follows.** Because consciousness is nothing fixed, it cannot be determined the way a thing is determined; its lack of a fixed essence *is* the ontological root of freedom (developed fully in Lesson 7).

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** Consciousness is *néant* not because it does not exist, but because it lacks the self-identity and plenitude of being-in-itself.

### PART C — Contrast with Husserl

| Axis | **Husserl** | **Sartre** |
|---|---|---|
| Status of the ego | Transcendental reduction discloses **constituting subjectivity**; the pure ego has habitualities and sedimented history | The ego is a **transcendent object of reflection**; consciousness is **non-egological** |
| What unifies experience | The ego, together with inner time-consciousness, unifies streams and object-identities | The **object and the intentional structure** unify consciousness; no inner unifier is needed |
| Core task | **Constitution** of meaning/noematic sense for a transcendental subjectivity | **Disclosure** of transcendent being and of the contingency ("de trop," in excess) of what is |
| Metaphysical tendency | A transcendental-idealist strand after the *Ideas* | Initially a **phenomenological realism** — being-in-itself is not constituted by consciousness |
| Place of negation | Analysed **within** intentional acts, judgement and fulfilment/frustration | Nothingness becomes the **ontological structure of the for-itself** itself |
| Result for the self | An ego that can be studied in its constituting life | Selfhood as a **task/project**, never a substance — bad faith becomes possible |
| Freedom | Not the organising problem of the method | The direct consequence of consciousness's lack of being |

### PART D — Sartre's objections to the transcendental ego, and the required qualification

**Sartre's four numbered objections:**
1. **Superfluity:** a transcendental ego duplicates consciousness with an unnecessary inner observer; intentionality already unifies experience through its objects.
2. **Opacity:** an ego *in* consciousness would introduce something non-transparent into what is, by its nature, translucent to itself.
3. **Priority:** a constituting ego would have to pre-exist its acts — conflicting with the primacy of the spontaneous field, and later with "existence precedes essence" itself.
4. **Reification:** it turns a spontaneous transcendental field into a substance-like thing, reopening the very substantialism phenomenology set out to bracket.

**Replies and the required qualification (the mark-winning paragraph):**
- **Husserlian reply:** the pure ego is *not* an inner object at all but the formal pole of acts; Husserl's analyses of habituality and time-consciousness answer real problems of identity-over-time that a strictly non-egological account must also solve.
- **Textual qualification:** the opposition is genuinely **contested**. In the *Logical Investigations*, Husserl himself denied finding an ego as an immanent item; the "pure ego" is admitted only later, in the *Ideas*. Scholars such as **Dan Zahavi** therefore read Sartre as a **non-egological reorientation *within* phenomenology**, not a wholesale rejection of Husserl.
- **Residual force:** even so, the two projects diverge in *aim* — Husserl explains how objectivity is **constituted**; Sartre explains how a being that is nothing can be **free and self-deceiving**.
- **Do not write** "Sartre refuted Husserl." Write: "Sartre keeps intentionality and pre-reflective self-awareness, removes the ego from consciousness, and converts the resulting emptiness into an ontology of freedom."

### PART E — Presuppositions and traps

**Presuppositions:**
- **P1:** intentionality is exhaustive — nothing is *in* consciousness except as its object.
- **P2:** consciousness is translucent; nothing opaque can inhabit it (this is what rules out both the ego and the Freudian unconscious, and generates bad faith instead of repression — Lesson 8).
- **P3:** being-in-itself is fully positive and self-identical; "nothing" is intelligible only as the contrast to that plenitude.
- **P4:** reflection is a modification of consciousness, not a neutral observation of it.

**Traps:**
- "Consciousness is nothing" does **not** mean consciousness does not exist or is an illusion. It means **no essence, no content, no self-coincidence**.
- Do not confuse Sartre's *néant* with Buddhist emptiness or Vedāntic negation; the reference class is Hegelian negativity and Husserlian intentionality, not an Indian-philosophy parallel, unless a question explicitly demands a cross-tradition comparison.
- Do not attribute to Husserl the view that there is *no* ego (that is early Husserl, and Sartre) — nor to Sartre the view that there is no self at all (there is a self, but only as a *constituted, worldly* object, never as an inhabitant of consciousness).
- Do not omit the shared ground (Part A); a pure-contrast answer reads as an unearned textbook list.
- Do not drop the *Transcendence of the Ego* reference — it is precisely where the 2026 examiner's difference is argued.

## Lesson 6 — Why can the self never coincide with itself?

### Two modes of being and an impossible fusion

```text
EN-SOI (being-in-itself)               POUR-SOI (being-for-itself)
════════════════════════               ═══════════════════════════
   A STONE.                               CONSCIOUSNESS.
   It simply IS.                          It is always BEYOND itself.
   Full. Solid. Self-identical.            It questions, negates,
   "It is what it is."                     projects, imagines.
   No gaps, no distance                    "It is what it is NOT,
   from itself.                            and is NOT what it is."
        │                                          │
        ▼                                          ▼
   No freedom needed —                    Freedom is built in —
   there is nothing TO free,               there is a permanent GAP
   because there is no gap                 between what it is
   between what it is and                  (facticity) and what it
   what it could be.                       is becoming (transcendence).
```

Caption: the stone has no distance from itself — it cannot fail to be a stone. Consciousness, by contrast, is never simply identical with what it presently is: it is always also its unrealised future project and always also more than its fixed past. That built-in gap is what Sartre calls nothingness, and it is the entire difference between en-soi and pour-soi.

**Plain-language start.** Ask "what is a stone?" and the honest answer is: a stone just is what it is, completely, with nothing left over. Now ask "what are you?" — and notice that any answer ("a student," "a philosopher," "kind") is instantly incomplete, because you are also whatever you choose to become next, and you can always step back and question, doubt or reject the very answer just given. That permanent incompleteness, that inability to simply *coincide* with any description of yourself, is what Sartre is naming with the pour-soi/en-soi distinction.

### PART A — The full contrast table (PYQ 2025 Q1(c), 10m; PYQ 2023 Q3(c), 15m)

| | **En-soi** (Being-in-itself) | **Pour-soi** (Being-for-itself) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Full, solid, self-identical, opaque | Nihilating, self-surpassing, never self-identical |
| Formula | "It is what it is" | "It is what it is not and is not what it is" |
| Examples | A stone, a table — they simply *are* | Consciousness — always *beyond* itself, intending what it is not, negating what it is |
| Freedom | None — determined, complete | Structurally inescapable, yet situated/factical |
| Temporality | Not defined by projective temporal transcendence | Ecstatic — related to past facticity and future project |
| Relationship | Groundless, brute ("*de trop*" — in excess, contingent) | Desires to *become* en-soi (achieve full self-identity) — impossible; a "for-itself-in-itself" (= God) is a contradiction |

### PART B — "Consciousness is what it is not and is not what it is"

- **"Is what it is not":** consciousness is always *ahead of itself*, projecting toward possibilities it does not yet possess. It *is* (in the sense of being-defined-by) its future — which does not yet exist.
- **"Is not what it is":** consciousness can never coincide with its past or its facticity. The waiter *is* a waiter (facticity) but simultaneously *is not* a waiter (he transcends the role, is free to quit) — this exact formula is the ontological engine behind bad faith.
- This **non-self-identity** is possible through **nihilation** (*néantisation*): consciousness introduces *nothingness* (*néant*) into being — it stands back from what is and negates it (questions it, imagines alternatives, projects beyond it). The pour-soi is "**the being by which nothingness comes into the world**."

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** The formula is the key to Sartre's entire system — it grounds freedom, bad faith, temporality and the "useless passion" of the pour-soi's desire to be en-soi.

### PART C — Lack, the impossible ideal, and "useless passion"

- The pour-soi is structured by **lack** (*manque*): it is always missing something — its own completion, its own coincidence with itself — and this lack is precisely what propels it toward projects and possibilities. Desire, for Sartre, is the lived experience of this structural lack.
- **The impossible ideal:** the pour-soi perpetually seeks to *become* en-soi — to achieve full self-identity — while *retaining* the self-presence and freedom of the pour-soi. To be simultaneously fully self-identical (en-soi) and fully self-aware, free consciousness (pour-soi) is the traditional philosophical/theological definition of **God** — and it is, for Sartre, a strict contradiction, since the two modes of being are mutually exclusive by definition.
- **"Man is a useless passion":** human reality is defined by this impossible desire — consciousness endlessly pursues a self-coincidence it can never achieve without ceasing to be consciousness at all. This is Sartre's deliberately deflationary closing formula for the entire ontology of *Being and Nothingness*.

### Facticity, transcendence and the failed God-project

Facticity includes body, past, place and social location—what is already given. Transcendence is projection beyond those givens toward possibilities. The for-itself seeks the impossible *pour-soi-en-soi*: complete, self-grounding solidity that remains free. Thing-like completeness would extinguish the freedom seeking it. “Useless passion” therefore describes a structural impossibility, not practical advice to despair. A persistent residual is how embodiment belongs to consciousness without recreating substance dualism.

Caption: the word "condemned" is doing real philosophical work — it signals that freedom, for Sartre, is not a comfortable achievement one might opt into, but a burden built into the very structure of being conscious, from which there is no exit, not even by inaction.

**Plain-language start.** Imagine being handed a decision you never asked to make, in a situation you never asked to be placed in, with no rulebook and no one to consult — and discovering that even walking away from the decision counts as a decision. That is Sartre's picture of the human condition: we are "thrown" (a word he borrows from Heidegger, and it should not be silently made Sartre's own) into situations not of our choosing, yet we can never simply *be* those situations the way a stone is a stone — we must always take up a stance toward them.

### PART A — Radical freedom

- **Freedom is not a property we possess; it is what we ARE.** The *pour-soi* is *constituted* by freedom — it is the nihilating, self-transcending movement of consciousness that continually surpasses what it is toward what it is not yet.
- **"Man is condemned to be free"** (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*, delivered in 1945 and published in 1946): this is the canonical slogan form supplied and popularised by the public lecture. *Being and Nothingness* (1943) had already stated the thought in first-person, plural and third-person participial variants, including "I am condemned to be free," the claim that human beings are not free to cease being free, and "man being condemned to be free." In each form, we did not choose existence yet cannot escape taking up a stance within situations. "Thrown" is Heidegger's term and should not be silently made Sartre's own; Sartre's point is compatible with, but distinct from, Heidegger's thrownness.
- **"I can always choose, but I ought to know that if I do not choose, I am still choosing"** (PYQ 2021, 15m): inaction, deferral and conformity are themselves *choices* — the attempt to escape choice is already a choice, and typically a choice of **bad faith**.

### PART B — Anguish, abandonment, despair

| Mood | Source | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Anguish** (*angoisse*) | I recognise that I am *free* — my future is radically undetermined; nothing compels me; I stand before the abyss of possibility | Anguish is not fear *of* an object but vertigo before one's own freedom: "I am not sustained by anything, nothing justifies me" |
| **Abandonment** (*délaissement*) | There is no God, no cosmic moral order, no *a priori* values written in an "intelligible heaven" | I must *invent* values through my choosing; I cannot lean on a pre-given ethical framework |
| **Despair** (*désespoir*) | I should not rely on outcomes beyond my action and reasonable probabilities | Sartre's lecture-term differs from Kierkegaard's theological account of despair — never conflate the two |

**Sartre's two canonical anguish examples (name both):**
- **The cliff path.** Walking a narrow path above a precipice, I feel *fear* of the drop — the loose stone, the gust of wind, the world's threat. Then I realise that nothing prevents me from throwing myself over: no barrier within me, no fixed nature. That vertigo is *anguish* — not fear of the abyss but anguish before my own freedom.
- **The gambler.** A man has resolved firmly, yesterday, never to gamble again. Standing at the table today, he discovers that yesterday's resolution is *there* but *powerless* — it must be remade now, and nothing guarantees he will remake it. Anguish is this discovery that my past self cannot bind my present self.

### PART C — Choice as legislating for all humanity

- Sartre adopts a quasi-Kantian universalisability principle: "**in choosing myself, I choose man**" — every choice implies an image of what human beings *ought* to be. If I choose to marry, I affirm that monogamy is valuable *for everyone*; I cannot choose "just for me" in moral isolation.
- This generates the *weight* of responsibility and the anguish that accompanies it — I am not merely deciding my own fate but *legislating* an image of humanity.
- **Objection:** this seems to conflict with the claim that there are no *a priori* values — whence the universalisability demand, if no prior moral framework is given? **Sartre's reply:** the *form* of universalisability is inherent in the *structure* of free choice itself, not imported from a pre-existing ethics — a reply critics find circular (see the criticism table below).

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** I am not merely deciding my own fate but legislating an image of humanity.

### PART D — Freedom and determinism (PYQ 2019 Q4(c), 15m)

- In the early Sartrean account, causal, psychological and social explanations cannot convert the for-itself into a thing whose project is wholly fixed in advance.
- **Key argument:** a *motive* (desire, emotion, situation) can only become a reason for action through the *meaning* I confer on it. But meaning-conferral is itself an act of the pour-soi (nihilation/transcendence, Lesson 5–6). Therefore, no motive determines me *unless I freely constitute it as a motive*.
- **"There are no excuses"** expresses responsibility for taking up motives and situations; it should **not** become the psychologically implausible claim that trauma, illness, coercion or affect can simply be dismissed at will.
- **Situation and freedom:** Sartre does not deny that facticity limits practical possibilities. Early Sartre nevertheless calls freedom "absolute" in the structural sense that the for-itself cannot become a completed thing: it must take up a stance. This is not omnipotence, and coercion, trauma and social structures can constrain both action and the intelligibility of alternatives.
- **Criticism:** is this psychologically and socially plausible? The Marxist critique: Sartre ignores structural oppression (poverty, racism) that limits not just physical but *cognitive* possibilities. Sartre partly concedes this later in the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960), where he integrates material scarcity and seriality into his account.

### Beauvoir, Marxism and political freedom

Beauvoir shows why “situated” cannot be decorative. Embodiment and oppression do not turn a person into a thing, but they can close projects, resources and recognition. Her *Ethics of Ambiguity* makes freedom reciprocal: willing my freedom coherently requires conditions in which others can act as freedoms too. The reply is politically stronger than early voluntarism; the residual is institutional. Existential responsibility motivates resistance but does not by itself distribute duties, resources or power.

### The double evasion of bad faith

```text
EVERY HUMAN REALITY HAS TWO DIMENSIONS:
   FACTICITY (the given: body, past, role, situation)
   TRANSCENDENCE (the self-surpassing: freedom to negate/project beyond)
                              │
        BAD FAITH = collapsing the two into ONE, to escape the tension
                              │
        ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
        ▼                                             ▼
  IDENTIFY WITH FACTICITY ALONE               IDENTIFY WITH TRANSCENDENCE ALONE
  "I am NOTHING BUT this role/thing"          "I am NOTHING BUT pure freedom,
  (denies freedom to be otherwise)             untouched by this situation"
  → the WAITER who over-performs              → the WOMAN who treats her
    "being a waiter"                            captured hand as a mere
                                                  neutral object, denying its
                                                  sexual meaning
```

Caption: bad faith is not simple lying — in an ordinary lie, the deceiver knows the truth and the deceived does not, and they are two different people. In bad faith, the deceiver and the deceived are the *same* consciousness, which is what makes it philosophically puzzling rather than merely dishonest.

**Plain-language start.** Think of someone who insists "I just *am* my job — nothing more" to avoid the anxiety of admitting they could quit or change; or someone who insists "this doesn't mean anything" about a situation that plainly does, to avoid admitting they are making a choice by staying in it. Both are managing the discomfort of freedom by pretending to be less free than they are — but Sartre's puzzle is: how can you fool *yourself*, when the one being fooled and the one doing the fooling are the very same consciousness?

### PART A — Definition and structure

- **Bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*) is a form of **self-deception** in which the pour-soi flees from its freedom by pretending to be a fixed *thing* (en-soi) — or, conversely, flees from its facticity by pretending to be pure transcendence.
- **The exact structure:** every human reality has facticity (the given: body, past, social position, situation) and transcendence (the self-surpassing: freedom to negate facticity, to project beyond what one presently is). **Bad faith** occurs when a person identifies exclusively with *one* dimension at the expense of the other.
- **Why bad faith is paradoxical:** to deceive myself, I must simultaneously *know* the truth (to conceal it) and *not know* it (to be deceived). Sartre's solution: consciousness is *non-self-identical* — it can hold truth and denial in a single **pre-reflective** stance without splitting into two persons (deceiver and deceived).

### PART B — Sartre's canonical examples

| Example | What happens | Structure of bad faith |
|---|---|---|
| **The waiter** | He performs the role with exaggerated, thing-like precision — every gesture a little too poised, a little too perfectly "waiter" | Sartre reads this as a denial that a social role exhausts the for-itself; role-performance alone is not automatically bad faith, but the over-identification that treats "being a waiter" as a fixed essence *is* |
| **The woman on the date** | A man takes her hand; she leaves it there, "not noticing" — she treats the hand as a mere physical thing, dissociating from the situation's meaning | She denies her *transcendence* (her freedom to accept or reject the advance) by reducing herself to pure *facticity* (a body-object); simultaneously she denies the *facticity* of the gesture (its sexual meaning) by treating it as purely neutral — a double lie |

### PART C — The sincerity paradox and the further canonical example

> **Terminology-sensitivity note.** Sartre's original 1943 French discussion uses period vocabulary that reads as dated and, by contemporary standards, insensitive when transposed directly into English exam-writing. What is philosophically examinable is the *structure of the paradox* Sartre is diagnosing, not the specific period terminology; this session states the structure precisely and names the standard secondary-literature label ("the sincerity paradox") rather than reproducing dated wording as though it were neutral technical vocabulary.

- **The paradox, stated structurally.** Sartre analyses a person who will not straightforwardly affirm an aspect of his own past conduct as defining "what he is," while also not straightforwardly denying that the conduct occurred — he refuses to be *fixed* as one thing, insisting instead that "I am not defined by any one of my acts; I am always beyond them, free to be otherwise." A friend or interlocutor then demands, "Be sincere — admit plainly what you *are*."
- **Why the demand for "sincerity" is itself a form of bad faith.** The demand to "be sincere" — to admit that one *simply is* a fixed thing, definable once and for all by a past pattern of conduct — asks a pour-soi to speak *as if* it were an en-soi. Sincerity, taken as a demand for total, fixed self-coincidence, does not escape bad faith; it is bad faith's mirror image, since it treats the free-for-itself as though it could, and should, collapse entirely into its facticity.
- **The upshot:** both the person who refuses to own his facticity at all (treating himself as pure, unattached transcendence) *and* the person who demands total, fixed sincerity from another (treating a free being as though it were a completed thing) are, on Sartre's analysis, each practising a version of bad faith — one by fleeing facticity, the other by imposing it. This is why Sartre's account of bad faith is not simply "a name for lying to yourself about your job," but a full analysis of the entire pour-soi/en-soi tension applied to personal identity claims generally.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** Sincerity, taken as a demand for total, fixed self-coincidence, does not escape bad faith; it is bad faith's mirror image.

### PART D — Bad faith, inauthenticity and alienation (PYQ 2018 Q1(d), 10m)

The three terms must be connected without pretending that they form one universal causal ladder:

- **Bad faith** is Sartre's technical account of self-evasion: consciousness treats either facticity or transcendence as the whole truth in order to escape the burden of holding both together.
- **Inauthenticity**, in Sartrean discussion, can describe a life organised by such evasion; in Heidegger's technical vocabulary it instead names Dasein's structurally ordinary, unowned absorption in *das Man*. Heideggerian inauthenticity is therefore not simply Sartrean bad faith under another name.
- **Alienation** names estrangement: the self may become detached from its own possibilities, fixed in a social role, or apprehended through the objectifying Look of another. Bad faith can contribute to this estrangement, but alienation also has interpersonal and social dimensions that are not reducible to one act of self-deception.

For the 2018 question, the defensible connection is comparative and qualified: Sartrean bad faith can sustain an inauthentic mode of living and can deepen alienation from one's transcendence, but none of the three terms should be presented as the necessary cause or inevitable result of the other two in every existentialist framework.

### Analytic and psychoanalytic pressure

Freud explains self-deception through repression: one mental system conceals from another. Sartre objects that the censor must somehow recognise what it excludes. His alternative is one non-self-identical consciousness whose pre-reflective awareness is not reflectively avowed. Analytic critics reply that this may rename opacity rather than explain it. Sartre’s best defence is dynamic: bad faith oscillates between “I am only this role” and “this role says nothing about me.” The residual is causal—why the evasion persists and how institutions reward it.

3. Bad faith's paradox: the deceiver and deceived are the same consciousness — solved by the pre-reflective/non-thetic structure of non-self-identical consciousness, not by a Freudian unconscious.
4. The sincerity paradox: demanding total, fixed "sincere" self-definition from another is bad faith's mirror image, not its cure.
5. Terminology-sensitivity: dated period vocabulary in the original example is not reproduced; the structural point is what is examinable.
6. Sartrean inauthenticity can describe a life organised by bad-faith evasion; Heideggerian inauthenticity is the distinct structure of unowned everyday absorption in *das Man*.
7. Alienation is estrangement from one's possibilities or objectification through social roles and the Other's Look; it is broader than bad faith.
8. For the 2018 PYQ, connect the terms conditionally: bad faith may sustain inauthentic living and deepen alienation, but no fixed causal sequence applies across Sartre and Heidegger.
9. Objection: bad faith presupposes what it denies (must know and not know); Sartre's reply uses pre-reflective/non-thetic awareness, not repression.
10. Objection: radical-freedom-based bad faith risks blaming people for adaptive responses to real oppression; situated freedom and the 1960 *Critique* partially answer this.
11. No fully worked-out positive "authenticity" is delivered in *Being and Nothingness* itself.
12. Never call bad faith "just lying" — an ordinary lie needs two people; bad faith needs only one, non-self-identical, consciousness.

### The keyhole, the footstep and the Other

```text
STAGE 1: ABSORBED                          STAGE 2: CAUGHT
════════════════════                        ═══════════════════════
Bent at a keyhole out of                    Footsteps in the hall.
jealousy/curiosity, wholly                  I AM SEEN.

No ego in consciousness —                    Instantly my whole being
just the scene and my                        is transformed: no longer
```

Caption: nothing changes in the room. What changes is that another consciousness has arrived as a *perspective on me* that I cannot occupy or control — and that arrival, not any new fact about the keyhole, is what produces shame.

**Plain-language start.** You can be completely absorbed in something — reading, working, even eavesdropping — with no sense of "myself" at all, just the activity. The moment you realise someone else has seen you doing it, something happens that no physical event in the room explains: you suddenly *have* a self, from the outside, that you did not construct and cannot inspect directly — a self seen through someone else's eyes.

### PART A — The keyhole example and the shame argument

**Bounded relevance note:** being-for-others is necessary for questions on alienation, bad faith or intersubjectivity, but it must not displace the printed freedom/choice spine in every Sartre answer.

**Exact printed subterms:** *le regard* (the Look) · *l'être-pour-autrui* (being-for-others) · *la honte* (shame) · *le corps-pour-autrui* (the body-for-others) · "*objet-autrui*"/"*sujet-autrui*" · "*la chute originelle*" (the original fall) · *conflit* as "the original meaning of being-for-others."

**The argument, numbered:**
1. In being looked at, I experience **shame** — and shame is not a private feeling I might have alone; it is by its structure **shame of myself *before* someone**.
2. "**Shame is by nature recognition**": in being ashamed I *recognise* that I am as the Other sees me. I do not infer the Other; I *live* his presence as the condition of my own new mode of being.
3. The Other is not reached by an explicit inference. Sartre contrasts the Look with Husserlian appresentation and Heideggerian being-with, though neither rival is fairly reduced to an argument from analogy or neutral co-existence. **The Other is given as the subject for whom I am an object.**
4. Therefore, "**the Other is the indispensable mediator between myself and me**": I acquire an **outside**, a "nature," a being I *am* without having chosen it and without being able to know it from within — my *being-for-others*.
5. The Look reveals a dimension of facticity: meanings of my acts and body are constituted from a perspective I cannot control. This is one limit on mastery, not the only constraint Sartre recognises.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** "The Other is the indispensable mediator between myself and me."

### PART B — The dialectic of concrete relations

Since I am objectified by the Other's freedom, I can respond in only two directions, and both fail:

| Attitude | Strategy | Forms | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Assimilate the Other's freedom** — possess the freedom that judges me *while it remains free* | Make myself the object the Other freely chooses | **Love**, language, **masochism** | Love wants to be loved *freely* — but to be loved is to be made an object, so the very freedom sought is destroyed in the possessing. Masochism collapses because I cannot succeed in being pure object while remaining conscious |
| **(b) Transcend the Other's freedom** — reduce him to an object so his look cannot fix me | Make the Other into an object | **Indifference**, **desire**, **sadism**, **hate** | An object cannot confer the recognition I wanted; the moment he looks back, the whole structure reverses. Hate wants the Other's *disappearance*, which would leave my being-for-others intact |

- Therefore, "**conflict is the original meaning of being-for-others.**" The famous line of *Huis clos* (*No Exit*, 1944) — "**L'enfer, c'est les autres**" ("Hell is other people") — follows from this structure.
- **Quote it, then correct the popular misreading.** Sartre himself explained, in a 1965 recorded preface to the play, that the line does **not** mean relations with others are always poisonous; it means that if one's relations with others are twisted, then the Other's judgement becomes the medium of one's own self-torment — and that the three characters in the play are dead, i.e. fixed, unable to change their acts. Making this correction is a high-value move in any answer that quotes the line.

### PART C — Presuppositions and the comparison of four accounts of the Other

**Presuppositions:** (P1) shame is irreducibly relational and cannot be analysed away as self-assessment; (P2) pre-reflective consciousness is egoless, so the ego that appears under the Look is genuinely *conferred*, not merely revealed; (P3) subject and object are exclusive — I cannot be looked-at and looking in the same act, which is what makes reciprocity impossible; (P4) there is no third term (a "we-subject" of the kind Marxists posit) that could stabilise the relation — Sartre discusses and rejects it in Part III of *Being and Nothingness*.

**Comparison — the four accounts of the Other:**

| Thinker | How the Other is reached | Basic relation |
|---|---|---|
| **Husserl** | Passive pairing and appresentation, not a discursive analogy-inference | The Other is non-originally given; an ownness-first circularity remains |
| **Heidegger** | Dasein is always already being-with (*Mitsein*) | Social existence is constitutive, though Sartre presses for the conflictual concrete encounter |
| **Sartre** | The **Look** — the Other is revealed in the *shame* of being objectified | **Conflict**; recognition is structurally unattainable |
| **Levinas** | The **face** (*visage*) of the Other, which commands before it is known | **Ethical asymmetry** — responsibility precedes freedom; explicitly a reply to both Husserl and Sartre |

### PART D — Bounded comparison with Hegel's master–slave recognition

- Hegel's master–slave dialectic also derives self-consciousness through a struggle for recognition between two consciousnesses, each seeking confirmation of its own certainty of self through the other. **The bounded point of agreement:** both Hegel and Sartre treat the encounter with another consciousness as a site of *struggle*, not automatic harmony, and both deny that recognition can be achieved by treating the other as a mere thing.
- **The bounded point of departure:** Hegel's dialectic is developmental — the struggle *resolves*, however unstably, into determinate social shapes (lordship and bondage, and eventually mutual recognition within *Sittlichkeit*, ethical life). Sartre's conflict is *structural and permanent*: on his analysis, no stable synthesis or mutual recognition is available within the ontology of *Being and Nothingness*, because subject/object exclusivity (P3) rules out reciprocity in principle, not merely in this or that historical stage.
- This comparison remains deliberately bounded; Hegel's dialectical method and its full resolution require separate treatment.

### Focused objections, replies and residuals

| Objection | Reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| Sartre generalises from a small set of **pathological** encounters (the peeping Tom, sadism, seduction) to the structure of all human relations. | Sartre's claim is ontological, not statistical: he analyses the *possibility-conditions* of any relation, and conflict is the structure even where it is unnoticed. | ⚠️ Strong objection; Sartre himself moves toward a more social account in the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960), where the "group-in-fusion" allows non-objectifying solidarity — the best evidence the objection landed. |
| Shame is not the primitive datum; **love, trust and being-cared-for** are equally basic and are not objectifications. | Sartre analyses love (Part B(a)) and argues its internal contradiction. | The analysis is compelling only if one accepts P3 (subject/object exclusivity). |
| **Levinas:** to encounter the Other only as a rival freedom is still to think him from the self. | — | Sartre has no reply within *Being and Nothingness*; this is the strongest single line of criticism against the whole account. |

**Executable verdict:** the Look gives a powerful non-inferential account of being exposed to another perspective, but Sartre may generalise conflict from selective examples. Later solidarity, feminist and postcolonial accounts test rather than simply repeat the subject/object model.

No PYQ from 2018–2026 is directly linked to this lesson; the Look is bounded-core doctrine required as background for alienation and for intersubjectivity comparisons that could appear in future papers. A critical discussion of Sartre's account of the Other should use the keyhole example, the shame argument, the dialectic of concrete relations and the four-account comparison table above.

### Beauvoir, Marcel and reciprocal recognition

Beauvoir retains ambiguity and vulnerability while arguing that freedoms can sustain one another under conditions resisting oppression. Marcel’s availability and Jaspers’s existential communication offer further non-objectifying relations. These accounts do not refute the Look: another can make me appear as an object. They challenge its universalisation. Hegel’s recognition can develop toward reciprocity; Sartre’s early account treats conflict as original. The residual is whether the Look captures all encounter or one revealing pathology.

### From entities to the question of Being

```text
HEIDEGGER'S OPENING COMPLAINT:
   Western philosophy has spent millennia asking
   "WHAT beings are there, and what are they like?"
   — and has FORGOTTEN to ask the prior question:
   "WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO SAY THAT SOMETHING 'IS' AT ALL?"
                              │
                              ▼
   ONTOLOGICAL DIFFERENCE: Being ≠ beings
   Being = the intelligibility BY VIRTUE OF WHICH
           entities show up AS entities at all
   Beings = the entities themselves (stones, numbers, you)
                              │
                              ▼
   Which entity should we interrogate to get at the
   Being-question? — DASEIN: the one entity that already,
   pre-theoretically, understands Being and for whom its
   own Being is an issue.
```

Caption: Heidegger is not asking "what exists?" (a question about beings) but "what does 'to exist'/'to be' even mean, such that we can ask about beings at all?" (a question about Being). Confusing these two questions is the single most common way to misread the entire project as a version of existentialist psychology rather than what it actually is: fundamental ontology.

**Plain-language start.** Imagine everyone in a room fluently discussing the properties of hundreds of different objects — colour, weight, function — without anyone ever asking what it even *means* for something to "exist" in the first place, as opposed to being imaginary, fictional or merely possible. That background, unasked question — Heidegger claims — is exactly the one the entire philosophical tradition since Plato has quietly skipped, chasing after answers about *particular kinds* of being while forgetting to ask about Being itself.

### PART A — The Being-question and the ontological difference

- Heidegger's project is **fundamental ontology**, not an existentialist psychology or a general ethics of authenticity (a correction that must open any serious Heidegger answer).
- The **ontological difference** distinguishes **Being** — the intelligibility in virtue of which entities show up as entities — from **particular beings**. Being is not one supreme entity among others (not "the biggest thing" or "God" in a list of things that exist); it is the very horizon within which anything can show up as *an* entity at all.
- **Dasein** is selected as the entity to interrogate because it already has a **pre-ontological understanding of Being** (an inarticulate, practical sense of what it is for things, and itself, "to be") and because its own Being is *at issue for it* — Dasein cares, in the most basic sense, about its own existing, in a way a stone or a hammer does not.
- Existential analysis (moods, death, authenticity — Lessons 11–13) is therefore the *route toward* the Being-question, not an isolated set of psychological topics pursued for their own sake.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** Existential analysis is therefore the route toward the Being-question; moods, death and authenticity are not isolated psychological topics.

### PART B — The existentiell/existential distinction

- **Existentiell (*existenziell*):** the **ontic**, factical, first-person self-understanding any Dasein already has of its own life and possibilities — the concrete, everyday sense in which *I* understand what I am doing, without any philosophical theory.
- **Existential (*existenzial*):** the **ontological** structures the philosophical analytic uncovers as making any such existentiell self-understanding possible in the first place — Care, being-in-the-world, temporality and the rest are *existentialia*, i.e. existential structures, not existentiell facts about any one person's biography.
- **Why the distinction matters for exam answers:** Heidegger's analytic is *existential* (it describes structures true of Dasein as such), even though its evidence is drawn from *existentiell* phenomena (moods, everyday coping, anxiety, death). Confusing the two levels — treating Care or being-toward-death as if they were empirical psychological claims about how people *feel*, rather than ontological structures of *how Dasein is*, is a standing examiner trap across Lessons 11–13.

### PART C — Dasein and its anti-Cartesian, anti-Husserlian orientation

- **Dasein** ("being-there"): the entity each of us is, insofar as its own Being is at issue and it understands Being. It is **not** merely a biological synonym for "human being" — the term is chosen precisely to avoid importing the assumptions ("rational animal," "subject," "consciousness") that Heidegger thinks have distorted the tradition.
- **Anti-Cartesian critique:** the Cartesian picture posits an inner, thinking subject (*res cogitans*) that must then somehow reach an external world (*res extensa*) — generating the classical "problem of the external world" and epistemological scepticism. Heidegger holds this entire picture is derivative: Dasein is never, even for a moment, a worldless inner subject that subsequently makes contact with things; it is **always already** practically absorbed *in* a world (fully developed as being-in-the-world in Lesson 11).
- **Anti-Husserlian critique:** Husserl's method begins with the **epoché** — a reflective suspension of the natural attitude's "general thesis" that the world exists, leading back to a constituting **transcendental ego**. Heidegger's objection is that this starting point is already an abstraction: to bracket the world and retreat to a transcendental ego is to assume that consciousness *could* be world-less — the very Cartesian picture that must be overcome. The more primordial condition is that Dasein is *always already* absorbed, concerned, involved: subject and object are *derivative* precipitates of practical involvement, not the bedrock on which phenomenology should try to build. This critique, applied to the **being-in-the-world versus transcendental ego** contrast, is central to the 2018 Q4(a) approach.
- **Do not equate** being-in-the-world with spatial containment (as if Dasein were simply "located inside" the world the way water is inside a glass) or with Sartrean consciousness; Heidegger's unitary structure is a different kind of claim entirely, developed fully in Lesson 11.

### Focused objections, replies and residuals

| Objection | Source | Reply / counter |
|---|---|---|
| **Obscurity** — *Being and Time* is deliberately obscure; neologisms obstruct rather than clarify. | Carnap (1932), Ayer, Russell, the analytic tradition generally | Heidegger: ordinary language is saturated with the metaphysical tradition; new terms are needed to break through to the phenomena themselves. Whether this justifies the opacity is contested. |
| **Fundamental ontology never delivered its second half** — Division III of *Being and Time* ("Time and Being") was never published; the project was abandoned. | Internal | Heidegger acknowledged this and underwent the "turn" (*Kehre*) — moving from Dasein-analytic toward the history of Being, language and art. Whether this is a failure or a deepening is debated. |
| Ontological formality permits political ambiguity — a fundamental-ontology framework that never decides substantive questions can shelter any commitment. | Critics linking empty resoluteness to Heidegger's 1933 politics | Fundamental ontology is not a political programme; that defence is textually relevant but does not settle whether formal authenticity lacks critical norms (developed fully in Lesson 12). |

No primary question isolates this preliminary, but it is indispensable to verified questions on being-in-the-world, authenticity and temporality.

### Analytic challenge to the Being-question

The analytic objection asks whether “Being” names a genuine question or misleading grammar. Heidegger replies that propositional analysis already presupposes a disclosed world in which anything can count as a subject of predication. Fundamental ontology studies enabling intelligibility, not an extra entity called Being. The reply preserves depth; the residual is methodological—phenomenological disclosure is harder to test or arbitrate than an explicit validity argument.

### The workshop before the spectator’s gaze

```text
READY-TO-HAND (Zuhandenheit) — the PRIMARY mode
   You are hammering. You are not thinking "this is an object
   with the property 'hardness'." The hammer WITHDRAWS —
   you are absorbed in nailing the board, which refers to
   the house, which refers to dwelling. A REFERENTIAL WEB,
   not an isolated object.
              │
              │  BREAKDOWN (the hammer's head flies off)
              ▼
PRESENT-AT-HAND (Vorhandenheit) — a DERIVED, founded mode
   NOW the hammer shows up as a bare OBJECT with properties —
   "this thing is broken, made of wood and iron, weighs X" —
   the detached, theoretical stare of natural science.
```

Caption: Descartes and traditional epistemology start from the present-at-hand object (a "thing with properties" that a knowing subject must somehow reach). Heidegger's claim is that this is *backwards*: the present-at-hand is a **derived**, breakdown-produced mode; the primary, everyday way anything shows up to us is as equipment, absorbed in a web of practical significance.

**Plain-language start.** While you are driving fluently, you do not experience the steering wheel as "a circular object with certain frictional properties" — you experience the *road*, the *turn*, the *destination*; the wheel practically disappears into your driving. Only when something goes wrong (the wheel jams) does it suddenly appear as a bare *thing*, an object you must now stare at and diagnose. Heidegger's entire theory of "worldhood" generalises this everyday observation into a rigorous alternative to Cartesian epistemology.

### PART A — Dasein and being-in-the-world as a unitary phenomenon

- **Being-in-the-world** (*In-der-Welt-sein*) is a **unitary** phenomenon — a single hyphenated structure, **NOT** three separate elements (subject + relation + world) glued together after the fact. It is designed to dissolve the Cartesian split between an inner subject and an external world.
- Dasein does not first *exist* and then subsequently *encounter* a world; it **IS** constitutively *in* a world — practically engaged, absorbed, concerned.

### PART B — The worldhood of the world; spatiality

- The "world" is **not** the totality of physical objects (the universe of natural science); it is the **referential context of meaning** within which things show up as significant.
- **Ready-to-hand** (*Zuhandenheit*): the primary mode in which we encounter entities — as **equipment** (tools, instruments) that we *use* in a practical context. The hammer is not first "an object with properties" and then "used for hammering"; it is *originally* encountered as equipment-for-hammering, within a totality of references (nails, wood, house, shelter, project).
- **Present-at-hand** (*Vorhandenheit*): entities are thematised as objects with properties — a mode prominent in theoretical inquiry, but a **founded, derivative** mode of disclosure, not the bedrock the Cartesian tradition assumed.
- **Breakdown analysis:** unusability, absence or obstruction first makes the equipment-network conspicuous and can open a shift toward explicit object-thematisation. Breakdown does not instantly erase all practical significance — the broken hammer is still encountered as *the-hammer-that-should-be-working*, not as a value-neutral lump of matter.
- **The referential totality (the "in-order-to," *Um-zu*):** every piece of equipment refers beyond itself — the hammer to the nail, the nail to the board, the board to the house, the house to dwelling-as-a-way-of-being. This structure constitutes the worldhood of the world as a meaningful whole.
- **Dasein's spatiality.** Dasein's spatiality is not the spatiality of a physical object located at coordinates; it is characterised by **de-severance** (*Ent-fernung* — literally "de-distancing," bringing-close) and **directionality** (*Ausrichtung*). Things are "close" or "far" in terms of practical relevance and handiness within one's concernful dealings, not merely in metres — a book on a far shelf that I am absorbed in reading can be "closer," existentially, than a paperweight touching my hand. This is the existential spatiality developed in *Being and Time* §§22–24.

### PART C — Being-with and the existentialia

- **Being-with** (*Mitsein*): Dasein's world is always already a *shared* world; other Dasein are encountered not primarily as present-at-hand objects but as co-Daseins already factored into the world's referential structure (the workshop already implies other workers). This constitutive social existence is what Lesson 9's comparison table credits to Heidegger's account of the Other.
- Heidegger replaces the traditional "categories" (which apply to things) with **existentialia** — the structures of Dasein's own being:

| Existentiale | German | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Attunement / State-of-mind** | *Befindlichkeit* | Dasein always already finds itself in a *mood* (*Stimmung*) — anxiety, boredom, joy. Mood is not a subjective "colouring" of an objectively given world; it is a fundamental mode of *disclosing* the world and one's situation. "We are always already attuned." |
| **Understanding** | *Verstehen* | Dasein projects itself upon possibilities — it *understands* its world in terms of what it can do, what is possible. Understanding is not theoretical cognition but practical *know-how* and projection upon potentiality-for-being. |
| **Discourse** | *Rede* | The articulation of understanding — Dasein's being-open to meaning is always linguistically structured. Discourse is the condition of possibility of both speech and listening. |

- These three (*Befindlichkeit*, *Verstehen*, *Rede*) constitute the **disclosedness** (*Erschlossenheit*) of Dasein — the "there" (*Da*) of Da-sein: the fact that Being is *disclosed* or opened up for this entity.

### PART D — Care (*Sorge*) as the being of Dasein

- **Care** is the unitary structural whole of Dasein's existence. Its formal definition:
  > "**Ahead-of-itself — already-in (a world) — alongside (entities encountered within-the-world)**"
  > (*Sich-vorweg — schon-sein-in — Sein-bei*)
- **"Ahead-of-itself"** = Dasein is always *projecting* toward its future possibilities (understanding, freedom).
- **"Already-in"** = Dasein is always *thrown* into a world and a situation it did not choose (facticity, attunement).
- **"Alongside"** = Dasein is always *absorbed* in its practical dealings with entities (concern, falling — falling itself is analysed as fallenness).
- Care is **not** a psychological attitude (worry, anxiety) but the **ontological structure** that makes all particular attitudes and activities possible — this is precisely the existentiell/existential distinction applied to Care itself.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** Care is not a psychological attitude but the ontological structure that makes all particular attitudes and activities possible.

### Recurring exam-safe move: refuse decomposition first

Every strong answer on being-in-the-world states, *before* anything else, that the hyphenated compound resists decomposition into subject + relation + world — because the single most common way to lose marks here is to describe Dasein as simply "a human being who happens to be located inside a world," which quietly reintroduces exactly the Cartesian container-picture Heidegger is refuting.


### Why thrownness does not become determinism

Thrownness means I find myself delivered into body, language, history and relations. Projection means I understand myself through possibilities. Fallenness means everyday absorption in public interpretations. Care unifies them as “ahead-of-itself—already-in—alongside.” Possibility is neither created from nothing nor mechanically fixed by conditions. The residual is normative: ontology explains how possibilities are taken up, not which possibilities are just.

### From das Man to anticipatory resoluteness

```text
Dasein, proximally and for the most part, is inauthentic
                (this is STRUCTURAL, not a moral failing)
                              │
        ┌─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┐
        ▼                                             ▼
   INAUTHENTICITY (das Man)                  AUTHENTICITY (Eigentlichkeit)
   ═══════════════════════                    ═══════════════════════════
   Idle talk (Gerede)                          Call of conscience —
   Curiosity (Neugier)                         called back from
   Ambiguity (Zweideutigkeit)                  dispersal to one's own
        │                                       null, groundless
   FALLING (Verfallen) —                        responsibility
   absorbed/dispersed in                             │
   the anonymous "they"                        Being-toward-death →
                                                anticipatory resoluteness
                                                (owns thrownness, gathers
                                                 existence into a whole)
```

Caption: authenticity is **not** an escape from being-in-the-world into some private, isolated inner sanctum, and inauthenticity is **not** a moral defect. Both are *modes* of the very same underlying structure (being-in-the-world, Care) — one disperses that structure into anonymous publicness, the other owns it.

**Plain-language start.** Most of the time, we do what "one" does — we speak the opinions "one" speaks, are curious about what "everyone" is curious about, without ever quite deciding for ourselves that this is how we want to live. This is not a character flaw; Heidegger insists it is simply how being-with-others works, most of the time, for everyone. Authenticity names the rarer possibility of *owning* — explicitly taking responsibility for — the very thrown existence one is already living, most vividly when confronted with the sheer, individualising fact of one's own mortality.

### PART A — The crucial preliminary: authenticity is not a moral virtue

**Authenticity (*Eigentlichkeit*) is NOT a moral virtue** and inauthenticity is **NOT** a moral failing. They are **existential modifications** — modes of Dasein's self-relation. Dasein is "*proximally and for the most part*" (*zunächst und zumeist*) inauthentic, and this is not a deficiency but a structural feature of being-in-the-world. This is the single most heavily tested clarification in the entire Heidegger portion of the syllabus (directly demanded by the linked 2019, 2022 and 2026 PYQs).

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** Dasein is proximally and for the most part inauthentic, and this is not a deficiency but a structural feature of being-in-the-world.

### PART B — Inauthenticity: *das Man* (the "they")

- Dasein exists with others (*Mitsein*, Lesson 11) and tends to lose itself in what "one" (*das Man*) does, says and thinks — the anonymous public.
- **Three marks of *das Man*:**
  - **Idle talk** (*Gerede*) — passing along received opinions without genuine understanding.
  - **Curiosity** (*Neugier*) — flitting restlessly from novelty to novelty.
  - **Ambiguity** (*Zweideutigkeit*) — everything seems accessible, nothing genuinely appropriated.
- **Falling** (*Verfallen*): Dasein's tendency to be absorbed in the world of its concern and in *das Man* — losing its "ownmost" self. Falling is **not** a theological "fall from grace"; it is an ontological structure, present in every Dasein, not a punishment for a prior transgression.

### PART C — Authenticity: the call of conscience and being-toward-death

- **The call of conscience** (*Ruf des Gewissens*): Dasein is called back from dispersal in *das Man* to its null/groundless responsibility for taking over its thrown projection. The silent call gives **no** moral rule or substantive programme — it does not tell Dasein *what* to do, only that it must own *its own* deciding.
- **Being-toward-death** (*Sein-zum-Tode*): death is Dasein's:
  - **Ownmost** possibility — no one can die for me;
  - **Non-relational** — it isolates me from others;
  - **Certain** — inevitable;
  - **Indeterminate as to when** — its timing is never fixed in advance.
  - **Inauthentic relation:** we flee death — "one dies" (*man stirbt*), "everyone dies someday" — covering over death's individualising force.
  - **Authentic relation: anticipatory resoluteness** (*vorlaufende Entschlossenheit*) — I face death as *my own* possibility, which discloses the finitude and urgency of my existence, freeing me from *das Man* and letting me take over my existence as a *whole*.
- **Individualisation by death does not abolish being-with or prescribe social withdrawal**; authenticity modifies *how* Dasein owns its already social possibilities — it is not a licence for hermit-like solitude.
- **Crucial correction, restated:** authenticity for Heidegger does **NOT** mean "living a morally good life" or "being a rugged individualist." It means *owning* one's thrownness and projecting upon one's ownmost possibilities — it is a *formal* modification of everydayness, not a substantive ethical prescription.

### PART D — Fear vs Angst, Heidegger's specific formula (recap and extension of Lesson 3's table)

- Heidegger (*Being and Time* §40): in anxiety "**the world has the character of complete insignificance**" — the totality of involvements (the workshop of ready-to-hand things, Lesson 11) collapses, and Dasein is thrown back on its bare *that-it-is*.
- Heidegger's compressed formula: **that in the face of which one is anxious and that for which one is anxious are the same — Being-in-the-world.** Anxiety therefore discloses Dasein as *solus ipse* (alone with itself) and frees it for authentic choosing.
- In "What is Metaphysics?" (1929) Heidegger adds that anxiety reveals *das Nichts* — the Nothing — the source of the phrase Carnap famously attacked; the correct rendering is "**nothing noths**"/"the nothing nothings," never "nothing noughts."

### Three temporal ecstases, one finite unity

```text
VULGAR/ORDINARY TIME                    PRIMORDIAL TEMPORALITY
════════════════════                    ══════════════════════════
...past-now → present-now →             THREE ECSTASES, held together
future-now → ...                        as ONE unified stretching-out
(an infinite row of                     (Dasein "stands outside itself"
"nows," like beads                      into each simultaneously):
on an endless string)
                                         FUTURE (Zukunft) — coming
                                           toward oneself; PRIMARY
                                         HAVING-BEEN (Gewesenheit) —
                                           the past I still AM
                                         PRESENT (Gegenwart) — making-
                                           present, absorbed dealing
```

Caption: clock-time pictures "now" as one bead in an infinite, direction-less string. Heidegger's primordial temporality is not a string of nows at all — it is a single, finite, *ecstatic* stretching-out in which Dasein is always already futural, already having-been, and already making-present, all at once, gathered around the finite horizon of death.

**Plain-language start.** Notice that you are never simply "in the present" the way a stone simply sits where it is. Right now you are already leaning toward what you intend to do next (future), already carrying what has made you who you are (having-been), and already dealing practically with what is in front of you (present) — three directions, one stretched-out act of existing. Heidegger calls this ecstatic unity "temporality," and argues it is the deep meaning of the Care-structure taught in Lesson 11.

### PART A — Temporality as the meaning of Care; the three ecstases; future-priority

- **The central thesis of Division Two of *Being and Time*:** the meaning of Care (and therefore of Dasein's being) is **temporality** (*Zeitlichkeit*).
- **Three ecstases of primordial temporality:**

| Ecstasis | Existential moment | Structural element of Care |
|---|---|---|
| **Future** (*Zukunft*) — "coming toward" oneself | Projection, anticipation, being-toward-death | "Ahead-of-itself" |
| **Having-been** (*Gewesenheit*) — the past that I *still am* | Thrownness, facticity, guilt | "Already-in" |
| **Present** (*Gegenwart*) — "making present" | Absorption in entities, practical engagement | "Alongside" |

- **Priority of the future:** for Heidegger, the future (as Dasein's projecting upon its ownmost possibility — death, Lesson 12) is the **primary** ecstasis. The past and present are *temporalised* from out of the future. Authenticity is *futural*: in anticipatory resoluteness, Dasein runs ahead to its death and thereby gathers its existence into a *whole*.
- **"Ecstatic" temporality:** the three dimensions are not containers or segments of a line; they are "**ecstases**" — Dasein *stands out* (*ek-stasis*) into each simultaneously. Temporality is a *unity* of future-past-present in which Dasein holds itself open.

### PART B — Primordial vs vulgar/ordinary time; historicality

| Primordial temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*) | Vulgar / ordinary time (*innerzeitige Zeit*) |
|---|---|
| Ecstatic, finite, unified, futural | A sequence of "nows" — past nows (gone), present now (here), future nows (not yet) |
| The temporality *of Dasein* — how Dasein exists its own being | "Time" as measured by clocks — the time *in which* events occur |
| Finite — bounded by death | Infinite — the "now" series is endless (both directions) |
| More primordial existential condition | Derived public/world-time interpretation and clock measurement |

- Heidegger derives ordinary now-time through world-time and public dating from more primordial temporality. Levelled interpretation can accompany falling, but clock-time is **not** thereby morally defective or practically dispensable — "vulgar" names a levelled conceptual interpretation, not a moral insult against clocks.
- **Historicality (*Geschichtlichkeit*) of Dasein:** Dasein inherits possibilities and projects them; history is an existential structure rather than merely an external sequence of past events.

> 🟨 **KEY LINE:** "Vulgar" names a levelled conceptual interpretation, not a claim that clock-time is morally defective or practically dispensable.

### PART C — The 2018 route: epoché, its rejection, and being-in-the-world versus the transcendental ego

**Attribution note:** *epoché* is **Husserl's** device, not Heidegger's; Heidegger rejects it because the reduction presupposes exactly what is most questionable — a worldless spectator-subject — whereas the primordial fact is Dasein's always-already **being-in-the-world**. The 2018 question therefore requires a fair account of Husserl's method before reconstructing Heidegger's alternative.

**What epoché is, attributed correctly.** In Husserl the **epoché** (bracketing, *Einklammerung*) suspends the "general thesis" of the natural attitude — the standing posit that the world exists independently — so that consciousness can be described purely as it gives itself. The **phenomenological reduction** then leads back (*re-ducere*) to the **transcendental ego**, the constituting field *for which* the world has sense. Husserl's is thus a philosophy that begins by *withholding the world*.

**Heidegger's rejection, restated in full (drawing on Lesson 10, Part C and Lesson 11).** Heidegger's objection is that this starting point is already an abstraction. To bracket the world and retreat to a transcendental ego is to assume that consciousness *could* be world-less — the very Cartesian picture (subject inside, world outside) that must be overcome. The more primordial condition is that Dasein is *always already* absorbed, concerned, involved: subject and object are *derivative* precipitates of practical involvement, not the bedrock. One cannot "bracket" the world because one is never outside it.

**Being-in-the-world vs the transcendental ego, with temporality now added.** Being-in-the-world is one unitary phenomenon, evidenced by the analysis of equipment: a hammer is first met as ready-to-hand, absorbed in an "in-order-to" web, only becoming a bare present-at-hand object of theoretical staring when it *breaks* — so the detached ego of theory is a *founded*, deficient mode, not the origin. Against Husserl's constituting ego stands Dasein's thrown, practical disclosedness (*Befindlichkeit*, *Verstehen*, *Rede*), unified as Care — and Care's *meaning* is exactly the ecstatic **temporality** taught in Parts A–B of this lesson. The transcendental ego, on Heidegger's analysis, is a timeless, constituting standpoint; Dasein, by contrast, *is* its temporality — finite, ecstatic, futural, bounded by death — which is precisely why a worldless, timeless transcendental spectator cannot be the primordial starting point phenomenology needs.

### Husserlian rejoinder → Heideggerian reply

| Objection | Reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| Husserlians reply that Heidegger has merely *relocated*, not answered, the constitution question — description still needs *some* methodological purification. | The decisive point stands: if the subject is always already worldly, the reduction cannot reach a presuppositionless residue in the way Husserl intends. | The dispute over whether "relocating" the constitution question counts as answering it remains a live methodological disagreement between the two projects, not a closed one. |

### Kant, Husserl and Heidegger on time

Kant treats time as an a priori form of inner sense under which appearances are experienced. Husserl analyses internal time-consciousness through retention, primal impression and protention. Heidegger relocates the question: temporality is the ecstatic unity through which Dasein projects a future, inherits a having-been and makes present. The gain is existential depth; the cost is that the derivation of public, measurable time from finite temporality remains incomplete.

### Three arcs converge in one argument

```text
THE THREE EXISTENTIAL ARCS
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
 KIERKEGAARD: System → single individual → subjective appropriation
                              → anxiety/despair → leap and faith

 SARTRE: no blueprint → intentional nothingness → situated freedom
                              → bad faith / the Look → responsibility

 HEIDEGGER: forgotten Being → Dasein-in-the-world → Care
                              → death / resoluteness → temporality

 SYNTHESIS: each diagnoses self-loss differently; none alone supplies
            a complete ethics, politics and institutional theory.
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
```

Caption: the three arcs now converge: Kierkegaard protects appropriation, Sartre exposes self-making and evasion, and Heidegger uncovers the world-and-time structure in which existence is possible. The synthesis tests where each solution succeeds and where it stops.

### PART A — Master comparison table (inter-thinker debates)

| Axis | Kierkegaard | Sartre | Heidegger |
|---|---|---|---|
| God | Central — faith in the absurd, the God-relation | Denied — atheism as *premise* | Bracketed — ontology, not theology |
| Core term for human being | *Den Enkelte* (the individual, the single one) | *Pour-soi* (for-itself, nihilating consciousness) | Dasein (being-there, the entity that questions Being) |
| Path/authenticity | Religious faith in pseudonymous/signed contexts | Bad-faith critique; positive authenticity remains underdeveloped in *Being and Nothingness* | Anticipatory resoluteness (being-toward-death), not a moral code |
| Freedom | Qualitative choice within a finite God-relation | Radical but situated freedom of the for-itself | Thrown projection/possibility, not Sartrean voluntarism |
| Temporality | Existence as *repetition* and *moment* (*Øjeblikket*) — the eternal entering time | Pour-soi as ecstatic (past/present/future); freedom nihilates the past | *Zeitlichkeit* — three ecstases, future-primacy, finite |
| Chief enemy | Hegel's System, the crowd, objective Christianity | Determinism, bad faith, the "serious" spirit | Cartesian dualism, *das Man*, metaphysics of presence |
| Relation to Hegel | Existing individual versus completed system | Uses Hegelian themes of negation/otherness but rejects teleological totality | Reopens the Being-question against metaphysics; not simple inheritance |
| Mood disclosing truth | Anxiety (*Angest*), despair | Anguish (*angoisse*), nausea (*la nausée*) | Anxiety (*Angst*) — discloses the nothing/thrownness |

### PART B — Master criticism map (movement-level, then thinker-specific)

**Movement-level objections require thinker-specific replies:**
- **Subjectivism/irrationalism:** Kierkegaard's subjectivity concerns appropriation; Sartre links choice with responsibility; Heidegger is doing ontology rather than validating opinion.
- **Pessimism:** anxiety, despair, death and nothingness are diagnostic/disclosive structures for these thinkers, not the claim that every life is emotionally miserable.
- **Social abstraction:** feminist, Marxist and postcolonial critics rightly pressure the early focus on solitary or formally free agents; facticity, being-with and later situated ethics provide partial rather than complete replies.

**Graded verdict bank (executable closing positions):**

| Sub-topic | 10-mark verdict | 15-mark verdict | 20-mark verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Existence precedes essence** | Sartre's atheistic argument denies a predetermined human blueprint; it does not erase biological/social facticity. | …It is Sartre's slogan, not existentialism's common creed; Heidegger repudiates the humanist inversion and Kierkegaard is theistic. | The doctrine foregrounds project/responsibility but leaves value-criteria and situated constraint underdeveloped. |
| **Radical freedom** | Freedom is the for-itself's non-coincidence with facticity, exercised only in situation. | …Motives acquire action-guiding significance within a project; they are not irrelevant causes or instantly dismissible feelings. | Coercion, trauma and material structures pressure early radicalism; later scarcity/seriality broaden the account without abandoning it. |
| **Bad faith** | Bad faith evades the tension of facticity and transcendence rather than merely lying. | …Pre-reflective/non-thetic awareness is Sartre's proposed answer to the self-deception paradox. | The account is influential but normatively incomplete because positive authenticity remains thin and its examples require contextual criticism. |
| **The Look / being-for-others** | Shame discloses myself as object for another subject; it is not an empirical proof of all other minds. | …Sartre improves on inferential models but may generalise conflict from selective encounters. | Love, solidarity, de Beauvoir, Fanon and later Sartre pressure subject/object exclusivity; these are bounded criticisms, not replacements. |

| **Kierkegaard vs Hegel** | No completed system substitutes for the temporal choice of the existing individual. | …Leap, either/or and indirect communication resist mediated systematic closure, but Hegel need not simply deny individuality. | Kierkegaard exposes first-person appropriation that system-view risks losing; he does not offer a rival comprehensive ontology. |
| **Indirect communication** | Ethical-religious appropriation cannot be handed over as neutral information. | …Pseudonyms stage existence-possibilities; attribution must follow their voices. | Revocation makes the method deliberately self-limiting, though whether it clarifies or evades doctrine remains disputed. |

### Provenance and translation discipline — final consolidated checklist

- The official wording contains a typographical error; use **Sartre** in all analysis.
- **Kierkegaard (Danish):** always cite the pseudonym, never "Kierkegaard says in [pseudonymous work]"; *Angest* renders as "dread" (Lowrie) or "anxiety" (Hong) — name the translation; "leap of faith" is the English tradition's fused phrase, not a literal Kierkegaardian term; *The Point of View* was written 1848, published posthumously 1859.
- **Heidegger (German):** cite *Being and Time* (1927) by section number; the book is unfinished — Division III was never written; "nothing noths"/"the nothing nothings" is correct, "nothing noughts" is wrong; the "Letter on Humanism" was written 1946, published 1947.
- **Sartre (French):** *Being and Nothingness* is 1943 (Barnes 1956 / Richmond 2018 translations) and already contains first-person, plural and third-person participial variants of condemnation to freedom, including "man being condemned to be free." *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, delivered in October 1945 and published in 1946, supplies and popularises the canonical slogan form "man is condemned to be free" and the public formula "existence precedes essence." "*L'enfer, c'est les autres*" is from *Huis clos* (1944), with Sartre's own 1965 correction; *Critique of Dialectical Reason* is 1960.
- Never write "the existentialists held that…" as a single shared-doctrine sentence — the discipline established in Lesson 1 and enforced throughout every lesson since.

|---|---|
| "Existence precedes essence" is shared by all three | Wrong — it is Sartre's slogan; Heidegger disowns it; Kierkegaard never uses it. |

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

**10-mark original question — Explain (150 words):** "Explain, with one example each, how Kierkegaard, Sartre and Heidegger each use a distinctive mood to disclose a distinctive philosophical structure."

*Model answer.* Each thinker uses a mood-family related to, but not identical with, ordinary fear, to disclose something no determinate object could disclose. Kierkegaard's anxiety (*Angest*), in Vigilius Haufniensis's analysis, discloses *possibility* — the man at the abyss's edge grows dizzy at his own freedom to fall or not, prior to any knowledge of good and evil; anxiety is the presupposition of the leap by which selfhood and sin enter. Sartre's anguish (*angoisse*) discloses *my own freedom* — walking the cliff path, I fear the drop but feel anguish at discovering nothing within me prevents my own leap; the gambler's anguish is the discovery that yesterday's resolution cannot bind today's freely-remade choice. Heidegger's anxiety (*Angst*) discloses *Being-in-the-world as such* — in anxiety the referential totality of equipment collapses into insignificance, and Dasein is thrown back on its bare, individualised "that-it-is," freed for authentic resoluteness. All three moods share the formal feature of having no determinate object, unlike fear; but their disclosive content — possibility, freedom, Being-in-the-world — is irreducibly thinker-specific, and no exam answer should compress the three into one generic "existentialist anxiety."

**15-mark original question — Discuss (200 words):** "Is Sartre's account of bad faith compatible with his claim that consciousness is wholly transparent to itself? Discuss with reference to the paradox of self-deception."

*Model answer.* Bad faith requires that I both know a truth (to conceal it) and not know it (to be deceived) — a structure that seems to demand the very opacity (an unconscious "part" that hides things from a conscious "part") that Sartre's transparency thesis (P2, Lesson 5) explicitly denies, since transparency rules out both a Freudian unconscious and any genuinely hidden content within consciousness. Sartre's proposed resolution is that consciousness is non-self-identical rather than divided into parts: a *pre-reflective, non-thetic* awareness can "hold" a truth without that truth being *thetically posited* (explicitly, reflectively affirmed) — so the same, single, transparent consciousness can simultaneously not-reflectively-avow what it pre-reflectively is aware of, without this requiring two separate loci of awareness. Fingarette and other analytic philosophers of mind press the objection that this still requires *some* asymmetry within one consciousness between what is "known" pre-reflectively and what is avowed reflectively — and ask whether that asymmetry does the same explanatory work as an unconscious, merely under a different name. The most defensible verdict is that Sartre's account is an internally motivated attempt (given his prior commitments to intentionality, non-egological consciousness and pre-reflective self-awareness, Lesson 5) to explain self-deception without repression, but its success depends on whether "non-thetic awareness without reflective avowal" is a stable middle position or merely a relabelling of the very opacity transparency was meant to exclude — a question the secondary literature keeps open rather than settling.

**20-mark original question — Compare and assess (250 words):** "Compare and contrast the routes by which Kierkegaard, Sartre and Heidegger each seek to overcome an impersonal, totalising picture of the human being — Hegel's System, a fixed human essence, and Cartesian subject–object dualism respectively. Which, in your assessment, offers the most philosophically defensible alternative?"

*Model answer.* Kierkegaard's route is the leap: against Hegel's claim that "the truth is the whole" and the individual is a moment inside Absolute Spirit's self-development, Kierkegaard argues that the existing individual — particular, temporal, passionate, deciding — cannot be a moment inside any completed system, since even the System's builder remains an unfinished, existing individual ("a logical system is possible; an existential system is impossible"). His positive alternative is qualitative, faith-based selfhood across three spheres, communicated only indirectly through pseudonyms, since ethical-religious truth is a mode of existing rather than a transmissible result. Sartre's route is ontological reversal: against any picture (theistic or otherwise) in which a fixed human essence precedes existence, Sartre denies the divine artisan and makes existence, project and radical freedom primary; his positive alternative is a self perpetually constituted by nihilating choice, structured by facticity and transcendence, whose freedom is inescapable even in non-choice. Heidegger's route is the most radical: he denies that the Cartesian subject–object split is even the right starting point, arguing that Dasein is always already practically absorbed in a meaningful world (being-in-the-world), disclosed through Care and temporality, prior to any subject-versus-object picture; his alternative to "essence" is that Dasein's "essence" simply *is* its manner of existing (Existenz), and he explicitly refuses Sartre's humanist reversal as still trapped within the metaphysics of subjectivity it claims to escape. Assessed comparatively: Kierkegaard's route is the most existentially powerful for first-person appropriation but offers no rival systematic ontology and risks fideism; Sartre's route generates a compelling ethics of responsibility but struggles to derive determinate values from pure freedom and under-describes structural, non-cognitive constraint (a charge partially met only in his later, incomplete Marxist turn); Heidegger's route is the most ambitious — dissolving the subject–object picture at its root rather than merely reversing one of its terms — but it purchases this ambition at the price of a famously unfinished project (Division III never written) and a formally empty account of authenticity that, notoriously, could not by itself distinguish an authentically-taken-up Nazi commitment from any other. No single route is straightforwardly "most defensible" without qualification; the strongest examination answer states this directly rather than picking a favourite by fiat, while still showing which specific problem each route solves that the others do not.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

*(Complete recall spine, gathered from all fourteen lessons — read this section as the final pre-exam register, after all teaching and practice content, before the coverage matrix and source ledger.)*

**Family and scope**
- Existentialism is a retrospective family label for three distinct projects (Kierkegaard theistic, Sartre atheistic, Heidegger ontological); only Sartre accepted the label.
- Printed core thinkers: Kierkegaard, Sartre and Heidegger. Nietzsche, Dostoevsky, Marcel, Jaspers, Camus and Beauvoir are bounded roots or extensions.
- Mnemonic: K-S-H = Faith, Freedom, Finitude.

**Kierkegaard**
- Target: Hegel's System ("the truth is the whole"); counter: the existing individual (*den Enkelte*) cannot be a moment inside any completed system.
- "Truth is subjectivity" (Climacus, *Postscript*, 1846): mode of appropriation for ethical-religious truth, not relativism; "an objective uncertainty held fast in the most passionate inwardness."
- Three stages/spheres: aesthetic (moment/enjoyment → boredom/despair) — ethical (universal duty → guilt) — religious (faith/absurd → paradox); movement is a qualitative leap, never automatic.
- Teleological suspension of the ethical: Abraham, analysed by Johannes de Silentio in *Fear and Trembling* (1843).
- Anxiety (*Angest*, Vigilius Haufniensis, *The Concept of Anxiety*, 1844): "the dizziness of freedom," object is a "nothing," discloses possibility.
- Despair (Anti-Climacus, *The Sickness unto Death*, 1849): misrelation in the self's relation to itself; forms include not-willing-to-be-oneself and defiant self-willing.
- Indirect communication: ethical-religious truth cannot be handed over as a result; six pseudonyms, each a personified existence-possibility; always cite the pseudonym.
- "Leap of faith" is the English tradition's fused phrase; Kierkegaard writes of "the leap" (*Springet*).

**Sartre**
- "Existence precedes essence": the paper-knife analogy; no divine artisan for humans; atheism is a premise, not a conclusion (lecture delivered in 1945, published in 1946).
- Consciousness: intentional, no contents, no inhabiting ego (*The Transcendence of the Ego*, 1936–37), pre-reflective/non-positional self-awareness; "nothing" = no fixed essence, not non-existence.
- En-soi ("it is what it is," full, opaque) vs pour-soi ("it is what it is not and is not what it is," nihilating); the impossible pour-soi-en-soi ideal = God; "man is a useless passion."
- Freedom: *Being and Nothingness* (1943) gives first-person, plural and third-person participial variants of condemnation to freedom, including "man being condemned to be free"; the lecture delivered in 1945 and published in 1946 supplies and popularises the canonical slogan form "man is condemned to be free"; non-choice is still choice; anguish/abandonment/despair; "in choosing myself I choose man" (quasi-Kantian universalisability).
- Bad faith: collapsing facticity/transcendence into one; waiter and woman-on-date examples; the sincerity paradox (demanding fixed sincerity is bad faith's mirror image); solved via pre-reflective non-self-identity, not repression.
- The Look: the keyhole example; "shame is by nature recognition"; "the Other is the indispensable mediator between myself and me"; "conflict is the original meaning of being-for-others"; "Hell is other people" needs its 1965 correction.
- Later self-revision: *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) — scarcity, seriality, the practico-inert.

**Heidegger**
- Fundamental ontology, not existentialist psychology; ontological difference: Being ≠ beings; Dasein has a pre-ontological understanding of Being.
- Existentiell (ontic, first-person) vs existential (ontological structures uncovered by analysis) — do not conflate the two levels.
- Being-in-the-world: one unitary phenomenon; ready-to-hand (primary, equipment) vs present-at-hand (derived, theoretical); the broken-hammer breakdown analysis; Dasein's spatiality as de-severance/directionality.
- Existentialia: *Befindlichkeit* (attunement), *Verstehen* (understanding), *Rede* (discourse) — together, disclosedness.
- Care (*Sorge*): "ahead-of-itself — already-in — alongside"; an ontological structure, not a psychological attitude.
- Authenticity/inauthenticity: existential modifications, NOT moral categories; Dasein is inauthentic "proximally and for the most part" (das Man, idle talk/curiosity/ambiguity, falling); authenticity via the call of conscience and being-toward-death (ownmost/non-relational/certain/indeterminate) → anticipatory resoluteness.
- Temporality: meaning of Care; three ecstases (future/having-been/present), future-priority; primordial (finite, ecstatic) vs vulgar time (infinite now-series); historicality.
- Epoché is Husserl's device; Heidegger rejects it because it presupposes a worldless subject, whereas Dasein is always already being-in-the-world.
- "Letter on Humanism" (written 1946, published 1947): explicit rejection of Sartre's humanist reading of "the essence of Dasein lies in its existence."

**Roots and extensions**
- Nietzsche: death of God → nihilism → revaluation/value-creation; a root of the crisis, not a printed core thinker.
- Dostoevsky: literary precursor on freedom, rebellion, guilt and responsibility.
- Camus: absurd = human demand for meaning confronting a silent world; revolt rejects suicide and consolation.
- Marcel: problem/mystery, fidelity and availability; a theistic-participatory alternative to objectification.
- Jaspers: boundary situations and existential communication.
- Beauvoir: ambiguity, situated freedom, reciprocity and oppression; a decisive ethical-political development.

**Critical verdicts**
- Essentialism: projects transform givens, but embodiment and shared capacities still constrain them.
- Marxism: situated freedom requires analysis of class, institutions and material reproduction.
- Structuralism: language and structures pre-form agency more deeply than early Sartre admits.
- Analytic philosophy: phenomenological disclosure is rich, but public criteria remain disputed.
- Theology: existentialism includes theistic lines; atheistic value-creation faces a grounding problem.

**Provenance discipline (never violate)**
- *Being and Time* (1927) — unfinished, Division III never written; cite by section.
- *Being and Nothingness* (1943) contains earlier first-person, plural and third-person participial formulations of condemnation to freedom, including "man being condemned to be free"; *Existentialism Is a Humanism* was delivered in 1945 and published in 1946, supplying and popularising the canonical slogan form "man is condemned to be free."
- *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) is the later Marxist-turn concession text.
- Always attribute Kierkegaard's pseudonymous claims to the pseudonym, never to "Kierkegaard."
