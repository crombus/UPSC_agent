## Lesson 1 — How Do We Reconstruct the Peninsular Transformation?

### Visual first — begin with zones, not modern state borders

```text
WESTERN DECCAN                    EASTERN COAST
Vatapi/Badami                     Kanchi/Tondaimandalam
Malaprabha basin                  Palar-Kaveri approaches
      |                                  |
      +------ Krishna-Tungabhadra -------+
              contested corridors
                       |
                  Vengi branch
                       |
              Bay of Bengal routes
```

*The peninsula contained interacting ecological and political zones; no fixed seventh-century Karnataka–Tamil Nadu border existed.*

### 1. Plain-language problem

If inscriptions say a king conquered a region, did he administer every village there? If a temple survives, does it prove that its patron controlled the whole landscape? The answer to both is no. This period is richly visible, but much of its archive was created to praise rulers, record gifts or monumentalise sacred power.

### 2. Working chronology

The core rivalry belongs broadly to the sixth through mid-eighth centuries CE. Earlier Pallava inscriptions and post-Satavahana polities supply the background; later Pallava survival, the Eastern Chalukyas and Rashtrakuta displacement form transition boundaries.

| Phase | Approximate marker | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| c. 300–550 CE | Early Pallavas, Kadambas, Gangas and expanding grants | Background to new regional polities |
| c. 550–630 | Simhavishnu, Mahendravarman I and early Badami Chalukyas | Consolidation of Kanchi and Vatapi cores |
| c. 609–668 | Pulakeshin II and Narasimhavarman I | Main military reversal sequence |
| c. 700–753 | Rajasimha, Vikramaditya II and later rivalry | Structural patronage and renewed campaigns |
| after c. 753 | Rashtrakutas, Eastern Chalukya continuity and later Pallavas | Transition, not simultaneous disappearance |

Dates remain approximate because regnal reconstructions differ and inscriptions privilege selected events.

### 3. Source families

| Evidence | What it can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Royal *prashasti* | Genealogy, titles, named campaigns, political ideals | Neutral victory account or exact permanent frontier |
| Copper-plate grant | Donor, donee, locality, dues and exemptions | Identical enforcement in every village |
| Temple or cave | Patronage, workshop capability, ritual landscape | Complete political reach or popular belief |
| Literature and hymns | Court culture, religious argument, devotional geography | Census-like social description |
| Archaeology and port evidence | Occupation, production, material sequence, exchange | Dynastic identity without an independent link |

The Aihole inscription is therefore both evidence and argument. Ravikirti tells us how Pulakeshin II's court wanted its power remembered.

### 4. Geography as mechanism

Kanchi linked an inland political-religious centre with the Coromandel coast. Vatapi sat within the western Deccan and the Malaprabha architectural zone. The Krishna-Godavari region mattered for Vengi; the Tungabhadra and adjoining doabs repeatedly became campaign corridors.

Geography enabled movement and constrained power, but it did not mechanically cause war. Courts fought over tribute, allies, productive zones, routes and prestige.

### 5. Example and limit

The capture of Vatapi by Narasimhavarman I demonstrates deep Pallava penetration into the Chalukya core. It does not demonstrate permanent Pallava administration of the western Deccan. A spectacular campaign is an event; a province requires continuing fiscal and political evidence.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If royal sources exaggerate, political history becomes unknowable.

**Reply:** Exaggeration is not invention in every detail. Historians compare rival inscriptions, dates, succession, findspots, grants and material sequences. The result is graded knowledge: a named campaign may be secure while troop numbers, boundaries and lasting control remain uncertain.

### 7. UPSC application

Use one sentence of source control in every long answer:

> The inscription securely records a court's claim and named political relationship, but its eulogistic genre prevents direct conversion into a neutral territorial map.

### Revision notes

1. Core period: approximately sixth to mid-eighth centuries.
2. Kanchi and Vatapi were the principal political poles.
3. Vengi was an eastern consequence of Chalukya expansion.
4. Campaign zone is not the same as administered province.
5. Prashastis combine data with royal rhetoric.
6. Grants are locality-specific legal-political documents.
7. Monuments reveal patronage and capability, not total sovereignty.
8. Literary genres shape what they show.
9. Modern linguistic-state borders must not be projected backward.
10. Triangulation is the method for every major claim.

## Lesson 2 — How Did the Two Regional Powers Emerge?
Progress: 2 / 14  |  Stage: Foundation  |  Subtopic: Origins, ruler ladders, capitals and legitimating claims

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Sharma's chapter on peninsula state formation and Upinder Singh's southern-polity narrative, queried.
- **CA search:** `"site:culture.gov.in Pallava Chalukya origins inscriptions 2026"`
- **CA found:** none substantively relevant in the official-source window.

### Visual first — two consolidations with different regional cores

```text
PALLAVA LINE                              BADAMI CHALUKYA LINE
early charters                            Pulakeshin I
      |                                        |
Simhavishnu                              Kirtivarman I
      |                                        |
Mahendravarman I                         Mangalesha
      |                                        |
Narasimhavarman I  <--- rivalry --->     Pulakeshin II
      |                                        |
Rajasimha and later line                 revival and Vikramaditya II
```

*Neither dynasty began with its most famous ruler; both inherited earlier political work.*

### 1. Pallava origin: what is secure?

Older theories derived the Pallavas from Pahlavas, northern migrants, Andhra groups or a single local community. The evidence does not settle one ethnic origin. What is secure is an early inscriptional presence connected with southern Andhra and Tondaimandalam, followed by a stronger Kanchi-centred formation.

Early Pallava charters appear in Prakrit and then Sanskrit. Later records increasingly combine Sanskrit prestige with Tamil documentary functions. This change shows institutional adaptation, not racial transformation.

### 2. From early Pallavas to Simhavishnu

Shivaskandavarman is an important early inscriptional anchor. The politically decisive later consolidation is associated with Simhavishnu in the late sixth century. His expansion provides the base inherited by Mahendravarman I.

Mahendravarman I matters in three linked domains:

- political defence against Pulakeshin II;
- early Pallava rock-cut patronage;
- authorship or association with the Sanskrit farce *Mattavilasa-prahasana*.

His titles **Mattavilasa**, **Vichitrachitta** and **Gunabhara** form the direct 2025 Prelims demand.

### 3. Chalukya emergence before Pulakeshin II

Pulakeshin I established independent power at Vatapi and fortified the capital. Kirtivarman I expanded against neighbouring powers; Mangalesha continued consolidation before a succession struggle with Pulakeshin II.

The dynasty's descent claims from Brahma, Manu, the Moon or an Ayodhya past are legitimating genealogies. They show a need to translate regional military success into recognised royal status.

### 4. Capitals were institutions

Kanchi was not merely a royal residence. It became a religious, literary, commercial and craft centre linked to coastal outlets. Vatapi was a fortified capital embedded in the Malaprabha region and connected to Aihole and Pattadakal.

```text
capital
  = court + treasury + ritual + artisans + records + routes
```

The capital concentrated authority, but power outside it depended on chiefs, officers, grants and local institutions.

### 5. Why genealogies mattered

A newly powerful lineage could possess armies without possessing uncontested social prestige. Genealogies, sacrifices and Brahmana patronage provided a language of legitimate kingship. This did not simply trick society; it created alliances and recognisable claims, although those claims remained unequal and contested.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If origin stories are myths, they are historically useless.

**Reply:** They are weak evidence for biological descent but strong evidence for political aspiration. A court choosing lunar, solar or Brahmanical ancestry tells us which forms of prestige it considered effective.

### 7. UPSC application

For an origin question, write:

```text
theory -> secure inscriptional region -> political consolidation
       -> genealogy as legitimation -> uncertainty preserved
```

### Revision notes

1. Pallava ethnic origin is disputed.
2. Tondaimandalam and Kanchi are the secure later anchors.
3. Early charters move from Prakrit toward Sanskrit.
4. Simhavishnu prepared later Pallava ascendancy.
5. Mahendravarman I combined political and cultural roles.
6. Pulakeshin I established the Vatapi base.
7. Kirtivarman I and Mangalesha preceded Pulakeshin II.
8. Origin myths supplied legitimacy, not literal migration history.
9. Capitals concentrated several institutions.
10. Political consolidation preceded the famous rivalry.

## Lesson 3 — Rivalry Without a Battle-List
Progress: 3 / 14  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Campaign sequence, political purpose, Vengi and connected regions

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Aihole and Pallava counter-campaign evidence in Sharma and Upinder Singh, queried.
- **CA search:** `"site:asi.nic.in Aihole inscription Pulakeshin II 2026"`
- **CA found:** no dated official update changing the source interpretation.

### Visual first — pressure, reversal, recovery

```text
Pulakeshin II consolidates Deccan and eastern expansion
                         |
             pressure on Mahendravarman I
                         |
         Narasimhavarman I counter-campaigns
                         |
              Vatapi captured, c. 642
                         |
           Chalukya recovery under successors
                         |
       Vikramaditya II campaigns against Kanchi
                         |
  Rashtrakuta displacement of Badami line, c. 753
```

*The sequence repeatedly changed direction; no victory created permanent supremacy.*

### 1. Why Pulakeshin II mattered

Pulakeshin II overcame an internal succession challenge and expanded across the Deccan. Ravikirti's Aihole inscription, dated 634–35 CE, celebrates campaigns against neighbouring powers and the check to Harsha near the Narmada.

Topic 22 owns Harsha's northern history. Here, the Narmada episode establishes that the Chalukya polity could resist northern expansion and project a Deccan-centred claim.

### 2. The eastern consequence: Vengi

Pulakeshin II's advance into the Krishna-Godavari region led to the establishment of an Eastern Chalukya branch under Kubja Vishnuvardhana. This is more important than one additional victory because it created a durable secondary polity.

```text
campaign -> delegated branch -> regional consolidation -> long afterlife
```

Later Eastern Chalukya-Chola entanglement belongs to Topic 27.

### 3. Pallava reversal

Pulakeshin's pressure reached close to Kanchi during Mahendravarman I's reign. Under Narasimhavarman I Mamalla, Pallava forces reversed the balance and captured Vatapi around 642. The title **Vatapikonda**, conqueror of Vatapi, memorialised that success.

The episode also involved the Sri Lankan prince Manavamma in Pallava political networks. This shows Bay of Bengal connectivity and alliance, not Pallava rule over Sri Lanka.

### 4. Recovery and renewed conflict

The Chalukyas revived under Vikramaditya I. Later, Vikramaditya II campaigned against Kanchi. Chalukya memory linked these victories with Pattadakal patronage, especially the Virupaksha temple associated with Queen Lokamahadevi.

Warfare thus produced political memories in titles, inscriptions and monuments. It did not by itself explain agrarian administration or social consent.

### 5. Competing explanations of the rivalry

| Explanation | Value | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Territorial resources | Productive zones and tribute mattered | Not every campaign sought annexation |
| Route control | Corridors connected capitals, coasts and Vengi | Routes alone did not cause rivalry |
| Prestige | Capturing a capital had enormous symbolic value | Prestige does not measure ground control |
| Alliance politics | Gangas, Pandyas, Sri Lankan actors and chiefs mattered | A two-dynasty frame can hide intermediaries |

The safest account combines material resources, strategic routes, allies and royal reputation.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because territory repeatedly changed hands, the wars had little structural significance.

**Reply:** Even temporary campaigns shaped succession, alliances, fortification, tribute, dynastic branching and monumental memory. Their significance lies in regional state formation, not in producing one final border.

### 7. UPSC application

Avoid a ruler-by-ruler battle list. Use:

```text
sequence -> changing objective -> political consequence
         -> source limit -> regional significance
```

### Revision notes

1. Aihole praises Pulakeshin II and requires genre caution.
2. Harsha's check is a Deccan boundary link, not Topic 23's northern narrative.
3. Vengi was a durable outcome of eastern expansion.
4. Narasimhavarman I captured Vatapi around 642.
5. Vatapikonda memorialised the Pallava reversal.
6. Chalukya power recovered after Vatapi's fall.
7. Vikramaditya II campaigned against Kanchi.
8. Sri Lankan involvement shows alliance and maritime connectivity.
9. Conflict included chiefs and neighbouring powers.
10. No victory yielded permanent all-peninsular supremacy.

## Lesson 4 — How Was Power Exercised Beyond the Capital?
Progress: 4 / 14  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Kingship, officers, chiefs and early local institutions

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **CA search:** `"site:culture.gov.in Pallava Chalukya administration inscription 2026"`
- **CA found:** no meaningful current official linkage.

### Visual first — layered rather than uniformly bureaucratic power

```text
KING AND COURT
war | grants | ritual | appointments
        |
princes, officers and subordinate chiefs
        |
territorial units and local intermediaries
        |
ur | sabha | nagaram | mahajana
        |
cultivators, artisans, traders and institutions
```

*Authority travelled through people and corporate bodies; it did not descend through one identical hierarchy everywhere.*

### 1. Kingship combined coercion and recognition

Kings commanded armies and issued grants, but they also staged sacrifices, adopted titles, patronised temples and circulated genealogies. Political capacity therefore had military, fiscal and ritual dimensions.

Grand titles advertise an ideal. They do not prove that every chief obeyed continuously.

### 2. Territorial vocabulary

Terms such as *rashtra*, *vishaya*, *kottam* and *nadu* occur in different places and periods. They should not be forced into one empire-wide administrative ladder. A similar word may carry different scope in different records.

### 3. Intermediaries

Subordinate rulers and local chiefs could supply troops, tribute and access to local society. Their relationship with the king ranged from close service to negotiated autonomy. A feudatory title therefore does not disclose the exact balance of power without context.

### 4. Local institutions

R.S. Sharma highlights:

- **ur** — an ordinary peasant settlement or village body;
- **sabha** — associated especially with Brahmana grant settlements;
- **nagaram** — linked with traders and commercial locality;
- **mahajana** — village elders in Chalukya-region evidence.

These categories show corporate organisation. They do not justify importing the detailed committee system of later Chola Uttaramerur inscriptions into the seventh century.

### 5. State models

| Model | What it illuminates | What it can hide |
|---|---|---|
| Centralised monarchy | Campaigns, grants and royal patronage | Uneven local capacity |
| Feudal tendency | Immunities, intermediaries and peasant burdens | Continuing royal initiative and commerce |
| Segmentary heuristic | Graded ritual and political control | Coercion and fiscal reach |
| Integrative model | Grants, cults and local elites building regions | Unequal extraction and conflict |

A balanced account treats the state as a changing network with strong cores, negotiated zones and multiple institutions.

### 6. Example and limit

A grant restricting entry by royal officers indicates a claimed privilege for the donee. It does not automatically prove that the king surrendered sovereignty or that officers never entered in practice.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling power layered is vague and avoids deciding whether the state was centralised.

**Reply:** The evidence supports different capacities by reign, region and function. A court may be highly effective in war or grant-making yet depend on local bodies for irrigation and collection. Precision requires specifying the function rather than imposing one label.

### 8. UPSC application

Use a graded formulation:

> Pallava and Chalukya states possessed real military and donative capacity, but exercised it through officers, chiefs, beneficiaries and corporate localities whose autonomy varied.

### Revision notes

1. Kingship joined military, fiscal and ritual authority.
2. Titles express ideals as well as capacity.
3. Territorial terms varied by dynasty and record.
4. Chiefs mediated troops, tribute and locality access.
5. Ur, sabha and nagaram were socially distinct institutions.
6. Mahajana appears in Chalukya-area rural management.
7. Later Chola committee detail must not be back-projected.
8. Immunity clauses record claims, not automatic implementation.
9. State models are analytical tools, not complete descriptions.
10. Capacity should be assessed function by function.

## Lesson 5 — From Grant to Agrarian Landscape
Progress: 5 / 14  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Brahmadeya, irrigation, cultivation and peasant burdens

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Sharma PDF pp. 308–314 and Upinder Singh's brahmadeya debate, queried.
- **CA search:** `"site:asi.nic.in Pallava Chalukya irrigation land grant 2026"`
- **CA found:** no dated official item adding substantive historical evidence.

### Visual first — a grant was a process, not a sheet of copper

```text
ROYAL OR ELITE GRANT
        |
named land + boundaries + dues + exemptions
        |
donees enter an existing or expanding locality
        |
irrigation + cultivation + revenue reorganisation
        |
new institutional power and social obligations
        |
cooperation | negotiation | burden | conflict
```

*The charter began a relationship whose ground effects depended on ecology and local society.*

### 1. Essential terms

- **Brahmadeya:** land or a village granted to one or more Brahmanas.
- **Agrahara:** often a tax-privileged Brahmana settlement or endowment.
- **Devadana:** resources assigned to a deity or temple.

These are working meanings, not identical legal packages. Each charter must be read for the specific dues, rights and exclusions it lists.

### 2. Why rulers made grants

Religious merit mattered, but grants also:

- rewarded and allied learned or ritual specialists;
- marked royal claims to land and revenue;
- encouraged settlement and cultivation in some zones;
- inserted beneficiaries into existing villages;
- connected courts with local elites and institutions.

Upinder Singh's corrective is important: grants could be integrative policies of an active state, not merely evidence that kings had lost control.

### 3. Irrigation and cultivation

Rainfall variability made tanks, channels, wells and local maintenance important. Royal initiative could assist major works, but cultivators, assemblies, beneficiaries and donors sustained many systems.

Technology enabled expansion only when labour, rights and maintenance were organised.

### 4. Existing inhabitants did not disappear

Court language may describe a grant as uncultivated or newly settled. That description can erase pastoral use, forest products, shifting cultivation or existing peasant claims. Some grants opened new fields; others redistributed rights over inhabited land.

### 5. Burdens and immunities

Sharma's Pallava evidence lists exemptions from taxes, provisions, official entry and forced labour. When such claims passed to beneficiaries, cultivators could face redirected dues or services.

The correct double effect is:

```text
agrarian extension and institutional support
                     +
redistribution of revenue and labour claims
```

### 6. Kalabhra evidence as a caution

Later Pandya memory, especially the Velvikudi charter tradition, depicts Kalabhras as overturning older Brahmana gifts. Sharma interpreted this as peasant protest against landed Brahmanas. Because the record is later and legitimates restoration, the existence and programme of one unified anti-Brahmana revolution cannot be treated as settled.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If grants expanded cultivation, describing them as burdensome is contradictory.

**Reply:** Institutions can increase output while distributing benefits and obligations unequally. More cultivated land does not reveal who controlled revenue, who performed labour or whose older rights were reduced.

### 8. UPSC application

For a grant answer, always write both sides:

> Grants could extend cultivation and integrate localities, while transferring fiscal privileges and strengthening unequal authority over cultivators and resources.

### Revision notes

### Visual first — not a one-way arrow

```text

   <-> BRAHMANAS receive land, patronage and authority
   <-> CHIEFS seek rank through genealogy and ritual
   <-> LOCAL CULTS gain wider Puranic identities
   <-> CULTIVATORS and ARTISANS sustain new institutions
   <-> REGIONAL LANGUAGES reshape received forms

Result: integration + localization + hierarchy + resistance
```

*Interaction was multidirectional, but the parties did not possess equal resources or archival visibility.*

### 1. Define the term carefully

**Brahmanization** describes the expansion of Brahmana settlements and authority, Vedic-Puranic ritual, Sanskritic political idioms, varna claims and temple-linked institutions.

It is not simply conversion to a religion. It includes land, status, revenue, social classification and political legitimacy.

### 2. Sanskritization is not enough

Sanskritization can describe the adoption of prestigious practices by groups seeking rank. It is useful for status claims but too narrow if it ignores land control, state power and institutional change.

### 3. Local agency and localization

Local chiefs did not merely receive a complete northern model. They selected genealogies, supported shrines and adapted political language. Local deities could be identified with Shiva, Vishnu or goddess forms while retaining regional myths, names and constituencies.

Regional language and temple practice therefore transformed Brahmanical traditions even as those traditions expanded.

### 4. Social incorporation was uneven

Where Brahmana settlements approached forest, pastoral or tribal zones, plough agriculture and caste classification could spread. Some chiefs gained high-status genealogies; some producing communities entered new jatis; others faced degraded or excluded status.

The phrase "tribe to peasant" describes one possible process, not a universal, voluntary or one-way sequence.

### 5. Varna and lived society

Varna supplied a normative hierarchy. Lived society contained numerous occupational, locality and kin groups. A ruler's Kshatriya or Brahmana claim does not prove the population fitted neatly into four varnas.

### 6. Women and agency

Queens and women donors appear in grants and monumental patronage. Queen Lokamahadevi's association with the Virupaksha temple and Trailokyamahadevi's with Mallikarjuna demonstrate elite female agency.

They do not prove general gender equality. Agency must be located by class, dynasty, institution and source.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Emphasising localization makes Brahmanization so broad that it explains everything.

**Reply:** The concept remains useful when tied to measurable mechanisms: grants to Brahmanas, ritual genealogy, Sanskritic titles, Puranic cult integration and new social classifications. Localization specifies how outcomes differed rather than dissolving the process.

### 8. UPSC application

Use a four-part thesis:

```text
material base -> ritual legitimation -> local adaptation -> unequal social outcome
```

*"Vesara" is a cautious descriptive aid, not a substitute for looking at the monument.*

- one unchanged plan across sites.

### Visual first — patronage was a chain

             |

             |

             |

*The patron's name matters, but a monument was a collective and continuing production.*

### 1. Patronage and attribution

Royal inscriptions foreground kings because kings had the resources and political motive to be remembered. Queenly commissions widen the picture. Workshop labour, local contributions and later repairs are less consistently named.

### 2. Temples as institutions

Temples could receive land and offerings, employ specialists, host ritual, attract visitors and anchor local exchange. Their scale differed. It is unsafe to say every temple owned and administered its village.

### 3. Sacred geography

Kanchi, Mamallapuram, Badami, Aihole and Pattadakal became political landscapes because monuments made dynastic and religious claims durable in specific places.

Sacred geography did not erase older local meanings. It layered new institutions and stories onto lived landscapes.

### 4. Queenly agency

Lokamahadevi and Trailokyamahadevi provide strong evidence that elite women could command resources and shape commemorative patronage. Their visibility arises from dynasty and institution; it cannot be generalised to all women.

### 5. Artisans and mobility

Shared or adapted forms may result from mobile craftspeople, imitation, conquest encounters or exchange. Style alone cannot prove ethnic migration or direct political control.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If unnamed labour is emphasised, royal patronage becomes historically secondary.

**Reply:** Royal resources and political programmes remain crucial, but intention cannot build stone. A complete explanation joins patron, institution, workshop and locality rather than replacing one with another.

## Lesson 14 — How Should We Interpret the Age?
Progress: 14 / 14  |  Stage: Advanced  |  Subtopic: Historiography, regional state formation and GS-I synthesis

### Visual first — four lenses, one qualified verdict

```text
FEUDAL-TENDENCY LENS
grants + immunities + intermediaries + peasant burden
                 \
INTEGRATIVE LENS ----> REGIONAL STATE FORMATION
alliances + local elites + cults + language
                 /

settlement + irrigation + producing communities
                 \
CONNECTED LENS ----> routes + ports + mobile artisans + plural religions

VERDICT: stronger regional orders built through unequal, non-linear integration
```

*No single model explains every locality or every century.*

### 1. Sharma's contribution

R.S. Sharma foregrounded land grants, Brahmana authority, rural expansion, peasant burdens and possible urban-commercial contraction. This prevents temple and dynasty narratives from hiding political economy.

His strongest insight is that cultural patronage rested on producing communities and redirected surplus.

### 2. Limits of a single decline model

Kanchi, Mamallapuram, monumental craft and port networks complicate universal decline. Grants could integrate new regions and express royal capacity. Regional states proliferated rather than merely fragmenting.

### 3. Integrative and regional approaches

Later approaches stress local chiefs, cults, grants and institutions as mechanisms that built wider regions. Integration was creative but not benign: it could deepen caste hierarchy, labour claims and unequal resource control.

### 4. Non-linear social change

Brahmanization, vernacular devotion and temple growth did not advance at one speed. Jain, Buddhist, pastoral, forest and local traditions persisted differently by region. Trade could contract in one node and expand around another.

### 5. Final interpretation

The period is foundational because it joined:

- regionally rooted courts;
- layered authority and local institutions;
- agrarian expansion and grants;
- Sanskritic and vernacular political cultures;
- temple and bhakti landscapes;
- selective urban and maritime vitality.

It was not the completed Chola system, a dark age, or a simple march of one culture over passive regions.

**Objection:** "Regional state formation" is a neutral phrase that hides exploitation.

**Reply:** It becomes evasive only if social costs are omitted. A complete formulation must include extraction, unequal incorporation, caste formation and archival silence alongside institution-building and cultural creativity.

- languages performed different political and devotional functions;
- monuments became evidence of patrons, workshops and sacred geography rather than decorative lists;
- dynastic decline became institutional reconfiguration;
- the final verdict became regional state formation through unequal, non-linear integration.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks — Explain why the Pallava-Chalukya conflict was more than a dynastic battle sequence. (150 words)

### Model answer

The Pallava-Chalukya conflict was a struggle for regional state formation rather than a self-contained list of victories.

Pulakeshin II's Aihole inscription records Chalukya expansion, the Narmada check to Harsha and pressure on the Pallava realm. Narasimhavarman I's later capture of Vatapi reversed the balance, while Vikramaditya II's Kanchi campaigns show that neither victory was final. These campaigns affected alliances with Gangas, Pandyas and Sri Lankan actors and produced the durable Eastern Chalukya branch in Vengi.

Conflict also reshaped political memory. Vatapikonda celebrated Pallava success, while the Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal associated Queen Lokamahadevi's patronage with Vikramaditya II's victory. Capitals, temples and inscriptions therefore converted warfare into sacred and literary geography.

Since prashastis magnify triumph and do not map stable administration, each campaign requires source control. The rivalry mattered because it reorganised alliances, regional centres and dynastic legitimacy even without permanent supremacy.

## 15 marks — Critically examine Brahmanization as an explanation of social change in peninsular India during the Pallava-Chalukya age. (250 words)

### Model answer

Brahmanization is useful when defined as a material-political process involving Brahmana settlements, grants, ritual legitimation, Sanskritic idioms, Puranic cults and varna-jati claims. It becomes misleading when reduced to one-way religious conversion.

Pallava and other southern charters granted villages and fiscal immunities to Brahmanas. Such grants could extend cultivation, transfer revenue claims and strengthen beneficiaries within local society. Courts also used Vedic sacrifices, genealogies and high-varna identities to convert regional military authority into recognised kingship. Brahmana settlements near forest or pastoral zones could promote plough agriculture and incorporate groups into new caste relations.

Yet the process was neither uniform nor passive. Many grants inserted donees into already inhabited landscapes rather than creating villages on empty land. Local chiefs selected legitimating traditions; regional deities retained names and social constituencies while entering wider Shaiva, Vaishnava or goddess frameworks. Tamil devotion and bilingual epigraphy show that regional languages transformed the cultural order alongside Sanskrit.

The process was reciprocal but unequal. Cultivators and service groups supplied surplus and labour, while some communities acquired status and others faced intensified hierarchy or exclusion. The late, partisan evidence for Kalabhra disruption also warns against reconstructing one simple resistance programme.

Thus Brahmanization explains grants, legitimacy and social classification, but must be combined with localization, agrarian political economy and regional agency.

## 20 marks — Assess the nature of regional state formation under the Pallavas and Chalukyas. (250 words)

### Model answer

Pallava and Chalukya states were neither modern centralised bureaucracies nor merely ceremonial kingships. They were regionally rooted political formations with strong courts and uneven authority mediated through chiefs, grants and local institutions.

Their military capacity is visible in Pulakeshin II's Deccan expansion, Narasimhavarman I's capture of Vatapi and Vikramaditya II's Kanchi campaigns. Yet prashastis require caution: campaign success did not automatically create lasting provincial administration. Durable power depended on agrarian revenue, fortified capitals, alliances and access to routes.

Land grants linked court and locality. Brahmadeya, agrahara and temple-related endowments could extend cultivation, transfer dues and create beneficiaries whose authority operated among cultivators and older resource users. Ur, sabha, nagaram and mahajana evidence shows local corporate organisation, although later Chola committee machinery cannot be back-projected.

Political legitimacy was also cultural. Genealogies, Vedic rites, Sanskrit prashastis, Tamil documentary practice, Bhakti shrines and monumental landscapes at Kanchi, Mamallapuram, Badami, Aihole and Pattadakal anchored dynasties in place. Ports, crafts and artisan mobility complicate any picture of complete rural-commercial collapse.

Feudal-tendency models rightly emphasise immunities and peasant burden; integrative approaches show that the same grants and cults could build regional polities. The best verdict is therefore layered state formation: real royal capacity combined with negotiated local power, unequal incorporation and regionally variable institutions.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Register 1 — Chronology and geography

- Core rivalry: approximately sixth to mid-eighth centuries CE.
- Kanchi/Tondaimandalam: Pallava political and cultural core.
- Vatapi/Badami and Malaprabha: Chalukya core.
- Simhavishnu precedes Mahendravarman I and Narasimhavarman I.
- Pulakeshin I, Kirtivarman I and Mangalesha precede Pulakeshin II.
- Pulakeshin II: Aihole, Harsha check, eastern expansion.
- Narasimhavarman I: Mamalla, Vatapikonda, capture of Vatapi.
- Vikramaditya II: renewed Kanchi campaigns.
- c. 753: Rashtrakuta displacement of Badami primacy.
- Eastern Chalukyas continue in Vengi; later detail belongs to Topic 27.

## Register 2 — Source discipline

- Prashasti = usable history plus political praise.
- Aihole is not a neutral biography or surveyed frontier.
- Grant formula must be separated from ground effect.
- Monument style cannot independently establish sovereignty.
- Satire reveals courtly religious representation, not demographics.
- Hagiography preserves sacred memory but requires chronology caution.
- Archaeology tests material sequence; attribution requires corroboration.
- Exact claim rule: source -> what it proves -> what it cannot prove.

## Register 3 — State and locality

- Courts possessed real military and donative capacity.
- Power moved through officers, chiefs and local bodies.
- Territorial vocabulary varied by region and record.
- Ur: ordinary peasant-locality association.
- Sabha: especially Brahmana grant-settlement association.
- Nagaram: merchant-linked corporate locality.
- Mahajana: Chalukya-area village elders in Sharma's account.
- Do not project Uttaramerur committees backward.
- Immunity is privilege, not automatic sovereignty.
- Best label: layered and regionally variable authority.

## Register 4 — Grants and agrarian expansion

- Brahmadeya: grant to Brahmanas.
- Agrahara: often tax-privileged Brahmana settlement/endowment.
- Devadana: grant to deity or temple.
- Grants could express royal initiative and integration.
- Some opened cultivation; others redistributed inhabited land.
- Tanks, wells and channels required organised maintenance.
- Immunities could redirect taxes, provisions and labour.
- Expansion and peasant burden could coexist.
- Kalabhra anti-landlord interpretation rests on late partisan evidence.
- Every charter needs rights-specific reading.

## Register 5 — Brahmanization and society

- Brahmanization includes land, ritual, status and temple-Puranic institutions.
- Sanskritization captures prestige adoption but not the whole political economy.
- Local chiefs actively used genealogies and ritual.
- Local deities and languages reshaped wider traditions.
- Varna was normative; jatis and occupations were more numerous.
- Incorporation of forest/pastoral groups was uneven.
- Reciprocity existed within unequal power.
- Elite women could act as patrons.
- Lokamahadevi and Trailokyamahadevi are located examples, not universal gender evidence.

## Register 6 — Economy and exchange

- Rural expansion did not imply total commercial collapse.
- Kanchi combined court, religion, weaving and commerce.
- Mamallapuram was a major port outlet.
- Monuments demonstrate specialist labour and surplus access.
- They do not prove universal prosperity.
- Nagaram connects locality and merchants.
- Later Ayyavole detail needs periodisation.
- Sri Lankan links show maritime connectivity, not overseas empire.
- Use "regional commercial reconfiguration."

## Register 7 — Religion and language

- Alvars: Vaishnava Tamil poet-saints.
- Nayanars: Shaiva Tamil poet-saints.
- Bhakti connected emotion, shrine and vernacular expression.
- It did not abolish caste or patriarchy.
- Jain and Buddhist institutions remained in competition.
- *Mattavilasa-prahasana*: courtly satire under Mahendravarman I.
- Sanskrit served transregional prestige.
- Tamil could serve documentary and devotional functions.
- Grantha is a script tradition, not a language or proof of empire.
- Bilingualism reveals layered political communication.

## Register 8 — Historical art context

- Pallava trajectory: cave -> monolithic/relief -> structural.
- Mandagapattu: material-innovation claim.
- Mamallapuram rathas: varied monolithic experiments, not Pandava structures.
- Great relief: Arjuna/Ganga ambiguity and political play.
- Shore Temple and Kailasanatha: Rajasimha context.
- Chalukya landscape: Badami -> Aihole -> Pattadakal.
- Durga temple name does not settle dedication.
- Virupaksha: Lokamahadevi and Kanchi victory memory.
- Vesara is a cautious descriptor, not an ethnic formula.
- Art and Culture owns detailed plan, tower and iconographic technique.

## Register 9 — Historiography and final verdict

- Sharma highlights grants, intermediaries and peasant burdens.
- Universal urban decline is not defensible.
- Integrative approaches show grants and cults building regions.
- Integration could be coercive and hierarchical.
- Regional change was non-linear.
- Early medieval is a process, not one threshold year.
- Dynastic decline did not erase institutions.
- Final verdict: competing courts created unequal regional orders through warfare, grants, locality, devotion, language and exchange.
