```text

                         |

                         |

         no durable successor empire, but
         no instantaneous universal collapse
```

The visual establishes the topic's basic grammar: contraction of one imperial formation and creation of several regional formations happened together.

### Plain-language intuition

Suppose a very large company loses control of its branches. One possibility is that everything closes. Another is that branch managers, suppliers and local institutions reorganise into several new companies. Post-Gupta India resembles the second pattern more than the first. The old imperial centre weakened, but political energy did not disappear. It moved into new capitals, alliances, subordinate rulers, landholding networks and regional courts.

### 1. Fix the chronological frame

The practical frame is **c. 550-750 CE**:

- around the mid-sixth century, imperial Gupta concentration had contracted;
- competing houses became more visible across northern and eastern India;
- Harsha ruled from **606 to 647 CE** in the standard chronology;
- his death did not create one successor empire;
- by the mid-eighth century, the Pala and Gurjara-Pratihara phases belonged to the next political configuration.

The dates are a scaffold, not a claim that every region changed in the same year. A Bengal copper plate, a Thanesar genealogy and an Odisha land grant describe different local rhythms.

### 2. The evidence families

| Evidence | Best historical use | Built-in limit |
|---|---|---|
| Bana's *Harshacharita* | Pushyabhuti genealogy, accession crisis, court ideals | courtly *akhyayika*, selective and eulogistic |
| Xuanzang's travel account | routes, monasteries, Harsha's court, assemblies, Nalanda | Buddhist pilgrim purpose, later writing and some hearsay |
| Banskhera and Madhuban charters | grants, officials, dues, royal camp and territorial language | issue-specific documents, not a complete state manual |
| Aihole inscription | Pulakeshin II's check on Harsha | rival Chalukya praise, not a neutral battle report |
| Eastern copper plates | land purchase, tax remission, local actors and agrarian expansion | region-specific elite records |
| Coins and seals | ruler, title, symbol, institutional identity and exchange | circulation does not map borders or mass prosperity |
| Archaeology | settlements, monasteries, material sequences and urban change | attribution and dating can remain uncertain |

### 3. Why source disagreement is useful

Bana builds a morally charged story of dynastic crisis and rescue. Xuanzang presents Harsha as an admired ruler inclined toward Buddhism. Aihole celebrates the Chalukya ruler who checked Harsha. These are not reasons to abandon the sources. Their differences reveal how courts and pilgrims wanted power to be remembered.

The historian's sequence is:

```text
claim
  -> identify speaker and genre
  -> ask what the source could observe
  -> compare a different source class
  -> state the narrowest defensible conclusion
```

### 4. The "dark age" trap

Calling the whole period a dark age makes three errors:

1. it treats reduced imperial unity as absence of government;
2. it overlooks eastern inscriptions, districts, grants and military organisation;
3. it mistakes uneven urban change for universal civilisational collapse.

The correction is not to invent uninterrupted prosperity. Some older centres declined, monetisation changed and landed intermediaries gained strength. The defensible verdict is **uneven regional reorganisation**.

### Example and its limit

Pundravardhanabhukti charters record land transactions, local functionaries and new cultivation in Bengal. They prove organised agrarian and administrative activity there. They do not prove that every eastern village followed an identical process or that beneficiaries' records fully represent cultivators' experience.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** "Regional reorganisation" is a polite expression that hides political fragmentation and insecurity.

**Reply:** The term does not deny conflict. It explains why fragmentation could produce both insecurity and new institutions. Harsha's wars, Shashanka's rivalry and post-Harsha turbulence belong beside land charters, Nalanda and regional courts.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** separate source-author pairs, dynasties, capitals and chronology.
- **Mains:** open with contraction plus creation; avoid both "Harsha restored the Gupta Empire" and "nothing existed after the Guptas."
- **Answer use:** one named source and one limitation should accompany every major territorial claim.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Chronology** -> c. 550-750 CE bridge -> Gupta contraction, Harsha and early Pala threshold -> regions changed at different speeds.
- **Regionalisation** -> power moves into several courts -> Maukharis, Pushyabhutis, Gauda and Kamarupa -> plurality is not statelessness.
- **Bana** -> accession and court ideology -> *Harshacharita* -> courtly design limits literal reading.
- **Xuanzang** -> routes, assemblies and monasteries -> Harsha and Nalanda -> pilgrim purpose shapes emphasis.
- **Aihole** -> rival limit-source -> Pulakeshin II checks Harsha -> victory rhetoric cannot fix every battle detail.
- **Eastern plates** -> land and local administration -> Pundravardhanabhukti -> beneficiary records underrepresent ordinary experience.
- **Dark-age trap** -> imperial contraction becomes total collapse -> contradicted by new states and institutions -> use uneven transformation.
- **Method** -> claim, source, corroboration, limit -> literary plus inscriptional evidence -> no single archive is complete.

### Visual first

```text
                          NORTH INDIA, c. 550-606 CE

     Valabhi / west          middle Ganga            Thanesar / north-west
       MAITRAKAS      LATER GUPTAS + MAUKHARIS         PUSHYABHUTIS
            \                  |                           /
             \                 |                          /
              +----------- competitive field -----------+
                                  |
                    east: GAUDA and KAMARUPA
                                  |
                    south: CHALUKYA counter-power
```

The diagram is relational, not a border map. It shows simultaneous centres whose alliances and rivalries made Harsha's rise possible.

### Plain-language intuition

Harsha did not walk into an empty landscape. Think of a chessboard after the strongest piece has been removed: several pieces become more important, and the next phase depends on their positions and alliances. The post-Gupta field included old lineages, former subordinates and rising regional houses.

### 1. The principal northern houses

| House | Main association | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Later Guptas | parts of Magadha and the middle Ganga region | imperial name survived without restoring the earlier empire |
| Maukharis | Kannauj | controlled the strategic centre linked to Harsha through marriage |
| Pushyabhutis | Sthanishvara/Thanesar | Harsha's dynasty; initially one power among several |
| Maitrakas | Valabhi | durable western polity and diplomatic comparator |
| Gauda | Bengal under Shashanka | major eastern rival in Harsha's accession and campaigns |
| Kamarupa | Brahmaputra valley under Bhaskaravarman | autonomous eastern state and political actor |

### 2. Why the Maukharis matter

The Maukharis are not a disposable preface. Rajyashri, Harsha's sister, married the Maukhari ruler **Grahavarman**. This alliance tied the Thanesar house to Kannauj. When Grahavarman was killed and Rajyashri imprisoned, a family crisis became a struggle over the middle Ganga political centre.

Thus:

```text
marriage alliance
      -> claim and obligation
      -> crisis at Kannauj
      -> Pushyabhuti intervention
      -> Harsha's shift toward Kannauj
```

### 3. Maitrakas and the western field

The Maitrakas ruled from Valabhi. Their presence prevents a simple "Guptas disappeared, Harsha replaced them" narrative. Harsha's relations with Valabhi involved pressure, diplomacy and wider prestige. Attendance or deference at an assembly does not by itself prove annexation.

### 4. The direct PYQ frame

UPSC Prelims 2021 Q34 asks which kingdoms held power in northern India between Gupta decline and Harshavardhana's rise. The printed set includes Guptas of Magadha, Paramaras of Malwa, Pushyabhutis of Thanesar, Maukharis of Kanauj, Yadavas of Devagiri and Maitrakas of Valabhi. The correct historical cluster is Later Guptas, Pushyabhutis, Maukharis and Maitrakas; Paramaras and Yadavas belong later.

The lesson is not just a list. Chronological elimination works by testing whether a house belongs to the sixth-century northern field.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A list of dynasties does not explain historical change.

**Reply:** Correct. The explanatory pattern is the multiplication of power centres, importance of marriage and subordinate relationships, and strategic rise of Kannauj. The list becomes history only when these relations are shown.

### UPSC integration

- Build a four-house core: Later Guptas, Maukharis, Pushyabhutis, Maitrakas.
- Add Gauda and Kamarupa when the question expands toward eastern India.
- Exclude later Paramaras and Yadavas from the immediate sixth-century frame.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Later Guptas** -> imperial afterlife in a regional form -> Magadha/middle Ganga -> not the earlier empire at full scale.
- **Maukharis** -> strategic Kannauj house -> Grahavarman and Rajyashri -> marriage drives political succession.
- **Pushyabhutis** -> Thanesar base -> Prabhakaravardhana to Harsha -> early genealogy remains thinner than later events.
- **Maitrakas** -> western regional power -> Valabhi -> diplomacy or deference is not automatic annexation.
- **Gauda** -> eastern counter-power -> Shashanka -> Bengal cannot be reduced to Harsha's province.
- **Kamarupa** -> Brahmaputra polity -> Bhaskaravarman -> alliance and autonomous agency coexist.
- **PYQ elimination** -> test dynasty against date and region -> 2021 Q34 -> later houses are deliberate distractors.

```text
PRABHAKARAVARDHANA strengthens THANESAR
                   |
```

The family tree is a causal map. Marriage, death and captivity transformed a regional dynasty into a claimant to Kannauj.

### Plain-language intuition

A succession crisis is not simply a sequence of family tragedies. In a royal house, marriage creates alliances, captivity threatens legitimacy, and rescue can become a political claim. Bana turns these events into drama because Harsha's authority needed a moral story as well as military success.

### 1. The secure late genealogy

The early Pushyabhuti line is not equally clear in every detail. The historically useful sequence becomes firmer with **Prabhakaravardhana**, presented by Bana as the ruler who strengthened Thanesar. His children were Rajyavardhana, Harsha and Rajyashri.

The correct method is not to memorise an impressive remote pedigree. It is to identify where evidence becomes politically dense.

### 2. Marriage as statecraft

Rajyashri's marriage to Grahavarman connected the Pushyabhutis with the Maukharis of Kannauj. It linked:

- a north-western base at Thanesar;
- a middle-Ganga strategic centre at Kannauj;
- two ruling families facing a competitive regional field.

Marriage did not mechanically merge the two states. It created claims, obligations and an alliance network that became decisive during crisis.

### 3. Bana's accession sequence

According to *Harshacharita*:

1. Prabhakaravardhana died around 605 CE.
2. Rajyavardhana succeeded.
3. A Malava ruler killed Grahavarman and imprisoned Rajyashri.
4. Rajyavardhana defeated the Malava force.
5. Shashanka of Gauda killed Rajyavardhana through stratagem.
6. Harsha became king in 606 CE and moved to rescue Rajyashri.

The broad crisis is historically important. The speeches, motives and precise treachery details belong to Bana's literary and courtly framing.

### 4. Rajyashri: political actor and symbolic figure

Rajyashri's marriage joins two dynasties; her imprisonment makes the Kannauj crisis immediate; her rescue helps Bana frame Harsha as protector and legitimate ruler. Some interpretations read her as symbolising royal fortune. That reading is analytically useful but must not erase the historical woman or turn literary symbolism into proven intention.

Her near-sati episode is evidence about an elite courtly narrative. It is not a census of all women's lives.

### 5. Thanesar to Kannauj

Thanesar remained the dynastic base, but Kannauj offered:

- Maukhari legitimacy;
- a central Ganga-Yamuna position;
- access to eastern and western routes;
- a fortifiable strategic node;
- a capital suited to wider north Indian ambition.

The shift was therefore both inherited and strategic.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because Bana is a court poet, the entire accession story is fiction.

**Reply:** Genre requires qualification, not total rejection. Named persons, relationships and the broad crisis fit the political context. What remains uncertain is the dramatic texture: motives, speeches and the exact manner of Rajyavardhana's death.

### UPSC integration

- Treat accession as **crisis-driven consolidation**, not smooth inheritance.
- Use Rajyashri's marriage to explain Kannauj, not as biographical decoration.
- Attribute treachery claims explicitly to Bana.
- Do not generalise Rajyashri's elite episode into a universal gender conclusion.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Prabhakaravardhana** -> strengthens Thanesar line -> Bana's late secure genealogy -> earlier pedigree remains uncertain.
- **Rajyashri-Grahavarman** -> marriage alliance -> Pushyabhuti-Maukhari bridge -> kinship does not equal immediate merger.
- **Crisis chain** -> death, killing, captivity and campaign -> *Harshacharita* -> dramatic detail needs attribution.
- **Rajyavardhana** -> brief succession and military response -> encounter with Shashanka -> manner of death is source-sensitive.
- **Harsha, 606 CE** -> succeeds during emergency -> rescue and consolidation -> not inheritance of a ready empire.
- **Kannauj** -> legitimacy plus geography -> middle Ganga centre -> capital shift has several causes.
- **Rajyashri evidence** -> elite political woman and literary symbol -> near-sati narrative -> not a social census.

```text
       WEST                         CORE                       EAST
   Valabhi / Maitrakas       Thanesar -> Kannauj       Gauda / Shashanka
       pressure,                direct authority          rivalry, unstable gains

                        ally or political partner,

```

This is a relationship map. It deliberately separates direct authority, pressure, rivalry and alliance.

### Plain-language intuition

An empire map tempts us to colour every place mentioned in a ruler's biography with the same shade. That is precisely the mistake to avoid. A ruler may campaign in one region, receive deference from another, ally with a third and administer only a core territory.

### 1. Harsha's secure political weight

Harsha was not a minor ceremonial figure. His authority rested on Thanesar, Kannauj and substantial Ganga-region resources. The Maukhari inheritance widened his core, while campaigns and assemblies projected prestige across northern India.

Yet the phrase "all northern India" is too flat. A safer model distinguishes:

- **direct core**;
- **strong influence or temporary military success**;
- **allied rulers**;
- **subordinate or tributary chiefs**;
- **autonomous rivals**.

### 2. Gauda and Shashanka

Shashanka ruled Gauda from Karnasuvarna and was a major eastern rival. Bana places him in Rajyavardhana's death narrative. Upinder Singh notes that Harsha defeated Shashanka but that Shashanka appears to have recovered lost territory. The east therefore cannot be represented as one permanent conquest.

Xuanzang later accused Shashanka of hostility to Buddhism and damage to the Bodhi tree. This is important evidence for Buddhist memory and political-religious conflict. It is not sufficient by itself to prove a uniform, empire-wide persecution policy.

### 3. Kamarupa and Bhaskaravarman

Bhaskaravarman ruled Kamarupa in the Brahmaputra valley. He intersected with Harsha's eastern politics and is often described as Harsha's ally against Gauda. Kamarupa had its own lineage, grants and political ambitions. Alliance did not dissolve its sovereignty.

After Shashanka's death, Bhaskaravarman extended influence into parts of Bengal. This reinforces his agency rather than making him an appendage of Kannauj.

### 4. Valabhi and other western relations

Harsha is said to have impressed his power on Valabhi. Later diplomatic accommodation and assembly attendance show a relationship, but sources do not justify treating Valabhi as a routinely governed province. The Maitrakas remained a significant western line.

Similarly, references to Sindh, Kashmir or Malava must be graded. Campaign, diplomatic exchange and acknowledgment of superiority are not identical.

### 5. Embassies and prestige

Upinder Singh notes exchanges of embassies with Tang China. Diplomacy projected status and linked Harsha's court to wider Asian politics. It did not create administrative control over every polity participating in exchange.

### Example and its limit

The attendance of rulers at Harsha's assemblies can indicate prestige and hierarchy. It cannot alone show that their taxes, appointments and village administration were controlled from Kannauj.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If Harsha could summon rulers and campaign widely, calling his power "graded" understates an empire.

**Reply:** Graded power can still be imperial in aspiration and substantial in effect. The distinction simply prevents prestige, alliance and direct government from being treated as the same relation.

### UPSC integration

- Use Shashanka to test the eastern limit of a Harsha-centred narrative.
- Use Bhaskaravarman to show alliance with autonomy.
- Use Valabhi to distinguish prestige from annexation.
- Reserve the hard southern limit for the next lesson's Aihole evidence.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Direct core** -> Thanesar-Kannauj and Ganga territories -> real administrative weight -> not a pan-Indian map.
- **Gauda** -> Shashanka's eastern state -> Karnasuvarna and rivalry -> Harsha's gains were not permanently final.
- **Buddhist accusation** -> Xuanzang on Bodhi-tree hostility -> source for memory and conflict -> not automatic proof of uniform persecution.
- **Kamarupa** -> Bhaskaravarman's Brahmaputra polity -> eastern alliance and intervention -> never reduce alliance to annexation.
- **Valabhi** -> Maitraka western centre -> pressure, diplomacy and assembly relations -> direct administration remains unproved.
- **Embassies** -> Tang exchanges -> prestige and connection -> diplomacy is not territory.
- **Graded authority** -> core, influence, allies and rivals -> different political relationships -> one colour on a map conceals them.

### Plain-language intuition

The strength of a ruler is tested not only by where he went, but by where another power stopped him. Harsha's northern prestige is real; the Chalukya record matters because it tells the story from the frontier he could not convert into stable control.

### 1. What Aihole actually contributes

Ravikirti's Aihole inscription praises Pulakeshin II and records the check suffered by Harsha. It is a rival *prashasti*, not an impartial dispatch. Yet its political direction is secure: Harsha did not absorb the Chalukya Deccan.

Scholars differ over the precise date and whether the event should be called a crushing defeat or a forced retreat. The safe formulation is:

> A confrontation near the Narmada checked Harsha's southward expansion and marked the practical limit of his stable reach.

### 2. Why rival praise can be good evidence

A victory eulogy may exaggerate the winner, but it can still identify:

- the rival being celebrated against;
- the direction of military pressure;
- the fact that the rival's advance did not produce annexation;
- the political importance assigned to the encounter.

The solution is symmetrical criticism: Bana's northern court praise and Aihole's Chalukya praise both require limits.

### 3. Graded sovereignty

Harsha's polity can be modelled as:

| Band | Relationship | Evidence discipline |
|---|---|---|
| Core | Thanesar, Kannauj and much of the Ganga plain | strongest case for direct rule |
| Extended reach | probable eastern territories and subordinated chiefs | test each claim against grants and local rulers |
| Prestige network | assemblies, diplomacy, allied rulers and era use | acknowledgment does not equal province |
| Frontier | Narmada and autonomous Chalukya state | campaign stopped; Deccan remained outside |

This model avoids two errors: inflating Harsha into an all-India ruler and shrinking him into a powerless ceremonial king.

### 4. Comparison with Gupta power

The Gupta Empire also used varied relationships, but Harsha's authority was more visibly personal, mobile and dependent on subordinate rulers. He inherited some administrative vocabulary, not the full territorial scale or dynastic continuity of Gupta rule.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because Aihole is a Chalukya eulogy, its claim of victory should be discarded.

**Reply:** Discarding all partisan evidence would erase most early-medieval political history. Cross-reading its broad result with the absence of Harshan Deccan administration supports a bounded conclusion without accepting every boast.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Ravikirti-Aihole-Pulakeshin II; Narmada as the remembered limit.
- **Mains:** write "major north Indian hegemon" and immediately add the southern check.
- **Trap:** Narmada is not a timeless civilisational boundary; it marks a specific political encounter.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Aihole** -> rival Chalukya *prashasti* -> Ravikirti and Pulakeshin II -> victory rhetoric requires caution.
- **Narmada** -> practical southern limit -> Harsha's advance checked -> not a permanent cultural frontier.
- **Direct core** -> administrative evidence and sustained control -> Thanesar-Kannauj-Ganga zone -> exact edges remain debated.
- **Prestige network** -> assemblies, allies and era use -> wider recognition -> not the same as provincial government.
- **Graded sovereignty** -> different relationships at different distances -> stronger than one-colour empire map -> avoids inflation and denial.
- **Gupta comparison** -> inherited terms but altered structure -> more mobile and mediated kingship -> do not call Harsha a simple restorer.

### Visual first

```text
                         KING AND MOBILE CAMP
                                  |
                  +---------------+---------------+
                  |                               |
          inherited territorial units       personal inspection,
          bhukti -> vishaya -> locality      assemblies and campaigns
                  |                               |
             named officers                 boats, horses, elephants
                  +---------------+---------------+
                                  |
                     mediated regional government
```

Harsha's government combined fixed territorial language with a court that moved to inspect, campaign and stage authority.

### Plain-language intuition

A government can rule from files and permanent offices, or by repeatedly bringing the ruler, army and court into contact with local elites. Harsha's administration used both. Copper plates show units and officers; the royal "camp of victory" shows power on the move.

### 1. Continuity from Gupta administration

Upinder Singh notes broad continuity in units and designations:

- **bhukti**: a large territorial unit, often translated as province;
- **vishaya**: a district-level unit;
- smaller localities and villages below them;
- high officials associated with grants and royal service.

The translations are aids, not exact modern equivalents. Functions varied by place and record.

### 2. Banskhera and Madhuban

These inscriptions reveal grant procedure, official titles, fiscal terms and the royal camp. The Banskhera charter is especially memorable for preserving Harsha's signature and calligraphic association.

Their strength is documentary specificity. Their weakness is narrow purpose: a grant charter records what mattered to that transaction, not every department of government.

### 3. The moving court

Xuanzang describes Harsha touring and dividing time between administration and religious works. The inscriptions' camp contains boats, horses and elephants. This suggests **itinerant kingship**: rule performed through movement, inspection, military presence and direct encounter.

```text
royal tour
   -> local chiefs and officers assemble
   -> disputes, grants, tribute and loyalty become visible
   -> king redistributes gifts and confirms hierarchy
   -> court moves onward
```

Movement could strengthen reach, but it also reveals dependence on local mediation.

### 4. Officials and local power

Bana mentions *vanapalas*, or forest guards. Upinder notes *sarva-palli-pati*, "chief of all villages." Titles show differentiated functions, but they should not be forced into a modern ministry chart.

Village communities, scribes, beneficiaries and regional elites participated in implementation. Central command and local agency were intertwined.

### 5. Revenue vocabulary

Harsha-age inscriptions preserve terms known earlier:

| Term | Safe exam sense |
|---|---|
| *bhaga* | share of produce or revenue claim |
| *bhoga* | dues, supplies or enjoyment-right depending on context |
| *kara* | tax or tribute |
| *hiranya* | cash or monetary due |

Xuanzang reports light taxation and a one-sixth grain share. This is useful testimony, not a complete all-India budget.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the king had to tour constantly, administration must have been weak.

**Reply:** Mobility can be a technique of government where communication is slow and authority is personal. The limit is that touring cannot prove equal administrative density in every locality.

### UPSC integration

- Pair *bhukti-vishaya* continuity with the innovation of mobile kingship.
- Use inscriptions for offices and dues; use Xuanzang cautiously for workload and tax impressions.
- Do not recreate a Mauryan-style central bureaucracy from scattered titles.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Bhukti-vishaya** -> territorial hierarchy continues -> Banskhera/Madhuban context -> functions vary regionally.
- **Grant charter** -> official and fiscal detail -> transaction-specific evidence -> not a complete constitution.
- **Itinerant kingship** -> authority moves with court and army -> royal camp and tours -> mobility reveals both reach and dependence.
- **Camp of victory** -> boats, horses and elephants -> military-administrative presence -> reported scale needs caution.
- **Officials** -> *vanapala*, *sarva-palli-pati* and high officers -> differentiated work -> avoid modern departmental equivalence.
- **Revenue** -> *bhaga, bhoga, kara, hiranya* -> continuity in fiscal vocabulary -> exact incidence is record-specific.
- **Xuanzang** -> light-tax and one-sixth report -> outsider observation -> not a universal fiscal manual.

### Visual first

```text
ROYAL CLAIM OVER LAND / REVENUE
               |
        grant or endowment
               |
  +------------+-------------+
  |                          |
beneficiary rights      service relationship

```

The same grant could extend cultivation and royal networks while strengthening an intermediary who later acted more independently.

### Plain-language intuition

Giving someone revenue rights can solve an immediate problem: reward service, support a monastery or bring new land under cultivation. But it can also create a local power-holder. That double effect lies at the centre of the samanta and feudalism debate.

### 1. What a samanta was

*Samanta* could describe a subordinate ruler or powerful chief integrated into a hierarchy. *Mahasamanta* marked higher status. The relationship varied:

- military support;
- tribute or gifts;
- court attendance;
- use of the overlord's era;
- local government under retained authority.

It did not always mean a salaried officer, nor did it always mean an independent enemy.

### 2. Grants as bundles of rights

A land grant might transfer selected combinations of:

- revenue collection;
- tax exemption;
- access to produce;
- control over waste or uncultivated land;
- administrative or judicial privileges;
- labour and service claims.

No single formula applied everywhere. The charter's clauses control the inference.

### 3. Agrarian expansion

In eastern India, grants often concerned fallow, forested or waterlogged land. Endowment could:

1. define boundaries;
2. attract settlement;
3. extend cultivation;
4. support Brahmanas or monasteries;
5. spread writing and official Sanskrit;
6. create new local hierarchies.

Agrarian growth and social subordination could therefore occur together.

### 4. Sharma's feudal tendency

R. S. Sharma linked land-based payment, reduced central cash resources, samantas and declining old urban centres to an emerging feudal order. Harsha's reported huge forces may include feudatory contingents rather than a centrally maintained standing army.

This model explains delegation and local landed power sharply. Its risk is treating diverse regions as one uniform path.

### 5. Regional-state correction

Later approaches stress that grants also produced state formation, cultural integration and new political centres. Local elites were not simply symptoms of decline; they could be agents of expansion.

The balanced conclusion is:

> The period shows feudal tendencies through transferred rights and intermediaries, but each region must be tested before declaring a complete feudal system.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** "Feudalism" imports a European category and should never be used for India.

**Reply:** The label becomes misleading when treated as identity. It remains analytically useful if broken into testable mechanisms: land-based service, transferred revenue, dependent cultivators, fortified local power and weakened central cash control.

### UPSC integration

- Define mechanisms before using the label.
- Show both integration and autonomy.
- Use eastern grants as regional evidence, not an all-India template.
- Topic 26 owns the full ancient-to-medieval historiographical debate; this lesson supplies the Harsha/eastern case.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Samanta** -> subordinate ruler or chief -> military and ceremonial obligations -> autonomy varied.
- **Mahasamanta** -> higher subordinate status -> era use and court hierarchy -> not a fixed modern rank.
- **Grant bundle** -> revenue, exemption and selected rights -> copper-plate clauses -> never assume total ownership transfer.
- **Agrarian expansion** -> fallow or forest land enters cultivation -> eastern charters -> growth can deepen hierarchy.
- **Feudal tendency** -> land-based service and intermediaries -> Sharma's model -> regional diversity limits totalisation.
- **Regional-state correction** -> grants build new centres -> Bengal, Odisha and Assam -> local agency is not mere decay.
- **Army numbers** -> possible feudatory contingents plus rhetoric -> not a precise standing-force census.

The economy changed by region and institution. One city's decline cannot stand for the whole subcontinent.

### Plain-language intuition

When an old railway junction loses importance, economic life does not necessarily vanish; traffic may shift to highways, regional towns or new institutions. The Pataliputra-Kannauj contrast works similarly. It reveals changing political geography, not a complete disappearance of exchange.

### 1. Sharma's capital-shift argument

Sharma associates Pataliputra's earlier strength with trade, money and river connections. As trade and coin use weakened in some zones and land-based payment expanded, strategic camps and fortified centres gained importance. Kannauj's elevated middle-doab position suited this order.

This is a structural argument, not simply a story that Harsha preferred one city.

### 2. Counter-indicators from the east

The same broad period supplies evidence that resists a universal decline thesis:

- Bengal land transactions used gold coins called *dinaras*;
- Shashanka and other eastern rulers issued coinage;
- military camps required revenue, animals, boats and supplies;
- Nalanda concentrated agrarian surplus from supporting villages;
- regional capitals and monasteries remained active nodes.

These examples do not prove universal prosperity. They show that monetisation and institutional concentration survived in selected settings.

### 3. Trade and routes

Kannauj's location facilitated movement across the doab by land and water. Bengal's river systems and camps with boats point to political use of waterways. Eastern coastal and riverine zones connected inland agrarian production with regional exchange.

Evidence for volume remains incomplete. Route existence is not a statistical measure of trade.

### 4. Agrarian surplus and institutions

Nalanda demonstrates how rural revenue could support a large non-agrarian institution. This is not evidence against agrarianisation; it shows one way agrarian surplus was concentrated and redistributed.

### 5. A balanced verdict

Use three tests:

```text
Which region?
Which indicator?
Which time span?
```

A fall in one city's prominence, a thin coin series and a flourishing monastery can all be true at once.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A few coins and monasteries cannot overturn broad evidence of urban decline.

**Reply:** They need not overturn it. They prevent overgeneralisation. The appropriate conclusion is differentiated contraction and growth, not a single national curve.

### UPSC integration

- Compare Pataliputra and Kannauj as different political-economic geographies.
- Use Bengal *dinaras* and Nalanda as counter-indicators.
- Avoid converting coin presence into high mass prosperity or coin scarcity into barter-only collapse.

### Mini recap and revision

- **Pataliputra** -> old imperial-commercial centre loses weight -> Sharma's structural argument -> not every town follows it.
- **Kannauj** -> strategic fortified doab node rises -> routes plus Maukhari inheritance -> city choice was not personal whim.
- **Dinara transactions** -> selected Bengal monetisation -> land purchase records -> do not infer economy-wide abundance.
- **Regional coinage** -> rulers advertise authority and exchange -> Shashanka and eastern issues -> circulation exceeds political borders.
- **Nalanda support** -> agrarian surplus sustains scholarship -> village revenues -> institution is not proof of universal welfare.
- **Routes** -> land and river movement -> doab and eastern waterways -> route evidence does not quantify traffic.
- **Verdict** -> uneven transformation -> contraction and new nodes coexist -> reject both total-collapse and universal-prosperity slogans.

The archive is socially unequal. Historical reconstruction must distinguish visibility from population size or importance.

### Plain-language intuition

If future historians had only government orders, university records and celebrity biographies, they would know much more about officials and famous people than ordinary households. Post-Gupta evidence has a similar tilt. The answer must say who produced the record and whose experience remains faint.

### 1. Visible social groups

Eastern charters name rulers, officials, scribes, merchants, artisans, landholders, cultivators and beneficiaries. They reveal social differentiation and participation in land transactions. They do not provide equal detail about labour conditions or household life.

### 2. Brahmanical integration and local agency

Land grants helped establish Brahmana settlements and Sanskrit legal-political culture. This process is often called Sanskritisation or Brahmanisation, but it was not a one-way replacement of passive communities. Local cults, chiefs, cultivators and ecological knowledge shaped the resulting regional society.

### 3. Gender and Rajyashri

Rajyashri mattered politically through marriage, succession and Bana's rescue narrative. Her near-sati episode demonstrates an elite courtly possibility and literary ideal. It cannot establish:

- prevalence among all women;
- one uniform regional custom;
- the absence of women's economic work;
- the social condition of non-elite households.

The best answer uses the episode and immediately names its social level and genre.

### 4. Law, punishment and insecurity

Xuanzang reports legal punishments and generally favourable royal order, yet he also experienced robbery. His descriptions may translate Indian practices for a Chinese audience and emphasise values such as filial piety.

The coexistence of royal ideals, harsh penalties and insecurity is more credible than either a perfectly peaceful realm or universal lawlessness.

### 5. Charity and welfare

Rest houses, gifts and religious charity appear in pilgrim accounts. They show:

- royal generosity as an ideal;
- support for travellers and religious communities;
- redistribution through assemblies and institutions.

They do not prove universal welfare coverage, equal access or a modern public-service state.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because elite sources omit ordinary people, social history is impossible.

**Reply:** It is incomplete, not impossible. Transaction participants, land categories, labour dues, settlement expansion and source silences allow bounded reconstruction. The limit must become part of the answer.

### UPSC integration

- Separate normative hierarchy from lived practice.
- Identify elite women without generalising their experience.
- Read Xuanzang's penal and welfare descriptions through audience and route.
- Mention invisible groups to demonstrate source awareness, not to invent their experience.

- **Archive bias** -> rulers and beneficiaries dominate -> charters and court texts -> visibility is not representativeness.

- **Rajyashri** -> dynastic marriage and crisis -> Bana's elite narrative -> never generalise to all women.
- **Punishment** -> Xuanzang reports legal severity -> audience and moral purpose matter -> not a uniform penal census.

- **Charity** -> gifts, rest houses and assemblies -> royal-religious redistribution -> not modern universal welfare.

### 3. Kannauj assembly

- magnified Harsha's patronage;
- placed the pilgrim-scholar in a prestigious role;
- gathered rulers and religious specialists;
- staged political hierarchy before an audience.

It was not a modern parliament or proof that doctrinal conflict had disappeared.

### 4. Prayaga assembly

The quinquennial Prayaga gathering involved lavish distribution and worship of the Buddha, Shiva and the Sun on successive days. Its logic joined:

```text
charity
   + multi-sect ritual
   + attendance of rulers
   + public redistribution
   = sovereignty performed as generosity
```

### 5. Scale and source caution

Xuanzang admired Harsha. His description is valuable, especially where he personally attended, but numbers, harmony and generosity may reflect political idealisation. The event's existence and broad character are stronger than every quantitative detail.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Political interpretation reduces genuine religious devotion to cynical propaganda.

**Reply:** The two can coexist. Royal belief may be sincere, while public ritual and patronage also organise hierarchy and legitimacy.

- Replace one-time conversion with plural and changing affiliations.

- Use Buddha-Shiva-Sun succession as a precise memory anchor.
- Do not call the assemblies secular conferences.


No source wins by itself. Agreement strengthens a claim; disagreement exposes perspective, chronology or unequal access.

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine three witnesses: the ruler's biographer, a visiting religious scholar and a legal clerk. Each notices different things and has different incentives. The historian's job is not to select the most attractive witness, but to ask what each could know and why each recorded it.

### 1. Bana and the *Harshacharita*

Strengths:

- proximity to Harsha's court;
- dynastic genealogy and accession narrative;
- rich description of court, countryside and camp;
- political ideals and elite social vocabulary.

Limits:

- *akhyayika* and courtly literary form;
- ornate Sanskrit and digression;
- patron praise;
- incomplete narrative ending before a full reign-history.

Bana is excellent for how court society imagined power, but not a transparent transcript.

### 2. Xuanzang

Strengths:

- travelled through many regions;
- attended Harsha's court and assemblies;
- described monasteries, routes, learning and regional polities;
- provides an external comparative perspective.

Limits:

- Buddhist purpose and admired patrons;
- mixed eyewitness, hearsay and remembered information;
- travel chronology and retrospective compilation;
- possible magnification of numbers and harmony.

Xuanzang is not an outsider free from bias; he is an outsider with a different bias.

### 3. Inscriptions

Strengths:

- names, titles, genealogy, offices, grants and fiscal clauses;
- stronger chronological anchors;
- direct record of specific transactions.

Limits:

- eulogistic introductions;
- formulaic language;
- survival and discovery bias;
- narrow transactional purpose.

The Banskhera charter can illuminate offices and Harsha's signature without becoming a complete biography.

### 4. Rival and later testimony

The Aihole inscription checks northern imperial claims. Later writers and traditions may preserve memories but must not silently fill contemporary gaps. A later repetition is not automatically independent confirmation.

### 5. The three-question source test

```text
ACCESS: Could this source know?
PURPOSE: Why did it speak?
CONTROL: What independent evidence checks it?
```

Apply this before extracting a fact.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If every source is biased, firm knowledge is impossible.

**Reply:** Bias is information. It tells us what the source wished to legitimise. Firm conclusions arise where access, purpose, independent control and material context converge.

### UPSC integration

- Introduce a Mains answer with source classes, not a list of books.
- Add one precise strength and limit for every major source.
- Use 2020 Prelims Q97's Sthanesvara pair only as a place-location support route; its other pairs belong elsewhere.
- Never replace evidence analysis with "sources are biased."

- **Bana** -> court access and dynastic detail -> *Harshacharita* -> praise and literary design limit literal reading.
- **Xuanzang** -> route and assembly observation -> Buddhist comparative lens -> eyewitness and hearsay must be separated.
- **Charters** -> offices, grants and dates -> Banskhera/Madhuban -> narrow legal purpose.
- **Aihole** -> rival control on northern claim -> Pulakeshin II praise -> partisan but indispensable.
- **Later memory** -> possible preservation -> distance and dependence -> repetition is not independent proof.
- **Access-purpose-control** -> source test -> converts bias into disciplined inference.
- **Triangulation** -> agreement plus contradiction -> bounded knowledge -> no favourite-source method.

### 1. Shashanka and Gauda

Shashanka made Gauda a major power and used Karnasuvarna as a capital. He contested the middle Ganga region and interacted with the Harsha-Kamarupa alignment. His kingdom demonstrates an eastern political centre acting on north Indian politics, not merely reacting to Kannauj.

Samatata in south-eastern Bengal followed its own trajectory, with rulers and connections not reducible to Gauda. Deltaic waterways facilitated cultivation, movement and regional exchange while also producing shifting frontiers.

### 3. Agrarian and cultural expansion

Charters from Bengal record grants, land categories, boundary-making, transactions and Brahmana settlements. Sanskrit political vocabulary spread, but regional society emerged through interaction with local communities and landscapes.

### 4. The Pala bridge

Later tradition describes disorder before Gopala's accession, often summarised as *matsyanyaya*. Gopala's selection or election should not be turned into modern democracy. It suggests elite action to stabilise a fragmented field.

The Palas:

- emerged in eastern India;
- competed for Kannauj;
- supported major Buddhist institutions;
- coexisted with Brahmanical patronage;
- linked eastern India to wider intellectual networks.

### Plain-language intuition

When a coalition depends heavily on one leader, the leader's death can end the coalition without destroying every member. Harsha's death weakened the Kannauj-centred arrangement, but regional states, grants, agriculture and institutions continued.

### 1. The succession problem

Harsha left no enduring direct successor. Chinese accounts connect a usurper often called Arunasva with conflict involving a Chinese mission and regional intervention. The episode's details rely on external records and should remain qualified.

The secure inference is a succession crisis, not a perfectly documented national revolution.

### 2. What fragmented

Fragmentation concerned:

- hegemonic coordination;
- tribute and alliance networks;
- control over strategic Kannauj;
- the capacity of one ruler to stage north Indian primacy.

It did not mean all villages, grants, monasteries, markets or regional courts stopped at once.

### 3. Kannauj after Harsha

Kannauj retained strategic and symbolic value. Later competition among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas and Rashtrakutas demonstrates that:

- Harsha's death did not make the city irrelevant;
- control of the middle Ganga plain remained prestigious;
- the political system became multipolar.

```text
        -> Parantaka I expands Chola power
```

This is a cross-cutting question. Topic 22 owns the Pala bridge; Topics 23 and 27 own Pallava and Chola depth.

### 5. Multi-causal explanation

Harsha's personal rulership, absence of a stable successor, strong subordinate and allied powers, regional agrarian growth and the durable attraction of Kannauj together explain transition. No single "weak king" or invasion explains the whole pattern.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If no successor maintained Harsha's scale, the state simply collapsed.

**Reply:** The hegemonic structure did collapse, but governance and state formation relocated. Distinguishing level of analysis converts a cliché into an explanation.

- Connect personal kingship to succession vulnerability.
- Use later Kannauj competition as evidence of continuing strategic value.
- Keep the cross-cutting chronology ownership explicit.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks | 150 words

### Question

Explain why Harsha should be described as a major north Indian hegemon rather than a pan-Indian emperor.

### Model answer

Harsha built the most influential north Indian kingship of the early seventh century, but the evidence supports graded hegemony rather than uniform subcontinental empire.

His direct core lay around Thanesar, Kannauj and much of the Ganga plain. Campaigns, alliances and subordinate rulers extended his prestige eastward; assemblies at Kannauj and Prayaga displayed a hierarchy broader than the directly governed zone. Banskhera and Madhuban charters reveal officers, territorial units and a mobile royal camp, yet they do not establish equal administrative density everywhere.

Kamarupa under Bhaskaravarman remained an autonomous ally with its own dynasty and grants. Valabhi combined conflict, marriage diplomacy and continued rulership. Most decisively, Ravikirti's Aihole inscription records Pulakeshin II checking Harsha near the Narmada, and no Harshan Deccan administration followed.

Thus Harsha's achievement lay in coordinating a powerful northern network through conquest, diplomacy, tours and ceremonial prestige. Distinguishing direct rule, subordination, alliance and rivalry preserves both the scale and the limit of his power.

## 15 marks | 250 words

### Question

Land grants in post-Gupta India were instruments of both political integration and decentralisation. Discuss with reference to northern and eastern India.

### Model answer

Post-Gupta grants were not simple gifts of private property. They transferred selected bundles of revenue, exemption, land-use and sometimes administrative or judicial rights. Their dual effect explains why historians connect them both with state expansion and local autonomy.

**Integration:** Charters defined boundaries, validated claims through royal authority and linked beneficiaries to rulers. Grants to Brahmanas and monasteries supported settlement in fallow, forested or waterlogged land. In Bengal, Assam and Odisha they helped extend cultivation, writing, Sanskrit political vocabulary and religious institutions. Supported villages enabled Nalanda to transform local agrarian surplus into transregional scholarship.

**Decentralisation:** Revenue assigned to beneficiaries reduced the share handled directly by the centre. Officers and *samantas* could be sustained through land-based rights and military-service relationships. Locally entrenched chiefs gained resources, followers and judicial leverage. Harsha's reliance on a mobile court and subordinate contingents suggests that effective authority often required negotiation.

R. S. Sharma interpreted these features as feudal tendencies: land-based payment, intermediary power and contraction of older monetary-urban structures. The model is illuminating but cannot be universalised. Bengal records transactions in *dinaras*; regional capitals and monasteries flourished; and grants could create new state capacity rather than merely reflect decline.

Therefore, grants must be read clause by clause and region by region. They expanded agrarian and cultural networks while simultaneously embedding authority in local intermediaries—a productive but unstable form of regional state formation.

## 20 marks | 300 words

### Question

The post-Gupta age is better understood as an age of regional state formation than as a political dark age. Critically examine with reference to Harsha and eastern India.

### Model answer

The phrase "dark age" treats the contraction of Gupta imperial unity as disappearance. Yet the sixth to eighth centuries display a denser, more regional political order whose sources and institutions differed from the earlier empire.

After Gupta decline, Later Guptas, Maukharis, Pushyabhutis and Maitrakas competed across northern India. Harsha used the Maukhari inheritance of Kannauj, campaigns, a mobile court, *samantas* and alliances to build a major hegemony. Banskhera and Madhuban show administrative continuity in territorial units and fiscal vocabulary. Xuanzang and Bana reveal touring kingship, charity and court ideology. The rival Aihole inscription, however, fixes a Narmada limit; Kamarupa and Valabhi show that influence was not annexation.

Regionalisation also had material foundations. Land grants extended cultivation and supported Brahmana and monastic institutions. While Sharma's feudal model highlights intermediary power, land-based service and contraction of some older towns, Bengal *dinara* transactions, Kannauj's rise and Nalanda's supported intellectual economy demonstrate uneven transformation rather than universal decline.

Eastern India supplies the clearest corrective. Shashanka made Gauda an independent force; Samatata followed a deltaic trajectory; Bhaskaravarman's Kamarupa was an autonomous ally; and Odisha's coast-river-hinterland zones produced several regional lineages. Later Pala consolidation joined agrarian power, Buddhist institutions and transregional networks without making society uniformly Buddhist.

The strongest objection is that Harsha's death caused rapid political fragmentation. It did end a personalised Kannauj-centred network, but regional states and institutions continued, while Kannauj remained a coveted centre.

Thus the age witnessed loss of one imperial scale alongside formation of new regional capacities. "Regional state formation" explains both fragmentation and creativity better than a dark-age label.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Register 1 - Core thesis and chronology

- Post-Gupta history is **regionalisation**, not a source-less dark age.
- Harsha's conventional reign: **606-647 CE**; some modern reconstructions place his death in 648.
- Later Guptas, Maukharis, Pushyabhutis and Maitrakas form the immediate northern mosaic.
- Harsha's rise followed the Maukhari-Pushyabhuti marriage alliance, Grahavarman's death, Rajyavardhana's death and Rajyashri's crisis.
- Kannauj became Harsha's main political centre; Thanesar remained dynastically important.
- Shashanka's death chronology is disputed; use approximate or attributed language.

## Register 2 - Political career and limits

- Harsha was a major **north Indian hegemon**, not a pan-Indian emperor.
- Distinguish direct core, subordinate relationships, alliances and rivalry.
- Gauda under Shashanka was an autonomous eastern competitor.
- Kamarupa under Bhaskaravarman was an autonomous ally.
- Valabhi demonstrates changing conflict, diplomacy and marriage ties.
- Ravikirti's Aihole inscription records Pulakeshin II checking Harsha near the Narmada.
- Rival *prashasti* evidence is partisan but indispensable.

## Register 3 - Administration

- Gupta-derived units such as *bhukti* and *vishaya* continued.
- Banskhera and Madhuban illuminate grants, offices, dues and the royal camp.
- Harsha's government combined territorial administration with **itinerant kingship**.
- The mobile camp joined court, army, inspection and redistribution.
- Named roles include forest guards and village-level chiefs; functions must not be modernised.
- Fiscal vocabulary includes *bhaga, bhoga, kara* and *hiranya*.
- Xuanzang's light-tax and one-sixth observations are useful but not universal statistics.

## Register 4 - Samantas and grants

- *Samanta* denotes a subordinate ruler/chief with variable service and autonomy.
- Obligations could include tribute, military aid, court attendance and era use.
- Grants transferred selected bundles of revenue, exemption and local rights.
- Eastern grants supported cultivation, settlement, Brahmana and monastic institutions.
- The same process could extend royal legitimacy and strengthen intermediaries.
- Sharma's feudal model identifies land-based service and decentralisation.
- Regional-state approaches stress expansion, integration and new centres.
- Best verdict: **feudal tendencies, regionally tested; not one complete uniform system**.

## Register 5 - Economy and society

- Pataliputra's relative decline and Kannauj's rise signal changed political-economic geography.
- Coin use and trade changed unevenly; Bengal *dinara* transactions qualify total-demonetisation claims.
- Nalanda concentrated agrarian surplus in a specialised institution.
- Route presence demonstrates connection, not volume.
- Charters show differentiated officials, scribes, merchants, artisans, cultivators and beneficiaries.
- The archive privileges elites; silence must be acknowledged without invented reconstruction.
- Rajyashri's near-sati is elite literary evidence, not a universal social statistic.
- Xuanzang's order, punishment, robbery and charity evidence should be read together.

## Register 6 - Religion and culture

- Early Pushyabhutis have solar association; Harsha also appears in Shaiva idiom and as a Buddhist patron.
- Avoid a neat exclusive-conversion story.
- Kannauj assembly: Mahayana-centred debate plus royal spectacle.
- Prayaga assembly: redistribution and Buddha-Shiva-Sun worship.
- Religious devotion and political legitimation can coexist.
- Nalanda predates Harsha, receives his support and later expands under the Palas.
- Xuanzang studied under Shilabhadra.
- Bana's *Harshacharita* and *Kadambari* illuminate court culture.
- *Ratnavali*, *Priyadarsika* and *Nagananda* are traditionally attributed to Harsha; qualify authorship.

## Register 7 - Sources

- Bana: court proximity, dynasty and ideology; literary praise and incomplete narrative.
- Xuanzang: travel, assemblies and monasteries; Buddhist purpose, hearsay and numerical caution.
- Charters: titles, dates, offices and rights; formulaic and transaction-specific.
- Aihole: rival control on imperial claim; Chalukya eulogy.
- Nidhanpur: Kamarupa lineage and grants; charter purpose and genealogy.
- Source test: **access -> purpose -> independent control**.
- Bias is not a reason for dismissal; it is part of interpretation.

## Register 8 - Eastern India

- Gauda and Samatata are distinct Bengal trajectories.
- Shashanka made Karnasuvarna-centred Gauda a major power.
- Gopala's reported selection signals elite consolidation, not modern democracy.
- Palas supported Nalanda and Buddhist networks while Brahmanical and local traditions persisted.
- Senas later rose in Bengal; no overnight religious replacement.
- Bhaskaravarman's Kamarupa had its own dynasty, court and grants.
- Odisha contained multiple regional zones and lineages linked by coast, river and hinterland.
- Art and Culture owns detailed temple form; Topic 22 owns regional political-social formation.

## Register 9 - Post-Harsha transition

- Harsha's lack of a durable successor weakened a highly personal network.
- Arunasva episode is chiefly known through external accounts; details require caution.
- The hegemony fragmented, not all governance or society.
- Kannauj retained symbolic and strategic importance.
- Later Pala-Pratihara-Rashtrakuta competition demonstrates multipolarity.
- Cross-cutting 2020 sequence: Pallava -> Pala -> Pratihara -> Chola.
- Best explanation is multi-causal: succession, mediated power, regional growth and strategic geography.

## Register 10 - High-value traps

1. Harsha's assembly attendees were not automatically provincial subjects.
2. Kamarupa was an ally, not a Harshan province.
3. Aihole is useful despite being praise; use bounded inference.
4. Grants do not mechanically prove total feudalism.
5. Xuanzang was foreign but not neutral.
6. Shashanka's anti-Buddhist image must be attributed.
7. Harsha supported Nalanda but did not single-handedly found it.
8. Pala patronage did not make all eastern society Buddhist.
9. Rajyashri's episode cannot represent all women.
10. Post-Harsha fragmentation was not simultaneous universal collapse.
