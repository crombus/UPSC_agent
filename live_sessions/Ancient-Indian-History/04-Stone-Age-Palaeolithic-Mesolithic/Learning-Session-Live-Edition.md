# The Stone Age: Palaeolithic and Mesolithic - Learning Session Live Edition

Imagine entering a landscape with no written names, no dated royal records and no eyewitness narrative. What survives is quieter: a flaked edge, a scatter of manufacturing waste, an animal bone, a hearth-like patch, a burial, a painted wall or sediment that last saw sunlight hundreds of thousands of years ago. The task is not to make these traces speak more confidently than they can. It is to reconstruct human choices by connecting technology, landscape, chronology and context while keeping every inference within its evidentiary limit.

This session therefore teaches the Stone Age as a problem of reasoning, not as a procession of tool names. The learner first acquires the grammar of periodisation, dating and lithic manufacture; then studies Lower, Middle and Upper Palaeolithic evidence; then reconstructs subsistence, mobility, Mesolithic regional variation, burials, rock art and the uneven transition toward food production. Topic 03 supplies the ecological prerequisite. Topic 05 takes over when established cultivation, domestication and village food production become the main subject.

### 1. Begin with the simplest intuition

If four classrooms adopt a new textbook in different years, the sequence “old edition to new edition” remains useful, but one date cannot describe all classrooms. Stone Age periodisation works similarly. It groups recurring technological and behavioural tendencies, while each site preserves its own sequence.

The **Palaeolithic**, or Old Stone Age, is conventionally divided into Lower, Middle and Upper phases. The **Mesolithic** is associated with regionally varied hunter-gatherer adaptations, often including microlithic composite tools, broader resource use, longer attachment to some places, burials and rock art.

### 2. Four levels that must not be collapsed

| Level | Meaning | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| Macro-phase | Broad comparative category such as Lower Palaeolithic | Organises large tendencies |
| Regional sequence | Order of change in a valley or ecological zone | Tests whether a broad model fits locally |
| Dated stratum | Archaeological layer linked to a dated sample or event | Anchors a particular context |
| Assemblage | Associated tools, waste, structures, fauna and other remains | Reconstructs activity and lifeway |

✅ **Fact:** R.S. Sharma gives broad textbook bands of about 600,000-150,000 BCE for the Lower Palaeolithic, 150,000-35,000 BCE for the Middle Palaeolithic, 35,000-10,000 BCE for the Upper Palaeolithic and 9000-4000 BCE for the Mesolithic.

⚠️ **Qualification:** These are source-specific orientation bands, not an updated all-India master clock. Upinder Singh emphasizes great regional variation, and the stratified Attirampakkam sequence places a local Acheulian-to-Middle Palaeolithic transition at about `385 ± 64 ka`.

### 3. A phase is not a people

An archaeological phase classifies patterns in material evidence. It does not automatically identify:

- one biological species;
- a race or ethnic group;
- a language;
- a political community;
- a universal level of intelligence.

Technologies can persist, spread, be learned or be independently reorganised. Human fossils are much rarer than tools in South Asia, so a tool-to-species equation usually outruns the evidence.

### 4. Continuity matters as much as change

The word “transition” can mislead if it suggests that one toolkit vanished overnight. Lower Palaeolithic forms could continue into contexts where flakes became more prominent. Microliths could appear before the conventional Holocene Mesolithic and survive in later food-producing settings. Period labels capture changing emphasis, not total replacement.

### Example and its limit

Attirampakkam demonstrates that a securely dated local sequence can revise a conventional boundary. It does not date every Middle Palaeolithic assemblage in India, prove simultaneous change elsewhere or identify the hominin makers.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If every region has different dates and overlap, the period labels become useless.

**Reply:** Their value is comparative, not legislative. They let us compare dominant tool-making strategies and lifeways while regional stratigraphy prevents false uniformity.

**Balanced assessment:** Use the broad scaffold to orient the examiner, then allow dated local sequences to qualify it.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Reject options that treat one phase as a biological population or a fixed all-India date.
- **Mains:** Write `broad phase -> diagnostic tendency -> regional example -> chronological limit`.
- **Boundary:** Detailed environmental geography belongs to Topic 03; detailed food-producing cultures belong to Topic 05.
- **Transition:** The next lesson explains what environmental change can and cannot cause.

### Revision notes

1. Periodisation is an analytical scaffold.
2. Regional dated sequences override rigid universal dates.
3. Phase, stratum and assemblage are different units.
4. Technology is not race, language or species.
5. Transition means changed emphasis with possible continuity.
6. Microliths are not confined to one narrow chronological box.
7. Textbook dates should be attributed to their source framework.
8. Attirampakkam is powerful because it is stratified and dated.
9. One site cannot date the entire subcontinent.
10. Use the phrase “regional mosaic with overlapping practices.”

The environment changes what is available and costly; human knowledge and organisation shape the response.

### 1. Geological time as a frame

The Pleistocene began about 2.6 million years ago and included repeated climatic oscillations. The Holocene began roughly 12,000 years ago. Most Palaeolithic evidence belongs within the Pleistocene, while most conventional Mesolithic contexts fall within the Holocene. Geological and cultural boundaries, however, are not identical.

Pleistocene changes affected rainfall, river behaviour, vegetation, animal distributions and sea level. These altered where water, food, shelter and raw material could be found. They did not issue one command to every community.

### 2. What is a proxy?

A **proxy** is an indirect indicator of a past condition.

| Proxy | Possible information | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| River terrace or sediment | Erosion, deposition and channel history | Artefacts may be moved or mixed |
| Pollen and phytoliths | Vegetation and plant use | Transport and preservation affect representation |
| Animal remains | Habitat, diet and seasonality | Recovery and identification bias |
| Lake sediment | Rainfall, erosion and water-level change | Usually represents a local or regional catchment |
| Stable isotopes | Diet, mobility or hydrological exposure | Baselines and sample size constrain interpretation |
| Volcanic ash | Chronological/environmental event | Presence does not establish one social outcome |

The age of a sediment, the age of a bone and the time when a tool was made are three separate claims unless their association is demonstrated.

### 3. River landscapes both preserve and disturb

The Soan, Narmada, Son and Belan valleys preserve major prehistoric evidence. Rivers deposit sediments that can seal artefacts, but they can also erode, transport and redeposit them. A tool in gravel may be older than the deposit that finally contains it, or it may have moved far from its place of manufacture.

This is why **primary context** matters. A primary context preserves an object close to where it was made, used or discarded. A secondary context has displaced it. Secondary finds remain evidence for regional presence, but they are weaker for reconstructing one activity event.

### 4. Regional examples

- In the Son and Belan valleys, palaeoenvironmental studies connect changing river systems with changing occupation possibilities.
- Thar sediments show that today's aridity cannot be projected unchanged into the Pleistocene.
- Early Holocene wetlands in the middle Ganga valley offered fish, turtles, plants and game that supported intensive foraging.
- The Toba eruption about 75,000 years ago left ash in peninsular river valleys, but its demographic and ecological effects in South Asia remain debated.

### 5. Avoid two opposite errors

**Environmental determinism** says climate directly causes one social result. **Environmental neglect** treats human communities as independent of water, plants, animals and landforms. Both fail.

The better chain is:

> environmental change -> altered resource pattern -> choices mediated by technology, knowledge, mobility and social organisation -> regionally variable outcome

### Example and its limit

The abundance of wetlands and aquatic fauna around middle-Ganga Mesolithic sites helps explain resource opportunities. It does not by itself prove permanent settlement, territorial ownership or a single annual schedule. Those claims require deposits, seasonality studies, structures and burials.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Since the climate affects every living population, it must be the primary cause of technological change.

**Reply:** Similar environmental changes can produce different responses, and similar technologies can persist across environmental shifts. Human choices, inherited techniques, social networks and raw-material access mediate adaptation.

**Balanced assessment:** Climate is indispensable context but rarely a sufficient explanation.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Distinguish geological epochs, archaeological phases and dated samples.
- **Applied 2026 link:** The Prelims demand on Pleistocene shifts in Yamuna, Sutlej, Ganga and Indus drainage is owned by Geography. Here it supports only the methodological point that changing river systems affect archaeological context.
- **Mains:** Use causal verbs such as “conditioned,” “altered” and “mediated,” not “inevitably produced.”
- **Transition:** To read human response, the learner must next understand how stone tools were actually made.

### Revision notes

1. Pleistocene and Holocene are geological epochs.
2. Cultural phase and geological epoch are not synonyms.
3. A proxy indirectly represents a past process.
4. Date the proxy and occupation independently.
5. Rivers preserve and redeposit evidence.
6. Primary context is stronger for activity reconstruction.
7. Present landscapes cannot be projected unchanged backward.
8. Toba's South Asian impact remains debated.
9. Wetlands provide opportunities, not automatic sedentism.
10. Climate changes opportunity sets; human mediation explains outcomes.

### 1. Plain-language intuition

When a carpenter's workshop contains wood shavings, unfinished boards, tools and completed furniture, we can reconstruct production rather than merely admire the final object. A lithic site works in the same way. The waste flakes may explain more than a single handaxe because they preserve the order of actions.

### 2. Essential vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Core | Stone mass from which pieces are removed | Preserves reduction planning |
| Flake | Piece detached from a core | May be used or further shaped |
| Blade | Elongated removal, generally more than twice as long as wide | Efficient blank for specialised edges |
| Retouch | Secondary modification of an edge | Shapes, sharpens or blunts |
| Debitage | Waste generated during reduction | Identifies manufacture and stages |
| Rough-out | Partly shaped unfinished tool | Reveals production sequence |
| Hammerstone | Percussor used to detach pieces | Indicates technique and force |
| Hafting | Attaching a stone element to a handle or shaft | Creates a composite implement |

The **chaîne opératoire**, or operational chain, follows raw-material selection, core preparation, blank production, retouch, hafting, use, maintenance and discard.

### 3. Context decides the inference

- A finished tool alone shows a manufactured form.
- Cores plus flakes show reduction.
- Rough-outs and high waste ratios suggest manufacture.
- Use-wear and residues help identify worked material.
- Spatial clusters can separate knapping, butchery, hearth and habitation areas.
- Non-local stone may indicate direct procurement, mobility or exchange.

No single clue decides every question. The strongest reconstruction comes from the whole assemblage.

### 4. Form is not function

A pointed edge looks suitable for piercing, but appearance alone does not prove actual use. **Microwear analysis** studies polish, striations and edge damage. Residue analysis may detect worked material. Experimental archaeology tests whether a proposed manufacturing or use sequence is feasible.

The result should still be phrased carefully: “consistent with cutting hide” is stronger than “probably unused,” but more responsible than “certainly a hide-working knife” when the evidence is incomplete.

### 5. Primary, semi-primary and secondary context

Upinder Singh distinguishes:

- **primary context:** close to the place of manufacture or use;
- **semi-primary context:** slightly displaced;
- **secondary context:** substantially removed.

A surface scatter may map a broad distribution. A sealed floor with refitting flakes can reconstruct a specific activity far more confidently.

### Example and its limit

At Isampur, limestone slabs, cores, rough-outs, finished and unfinished tools, hammerstones and large quantities of debitage support quarrying and manufacture. Use-marks on some tools and the wider assemblage also support habitation or subsistence activity. The site is not merely a “factory,” nor does the debris reveal the language or species of the workers.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Stone tools are too simple and repetitive to reveal complex behaviour.

**Reply:** Platform preparation, raw-material transport, standardised forms, repair and spatial organisation reveal learned skill, planning and landscape knowledge. Simplicity of surviving material is not simplicity of action.

**Balanced assessment:** Lithics are abundant and behaviourally rich, but organic equipment and plant use are systematically under-preserved.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Separate a reduction strategy from a finished tool type.
- **Mains:** Explain technology as an operational sequence, not a catalogue.
- **Common trap:** A beautiful tool is not automatically more informative than production waste.
- **Transition:** With the grammar in place, we can now read Lower Palaeolithic assemblages.

### Revision notes

1. A core is the parent mass; a flake is a detached blank.
2. A blade is an elongated flake, not every sharp stone.
3. Retouch modifies an edge after detachment.
4. Debitage records manufacture.
5. Rough-outs reveal incomplete production stages.
6. Hafting turns stone into part of a composite tool.
7. Operational chains reconstruct sequences of action.
8. Form suggests function; wear and residue test it.
9. Refitting and spatial clusters strengthen activity inference.
10. Organic tools are under-represented by preservation.

### 1. What is the Acheulian?

The **Acheulian** is a Lower Palaeolithic technological tradition strongly associated with bifacial large cutting tools, especially handaxes and cleavers.

- A **handaxe** is shaped on two faces, often with a pointed or ovate working form.
- A **cleaver** has a broad transverse cutting edge.
- A **chopper** is usually shaped by removals from one side of a cobble or core.

Acheulian assemblages also contain cores, flakes, scrapers, hammerstones and waste. “Acheulian equals handaxe” is a useful first memory hook but an incomplete archaeological definition.

### 2. Soanian evidence and its caution

The Soan or Sohan valley in the Potwar region became central to early prehistoric research. Pebble tools, flakes and other artefacts occur in terrace and gravel contexts. Older research tried to match terrace sequences mechanically with European glacial stages. Much of that correlation is no longer accepted.

The safe lesson is double:

1. the north-west preserves important core-flake and pebble-tool evidence;
2. fluvial context can redeposit material, so technological comparison must be joined to geomorphology and dating.

The labels **Soanian** and the older **Madrasian** are historical-technological classifications. They are not races or civilisations.

### 3. Site distribution is not random

Lower Palaeolithic evidence is widespread in rocky margins, river valleys and peninsular landscapes. Sites often connect:

- dependable water;
- workable stone;
- plant and animal resources;
- sheltered or defensible locations;
- movement corridors.

Important regions include the Soan-Siwalik zone, Narmada valley, Son-Belan valleys, central India, Hunsgi-Baichbal valley, Tamil Nadu and Bhimbetka.

### 4. What a large cutting tool reveals

Bifacial shaping requires selecting suitable stone, visualising a form, managing angles and removing flakes in a sequence. Symmetry can improve handling or edge control, but not every handaxe had one identical purpose. Experimental and wear studies suggest chopping, cutting, butchery, woodworking and other tasks were possible.

The tool records technical knowledge. It does not prove one species, one gender of maker or one specialised occupation.

### 5. The settlement-system idea

A quarry, a manufacturing locality, a short task site and a recurrent camp may belong to one connected mobility system. Counting each as an isolated “culture” would miss the landscape-level organisation.

```text
STONE SOURCE -> QUARRY/WORKSHOP -> TASK LOCATION -> CAMP/SHELTER
       ^                                                |
       +-------------- seasonal return ----------------+
```

### Example and its limit

Bhimbetka preserves evidence from multiple prehistoric and later phases, including Lower Palaeolithic tools and recurrent shelter use. Its long sequence demonstrates the attractiveness of a landscape over time. **Balanced assessment:** Distribution maps technological relationships and questions; it does not directly map ethnicity.

### 1. Attirampakkam: the power of a dated sequence

Attirampakkam lies in the Kortallaiyar river basin of Tamil Nadu. Excavations exposed a long stratified sequence with Acheulian assemblages and later technological change.

✅ **Fact:** Burial dating and palaeomagnetic work placed early Acheulian levels at not less than about 1.07 million years, with a pooled average of about `1.51 ± 0.07 million years`.

✅ **Fact:** Luminescence dates place the end of the Acheulian and the beginning of a local Middle Palaeolithic transition at about `385 ± 64 ka`, with the Middle Palaeolithic continuing there to about `172 ± 41 ka`.

The sequence shows declining bifaces, more small tools, Levallois flake and point techniques, and blade-making developments. The numbers belong to this stratified site and its sampled sediments. They do not date all India.

### 2. Why Attirampakkam matters conceptually

Before precise dating, typological schemes could make South Asian transitions appear much later. Attirampakkam forces a revised question: not “When did India copy a sequence known elsewhere?” but “How did different South Asian regions participate in early technological transitions?”

No diagnostic hominin fossil occurs in the sequence. Therefore the dates revise technological chronology but do not identify the makers.

### 3. Hunsgi-Baichbal: a valley as a settlement system

The Hunsgi-Baichbal valleys in Karnataka preserve sites of different size and composition:

- limited assemblages that may represent specialised tasks;
- larger concentrations that may represent temporary camps;
- extensive sites with varied artefacts suggesting longer use;
- local and non-local raw materials;
- possible shelter arrangements marked by large stones.

This variation supports a landscape of repeated movement rather than random wandering.

### 4. Isampur: manufacture in action

Isampur lies near a seasonal stream and siliceous limestone sources. The Acheulian assemblage includes:

- cores of different shapes;
- large flakes;
- chopping tools, knives, handaxes, cleavers and scrapers;
- numerous unfinished tools;
- hammerstones;
- abundant debitage;
- evidence of quarrying and successive manufacturing stages.

Middle Palaeolithic evidence includes chert flake tools, cores, hammerstones and prepared-core reduction. Weathered and used tools also indicate habitation and food-processing activity. The site therefore combines extraction, production and use.

✅ **Fact with scope:** Upinder Singh reports ESR dates of about 1.2 million years on bovid tooth enamel from Isampur, making it one of the earliest dated Lower Palaeolithic contexts in the subcontinent in that source.

### 5. Three lessons about scientific dates

1. A date belongs first to the sampled material or depositional event.
2. Association with human activity must be demonstrated stratigraphically.
3. Regional extrapolation requires other independently dated sequences.

Precision without scale control becomes false certainty.

### Example and its limit

The high proportion of unfinished tools and waste at Isampur strongly supports manufacture. It does not mean the site served no other activity; use-wear and occupation evidence broaden its function.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If dates are attached to sediments or tooth enamel rather than directly to tools, they are too indirect to matter.

**Reply:** Secure stratigraphic association is a normal archaeological bridge between dated samples and artefacts. The bridge must be demonstrated, not dismissed or assumed.

**Balanced assessment:** Scientific dating gains historical power from context and loses power when association is weak.

### 1. Hominid, hominin and species

- **Hominid** is a broad biological group including modern and extinct great apes and humans.
- **Hominin** is narrower: modern humans, extinct human species and their immediate ancestors.
- A **species** is a biological classification, not a tool industry.

Evolution was not a neat ladder in which one form waited for another to disappear. Different hominin populations overlapped, migrated and sometimes interbred.

### 2. Hathnora in the Narmada valley

In 1982, Arun Sonakia discovered a fossilized cranial fragment near Hathnora on the northern bank of the Narmada. It occurred in sandy-pebbly gravel with vertebrate fossils and a few late Acheulian tools.

Different researchers have classified the fragment as:

- an advanced form of *Homo erectus*;
- an archaic form of *Homo sapiens*;
- an intermediate or distinct archaic hominin.

The age and exact taxonomic placement remain debated. Later central Narmada discoveries include a clavicle and fragments of long bones, but the overall fossil sample remains sparse.

The safest statement is:

> Hathnora provides rare, important evidence of archaic hominin presence in South Asia, while its precise age, taxonomy and relation to nearby tool-makers remain contested.

### 3. Association is not authorship

A fossil and tools in the same broad gravel formation can be related archaeologically without proving that the individual made those specific tools. Depositional processes, chronological resolution and sample scarcity matter.

This distinction protects against statements such as:

- “All Indian Acheulian handaxes were made by *Homo erectus*.”
- “Middle Palaeolithic flakes prove the arrival of modern humans.”
- “One fossil classifies every nearby assemblage.”

### 4. Denisovans and what the PYQ actually tests

Denisovans were an archaic human population identified through remains and ancient DNA associated with Denisova Cave in Siberia. Their genetic contribution appears in some living populations.

The concept teaches three controls:

1. “Denisovan” refers to an archaic human population, not dinosaurs or a geological period.
2. Genetic relationship does not mean a Denisovan fossil has been securely identified in India.
3. Population history can include mixture; it is not a clean sequence of total replacements.

### 5. Genetics has a boundary

Ancient DNA can illuminate ancestry, relatedness and population interaction when suitable samples survive. It cannot directly reveal language, caste, religion or archaeological “culture.” South Asian tropical conditions often reduce DNA preservation, making claims especially sample-sensitive.

### Example and its limit

Hathnora is invaluable because it adds biological evidence to a lithic-rich region. It does not settle one species label, provide a complete skeleton or authorize the memorable but misleading equation “Narmada person = maker of all Indian handaxes.”

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Tool complexity should allow archaeologists to infer the species that made it.

**Reply:** Learned technologies can be shared by different populations, and related populations can use different strategies in different settings. Biological identity requires biological evidence.

**Balanced assessment:** Tools reveal behaviour directly; fossils and DNA address biology, but the two records must be connected rather than collapsed.

- **Prelims:** Eliminate category errors—animal, cave-system and geological-period options.
- **Mains:** Use Hathnora as a paragraph on evidence value plus residual uncertainty.
- **Transition:** The next lesson returns to behaviour and asks what prepared-core technology changed.

The visual shifts attention from the final object to advance planning built into the core.

### 1. What changed?

Many Middle Palaeolithic assemblages show greater emphasis on:

- flakes rather than only large core tools;
- points, scrapers and borers;
- fine-grained raw materials;
- prepared-core strategies;
- lighter, more flexible toolkits.

Older handaxes, cleavers and choppers did not disappear universally. The change concerns assemblage balance and reduction organisation.

### 2. Levallois in plain language

Think of shaping a pencil before producing the exact shaving you want. In a Levallois sequence, the knapper prepares the core's convexities and striking platform so that a planned flake can be detached. The flake often needs relatively little further shaping.

The prepared object remaining after removals is sometimes called a “tortoise core” because of its form. Levallois is one prepared-core method; discoid reduction is another. The technique demonstrates planning, but planning is not exclusive to one species or period.

### 3. Regional evidence

- **Attirampakkam:** declining bifaces, prepared cores, flakes and points within a long stratified transition.
- **Nevasa/Chirki-Nevasa:** stratified flake-tool evidence behind the regional label “Nevasan.”
- **Didwana:** Middle Palaeolithic dates in a changing Thar landscape.
- **Son and Belan valleys:** numerous sites, including manufacturing localities and varied raw materials.
- **Kalpi:** Middle Palaeolithic stone and bone tools associated with vertebrate remains in a dated Yamuna-bank context.

“Nevasan” should remain a regional archaeological term, not a pan-Indian stage or people.

### 4. Raw material and function

Agate, jasper, chalcedony, chert and quartz can allow finer control than coarse quartzite. Yet raw-material preference depends on local availability, desired edge, transport cost and inherited skill. Smaller flakes therefore cannot be explained through climate or cognitive “advance” alone.

### Example and its limit

Attirampakkam demonstrates prepared-core planning at an unexpectedly early regional date. It does not prove the technique diffused from one centre or arrived with a biologically new population.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Prepared-core methods are so distinctive that they must indicate population replacement.

**Reply:** A technological break can result from innovation, learning, changing task needs, raw-material constraints, interaction or migration. Population replacement is only one hypothesis and needs biological or wider archaeological evidence.

**Balanced assessment:** Technological reorganisation is secure; its demographic cause remains a separate question.

- **Trap:** “More flakes” does not mean every biface vanished.
- **Transition:** The next lesson follows the expansion of blade, bone and ornament evidence.

### 1. Blades and burins

A **blade** is an elongated flake whose length is generally more than twice its width. Parallel-sided blades provide long cutting edges and can be retouched or hafted.

A **burin** is made on a blade or flake to create a strong chisel-like edge. It may be used for grooving or engraving wood, bone or antler. It is not merely any pointed stone.

Upper Palaeolithic assemblages also retained flakes, scrapers and heavier implements. Diversification did not erase earlier techniques.

### 2. Kurnool and the missing organic toolkit

The Kurnool and Muchchatla Chintamanu Gavi cave contexts in Andhra Pradesh are important for bone tools and faunal remains. Organic materials usually decay in open-air sites, so cave preservation reveals part of a toolkit that may once have been more widespread.

This is a preservation lesson: where stone dominates the record, it does not follow that wood, fibre, hide and bone were unimportant.

### 3. Ostrich-eggshell evidence

Ostrich eggshell fragments and beads occur in Upper Palaeolithic contexts at sites including Patne and Bhimbetka. Some fragments preserve deliberate engraving; perforated discs indicate careful bead manufacture.

These finds support:

- ornamentation;
- social display or communication;
- skilled production;
- possible movement or exchange of material.

They do not reveal the exact identity, rank, religion or verbal meaning assigned to the ornament.

### 4. Special objects and competing readings

The damaged carved bone from Lohanda Nala has been interpreted as a figurine by some and a harpoon by others. The triangular stone and rubble platform at Baghor I have invited ritual interpretation, partly through comparison with later local practices.

The method is:

```text
OBJECT -> CONTEXT -> WEAR/FORM -> COMPARISON -> ALTERNATIVES -> CONFIDENCE
```

Analogy can suggest a possibility. It cannot demonstrate uninterrupted belief across thousands of years.

### 5. Symbolism without a “sudden mind”

Ornaments, engravings, ochre and deliberate special placement show capacities for communication and meaning. They should not be used to claim that earlier hominins lacked symbolic ability or that “modern behaviour” appeared in one instantaneous revolution.

### Example and its limit

Ostrich-eggshell beads at Patne and Bhimbetka demonstrate deliberate ornament production. They do not prove a named clan system, trade route or religious doctrine.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the exact meaning is unrecoverable, calling an object symbolic is speculation.

**Reply:** Repeated standardisation, perforation, engraving and bodily placement can distinguish communicative or ornamental use from ordinary cutting. The broad symbolic inference can be strong even when its precise code is unknown.

**Balanced assessment:** Archaeology can establish meaningful marking more confidently than it can translate the meaning.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Blade and burin are Upper Palaeolithic markers; bone tools are prominent in Kurnool cave evidence.
- **Mains:** Pair each symbolic example with the limit on interpretation.
- **Trap:** Ornament does not equal a named religion.
- **Transition:** The next lesson reconstructs daily life beyond the surviving toolkit.

### 1. Beyond the “big-game hunter”

Palaeolithic food could include:

- fruits, berries, seeds, tubers and leafy plants;
- insects, birds and small mammals;
- large game;
- fish, turtles and shellfish;
- scavenged resources;
- seasonally concentrated foods.

Plant remains and fibre equipment usually preserve less well than stone and bone. Therefore archaeological visibility is not dietary proportion. A meat-heavy excavation record can coexist with substantial gathering.

### 2. Mobility as knowledge

Mobility may be scheduled around ripening plants, animal movement, water availability and stone procurement. Repeated visits to the same locations imply memory and socially transmitted knowledge.

Different sites can serve different roles:

| Site type | Likely activity | Evidentiary caution |
|---|---|---|
| Quarry/workshop | Raw-stone extraction and reduction | May also include habitation |
| Task site | Butchery, hunting or specialised work | Small assemblage can be difficult to date |
| Recurrent camp | Multiple domestic and productive activities | Reuse may create a palimpsest |
| Rock shelter/cave | Protection and repeated occupation | Over-represented in preservation |
| Aquatic locality | Fishing, shellfish or wetland use | Seasonal intensity needs faunal study |

A **palimpsest** is a deposit in which repeated activities overlap, making one event difficult to isolate.

### 3. Fire: powerful but contextual

Fire can provide warmth, protection, cooking, light, material transformation and social focus. Yet:

- a dark patch is not automatically a hearth;
- charcoal can be redeposited;
- natural fires can burn sediments;
- a global claim about early fire use does not date one Indian context.

Strong evidence combines an anthropogenic feature, burnt material, spatial pattern and secure stratigraphy.

### 4. Shelter and residence

Rock shelters such as Bhimbetka preserve recurrent use, but caves are not the only homes. Branch, grass, leaf and reed structures usually disappear. At Hunsgi, stone arrangements have been interpreted as possible supports for temporary shelters.

Residence duration should be inferred from a bundle:

- deposit thickness;
- repeated floors;
- hearths;
- structure;
- heavy equipment;
- seasonality;
- food remains;
- burials;
- rebuilding or maintenance.

### 5. Cooperation without invented institutions

Standardised tool-making implies teaching and learning. Large-game acquisition, movement of raw material and shelter construction can require cooperation. Sharing and reciprocity are plausible.

But a toolkit cannot directly establish:

- a chief;
- hereditary rank;
- fixed gender roles;
- a named kinship system;
- private ownership.

Ethnographic analogy can widen possibilities, but modern hunter-gatherers are not living fossils.

### Example and its limit

The combination of quarry, manufacturing and habitation evidence in the Hunsgi-Isampur landscape supports planned movement and learned production. It does not reveal the exact size, kinship structure or gendered division of every prehistoric group.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Without texts, social history is impossible; only tools can be described.

**Reply:** Spatial organisation, standardisation, transport, food remains, structures and repeated occupation reveal cooperation, learning and scheduling. What remains inaccessible is the exact institutional vocabulary.

**Balanced assessment:** Archaeology can reconstruct social capacities more securely than named social offices.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** A hearth claim needs context; a cave does not mean permanent residence.
- **Mains:** Organise lifeways as `resource breadth -> mobility -> site system -> cooperation -> preservation limit`.
- **Trap:** “Hunter-gatherer” is not synonymous with random movement or meat-only diet.
- **Transition:** The Mesolithic reorganises portable technology and intensifies several ecological niches.

The important innovation is not merely reduced size. It is modular design.

### 1. What is a microlith?

A microlith is a deliberately shaped small stone element, often under about 5 cm, commonly made on fine-grained stone and frequently designed for hafting.

**Non-geometric forms** include backed blades, points, scrapers and borers. **Geometric forms** include triangles, trapezes and lunates. Backing deliberately blunts an edge so it can be set safely into a shaft or handle.

Small fragments created accidentally are not microliths. Context, manufacture and form matter.

### 2. Composite-tool advantage

A composite tool combines stone inserts with wood, bone, fibre or adhesive. It can be:

- light to carry;
- specialised for different tasks;
- repaired by replacing one insert;
- produced from small quantities of fine stone;
- adapted to arrows, barbs, knives and other implements.

The design fits mobile lifeways, but it is not restricted to high mobility.

### 3. Chronological caution

Microliths were once treated as a simple marker of the post-Pleistocene Mesolithic. Evidence complicates that rule:

- Mehtakheri has reported microliths dated as early as about 48,000 years ago.
- Jwalapuram Locality 9 has microlithic evidence in Upper Palaeolithic levels around 35,000 BP.
- Sri Lankan cave sequences show long-lived microlithic rainforest adaptation.
- Microliths continue at Bagor and elsewhere into ceramic and food-producing phases.

Therefore:

> microlithic technology is a technological clue, not an exclusive index fossil for one period.

### 4. Broad-spectrum subsistence

**Broad-spectrum foraging** means using a wider combination of:

- fish, turtles and shellfish;
- small and large game;
- seeds, tubers and wild grains;
- forest, wetland, grassland and coastal resources.

It does not mean agriculture. Intensified plant processing, grinding or storage can occur within foraging.

### 5. Semi-sedentism is a bundle

**Semi-sedentism** refers to repeated or prolonged residence while mobility continues. Evidence may include thick deposits, huts, hearths, heavy grinding tools, stored resources, commensal animals, seasonality data and cemeteries.

No single marker decides:

- A hut can be seasonal.
- A cemetery can mark repeated return.
- A grinding stone can be cached.
- Pottery can be acquired by foragers.

### 6. Climate without a single-line transition

Early Holocene changes expanded some wetlands and forests while altering coastlines and seasonal resources. Communities responded by diversifying food, reorganising toolkits or revisiting rich localities. Not every group became more sedentary, and no environmental event mechanically produced farming.

### Example and its limit

Microliths at Baghor II, including a very high proportion of manufacturing waste, support specialised production and removal of finished tools. They do not establish the complete subsistence pattern by themselves.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because microliths can occur before and after the Mesolithic, the category has no chronological value.

**Reply:** Microliths remain diagnostically important when joined to stratigraphy, associated subsistence evidence and regional sequence. The error lies in using size alone as a date.

**Balanced assessment:** Technology identifies a practice; context locates that practice in time and economy.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Distinguish microlith, backing and composite tool.
- **Mains:** Explain Mesolithic change through technology, resource breadth and residence, not “small tools after ice age.”
- **Trap:** Grinding stone does not automatically equal cultivation.
- **Transition:** The next lesson maps how these possibilities were combined differently across Indian regions.

### Revision notes

2. Backing prepares an edge for hafting.
3. Geometric forms include triangles, trapezes and lunates.
4. Composite tools are modular and repairable.
5. Microliths can pre-date the Holocene.
6. They can continue into food-producing contexts.
7. Broad-spectrum subsistence remains compatible with foraging.
8. Grinding proves processing, not cultivation.

### 1. Bagor: long sequence, strong evidence, disputed claim

Bagor lies near the Kothari river in eastern Rajasthan. It preserves:

- a long sequence with a-ceramic and ceramic phases;
- abundant microliths, including geometric forms;
- stone-paved floors and possible shelter outlines;
- butchery areas;
- querns and rubbing stones;
- wild fauna and reported domestic sheep/goat and cattle;
- increasing plant processing in later phases;
- continuing microliths into later occupations.

The domestic-animal identifications and their phase association have been debated. The correct phrasing is **reported but questioned**, not “Bagor conclusively proves the earliest pastoralism.”

### 2. Langhnaj and Adamgarh

**Langhnaj** in north Gujarat yielded microliths, human burials, wild fauna and some pottery. Faunal evidence suggests a mixed savannah, forest and wetland setting rather than a simple arid stereotype.

**Adamgarh** near the Narmada preserves Palaeolithic, Mesolithic and later evidence. Domestic cattle, sheep, goat, pig and dog have been reported, but two very different radiocarbon ranges, stratigraphic uncertainty and identification problems weaken the early-domestication claim.

Pottery at a forager site does not automatically transform its economy into farming. Objects can move between neighbouring communities.

### 3. The middle-Ganga burial cluster

Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha and Damdama lie in wetland-rich settings and preserve human burials within or near habitation areas.

**Sarai Nahar Rai**

- geometric microliths;
- shells and animal bones, including aquatic resources;
- multiple burials;
- one skeleton with a projectile embedded in the ribs;
- grave goods including tools, shells and bones.

**Mahadaha**

- habitation and butchery areas;
- non-local chert, quartz, chalcedony, crystal and agate;
- numerous burials, including paired burials;
- wild animals, turtles and wetland resources;
- seasonality evidence.

**Damdama**

- thick occupation deposit;
- hearths and floor traces;
- microliths, bone objects, querns and mullers;
- charred wild grain;
- many burials, including multiple burials.

Together they challenge the image of ceaseless short visits.

### 4. Seasonality and residence

Animal teeth can preserve the age and season of death. Studies of swamp deer and hog deer at Mahadaha and Damdama suggest occupation in different seasons. The presence of a commensal bandicoot rat has also been used to support sustained food availability near habitation.

These lines make prolonged or year-round occupation plausible at particular sites. They do not prove every Mesolithic community was sedentary.

### 5. What burial proves—and what it does not

A deliberate burial directly establishes treatment of the dead. Orientation, grave form, body position and associated objects can reveal repeated mortuary choices.

Burials may support hypotheses about:

- memory and group identity;
- attachment to place;
- ritual;
- personal equipment;
- social distinction;
- violence or trauma.

They do not automatically prove:

- a doctrine of afterlife;
- ancestor worship;
- hereditary classes;
- territorial ownership;
- one religious system.

An embedded projectile indicates injury and possibly interpersonal violence. It does not by itself reconstruct warfare.

### 6. Territory and the strongest interpretation

One argument links cemeteries and recurring burial orientation with corporate claims over rich wetland resources. This is analytically powerful because burials materially attach the dead to a place.

Its limit is equally important: territoriality is inferred through analogy and pattern; it is not a direct inscription of ownership.

### Example and its limit

Mahadaha's non-local lithic materials show movement or transfer across distance. They do not establish commercial trade, specialised merchants or political control.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Formal cemeteries prove that Mesolithic groups had permanent villages and social classes.

**Reply:** Cemeteries demonstrate repeated place attachment and organised mortuary action. Permanent residence and hereditary hierarchy require independent evidence from seasonality, structures, settlement duration and patterned inequality.

**Balanced assessment:** Burial greatly expands social history, but its meanings remain multiple.

- **Trap:** “Domestic animal reported” is not “domestication securely established.”
- **Transition:** The final lesson reads images and explains why the Mesolithic-to-Neolithic boundary is overlap rather than a sudden revolution.

### 1. Bhimbetka as a long archive

Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh contains hundreds of rock shelters with a long sequence of tools, occupation and art. The paintings include animals, hunting, gathering, dancing and other scenes. They span prehistoric and later periods.

This means:

- Bhimbetka is not exclusively Mesolithic;
- not every painting is contemporary with every excavated tool;
- long continuity of place does not mean continuity of one community;
- superimposition can order some images relatively.

### 2. Dating rock art

Relative approaches use:

- superimposition;
- style and technique;
- weathering;
- subject matter;
- association with archaeological deposits;
- comparison across sites.

Scientific possibilities include radiocarbon dating of suitable organic pigment or accretions, luminescence and uranium-series approaches to associated mineral deposits. These often date material over or under an image rather than the painting event directly.

The safest question is: **What exactly was dated?**

### 3. Reading scenes without over-reading

A hunting scene establishes a representation of hunting. It may relate to memory, instruction, identity, ritual or narrative. It is not a census of diet. A masked dancer may indicate performance or special role; “priest” or “shaman” requires caution. A depicted animal helps reconstruct known fauna or cultural attention, but image frequency does not equal consumption frequency.

Ethnographic analogy can suggest how images may relate to ritual or community identity. It cannot supply a direct translation.

### 4. Transition is overlap

The Mesolithic-to-Neolithic transition is not a switch from wandering hunters to settled farmers. Regional evidence may combine:

- continued hunting, fishing and gathering;
- intensified plant processing;
- storage;
- pottery acquired or made by foragers;
- management of animals or plants;
- disputed early domestication;
- interaction with cultivators;
- continued microliths;
- gradually more persistent settlement.

Topic 04 owns the foraging side and the evidence discipline of this boundary. Topic 05 owns securely established cultivation, domestication, polished-tool and village sequences.

### 5. Five boundary tests

| Claim | Minimum evidence needed | What is insufficient alone |
|---|---|---|
| Plant processing | Use-wear, residues, grinding or plant remains | Cultivation conclusion |
| Cultivation | Plant evidence plus field/weed/contextual indicators | One wild grain |
| Domestication | Morphology, management profile, DNA or convergent evidence | One species name |
| Sedentism | Multi-season occupation and structural/depositional bundle | One hut or burial |
| Farming village | Sustained food production plus settlement evidence | Pottery or polished stone alone |

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10-mark question 1

**Question:** Explain why lithic technology should be studied as an operational chain rather than as a list of tool types. Answer in 150 words.

### Model answer

Lithic technology records a sequence of human decisions, not merely finished forms. The operational chain begins with raw-material selection and procurement, proceeds through platform preparation, core reduction and blank production, and continues through retouch, hafting, use, repair and discard.

This approach improves reconstruction in three ways. First, cores, rough-outs, hammerstones and debitage reveal manufacturing stages, as at Isampur. Second, microwear and residues distinguish likely uses from visual guesswork. Third, spatial distribution links quarry, workshop, task site and camp within a mobility system, as in the Hunsgi-Baichbal landscape.

A handaxe alone may travel far from its production place, whereas refitting flakes in primary context can identify a specific activity. The chain also reveals planning and transmitted skill without assigning technology to one species. Thus, typology classifies objects, while operational analysis reconstructs behaviour; both require stratigraphy and contextual limits.

## 10-mark question 2

**Question:** What does the Hathnora evidence contribute to the study of human evolution in India? Answer in 150 words.

### Model answer

The Hathnora cranial fragment, discovered by Arun Sonakia in 1982 in the Narmada valley, is important because authenticated hominin fossils are rare in South Asia despite abundant stone tools. Found in gravel with vertebrate fossils and nearby late Acheulian material, it places archaic hominin evidence within a major prehistoric landscape.

Its importance is accompanied by uncertainty. Researchers have variously classified it as advanced *Homo erectus*, archaic *Homo sapiens* or an intermediate form. Its exact age and depositional relation to nearby artefacts remain debated. Later clavicle and long-bone finds broaden the Narmada fossil record but do not settle one taxonomic sequence.

Therefore, Hathnora demonstrates that biological and technological records must be connected cautiously. It establishes rare archaic hominin presence and makes the Narmada central to population-history research, but it cannot prove that one species made all Acheulian tools in India.

## 15-mark question 1

**Question:** Examine the significance of Attirampakkam and Isampur for revising our understanding of the Indian Lower Palaeolithic. Answer in 250 words.

### Model answer

Attirampakkam and Isampur move the Indian Lower Palaeolithic beyond typological description by providing different kinds of contextual strength.

**Attirampakkam and chronology:** The Kortallaiyar-basin site preserves a deep stratified sequence. Burial dating and palaeomagnetic evidence place early Acheulian levels at not less than about 1.07 million years, with a pooled estimate of about `1.51 ± 0.07 million years`. Luminescence dates locate a local Acheulian-to-Middle Palaeolithic transition around `385 ± 64 ka`. Declining bifaces, prepared cores, flakes and points reveal gradual technological reorganisation. This challenges late, uniform models of South Asian transition.

**Isampur and activity:** Located near limestone sources in the Hunsgi valley, Isampur preserves slabs, cores, large flakes, rough-outs, unfinished tools, hammerstones and extensive debitage. It therefore reveals quarrying and successive stages of manufacture. Finished and used tools, scrapers and occupation evidence show that production coexisted with habitation and subsistence. ESR dates on associated bovid enamel provide an early chronological anchor in the cited account.

Together, the sites demonstrate that chronology must be attached to stratified contexts and behaviour reconstructed from whole assemblages. Attirampakkam answers when local technological change occurred; Isampur answers how procurement, production and use were organised.

Their limits are equally instructive: neither site dates all India, and neither identifies the hominin makers. They revise a regional mosaic rather than create a new universal timetable.

## 15-mark question 2

**Question:** “Mesolithic change was a reorganisation of technology, resources and residence, not merely a reduction in tool size.” Discuss. Answer in 250 words.

### Model answer

The defining Mesolithic shift cannot be reduced to tiny stones. Microliths were deliberately shaped bladelets and flakes, often backed and hafted into composite arrows, barbs or cutting implements. Their modular design improved portability, repair and specialised use.

Technological reorganisation accompanied wider exploitation of ecological niches. Middle-Ganga sites preserve fish, turtles, wild animals and plant-processing evidence; coastal and shelter sites reveal other combinations. This broad-spectrum strategy did not equal agriculture. Grinding, storage or wild-grain use can intensify within foraging.

Residence also became more varied. Thick deposits, hearths, possible huts, heavy grinding tools, seasonality studies, commensal species and cemeteries at sites such as Mahadaha and Damdama support repeated or prolonged occupation. Mobility nevertheless continued, as shown by non-local stone and regional procurement networks.

Regional comparison prevents a single model. Bagor preserves a long microlithic sequence and disputed domesticates; Langhnaj combines burials with mostly wild fauna; Adamgarh's early domestication claim remains stratigraphically uncertain. Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha and Damdama show wetland adaptation and mortuary practice.

Finally, microliths pre-date the conventional Holocene Mesolithic at some sites and continue into ceramic or food-producing phases. Mesolithic change was therefore an uneven recombination of composite technology, resource breadth and place attachment, not a synchronous post-glacial stage or automatic prelude to farming.

## 20-mark question 1

**Question:** Analyse the regional development of Palaeolithic cultures in the Indian subcontinent. How do scientific dating and contextual archaeology modify older linear narratives? Answer in 250-300 words.

### Model answer

Indian Palaeolithic history is best understood as a set of regional sequences rather than a uniform Lower-Middle-Upper ladder. The broad phases remain useful, but their dates, assemblage balance and settlement contexts vary.

**Lower Palaeolithic:** Acheulian bifaces, cleavers, cores and flakes occur widely in peninsular, central and river-margin settings. Soan-Siwalik pebble and flake assemblages require special fluvial caution because terraces can redeposit material. In the Hunsgi-Baichbal valleys, varied localities suggest quarries, workshops, task sites and longer-use camps. Isampur preserves raw slabs, rough-outs, hammerstones and waste, allowing reconstruction of manufacturing activity.

**Chronological revision:** Attirampakkam's stratified Acheulian levels and scientific dates push southern evidence much earlier than older textbook bands. Its transition around `385 ± 64 ka` shows declining bifaces and increasing prepared-core flakes and points. The date is powerful precisely because it is local and contextual; it cannot be projected across India.

**Middle Palaeolithic:** Attirampakkam, Nevasa, Didwana and Son-Belan evidence shows growing emphasis on flakes, scrapers, points and prepared-core methods such as Levallois. Older forms continued, undermining abrupt replacement models.

**Upper Palaeolithic:** Blade-burin technologies, Kurnool bone tools, and ostrich-eggshell ornaments at Patne and Bhimbetka enlarge the visible technological and symbolic repertoire. Preservation remains uneven, especially for organic equipment.

Contextual archaeology also separates behaviour from biology. The Hathnora fossil provides rare archaic hominin evidence but does not identify all tool-makers. Scientific dates belong first to sampled events, and river deposits, site formation and error ranges must be controlled.

Thus, modern research replaces a rigid evolutionary ladder with overlapping regional adaptations. Technology records learned choices; chronology is site-specific; and environmental change is mediated by mobility, knowledge and social organisation.

## 20-mark question 2

**Question:** Evaluate the archaeological evidence for Mesolithic social and economic life in India, with special reference to regional sites, burials and rock art. Answer in 250-300 words.

### Model answer

Mesolithic archaeology reveals diversified foraging, composite technology, variable residence and increasingly visible social expression, but the evidence differs sharply by region.

**Technology and economy:** Backed blades, triangles, trapezes and lunates were often hafted into repairable composite tools. Broad-spectrum subsistence combined plants, game, fish, turtles and other aquatic resources. Grinding indicates processing, not necessarily cultivation, and microliths continued into ceramic and food-producing contexts.

**Western and central regions:** Bagor preserves a long sequence, stone floors, butchery areas, plant processing and reported domestic animals; the domestication identifications remain questioned. Langhnaj combines microliths, burials and mostly wild fauna within a semi-arid and wetland mosaic. Adamgarh's early domestication claim is weakened by uncertain dates and stratigraphy. Bhimbetka preserves recurrent occupation and multi-period rock art.

**Middle Ganga:** Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha and Damdama contain habitation evidence, wetland fauna and formal burials. Mahadaha's non-local stone suggests procurement or exchange, while seasonality studies at Mahadaha and Damdama support occupation across different seasons. Thick deposits, hearths and burials indicate strong place attachment, but not automatically permanent villages.

**Mortuary evidence:** Deliberate burial, orientation and grave goods establish organised treatment of the dead. They may indicate memory, identity or distinction. One embedded projectile supports interpersonal injury, not a complete theory of warfare. Afterlife, hierarchy or territorial ownership remain interpretive hypotheses.

**Rock art:** Bhimbetka and other shelters depict animals, hunting, gathering and performance. Superimposition provides relative order, while scientific dating often remains indirect. Images reveal representation and communication but cannot be read as transparent records of diet, gender or religion.

Overall, Mesolithic India was not one transitional culture. It comprised regional combinations of mobility, residence, resource intensification, interaction and symbolic practice, with foraging continuing through the uneven boundary toward food production.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Register 1 - Chronology and periodisation

- Stone Age phases are analytical categories, not races or universal dates.
- Lower Palaeolithic: large core tools and Acheulian bifaces prominent.
- Middle Palaeolithic: flakes, points, scrapers and prepared-core strategies become more prominent.
- Upper Palaeolithic: blades, burins, bone tools and more visible ornament.
- Mesolithic: microlithic composite technology, broad-spectrum foraging, varied residence, burials and art.
- R.S. Sharma's broad bands are 600,000-150,000 BCE; 150,000-35,000 BCE; 35,000-10,000 BCE; and 9000-4000 BCE.
- These dates are textbook orientation, not a final all-India chronology.
- Attirampakkam's local transition is about `385 ± 64 ka`.
- Microliths can occur before the Holocene and continue into later food-producing phases.
- Formula: `macro-phase -> regional sequence -> dated stratum -> assemblage`.

## Register 2 - Environment and dating

- Pleistocene began about 2.6 million years ago; Holocene about 12,000 years ago.
- Geological epochs and archaeological phases classify different things.
- Proxy examples: sediment, terrace, pollen, phytolith, fauna, isotope and lake core.
- A proxy has a catchment and scale.
- Date the environmental event and occupation independently.
- River gravels can preserve, move and mix artefacts.
- Primary context is strongest for activity reconstruction.
- Toba ash marks an event; its South Asian human impact remains debated.
- Climate changes opportunities and costs.
- Technology, knowledge, mobility and organisation mediate response.

## Register 3 - Lithic operational chain

- Raw material -> core -> blank -> retouch/backing -> haft/use -> maintenance -> discard.
- Core: parent stone mass.
- Flake: detached piece.
- Blade: elongated flake, generally more than twice as long as wide.
- Retouch: secondary edge modification.
- Debitage: production waste.
- Rough-out: incomplete shaped tool.
- Hafting: attaching stone to wood or bone.
- Microwear and residue test function.
- Refitting links separated pieces to one reduction event.
- Production debris may be more informative than an isolated finished tool.

## Register 4 - Lower Palaeolithic

- Acheulian is a technological assemblage tradition.
- Diagnostic large cutting tools: handaxes and cleavers.
- Assemblages also include choppers, flakes, cores, scrapers, hammerstones and waste.
- Soanian evidence is associated with north-western terrace and gravel contexts.
- Soanian and older Madrasian labels do not identify races.
- Site distribution often links water, raw stone and food.
- Quarries, workshops, task sites and camps may form one settlement system.
- Bhimbetka preserves multi-period occupation, not one Lower Palaeolithic moment.
- Similar tools do not independently prove one migration.

## Register 5 - Attirampakkam, Hunsgi-Baichbal and Isampur

- Attirampakkam: Kortallaiyar basin, Tamil Nadu.
- Early Acheulian levels: not less than about 1.07 million years; pooled estimate about `1.51 ± 0.07 million years`.
- Local Acheulian-Middle transition: about `385 ± 64 ka`.
- Middle Palaeolithic continued there to about `172 ± 41 ka` in the cited account.
- Sequence: declining bifaces, more small tools, Levallois flakes/points and blade developments.
- No human fossil identifies the makers.
- Hunsgi-Baichbal: varied site types in a connected valley landscape.
- Isampur: limestone quarry, production and habitation.
- Isampur evidence: slabs, cores, flakes, rough-outs, hammerstones, debitage and used tools.
- ESR dates on associated bovid enamel are about 1.2 million years in Upinder Singh's account.

## Register 6 - Hominins and Denisovans

- Hominid is broader; hominin is the human-line group.
- South Asian hominin fossils are sparse compared with lithics.
- Hathnora cranial fragment discovered by Arun Sonakia in 1982.
- Found in Narmada gravel with fauna and nearby late Acheulian material.
- Proposed labels include advanced *Homo erectus*, archaic *Homo sapiens* and intermediate form.
- Exact age and taxonomy remain debated.
- Association does not equal tool authorship.
- Denisovans were an archaic human population identified through fossil/genetic evidence.
- Denisova Cave is in Siberia.
- No securely identified Denisovan fossil in India follows from the concept.
- 2019 Prelims Q92 official local key is unavailable; B is a high-confidence inferred answer.

## Register 7 - Middle Palaeolithic

- Emphasis shifts toward flakes, points, scrapers and borers.
- Older bifaces can persist.
- Levallois prepares core convexities and platform before target removal.
- Levallois is a method, not a finished tool.
- Discoid reduction is a different prepared-core approach.
- Attirampakkam supplies early regional transition evidence.
- Nevasa/Chirki-Nevasa supports the regional “Nevasan” label.
- Didwana, Son and Belan preserve varied regional sequences.
- Fine-grained stone allows controlled flaking but does not alone explain change.
- Technology does not prove population replacement.

## Register 8 - Upper Palaeolithic

- Parallel-sided blades expand cutting-edge production.
- Burins create chisel-like working edges.
- Older flake and heavy tools continue.
- Kurnool caves preserve bone tools and rich fauna.
- Cave preservation reveals an otherwise under-visible organic toolkit.
- Patne and Bhimbetka preserve ostrich-eggshell ornaments.
- Engraving, perforation and bodily placement support symbolic communication.
- Lohanda Nala object has competing figurine/harpoon interpretations.
- Baghor I has a special platform-and-stone context, but analogy cannot prove exact belief.
- Broad symbolic inference is safer than named ritual or religion.

## Register 9 - Lifeways

- Diet included plant foods, game, fish, turtles, shellfish and small animals.
- Plant foods are under-represented by preservation.
- Mobility was scheduled around seasonal resources and raw material.
- Site system: quarry -> task site -> camp -> shelter -> aquatic locality.
- Palimpsest: overlapping remains from repeated activity.
- Fire requires anthropogenic and secure-context evidence.
- Rock shelters are preservation-biased samples.
- Residence duration needs deposits, structures, hearths, seasonality and other indicators.
- Standardised production supports learning and teaching.
- Archaeology can infer cooperation more safely than chiefs, caste or fixed gender roles.

## Register 10 - Mesolithic technology and economy

- Microliths are deliberately shaped small elements, often under about 5 cm.
- Non-geometric: backed blades, points, scrapers, borers.
- Geometric: triangles, trapezes, lunates.
- Backing blunts an edge for hafting.
- Composite tools are portable, replaceable and repairable.
- Mehtakheri reports early microliths around 48,000 years ago.
- Jwalapuram Locality 9 has microlithic evidence around 35,000 BP.
- Microliths continue into later ceramic and food-producing contexts.
- Broad-spectrum subsistence uses varied plant, animal and aquatic resources.
- Semi-sedentism requires an evidence bundle, not one hut or burial.

## Register 11 - Regional Mesolithic map

- Bagor: Rajasthan, Kothari river, long microlithic sequence, floors, butchery and disputed domesticates.
- Langhnaj: north Gujarat, microliths, burials, mainly wild fauna and some pottery.
- Adamgarh: Narmada region, long sequence and questioned early domestication.
- Bhimbetka: repeated shelter use and multi-period art.
- Sarai Nahar Rai: oxbow-lake setting, geometric microliths, burials and projectile injury.
- Mahadaha: habitation/butchery zones, non-local stone, burials and aquatic resources.
- Damdama: thick deposit, hearths, grinding, wild grain and many burials.
- Chopani Mando: sequence with huts, grinding and wild-rice evidence.
- Baghor II: microlith manufacture with very high waste proportion.
- Pottery at a forager site does not automatically establish farming.

## Register 12 - Burial, rock art and transition

- Burial directly establishes deliberate treatment of the dead.
- Repeated form and orientation establish mortuary pattern.
- Grave goods can represent personal equipment, ritual or identity.
- Afterlife, hierarchy and territory require additional evidence.
- Bhimbetka paintings span multiple periods.
- Superimposition gives relative order.
- Scientific dating may target pigment, accretion or patina.
- A hunting image is representation, not a dietary census.
- Ethnographic analogy suggests hypotheses but cannot translate the image.
- Grinding proves processing, not cultivation.
- Domestication requires morphology, management profiles or convergent evidence.
- Transition involved overlap among foraging, management, herding, cultivation and exchange.
- Topic 05 owns established Neolithic and Chalcolithic systems.
