# Central Asian Contacts: Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Pahlavas and Kushanas - Learning Session Live Edition

**Subject:** Ancient Indian History | **Topic:** 16 | **Level:** Foundation to advanced | **Evidence date:** 25 September 2026

## Learner-first contract

This session studies the centuries from roughly 200 BCE to 300 CE as a connected frontier age. It does not treat every incoming group as one dynasty, every coin find as a border, or every cultural resemblance as proof of ethnic origin. Each lesson begins with the problem a historian must solve, then introduces the evidence, terminology, limits and exam use.

The governing insight is:

> **Movement created political conflict, but sustained contact produced new languages of rule, exchange, worship and representation.**

### Begin in plain language

Think of the north-west as a set of connected gateways, not a permanently open door and not an impenetrable wall. Passes through the Hindu Kush channelled movement; river valleys and cities then redirected it. Whoever held Bactria, Kabul, Gandhara, Taxila or Mathura gained access to routes, but holding one node did not guarantee control over every village beyond it.

The broad period begins around the second century BCE, after Mauryan power fragmented. Indo-Greeks emerged from the Graeco-Bactrian world; Shaka groups moved through Central Asia into several Indian regions; Pahlavas created Indo-Parthian formations; and the Guishuang branch of the Yuezhi built Kushana power. Their histories intersected with Shungas, Kanvas, Satavahanas and Tamil polities.

### Definitions and distinctions

| Term | Meaning here | Essential limit |
|---|---|---|
| Indo-Greek | Greek-speaking dynasties ruling south of the Hindu Kush | Several houses and simultaneous kings existed |
| Shaka | Scythian or Iranian-speaking mobile groups and ruling houses | The name covers distinct regional branches |
| Pahlava | Parthian-related rulers in Afghanistan and the north-west | Not another name for every Shaka |
| Yuezhi | A wider confederation described in Chinese sources | Not identical with Kushana at every stage |
| Kushana | The Guishuang branch that achieved wider consolidation | Its territorial depth changed by ruler and region |

Routes are **corridors**, meaning channels that make movement easier, not lines of guaranteed sovereignty. Bactria linked Iranian, steppe and Central Asian worlds. Gandhara and Taxila connected those worlds with the Punjab. Mathura joined western and north-western routes to the Ganga-Yamuna system. Lower Indus and Gujarat ports connected overland circulation to the Indian Ocean.

### Example with a limit

A Kushana coin found east of the strongest imperial core proves that the coin travelled. It may indicate trade, tribute, payment, imitation or political influence. Without an inscription, mint context or administrative evidence, it cannot alone prove annexation.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A broad sequence from Indo-Greeks to Shakas to Pahlavas to Kushanas is useful, so why reject it?

**Reply:** Keep it as a memory ladder, not as a universal political calendar. Replacement occurred at different times in Gandhara, Mathura and western India. The sequence is strongest when paired with regional overlap.

### UPSC application

**Prelims:** eliminate options that merge all groups or convert a route into a border. **Mains:** open with a map-like sentence naming Bactria, Gandhara, Taxila, Mathura and western ports; then state that contact included conquest, migration, trade and settlement.

### Mini recap

1. The study window is roughly 200 BCE-300 CE.
2. The Hindu Kush filtered movement; it did not isolate the subcontinent.
3. Bactria was a major formation zone north of the Hindu Kush.
4. Taxila and Mathura were strategic nodes, not proof of uniform rule.
5. Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Pahlavas and Kushanas were distinct.
6. Regional chronologies overlapped.
7. Coin circulation and political sovereignty are different map layers.
8. “Foreign” describes origin better than permanent cultural separation.

## Lesson 2 - A source matrix before a ruler list

### Visual first

| Historical question | Best starting source | What must still be checked |
|---|---|---|
| Who issued a coin? | Name, portrait and legend | Attribution, imitation and overstrike |
| Who made a dated claim? | Inscription | Era, damaged reading and praise formula |
| What occupied a site? | Stratified archaeology | Date and political attribution |
| How was a ruler remembered? | Literary tradition | Genre, redaction and distance from event |
| How did migration unfold? | Chinese/classical accounts | Translation, ethnonyms and corroboration |

*What the visual shows:* the strongest source changes with the question being asked.

### Begin in plain language

This period has no continuous royal archive. Historians therefore assemble a mosaic. Coins may name rulers whom literature ignores. Inscriptions may date a donation but exaggerate conquest. Archaeology may reveal an urban phase without naming its king. A Buddhist dialogue may preserve intellectual memory while reshaping biography.

### Five evidence rules

1. **Coins:** read metal, weight, portrait, legend, script, deity, monogram, overstrike and find context together.
2. **Inscriptions:** distinguish the datable statement from royal praise. The Junagadh record is both evidence of reservoir repair and a *prashasti*, or eulogy.
3. **Archaeology:** distinguish a settlement's foundation from its best-preserved layer. Much visible Sirkap evidence belongs to Shaka-Parthian phases even though the city's foundation is associated with Indo-Greek rule.
4. **Literature:** identify genre before extracting events. The *Milindapanha* is a philosophical dialogue, not minutes of a royal interview.
5. **External accounts:** Chinese histories help reconstruct Yuezhi movement, but translated names and court-centred geography require correlation with coins and sites.

The key distinction is **attestation versus extension**. An inscription attests what it says at a particular place and time. The historian extends that claim only when other evidence supports a wider conclusion.

### Worked example: an overstrike

Gautamiputra Satakarni's restriking of Nahapana's silver coins is stronger evidence of political reversal than a later victory story alone. Yet an overstrike does not narrate every battle. Metal could be reused, and the exact geographical sequence still needs hoards, inscriptions and chronology.

### Confidence ladder

```text
Named contemporary object
        +
secure find or inscription context
        +
independent source convergence
        v
HIGHER-CONFIDENCE CLAIM

later story + isolated portable find + assumed border
        v
LOWER-CONFIDENCE CLAIM
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Source criticism makes every conclusion sound uncertain.

**Reply:** It separates degrees of confidence. Menander's existence and coinage are secure; every episode in his later literary biography is not. Good history is not indecision but calibrated certainty.

### UPSC application

In a Mains paragraph use the sequence **claim -> named evidence -> inference -> limit**. In Prelims, watch for a correct object paired with an excessive conclusion, such as “coin find equals direct rule.”

### Mini recap

1. Coins are strongest for ruler attribution and monetary communication.
2. A coin distribution map is not automatically a political map.
3. Inscriptions can be contemporary and still self-promotional.
4. Archaeological layers require dating before dynastic attribution.
5. Literary genre controls historical use.
6. Chinese ethnonyms require contextual translation.
7. Overstrikes can indicate succession, conflict or metal reuse.
8. Corroboration raises confidence without removing all uncertainty.

```text

      `-- movements south of Hindu Kush
              |
       several Indo-Greek houses

```

*What the visual shows:* a common Hellenistic-Bactrian background produced several principalities, not one stable Indo-Greek empire.

### Begin in plain language

“The Indo-Greeks” is a convenient collective name, but it can hide political competition. Bactrian Greek rulers operated north of the Hindu Kush; rulers described as Indo-Greek held territories south of it. Demetrius is associated with an early second-century BCE crossing, but the route and maximum extent of his campaigns remain debated. Eucratides and other lines show rival royal houses. Menander became the best remembered ruler, not proof that all Indo-Greek territory was unified under him.

### Political plurality

| Ruler or site | What it helps establish | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Demetrius | Early movement south of the Hindu Kush; elephant-scalp portrait type | A securely mapped march to Pataliputra |
| Eucratides | Rival dynastic power and conflict in the Bactrian world | One agreed succession for every region |
| Menander | Extensive coin corpus, Sagala association and later Buddhist memory | A verbatim religious biography |
| Antialcidas | Coin-attested king linked to Heliodorus's embassy | The full boundaries of his kingdom |
| Sirkap | An urban foundation associated with Indo-Greek Taxila | An entirely Indo-Greek visible cityscape |

The technical term **numismatic sequence** means reconstructing relative order through portraits, legends, monograms, shared types and overstrikes. It is especially important because written sources name far fewer rulers than coins do.

### Why political fragmentation could coexist with innovation

Competing rulers needed recognisable authority. Portraits identified the king. Greek and Prakrit legends addressed different literate environments. Indian weight standards and square coin forms adapted royal money to regional habits. Shared conventions could spread across rivals because a useful monetary language does not require political unity.

### Example with a limit

The wide distribution of Menander's coins suggests significant circulation and authority. It cannot by itself establish that Menander directly taxed every place where a coin was later deposited.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the political map is uncertain, calling these rulers “Indo-Greek” imposes a false unity.

**Reply:** The label remains useful for related dynasties operating in the subcontinental north-west with recognisable Greek political and numismatic traditions. It becomes misleading only when converted into one genealogy or one territory.

### UPSC application

**Prelims:** connect Demetrius, Menander and Antialcidas to the correct source. **Mains:** organise the answer around political plurality, coin-led reconstruction and adaptation to regional audiences.

### Mini recap

1. Graeco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek are related but geographically distinct labels.
2. Demetrius is an early crossing marker, not a certain all-India conqueror.
3. Several Indo-Greek rulers were contemporaries or rivals.
4. Many are known primarily from coins.
5. Menander is linked with Sagala and a large coin corpus.
6. Antialcidas is linked with the Heliodorus embassy.
7. Sirkap's foundation and surviving layers must be separated.
8. Shared coin forms do not prove political unity.

## Lesson 4 - Coins, Menander and Heliodorus: three windows on contact

Progress: 4 / 15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Numismatic communication, Buddhist memory and diplomacy

### Visual first

| Evidence | Person or practice | Historical use | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bilingual portrait coin | Indo-Greek king | Authority, language, audience, circulation | Not a census or border |
| *Milindapanha* | Milinda and Nagasena | Buddhist reasoning and ruler memory | Not a court transcript |
| Besnagar pillar | Heliodorus, Antialcidas, Bhagabhadra | Diplomacy and Bhagavata affiliation | Not mass conversion |

*What the visual shows:* political contact appears differently in money, literature and inscription.

```text
COIN -> public authority
TEXT -> remembered debate
PILLAR -> named diplomacy and devotion
```

### Begin in plain language

An Indo-Greek coin can speak to two audiences without proving that every user spoke both languages. The *Milindapanha* can preserve a king's memory without recording his words verbatim. The Heliodorus pillar can establish one ambassador's devotion without describing every Indo-Greek's religion. Historical value grows when we respect these differences.

### Reading the coins

Indo-Greek issues commonly combine royal portraits, Greek legends and Prakrit rendered in Kharoshthi; some regional issues use Brahmi. Portraits and names permit attribution. Reverse deities and symbols communicate legitimacy. Agathocles' series showing Balarama and Vasudeva is important early evidence of Indian cult imagery within an Indo-Greek royal medium. It does not prove a private conversion.

The familiar claim that Indo-Greeks issued India's “first gold coins” requires qualification. R.S. Sharma uses that formulation, but securely extensive subcontinental gold coinage is especially associated with Kushana rulers. Metal, ruler and chronology must be specified.

### Menander and the dialogue

Menander, remembered as Milinda, belongs broadly to the mid-second century BCE and is associated with Sagala. The *Milindapanha* stages questions between Milinda and the monk Nagasena. Its analogies illuminate Buddhist analysis of identity, causation and rebirth. Its layered composition prevents its use as a stenographic report or complete political biography.

### Heliodorus at Besnagar

Heliodorus, son of Dion and ambassador of Antialcidas, came from Taxila to the court of Bhagabhadra. In a Prakrit inscription written in Brahmi, he calls himself a *Bhagavata* and dedicates a Garuda pillar to Vasudeva. The evidence joins diplomacy, mobility and religious agency.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** These examples show only elite behaviour and cannot prove broad cultural exchange.

**Reply:** They do privilege elite and literate actors. Yet coins circulated beyond courts, diplomacy crossed regions and religious communities preserved the encounter. The appropriate conclusion is significant contact with uneven social visibility, not universal assimilation.

### UPSC application

Use a three-source paragraph: coin for political communication, *Milindapanha* for remembered intellectual exchange, and Heliodorus for an individual's diplomatic-religious agency. Always state each limit.

### Mini recap

1. Indo-Greek coins often combine Greek and Prakrit/Kharoshthi.
2. Portrait, legend and reverse motif should be read together.
3. Agathocles' imagery does not automatically reveal private belief.
4. Menander is securely coin-attested.
5. Milinda debates Nagasena in a Buddhist literary work.
6. Heliodorus was an ambassador, not an Indo-Greek king.
7. The Besnagar pillar honours Vasudeva through a Garuda standard.
8. Individual affiliation cannot establish collective conversion.

## Lesson 5 - Shaka movements and regional branches

Progress: 5 / 15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Maues, Azes, Rajuvula and western formations

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** queried in the specified R.S. Sharma and Upinder Singh discussions of Shaka branches.
- **CA search:** “site:asi.nic.in Shaka Western Kshatrapa Mathura Taxila 2026”
- **CA found:** none substantively direct in the six-month window.

### Visual first

```text
Central Asian displacement and political pressure
                    |
                    v
        SHAKA MOVEMENTS INTO DISTINCT REGIONS
          /              |                 \
Gandhara-Taxila       Mathura          Western India
Maues, Azes lines     Rajuvula         Kshaharatas
                                       Kardamakas
          \              |                 /
     overlap with Indo-Greek, Pahlava, Kushana and Satavahana power
```

*What the visual shows:* “Shaka” names several connected movements and houses rather than a single centralised empire.

### Begin in plain language

Pressure in the steppe and Central Asian world helped redirect Shaka groups southward. Once in the subcontinent, they did not remain one travelling body. Ruling houses developed in the north-west, at Mathura and in western India. Their coins and titles reused existing political languages while establishing new regional authority.

### Regional branches

| Region | Rulers or line | Best evidence | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gandhara-Taxila | Maues/Moga; Azes lines | Coins and inscriptions | Exact sequence and era links remain debated |
| Mathura | Rajuvula; Shodasa | Lion capital, coins and title progression | Regional authority, not all-India rule |
| Western India | Kshaharatas: Bhumaka, Nahapana | Coins and donative inscriptions | Must be separated from Kardamakas |
| Western India | Kardamakas: Chashtana, Rudradaman | Coins and Junagadh inscription | Long-lived regional formation |

Maues is an early Shaka ruler in the north-west. Moga in inscriptional evidence is identified with Maues. Azes belongs to a later north-western coin sequence, but the older link between an Azes era and Vikrama Samvat has been questioned.

Rajuvula was a Shaka kshatrapa and later mahakshatrapa connected with Mathura. Upinder Singh associates him with the defeat of Indo-Greek Strato III and the capture of Sagala in the early first century CE. This illustrates regional replacement, not a single date ending all Indo-Greek rule.

### Adaptation without disappearance

Shaka rulers used Greek-derived coin forms, Kharoshthi or Brahmi scripts, Indian religious symbols and Iranian-derived titles. Adaptation does not mean that origin vanished; it means rulers selected forms intelligible to their subjects and rivals.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Dividing Shakas into branches makes the subject an unmemorable list.

**Reply:** Use a geographical structure: north-west, Mathura, west. Each branch then carries one ruler, one source and one historical problem. Geography simplifies without falsely unifying.

### UPSC application

**Prelims:** separate Maues, Rajuvula, Nahapana and Rudradaman. **Mains:** explain how mobility produced regional state formation through adaptation, titles and route control.

### Mini recap

1. Shaka movements followed wider Central Asian displacements.
2. Shaka rule developed in several regions.
3. Maues/Moga belongs to the north-western phase.
4. Azes-era associations are debated.
5. Rajuvula belongs to the Mathura branch.
6. Rajuvula's victory over Strato III was regional.
7. Kshaharata and Kardamaka are distinct western lines.
8. Adapted coinage and titles show political communication.

3. settlement and control in Bactria;

5. Kushana consolidation;

Brahmanical texts attempted to classify Shakas and Pahlavas within normative social schemes, sometimes as degraded or “fallen” kshatriyas. Such classification shows an effort to domesticate political newcomers conceptually. It is not census evidence for how every community lived.

### Military and agricultural claims

R.S. Sharma links Central Asian contacts with cavalry improvements, bridles, saddles, armour, clothing and some crops. These claims should be separated by evidentiary strength. Mounted warfare and frontier costume are visible in coins, sculpture and political practice. The **stirrup** claim requires particular caution: early toe-loops or mounting aids are not automatically the fully developed paired stirrup system familiar from later cavalry.

### Example with a limit

The headless Kanishka statue from Mathura shows a ruler in tunic, boots and heavy dress. It is excellent evidence for represented kingship and costume. It does not prove that every Kushana subject dressed similarly.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Elite inscriptions and royal images cannot tell us about social integration.

**Reply:** They do not represent all social levels, especially peasants, women and labourers. They still reveal how power negotiated language, religion, office and public patronage. The conclusion must be explicitly elite-weighted.

### UPSC application

Replace the phrase “foreigners were absorbed” with a mechanism: **service + marriage + language + patronage + urban association + reciprocal adaptation**. Add the evidence limit.

1. Integration was negotiated and regionally varied.
2. Political legitimacy used local and transregional forms.
3. Heliodorus and Suvishakha are named mobile actors.
4. Normative varna labels are not social censuses.

### Begin in plain language

Gandhara art is not Greek art with Buddhist labels attached. Its subjects, ritual settings and narrative purposes were Buddhist, while its treatment of anatomy, hair, garments and decorative forms drew on Hellenistic and Greco-Bactrian conventions. Central Asian elements entered through Kushana patronage, frontier dress, political mobility and networks joining Bactria with Gandhara.

Mathura provides the essential comparison. Its red-sandstone workshops grew from older local traditions and made Buddhist, Jaina, Brahmanical and royal images. The inscribed headless Kanishka statue combines Central Asian-style dress with Mathura craftsmanship and Kushana royal representation. The Mat *devakula*, or royal shrine, preserves portraits, though the exact ritual status of the rulers remains debated.

### The Buddha-image debate

Anthropomorphic Buddha images became prominent at both Gandhara and Mathura in the early centuries CE. Earlier Buddhist art often represented the Buddha through symbols or narrative absence, but symbols and images later coexisted. Priority between the two centres, the exact role of Mahayana and the causal weight of Hellenistic naturalism remain debated.

|---|---|---|---|

| Prakrit | Kharoshthi or Brahmi | Bilingual coins; Heliodorus inscription | Regional public communication |
| Bactrian | Greek | Rabatak; later Kushana coins | Imperial language adaptation |

*What the visual shows:* language and script are separate choices; multilingual rule was structured rather than chaotic.

```text
LANGUAGE answers: what was expressed?

```

### Begin in plain language

A script is a writing system; a language is what is being expressed. Greek letters can write Greek or Bactrian. Prakrit can appear in Kharoshthi or Brahmi. Confusing these pairs creates classic Prelims errors.

Indo-Greek bilingual coins frequently pair Greek with Prakrit in Kharoshthi. The Heliodorus pillar uses Prakrit in Brahmi. Kanishka's earlier coinage used Greek, while later Kushana issues used Bactrian written in Greek script. Rudradaman's Junagadh *prashasti* marks a major expansion of long Sanskrit royal epigraphy, but it did not instantly displace Prakrit.

### Calendars without false founders

The Vikrama era traditionally begins in 58-57 BCE; links with Azes are debated. The Shaka era begins in 78 CE; its founder is disputed. Kanishka's accession is commonly placed around 127 CE in the modern chronology used here. The *Yavanajataka* is important partly because its chronological reasoning treats the Shaka and Kushana eras separately.

### Selective scientific exchange

Greek astronomical and astrological concepts entered Sanskrit technical traditions through identifiable terms and texts such as the *Yavanajataka*. The strongest method is specific:

```text
named text or term
      +
plausible chronology
      +
identifiable technical correspondence
      =
bounded claim of transmission
```

It is unsafe to claim that Central Asian or Greek contact introduced Indian theatre, medicine, astronomy or science wholesale. Exchange was selective, translated and combined with existing traditions.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Multilingual coins simply reveal confusion among unstable rulers.

**Reply:** Their repeated and patterned pairings suggest deliberate communication with different users. Language choice could serve accessibility, prestige and sovereignty at once.

### UPSC application

Build a four-column language-script table from memory. In Mains, use *Yavanajataka* for selective transmission, not for a vague claim that “all science came from Greece.”

### Mini recap

1. Language and script are different variables.

3. Kharoshthi was prominent in the north-west.
4. Rabatak is Bactrian written in Greek script.
5. Junagadh is a major long Sanskrit royal inscription.
6. Prakrit continued after Sanskrit's courtly rise.
7. Vikrama-era and Azes links are debated.
8. Shaka era and Kanishka accession should be separated.
9. Scientific exchange must be demonstrated term by term.


*What the visual shows:* decline was a transformation of power and networks, not the instantaneous disappearance of people or cultural forms.

### Begin in plain language

Large transregional states rarely end for one reason. Kushana authority contracted unevenly from the third century CE. Succession and regionalisation weakened cohesion; Sasanian expansion affected western and Central Asian zones; local powers gained room; and routes and monetary conditions changed. The evidence does not justify one dramatic collapse date across all regions.

### Political transformation

Later Kushanas continued after Vasudeva I, while Kushano-Sasanian rulers operated in western parts of the former imperial sphere. Nagas and other regional powers became more visible in northern India. Different zones left the high imperial arrangement at different times.

### What endured

- bilingual and multilingual political communication;
- portrait coinage, royal titles and monetary traditions;
- satrapal and intermediary forms of rule;
- Buddhist routes into Central Asia and China;
- Gandhara and Mathura artistic production;
- courtly Sanskrit's growing political use;
- memories of Menander, Kanishka and other rulers;
- cities and religious institutions shaped by long-distance networks.

### Three historiographical models

| Model | Contribution | Weakness if used alone |
|---|---|---|
| Invasion model | Preserves conflict, displacement and coercion | Reduces centuries to military entry |
| Assimilation model | Explains adoption of local forms | Makes change seem one-way and frictionless |
| Connected-frontier model | Integrates routes, regional agency and reciprocal adaptation | Can understate violence unless explicitly retained |

The best synthesis combines them: conquest mattered; integration was negotiated; exchange generated new forms; benefits and visibility were unequal.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** “Cultural synthesis” romanticises conquest and elite domination.

**Reply:** It does if detached from coercion. A defensible synthesis names warfare, political inequality and elite-heavy evidence while still explaining genuine institutional and cultural creation.

### UPSC application

Conclude a Mains answer with transformation rather than praise: these contacts altered political vocabulary, money, routes, religion and visual culture, but effects varied by region and social group.

1. Kushana contraction was regionally uneven.
2. No single cause explains decline.
3. Sasanian pressure mattered in western zones.
4. Regional rulers gained autonomy.
5. Political contraction did not end cultural continuities.
6. Invasion, assimilation and connection are partial models.
7. Reciprocal adaptation must retain coercion and inequality.
8. Significance lies in new institutions and forms, not “foreignness” alone.
9. Topic 16 ends before full Gupta political history.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10-mark practice 1

**Question:** Explain why Indo-Greek coinage is both a rich source and a dangerous substitute for a political map. (150 words)

### Model answer

Indo-Greek coinage is unusually rich because named portraits, royal epithets, metals, weights, monograms and bilingual legends permit the identification and relative sequencing of rulers who are often absent from literary narratives. Greek and Prakrit legends, commonly using Greek and Kharoshthi scripts, also reveal deliberate communication with plural audiences. Reverse deities and Indian motifs show adaptation of royal symbolism, while overstrikes may indicate rivalry, succession or metal reuse.

However, a coin is portable. Its findspot may reflect trade, tribute, soldiers' pay, hoarding, imitation or later deposition rather than direct taxation. A shared type can cross rival kingdoms, and a stray find does not identify its mint. Therefore coin distribution should be tested against inscriptions, hoard composition, wear, die studies and stratified archaeology.

Indo-Greek coins are best treated as political texts and economic objects, not ready-made frontiers.

## 10-mark practice 2

**Question:** How does the Heliodorus pillar illuminate cultural interaction in post-Mauryan India? (150 words)

### Model answer

The Besnagar pillar records Heliodorus, son of Dion and ambassador of the Indo-Greek king Antialcidas, at the court of Bhagabhadra. Written in Prakrit in Brahmi script, it identifies Heliodorus as a Bhagavata and dedicates a Garuda standard to Vasudeva.

The pillar illuminates three processes. First, it documents diplomacy between Taxila-linked Indo-Greek power and the Vidisha region. Second, it shows the mobility and cultural agency of an individual envoy rather than a sealed ethnic community. Third, its language, script and devotional vocabulary reveal participation in a local religious world.

Its limit is equally important. One ambassador's profession does not establish collective Indo-Greek conversion, a Bhagavata state religion or the beginning of Vaishnavism. Used carefully, the pillar demonstrates that political contact could produce personal affiliation and shared public expression without erasing distinct origins.

## 15-mark practice 1

**Question:** Discuss the character of Shaka satrapal rule with reference to western India. (250 words)

### Model answer

Shaka rule in western India developed through regional houses rather than one continuously centralised empire. Its characteristic titles, *kshatrapa* and *mahakshatrapa*, had Iranian ancestry but acquired flexible meanings in Indian settings. A holder could be a subordinate governor, junior dynast or effectively sovereign ruler.

The Kshaharata line is represented above all by Nahapana. His coins and the Nashik-Karle donations of his son-in-law Ushavadata connect political authority with routes, religious patronage and northern Maharashtra. Gautamiputra Satakarni's restriking of Nahapana's silver coins provides strong material evidence of political reversal, although it cannot narrate every campaign.

The Kardamaka line, founded by Chashtana, established more durable Western Kshatrapa power. Rudradaman I's Junagadh inscription, dated Shaka year 72, combines genealogy, conquests, Sanskrit royal praise and repair of the Sudarshana reservoir under Suvishakha. Its reference to victories over a Satakarni and kinship-based restraint shows warfare and interdynastic relation together.

Satrapal organisation therefore offered flexible regional control through graded titles, local officers, mints and patronage. The same flexibility could strengthen regional autonomy and produce succession tensions. Its institutions should not be treated as an unchanged copy of Achaemenid administration. Western Shaka rule was an adapted regional state form whose legitimacy rested on monetary authority, public works, martial claims and participation in local social-religious networks.

## 15-mark practice 2

**Question:** Examine the nature and limits of religious pluralism under the Kushanas. (250 words)

### Model answer

Kushana religious pluralism is visible most clearly in coins, inscriptions, monuments and donations. Kanishka and Huvishka issued coins depicting or naming figures from Buddhist, Iranian, Greek and Indian traditions, including Buddha, Maitreya, Nana, Mithra, Atash, Helios, Selene and Oesho. Vima Kadphises used Shiva-related imagery, while Kanishka is strongly associated with Buddhist monuments and patronage at Purushapura and across wider networks.

This variety reflects an empire spanning Bactria, Gandhara, Punjab and Mathura. Royal imagery could address different constituencies, connect kingship with several sacred idioms and support institutions important to routes and cities. Monks, merchants, translators and donors, not kings alone, sustained Buddhist transmission toward Central Asia and China.

Yet plural imagery cannot be reduced to modern neutrality. A coin type was also a claim of sovereignty, and it does not reveal the ruler's complete private creed. Kanishka's Buddhist patronage did not create Mahayana, whose development was longer and multi-centred. The council associated with him survives in later traditions whose location and doctrinal outcome remain debated. Nor does elite patronage measure ordinary religious practice throughout the empire.

Kushana pluralism was thus both genuine patronage and imperial communication. Its historical importance lies in enabling multiple cults and networks while preserving royal hierarchy and regional variation.

## 20-mark practice 1

**Question:** “The Kushana Empire was a network of graded sovereignties rather than a uniformly administered territorial block.” Analyse. (Answer in 250-300 words.)

### Model answer

The Kushana Empire connected Bactria, Afghanistan, Gandhara, Punjab and Mathura, with claims and influence farther into the Ganga plain. Its scale is clear, but the evidence does not support identical administration across this entire space.

At the centre, rulers projected exalted authority through titles such as *shaonano shao*, “king of kings,” and *devaputra*, “son of god.” Coins spread royal portraits and plural divine imagery; inscriptions and royal shrines at sites such as Rabatak, Surkh Kotal and Mat joined dynasty, deity and sovereignty. Purushapura, Kapisa-Begram, Bactrian centres and Mathura supplied several political and economic nodes rather than one exclusive capital.

Below the ruler, satraps, local kings, officers, cities, guilds and religious establishments mediated power. Route control, tribute, monetary circulation and military mobility could extend influence without requiring the same provincial machinery everywhere. Rabatak names eastern cities including Saketa, Kaushambi, Pataliputra and Champa, but a royal claim cannot by itself establish equal taxation or permanent garrisons in each place. Likewise, coin finds map movement more securely than borders.

This layered structure was an advantage: it joined distant regions and reduced the cost of governing varied populations. It was also a vulnerability because regional intermediaries and succession disputes could loosen cohesion. Later contraction occurred unevenly under internal regionalisation, Sasanian pressure and emerging local powers.

Therefore Kushana sovereignty was substantial but graded. Its strength lay in connecting cores, corridors and intermediaries; its geography should be drawn as zones of differing political depth rather than a uniform modern-style territory.

## 20-mark practice 2

**Question:** Assess the political, economic and cultural significance of Central Asian contacts in India between c. 200 BCE and 300 CE. (Answer in 250-300 words.)

### Model answer

Central Asian contacts after the Mauryas were not one invasion but a succession of overlapping Indo-Greek, Shaka, Pahlava and Kushana formations. Their significance arose from conflict and mobility combined with long residence and institutional adaptation.

Politically, Indo-Greek principalities expanded portrait and bilingual coinage; Shaka rulers adapted satrapal titles to regional power; Pahlavas maintained a distinct north-western formation; and Kushanas built a multi-centred empire linking Bactria with Gandhara and Mathura. Heliodorus's embassy, Rudradaman's Junagadh inscription and Rabatak reveal diplomacy, public works, genealogy and exalted kingship. These states remained uneven, and portable coins or royal claims cannot define precise borders.

Economically, overland corridors joined the Tarim Basin, Bactria and northern India, while lower-Indus and western ports entered Indian Ocean circuits. Gold, silver and copper issues, urban workshops, guild endowments and monasteries indicate high-value exchange and institutional finance. Yet trade did not replace agriculture or distribute prosperity uniformly.

Culturally, multilingual coinage and inscriptions joined Greek, Prakrit, Bactrian, Kharoshthi, Brahmi and Sanskrit. Religious patronage embraced Buddhist, Vaishnava, Shaiva, Iranian and Greek forms. Gandhara and Mathura became distinct but interacting artistic centres. Buddhist movement toward Central Asia expanded, and selective astronomical concepts entered Sanskrit technical traditions.

The limits matter: cultural synthesis was elite-heavy, regionally varied and inseparable from conquest. Nevertheless, these contacts transformed political vocabulary, money, routes, worship and representation. Their deepest legacy was not foreign rule alone but the creation of new connected forms through unequal yet reciprocal interaction.

## Core thesis

Between roughly 200 BCE and 300 CE, north-western, northern and western India interacted intensively with Bactria, Central Asia, Iran and the wider Hellenistic world. Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Pahlavas and Kushanas were distinct, overlapping formations. Their importance lies in political conflict, route-centred state building and reciprocal but unequal adaptation.

| Mid-second century BCE | Menander/Milinda | Coins firmer than literary biography |
| First century BCE-first century CE | Shaka regional branches | Replacement differed by region |
| Early first century CE | Rajuvula and Gondophares phases | Shaka and Pahlava remain distinct |

## Routes and regions

- Bactria connected Iranian, steppe and Central Asian worlds.
- Hindu Kush passes channelled movement into Kabul-Gandhara.
- Taxila linked frontier traffic with Punjab routes.
- Mathura joined western movement to the Ganga-Yamuna system.
- Lower Indus and Gujarat connected inland routes with the Indian Ocean.
- Routes enabled movement; they did not guarantee uniform sovereignty.

## Source discipline

- **Coins:** ruler, portrait, legend, metal, weight, deity, overstrike, circulation.
- **Inscriptions:** date, donor, lineage, public work, title and royal claim.
- **Archaeology:** settlement phase, workshop, monument and material context.
- **Indian texts:** doctrine and remembered rulers, controlled by genre and redaction.
- **Chinese/classical texts:** migration, geography and trade, controlled by translation and outsider categories.
- **Golden rule:** portable object first proves movement; royal claim first proves representation.

## Indo-Greeks

- Emerged from Graeco-Bactrian political developments.
- Several rulers and houses operated concurrently.
- Demetrius marks an early movement south of the Hindu Kush.
- Menander is known through extensive coins and remembered as Milinda.
- *Milindapanha* is a Buddhist dialogue involving Nagasena, not a court transcript.
- Antialcidas sent Heliodorus to Bhagabhadra's court.
- Heliodorus called himself a Bhagavata and dedicated a Garuda pillar to Vasudeva.
- Bilingual portrait coinage expanded identifiable royal communication.
- Agathocles' Balarama-Vasudeva images show cult recognition, not certain conversion.

## Shakas and satrapal rule

- Shaka power had north-western, Mathura and western branches.
- Maues/Moga belongs to early north-western Shaka authority.
- Rajuvula belongs to Mathura and defeated Strato III at Sagala.
- *Kshatrapa* and *mahakshatrapa* were flexible titles.
- Nahapana was a Kshaharata; Ushavadata's donations illuminate routes and patronage.
- Gautamiputra's overstrikes support Nahapana's displacement.
- Chashtana and Rudradaman belonged to the Kardamaka line.
- Junagadh joins Sanskrit praise, genealogy, conquest and reservoir repair.
- Full Satavahana history remains outside this topic.

## Pahlavas

- Pahlavas were Indo-Parthians, not simply Shakas.
- Gondophares ruled in Afghanistan-Gandhara in the early first century CE.
- Coins and Takht-i-Bahi provide the strongest evidence.
- The St Thomas link is a later tradition requiring corroboration.
- Kujula overstrikes connect but do not collapse Pahlava and Kushana phases.

## Kushana formation and rule

- Great Yuezhi movement is reconstructed mainly from Chinese histories.
- Bactria became the formation zone.
- Guishuang was one of five principalities.
- Kujula Kadphises consolidated Kushana power.
- Vima Kadphises is associated with major gold/copper issues.
- Rabatak clarifies the line leading to Kanishka.
- Rabatak is Bactrian in Greek script.
- Kanishka is commonly dated c. 127 CE in the modern chronology used here.
- *Shaonano shao* and *devaputra* expressed exalted sovereignty.
- Purushapura, Bactrian centres, Kapisa-Begram and Mathura formed a multi-centred system.
- Eastern city claims are not proof of equal administrative depth.

## Economy

- Silk Routes were plural and changing.
- Kushana and Western Kshatrapa regions linked overland and maritime networks.
- States taxed, protected and contested routes; merchants and institutions carried exchange.
- Gold coinage indicates high-value capacity, not universal prosperity.
- Silver and copper served other regional and transactional scales.
- Guild endowments joined corporate finance, charity and religion.
- Agriculture, crafts, tribute and taxation remained important.
- Detailed commerce and urbanisation belong to Topic 19.

## Society and military contact

- Integration occurred through office, marriage, patronage, language and urban association.
- Brahmanical classifications were normative attempts to place newcomers.
- Heliodorus and Suvishakha show mobile individual careers.
- Elite sources illuminate rulers better than peasants, women and labourers.
- Cavalry, armour, boots and frontier dress reflect military-cultural contact.
- Early mounting aids must not be equated automatically with mature paired stirrups.

## Religion

- Menander is remembered in Buddhist dialogue tradition.
- Heliodorus supplies Bhagavata evidence.
- Vima's coins show Shiva/Oesho-related imagery.
- Kanishka and Huvishka coins display a plural pantheon.
- Kanishka was an important Buddhist patron.
- Mahayana was not created by one king or one council.
- Council location and conclusions remain debated.
- Buddhist spread relied on monks, merchants, translators and routes.

## Art as historical evidence

- Gandhara: north-western, Buddhist, Hellenistic/Greco-Bactrian visual forms, Central Asian patronage and costume.
- Mathura: red sandstone, older local idiom, Buddhist-Jaina-Brahmanical range, royal portraits.
- Both centres contributed to early anthropomorphic Buddha imagery.
- Priority and sole causation remain debated.
- Style does not prove artist ethnicity.
- The 2019 GS-I Q1 route is co-owned: Topic 16 supplies historical contact, patronage, costume and mobility; Indian Art and Culture controls form, style and iconography.

## Language, eras and knowledge

- Indo-Greek coins often pair Greek with Prakrit/Kharoshthi.
- Heliodorus uses Prakrit in Brahmi.
- Bactrian could be written in Greek script.
- Junagadh marks the rise of long courtly Sanskrit epigraphy.
- Shaka era began in 78 CE; founder disputed.
- Kanishka's accession is commonly placed around 127 CE.
- *Yavanajataka* supports selective astronomical-astrological exchange.
- Technical transmission requires named terms, texts and chronology.

## Decline and significance

- Kushana contraction was multicausal and regionally uneven.
- Succession, regionalisation, Sasanian pressure and changing networks mattered.
- Later Kushanas and Kushano-Sasanian formations continued parts of the political tradition.
- Political decline did not erase coinage, art, religion, routes or memory.
- Best verdict: conflict and connectivity produced new forms through unequal reciprocal adaptation.
