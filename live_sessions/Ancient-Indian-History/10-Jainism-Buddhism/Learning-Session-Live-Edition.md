# Jainism and Buddhism - Learning Session Live Edition

Two people may leave the same sixth-century BCE town, reject sacrificial status as the only road to liberation, form disciplined renunciant communities, and still disagree about the deepest question: **what is it that is liberated?** Jainism answers with an enduring conscious *jiva* obscured by karmic matter. Buddhism analyses experience without a permanent independent self and explains bondage through conditioned craving and ignorance. Their shared setting matters, but their different causal models matter more.

This session therefore does not begin with a list of founders and councils. It first teaches how layered religious sources become historical evidence. It then builds Jainism from Mahavira's inherited Tirthankara tradition through karma, vows, knowledge and community; builds Buddhism from the Buddha's source-sensitive life through diagnosis, conditionality, practice and sangha; and only then explains councils, schools, patronage, spread, contraction and comparison. Topic 11 owns full Mahajanapada geography and interstate politics. Topic 13 owns full state, taxation, varna and social-structure analysis. Art and Culture owns detailed stupa, cave, sculpture and iconographic development. Philosophy Optional owns specialist metaphysical and epistemological debate.

The date of a surviving manuscript is not automatically the date of the teaching it preserves.

### 1. Begin with genre, not reverence or suspicion

Buddhist *suttas/sutras* and Jain *agamas* are indispensable because they preserve doctrine, debates, monastic rules and remembered lives. They are not modern biographies. Communities transmitted, classified and sometimes expanded them over centuries. A miracle story may reveal sacred memory without functioning as an eyewitness report. A disciplinary rule may reveal an institutional problem even when its narrative origin is stylised.

| Source | Strongest historical use | Built-in limit |
|---|---|---|
| Discourses and dialogues | Concepts, opponents, teaching settings | Layered and school-specific |
| Vinaya/monastic rules | Procedures, disputes, material needs | Norm does not prove universal practice |
| Jain monastic/canonical texts | Vows, community memory, sectarian positions | Redaction and canon differ by sect |
| Inscription | Donor, title, place and dated association | Formulaic and purposeful |
| Stupa, cave, image, relic deposit | Patronage, ritual and institutional presence | Meaning and phase require context |
| Chronicle or pilgrim account | Missions, routes and later institutional memory | Later regional standpoint |

### 2. Use a confidence ladder

```text
traditional claim
      + parallel textual memories
      + linguistic/layer analysis
      + dated inscription or archaeology
      = bounded historical conclusion
```

✅ **Fact:** Ashoka's Lumbini pillar inscription provides an early epigraphic association between Lumbini and the Buddha.

⚠️ **Inference:** It strengthens the birthplace tradition; it does not independently verify the four sights, every journey, or every speech in later biographies.

The same discipline applies to modern relic exhibitions. The 2026 Leh exposition is a verified fact about present custody, veneration and heritage policy. The relic label and chain of custody must still be evaluated historically; a modern official ceremony cannot prove every ancient biographical detail.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Because the texts were redacted later, nothing about Mahavira or the Buddha is historically recoverable.

**Reply:** Later transmission reduces precision; it does not erase converging evidence. Rival traditions remember identifiable teachers, places and communities. Parallel recensions, inscriptions, archaeology and internal textual layers let historians grade claims rather than choose between literal belief and total scepticism.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish canon, inscription, monastery, relic and later chronicle.
- **Mains:** attach a source-limit sentence to every major claim.
- **Boundary:** Topic 02 owns the full taxonomy of sources; this lesson applies that method.
- **Next link:** With the evidence rules fixed, the social setting can be explained without turning context into destiny.

### Mini recap

1. Sacred value and historical value are different questions.
2. Composition, recitation, redaction and manuscript are separate dates.
3. Normative rules reveal ideals and problems, not universal compliance.
4. Inscriptions are dated anchors but still selective.
5. Material remains show institutions and patrons more securely than doctrine.
6. Modern heritage events concern custody and memory, not automatic ancient proof.
7. Convergence raises confidence.
8. Every conclusion needs a reach and a limit.

```text
     Jain                  Buddhist               Ajivika and
   disciplines             Middle Way             other positions
```

No single arrow “caused” Jainism or Buddhism.

### 1. What *shramana* means

*Shramana* broadly refers to renunciant or striving traditions outside, or at the edge of, household-centred Vedic ritual authority. The field included Jain, Buddhist, Ajivika, materialist and other teachers. *Parivrajaka* means wandering renunciant; it is not the name of one sect. The variety matters: some defended severe asceticism, some determinism, some scepticism, and some materialist denial of rebirth.

### 2. Why this setting widened the audience

The middle Gangetic region saw expanding cultivation, towns, routes, political consolidation and social differentiation. Rulers, householders, artisans and traders could support itinerant teachers and settled rainy-season communities. New wealth did not automatically produce one doctrine; it created audiences, resources and new moral problems.

| Simplification | Better explanation |
|---|---|
| “Expensive sacrifice caused Buddhism” | Ritual criticism interacted with renunciation, ethics and liberation debates |
| “Merchants invented Jainism” | Lay support mattered, but ascetic lineages and broader patronage also mattered |
| “Iron created heterodoxy” | Material change widened conditions; ideas were not mechanical outputs of tools |
| “Both were anti-Brahmin revolts” | They disputed exclusive authority yet also debated, borrowed and coexisted |
| “They established modern equality” | Salvific access widened, while social hierarchy and patriarchy persisted |

### 3. Appeal was multidirectional

Plain-language intuition: a householder did not have to become a monk to enter the moral economy. Food, robes, medicine, shelter and donations supported renunciants; teachings, merit and prestige flowed back. Rulers could patronise institutions without surrendering kingship. Women and lower-status people could gain religious roles, but access did not erase every hierarchy.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If economic and political change created the audience, doctrine was only a reflection of material interests.

**Reply:** Context explains opportunity, not doctrinal content. Jain karmic materialism, Buddhist no-self, Ajivika determinism and materialist denial emerged in overlapping conditions but gave radically different answers. Ideas must be studied as causal arguments and lived disciplines.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** shramana, parivrajaka and upasaka are related but not interchangeable.
- **Mains:** use a matrix of material, social, intellectual and ethical factors.
- **Boundary:** Topic 11 owns Mahajanapada detail; Topic 13 owns full social structure.
- **Next link:** Jainism must now be located within an older Tirthankara lineage rather than described as Mahavira's invention.

### Mini recap

1. Shramana names a plural field.
2. Urbanisation supplied audiences and support, not one inevitable doctrine.
3. Ritual critique was one factor among several.
4. Household-renunciant reciprocity made institutions durable.
5. Political patronage did not equal doctrinal control.
6. Widened salvation did not abolish social hierarchy.
7. Jain, Buddhist and Ajivika answers diverged sharply.
8. Context is explanatory only when monocausal claims are rejected.

### Visual: succession before biography

```text

        ...
Parshvanatha (23rd; plausible earlier historical teacher)
        |
Mahavira / Vardhamana (24th)
        |
renunciation -> severe austerity -> kevala -> teaching community -> liberation
```

Mahavira is the twenty-fourth Tirthankara in Jain tradition, not a founder who created Jainism from nothing.

### 1. Names carry source clues

*Mahavira* means “great hero”; *Vardhamana* is his personal name in tradition. Buddhist texts call him **Nigantha Nataputta/Nayaputta**, providing an external rival memory of a Jain teacher and community. A *Tirthankara* is a “ford-maker”: one who discovers and teaches a crossing from bondage to liberation.

Parshvanatha, the twenty-third Tirthankara, is associated with a fourfold restraint. Jain tradition presents Mahavira as making celibacy explicitly separate in a five-vow discipline. The comparison may preserve an earlier ascetic stratum, but the detailed biographies are transmitted in later sectarian texts.

### 2. Historical core and sacred elaboration

| Claim | Confidence | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Mahavira was an influential renunciant teacher | High | Jain and rival Buddhist memories |
| He operated in the sixth-fifth century BCE Gangetic setting | Broadly high | Relative chronology and shared persons/places |
| He inherited an older ascetic tradition | Plausible | Parshva memory and doctrinal continuity |
| Exact birth/death years | Disputed | Sectarian chronological systems differ |
| Every named journey, miracle and dialogue | Uneven | Hagiographic expansion and late redaction |

Traditional accounts link him with a Kshatriya clan, renunciation, prolonged austerity, attainment of **kevala** or perfected knowledge, and a teaching career. The historical answer should preserve the sequence but label exact ages and dates as traditional where necessary.

### 3. Do not turn severity into caricature

Mahavira's austerity expresses a causal diagnosis: action driven by passion admits karmic matter and binds the soul. Bodily and mental restraint are therefore technologies of liberation, not punishment for its own sake. The next lesson will explain that mechanism.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If Parshva came earlier, Mahavira was merely a minor reformer.

**Reply:** Continuity does not remove historical importance. Mahavira became the decisive organiser and teacher of the community remembered by surviving Jain traditions. The precise relationship between Parshva's followers and Mahavira's system remains a historical question, not grounds for erasing either figure.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** 23rd Parshva; 24th Mahavira; Nigantha Nataputta.
- **Mains:** separate traditional biography, cross-tradition corroboration and historical core.
- **Trap:** “founder” is too crude; “systematiser within an older lineage” is safer.
- **Next link:** Mahavira's path becomes intelligible only when jiva and karmic bondage are mapped.

### Mini recap

1. Tirthankara means ford-maker.
2. Mahavira is the twenty-fourth Tirthankara.
3. Parshva is the twenty-third and plausibly historical.
4. Nigantha Nataputta is a Buddhist-text name for Mahavira.
5. Exact chronologies vary by tradition.
6. Biography mixes recoverable core and sacred elaboration.
7. Five-vow development is remembered against Parshva's fourfold restraint.
8. Austerity follows a causal theory of bondage.

## Lesson 4 - Jain Reality and the Mechanics of Karma

### Visual: liberation as stopping and cleaning

```text
JIVA: conscious, enduring, capable of perfected knowledge
                       |
action + passion ------+------> ASRAVA: karmic influx
                                      |
                                      v
                              BANDHA: bondage
                                      |
             restraint ------> SAMVARA: stop new influx
             austerity ------> NIRJARA: shed accumulated karma
                                      |
                                      v
                                 MOKSHA
```

Think of a clear lamp enclosed by accumulating dust: the lamp is not created by cleaning, but its luminosity becomes unobstructed.

### 1. Jiva and ajiva

Jain ontology distinguishes **jiva**, conscious living substance, from **ajiva**, non-conscious categories. Ajiva includes matter (*pudgala*), space, time, and the media associated with motion and rest. Here **dharma-dravya** and **adharma-dravya** are cosmological conditions of motion and rest; they do not simply mean moral merit and sin.

Jivas are plural and enduring. Their capacities are obscured by karma. This differs fundamentally from Buddhist *anatta*, which denies a permanent independent self. Do not merge the systems merely because both discuss karma and liberation.

### 2. Karma is not only a moral score

In Jain thought, karma is subtle matter that becomes associated with jiva through activity coloured by passions such as anger, pride, deceit and greed. The sequence is:

1. **Asrava** - influx of karmic matter.
2. **Bandha** - binding of that karma to jiva.
3. **Samvara** - restraint that blocks fresh influx.
4. **Nirjara** - shedding accumulated karma.
5. **Moksha** - release of the purified jiva.

This is why conduct, carefulness and austerity are structurally central. Knowledge alone cannot remove bondage if conduct continues to admit new karma.

### 3. Life is distributed widely

Jain classifications recognise living beings embodied in earth, water, fire, air and plants, alongside mobile beings with more senses. This produces a radical extension of non-harm. It is a religious ontology, not a claim that modern biology classifies fire as an organism.

The four principal **gatis**, or rebirth destinations, are divine beings (*deva*), humans (*manushya*), hell beings (*naraki*) and animals/plants or non-human embodied life (*tiryancha*). A yaksha is not the missing fourth category.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Material karma makes Jain ethics mechanically physical and leaves no role for intention.

**Reply:** The material model does not remove psychology. Passions and activity determine influx and bondage; disciplined intention and conduct enable stoppage and shedding. The system joins ontology, psychology and ethics rather than reducing karma to ordinary dust.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** sequence asrava-bandha-samvara-nirjara-moksha; four gatis.
- **Mains:** explain why rigorous restraint follows logically from material karmic bondage.
- **Comparison:** Jain karmic matter is not Buddhist intentional karma.
- **Next link:** The liberation mechanism now explains why vows and the Triratna are graded for monks and laity.

### Mini recap

1. Jiva is conscious, plural and enduring.
2. Ajiva includes more than ordinary matter.
3. Karma is subtle material bondage in Jain thought.
4. Passion and activity drive influx.
5. Samvara stops new karma.
6. Nirjara sheds accumulated karma.
7. Moksha perfects rather than annihilates jiva.
8. Earth-, water-, fire-, air- and plant-bodied life expand ahimsa.
9. Yaksha is not one of the four gatis.

### Visual: one path, different intensity

```text
                 JAIN TRIRATNA
      right faith + right knowledge + right conduct
                           |
              FIVE ETHICAL RESTRAINTS
 ahimsa | satya | asteya | brahmacharya | aparigraha
                 /                     \
                v                       v
       MAHAVRATAS: ascetics     ANUVRATAS: householders
       comprehensive rigor       limited, practicable form
                \                       /
                 +---- samvara/nirjara -+
```

The lay path is not a different religion; it is a graded discipline within the same liberation horizon.

### 1. Triratna is integration, not sequence alone

The Jain **three jewels** are right faith or orientation, right knowledge and right conduct. Correct belief without understanding is unstable; knowledge without conduct does not stop karmic influx; conduct without right orientation lacks the full path. The three mutually support liberation.

### 2. Five vows and their logic

| Vow | Plain meaning | Karmic logic | Practical qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ahimsa | Avoid injury | Harmful activity and passion bind karma | Ascetic care is stricter than household practice |
| Satya | Truthfulness | Deception expresses attachment and harm | Truth is constrained by non-harm |
| Asteya | Do not take what is not given | Appropriation feeds greed | Includes honest exchange |
| Brahmacharya | Celibacy/chastity | Sensual attachment sustains bondage | Full celibacy for renunciants; graded restraint for laity |
| Aparigraha | Non-possession/non-attachment | Possession intensifies attachment | Lay limits differ from mendicant renunciation |

**Ahimsa** is the organising ethic, but it should not be isolated from careful speech, non-possession and control of passion. Practices concerning food, movement, water, occupations and seasonal residence make the ontology of widespread life operational.

### 3. How a renunciant movement becomes durable

If only total renunciation counted as participation, a community could not reproduce materially. Jainism developed a fourfold community of monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen. Lay followers practised limited vows, fasting, charity, pilgrimage and support of ascetics. Ascetics embodied the highest discipline and supplied teaching; householders supplied resources and local continuity.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Graded lay vows dilute an uncompromising ascetic doctrine.

**Reply:** Grading is an institutional solution, not necessarily doctrinal surrender. The ascetic ideal remains the standard while householders reduce harm and attachment within constrained lives. The tension between ideal and practicability is part of Jain history.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish Jain Triratna from Buddhist Three Refuges and Eightfold Path.
- **Mains:** connect ahimsa to ontology, karma and everyday discipline.
- **Trap:** aparigraha is not mere poverty; attachment and possession are linked.
- **Next link:** Ethical restraint is joined by a method for handling partial knowledge without declaring every view equally true.

### Mini recap

1. Triratna combines faith, knowledge and conduct.
2. Five vows form one ethical system.
3. Ahimsa follows the wide distribution of jiva.
4. Mahavratas are ascetic great vows.

## Lesson 6 - Jain Ways of Knowing: Many-Sidedness without “Anything Goes”

### Visual: object, standpoint and statement

```text
REALITY HAS MANY ASPECTS
        ANEKANTAVADA
              |
   observer uses a partial standpoint
           NAYAVADA
              |
 statement is qualified by conditions/respect
           SYADVADA
```

Many-sidedness does not say every claim is equally correct. It asks: from which standpoint, under what conditions, and about which aspect?

### 1. Three related terms, three jobs

**Anekantavada** holds that reality is complex or many-sided and cannot be exhausted by one unqualified description. **Nayavada** analyses partial standpoints: a statement may grasp one legitimate aspect while failing as a total account. **Syadvada** disciplines predication through a qualification often rendered “in a certain respect” or “conditionally.”

Consider a clay jar:

- As substance, clay persists.
- As a particular mode, the jar begins and may break.
- “It exists” is true in one respect.
- “That jar no longer exists” may be true after breaking.
- The statements are not contradictions when time, mode and standpoint differ.

This is not indecision. It is a rule against turning partial truth into exhaustive truth.

### 2. The sevenfold scheme and its limit

Later Jain thinkers systematised **saptabhangi**, seven conditioned modes of assertion involving affirmation, negation and inexpressibility in qualified combinations. For Ancient History, know the logic and chronology: mature technical formulation developed over time and should not be placed verbatim into Mahavira's first sermon.

| Error | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| “Anekantavada means reality is unknowable” | Jainism affirms knowledge, culminating in kevala |
| “Syadvada says yes and no in the same respect” | Qualification separates respect, mode or condition |
| “Naya is the whole truth” | A naya is a partial standpoint |
| “All later formulae are Mahavira's exact words” | Doctrine has transmission and systematisation history |

### 3. Ethical significance

Intellectual non-absolutism can support restraint in judgement, but it should not be romanticised into modern pluralism without remainder. Jain thinkers defended definite truths, a structured ontology and a demanding path. The doctrine limits one-sided assertion; it does not suspend all discrimination.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If opposite statements may both be conditionally valid, syadvada violates non-contradiction.

**Reply:** The conditions do the logical work. “The jar exists as clay” and “the intact jar does not exist after breaking” differ in time and respect. Jain predication rejects unqualified absolutisation, not disciplined consistency.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** anekanta = many-sided reality; naya = standpoint; syat = conditioned assertion.
- **Mains:** use one concrete example, then add the later-systematisation caveat.
- **Comparison:** Buddhist dependent origination is not another name for anekantavada.
- **Next link:** Ideas survive through communities, texts and disagreements; the next lesson maps that institutional history.

### Mini recap

1. Anekantavada concerns reality's many aspects.
2. Nayavada concerns partial standpoints.
3. Syadvada concerns qualified predication.
4. Conditional statements need not be contradictions.
5. Jainism does not teach total scepticism.
6. Kevala remains perfected knowledge.
7. Saptabhangi is a mature systematic formulation.
8. Doctrinal history must not be collapsed into founder biography.

### 1. Canons preserve memory through disagreement

Shvetambara tradition associates preservation efforts with Pataliputra and a later redaction at **Valabhi**. Digambara traditions hold that the original canon was lost and do not accept the Shvetambara agamas as the intact original teaching. The historical conclusion is not that one council mechanically created Jainism. It is that memory, famine narratives, migration, recitation and sectarian authority shaped different canon claims.

### 2. Digambara and Shvetambara

| Dimension | Digambara | Shvetambara | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name | “Sky-clad” | “White-clad” | Clothing is not the only difference |
| Canon | Original canon lost | Agamas preserved/redacted | Claims are tradition-specific |

| Images/ritual | Distinct iconographic and ritual conventions | Distinct conventions and subsects | Both histories changed |

Udayagiri-Khandagiri and the Hathigumpha inscription illuminate Kalinga patronage, though royal inscription remains self-representational. Karnataka, Tamil regions, Gujarat and Rajasthan later became important through courts, ascetics, merchants, householders, pilgrimage centres and literature in Prakrit, Sanskrit, Kannada and other languages.

Jain endurance was not the result of one merchant class or of never changing. Distributed lay networks, graded vows, regional patronage, textual labour, temples, pilgrimage and reform allowed adaptation while the ascetic ideal remained authoritative.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Jainism survived in India because it avoided large institutions and politics.

**Reply:** Jain communities built monuments, cultivated courts and depended on wealth and organisation. Their distribution differed from some late Buddhist mahaviharas, but “small and apolitical” is false. Continuity combined regional institutions with household participation.

**Reply:** Recurrent structure across early traditions, identifiable places, rival references and early imperial recognition support a historical teacher and movement. Source criticism narrows claims; it does not require deleting the person.

The Four Noble Truths are not four disconnected beliefs. They form diagnosis, cause, possibility of cure and treatment.

### 1. Dukkha is wider than pain

*Dukkha* includes obvious suffering, distress caused by change, and the instability of conditioned existence when grasped as permanent or fully controllable. Buddhism does not say every moment feels painful. It says conditioned things cannot provide secure satisfaction under craving.

The origin is commonly expressed through craving (*tanha/trishna*) intertwined with ignorance. |---|---|---|
| Right view | Right speech | Right effort |
| Right intention | Right action | Right mindfulness |

### 3. Middle path is trained transformation

The path avoids self-indulgence and self-mortification but remains demanding. Ethical livelihood reduces harm; mindfulness exposes changing processes; concentration stabilises attention; view understands causation. Liberation is not purchased by ritual or granted by birth.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The first truth makes Buddhism pessimistic.

**Reply:** A diagnosis is not pessimism if it identifies a cause, cessation and workable path. The framework is therapeutic: suffering is neither denied nor treated as eternal fate.

## Lesson 10 - Dependent Origination, Impermanence and No-Self

Progress: 10/15 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Conditionality, aggregates, karma and continuity

### Visual: a flame passed from lamp to lamp

```text
conditions at moment 1 --> conditions at moment 2 --> conditions at moment 3
       neither identical throughout nor wholly disconnected

ignorance conditions formations -> consciousness ... -> craving -> grasping
-> becoming -> birth -> ageing-and-death

remove sustaining conditions -> the process can cease
```

Continuity does not require an unchanging substance, just as a flame continues causally without one permanent particle travelling unchanged.

### 1. Dependent origination

**Pratityasamutpada/paticcasamuppada** means conditioned arising: when relevant conditions are present, a phenomenon arises; when they cease, it ceases. The familiar twelve-link formulation analyses the rebirth-and-suffering process. It is not a first creation story and not fatalism. Because causes are conditioned, intervention is possible.

### 2. Anicca and anatta

**Anicca/anitya** means impermanence. Bodies, feelings, perceptions, formations and consciousness change. These five **aggregates** (*skandhas/khandhas*) provide an analytical account of the person.

**Anatta/anatman** means no permanent, independent and controlling self can be identified in or beyond these conditioned aggregates. It does not mean persons do not exist conventionally, actions have no consequences, or nothing exists.

### 3. Karma without a permanent carrier

Buddhist karma centres on **intention** (*cetana*) and ethically charged action. A causal stream connects action and result without an unchanged soul moving between bodies. This differs from:

| Jainism | Buddhism |
|---|---|
| Enduring plural jiva | No permanent independent self |
| Karma as subtle material bondage | Karma centred on intention and causal consequence |
| Samvara and nirjara | Cessation through ending ignorance and craving |
| Purified jiva remains perfected | No-self analysis avoids eternalist substance |

The two traditions share responsibility and rebirth while explaining continuity differently.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Without a permanent self, the person who receives karmic results is unrelated to the actor.

**Reply:** Buddhist analysis rejects both identity and total difference. Later states arise dependently from earlier ones. The causal stream is continuous enough for responsibility without requiring an immutable owner.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** anicca, anatta, dependent origination and intentional karma.
- **Mains:** use the “neither identical nor wholly different” continuity formula.
- **Trap:** dependent origination is not predestination; anatta is not moral nihilism.
- **Next link:** Doctrine becomes a social institution through Vinaya, ordination, donors and gendered communities.

### Mini recap

1. Dependent origination means conditioned arising and cessation.
2. It is neither a first cause nor fate.
3. Anicca means impermanence.
4. Five aggregates analyse the person.
5. Anatta denies a permanent independent self.
6. Conventional persons and ethical effects remain.
7. Buddhist karma emphasises intention.
8. Jain and Buddhist rebirth models must not be merged.

### Visual: do not draw one universal council line


“Fourth council” has no safe meaning until the tradition and region are named.

### 1. First and second council memories

The First Council is conventionally placed at **Rajagriha** after the Buddha's parinirvana, with Ananda associated with discourses and Upali with discipline. The Second is placed at **Vaishali** and linked to disputed monastic practices. These traditions express concern for correct recitation and discipline, but details differ among schools and were written down later.

### 2. Third and fourth are labels with owners

Theravada chronicles associate a Third Council at Pataliputra under Ashoka with Moggaliputta Tissa and purification of the sangha. That is a Theravada historical memory, not a neutral inscriptional event-list.

Two different traditions are commonly called “Fourth Council”:

- A northern Sarvastivada-related council associated with Kanishka and Kashmir.
- The writing down of the Pali canon at Aluvihara in Sri Lankan Theravada memory.

Conflating them destroys geography, school and chronology.

### 3. Canon is plural

The Theravada Pali **Tipitaka** contains Vinaya, Sutta and Abhidhamma baskets. Other Buddhist schools preserved their own Vinayas, Agamas and Abhidharma traditions, sometimes surviving in Sanskrit fragments, Chinese or Tibetan translation. Chinese translation date is not original composition date, but translation may preserve an Indic work otherwise lost.

The canon was not “written by Buddha.” Oral recitation, school formation, compilation, translation and manuscript preservation are successive processes.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If council accounts conflict, the councils are fabricated and irrelevant.

**Reply:** Conflicting memories may prevent a single literal reconstruction, yet they reveal what communities needed to legitimate: discipline, textual custody, orthodoxy and lineage. Disagreement itself is institutional evidence.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Rajagriha, Vaishali, Pataliputra, Kashmir and Aluvihara.
- **Mains:** always prefix “according to Theravada tradition” or the relevant school.
- **Trap:** Theravada canon is not the only Buddhist canon.
- **Next link:** Canon plurality prepares the map of early schools and gradual Mahayana emergence.

1. Rajagriha is linked to first-council recitation.
2. Vaishali is linked to disciplinary dispute.
3. Third-council detail is especially Theravada memory.
4. “Fourth council” is ambiguous.
5. Kanishka-Kashmir and Aluvihara are distinct traditions.
6. Tipitaka has three baskets.
7. Other schools preserved different canons and Vinayas.
8. Contradiction can reveal institutional competition.

Branches overlap in time. A school label is not a sealed box.

### 1. Terms UPSC likes to cross-wire

**Theravada** is a surviving Pali lineage derived from Sthavira traditions; it is not identical with every ancient Sthavira school. **Mahasanghika** names a broad early lineage; **Lokottaravada** belongs within its family and emphasised the supramundane Buddha. **Sarvastivada** became influential in north-west India, Kashmir and Central Asia.

The official 2024 Prelims Q64 asks about **Sanghabhuti**, whose commentary concerns the Sarvastivada Vinaya; official Set-A key: C. Do not confuse him with **Sanghabhadra**, a different Vaibhashika scholar.

Dhanyakataka in the Andhra region is associated with Mahasanghikas. This is the direct core of 2023 Prelims Q41, whose official local key was not available in the audited set.

### 2. Mahayana emerged gradually

Mahayana did not begin at one council or replace an earlier “Hinayana” church overnight. Diverse sutras, Bodhisattva practices, cults, scholastic arguments and communities appeared over time around the early centuries BCE/CE.

**Maitreya** is the future Buddha and is recognised beyond Mahayana, although Mahayana also venerates him as a Bodhisattva in Tushita. **Paramitas** are perfections cultivated on the Bodhisattva path; a common six are generosity, ethical discipline, patience, energy, meditation and wisdom, with some lists expanding to ten.

“Hinayana” is a later polemical label and should not be used as a neutral synonym for Theravada.

### 3. Vajrayana is later

Vajrayana developed in later first-millennium Mahayana environments through mantras, mandalas, initiations, deity practices and tantric texts. It must not be projected into the Buddha's lifetime or the earliest councils. Detailed philosophical systems belong to Philosophy Optional; here the issue is historical sequence.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Mahayana's new texts and Bodhisattvas show a clean break from early Buddhism.

**Reply:** Innovation was real, yet it worked with inherited karma, rebirth, Buddhahood, monastic institutions and earlier narrative worlds. Gradual emergence explains continuity, contest and regional plurality better than either complete identity or one dramatic schism.

- **Trap:** Theravada is not a synonym for “Hinayana.”
- **Next link:** Schools travelled because institutions, routes, donors, rulers and translators worked together.

### 1. Comparison by dimensions

| Person | Plural enduring jivas | Conditioned aggregates, anatta |

| Liberation | Samvara, nirjara, moksha | Cessation, nirvana |

Nalanda and Vikramashila suffered political violence, but this late factor cannot explain earlier regional contraction. Jainism retained a dense ascetic-lay relationship, strong regional communities, courts, pilgrimage centres, manuscript cultures and vernacular literature. Both traditions expanded ethical debate about intention, non-harm, renunciation and spiritual worth; created monastic and lay institutions; generated textual and educational networks; and shaped patronage and sacred geographies. Detailed architecture and iconography belong to Art and Culture. Modern constitutional equality should not be projected backwards: these communities widened access yet retained gender, rank and social accommodation.

**Objection:** Since Buddhism declined in much of India while Jainism persisted, Jain organisation was simply superior.

**Reply:** Outcomes followed different geographies, scales, patrons and transregional opportunities. Buddhist institutions also achieved extraordinary Asian durability. Comparison should explain configurations, not rank religions by one territorial endpoint.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10-mark Question 1

**Explain why the rise of Jainism and Buddhism cannot be reduced to urbanisation or varna protest alone. Answer in 150 words.**

### Model answer

The expansion of towns, agrarian production, routes and states in the middle Gangetic region created mobile audiences and resources for renunciant teachers. Traders and householders could support monasteries, while warfare, hierarchy and costly ritual sharpened ethical questions. These changes explain opportunity, not doctrinal inevitability.

The same setting produced sharply different positions. Jainism affirmed enduring jivas, material karmic bondage and rigorous restraint. Buddhism taught conditioned arising, no permanent self and a Middle Way. Ajivikas defended determinism, while materialists challenged rebirth. Such diversity cannot be derived from one economic interest.

Nor were these movements simply modern social revolutions. They questioned exclusive sacrificial authority and widened access to liberation, but retained monastic rank, gender asymmetry and accommodation with rulers and household society. Their rise is best explained through the interaction of material change, intellectual debate, renunciant lineages, ethical anxiety and institutional innovation. Context widened the field; doctrine and community shaped the distinct outcomes.

## 10-mark Question 2

**Show how Jain doctrine connects ontology, ethics and liberation. Answer in 150 words.**

### Model answer

Jain ethics follows from its account of reality. Every conscious *jiva* is enduring and capable of perfected knowledge, but activity driven by passion causes subtle karmic matter to enter (*asrava*) and bind it (*bandha*). Because life extends to earth-, water-, fire-, air- and plant-bodied beings, injury has an unusually wide field.

The five vows operationalise this diagnosis: ahimsa limits injury; satya and asteya restrain harmful deception and appropriation; brahmacharya controls sensual attachment; aparigraha limits possession and grasping. Ascetics undertake these as *mahavratas*, while householders follow graded *anuvratas*, making a durable fourfold community possible.

Right faith, right knowledge and right conduct form the Triratna. Restraint produces *samvara*, stopping new influx; austerity and disciplined conduct enable *nirjara*, shedding accumulated karma. Moksha is therefore not divine reward or annihilation, but liberation of the purified jiva. Ontology explains bondage, ethics interrupts its causes, and practice completes release.

## 15-mark Question 1

**Compare Jain and Buddhist conceptions of self, karma and liberation. Answer in 250 words.**

### Model answer

Jainism and Buddhism share the shramana vocabulary of karma, rebirth, renunciation and liberation, but their causal structures differ fundamentally.

Jainism affirms innumerable enduring conscious substances or *jivas*. Their intrinsic knowledge and energy are obscured when activity and passions draw in subtle karmic matter. *Asrava* produces *bandha*; restraint brings *samvara*; austerity and discipline cause *nirjara*. Moksha is the perfected jiva freed from karmic covering. The Triratna and five vows, especially ahimsa and aparigraha, follow from this ontology.

Buddhism analyses the person through changing aggregates and teaches *anatta*: no permanent, independent and controlling self can be found. This is not denial of conventional persons or moral consequence. Intentional action creates effects within a dependent causal continuum that is neither wholly identical nor disconnected across moments and lives. Ignorance and craving sustain *dukkha*; their cessation is nirvana. The Eightfold Path integrates wisdom, ethical conduct and mental discipline.

Thus Jain karma is materially conceived bondage to an enduring soul, whereas Buddhist karma centres on intention without an eternal carrier. Jain practice tends toward rigorous stopping and shedding; Buddhist practice follows a Middle Way between indulgence and disabling mortification.

Yet the contrast should not become caricature. Both traditions contain disciplined monasticism, lay ethics, compassion, ritual and later devotional forms. Their shared historical setting explains common questions; their different accounts of person and causation explain different paths.

## 15-mark Question 2

**Examine how council and canon traditions illuminate, but also complicate, the history of Jainism and Buddhism. Answer in 250 words.**

### Model answer

Council and canon traditions reveal how religious communities remembered authority, managed discipline and defended textual continuity. They are therefore institutional evidence, but not neutral transcripts.

In Jain history, Shvetambara narratives associate preservation efforts with Pataliputra and later redaction of the agamas at Valabhi. Digambaras maintain that the original canon was lost and reject the claim that the Shvetambara collection is intact. These rival positions illuminate migration memories, recitational anxiety and sectarian authority. They do not permit one universally accepted canon chronology.

Buddhist traditions conventionally place a first recitation at Rajagriha and a disciplinary dispute at Vaishali. Theravada chronicles connect a third council at Pataliputra with Ashoka and Moggaliputta Tissa. “Fourth council” is especially ambiguous: northern tradition links Kanishka and Kashmir with Sarvastivada scholarship, while Sri Lankan memory associates Aluvihara with writing down the Pali canon.

Canon is consequently plural. Theravada preserves the Pali Tipitaka, while other schools transmitted Vinayas, Agamas and Abhidharma works through Sanskrit fragments and Chinese or Tibetan translations. A surviving translation dates reception, not necessarily original composition.

Conflicting accounts do not make councils historically useless. They reveal struggles over recitation, discipline, orthodoxy and lineage. A strong reconstruction therefore identifies the transmitting school, separates event from later legitimation, and cross-checks texts against inscriptions, manuscripts and archaeology.

## 20-mark Question 1

**Analyse the role of institutions, patronage and material culture in the expansion of Jainism and Buddhism. Answer in 250-300 words.**

### Model answer

Jainism and Buddhism became durable not through doctrine alone but through institutions that connected renunciants, householders, patrons and places.

Both developed organised ascetic communities supported by lay followers. Jain monks and nuns embodied rigorous vows, while laymen and laywomen adopted graded restraints, donated resources and sustained regional continuity. Buddhist Vinayas regulated monks and nuns; upasakas and upasikas provided food, robes, medicine and residences in exchange for teaching and merit. Rainy-season residence encouraged permanent establishments.

Patronage was multilevel. Ashoka's inscriptions and sacred-site actions show major Buddhist support, although his dhamma was broader than Buddhism. Kanishka and later rulers supported northern institutions, while merchants, artisans, women and local groups appear in donor records. Jain communities likewise received support from Kalinga, Karnataka and western Indian courts, but cannot be reduced to royal or merchant sponsorship.

Material culture made networks visible. Stupas organised relic veneration and pilgrimage; caves and viharas provided residence and ritual space; inscriptions named donors and communities. Kankali Tila and Udayagiri-Khandagiri illuminate Jain patronage, while Sanchi, the lower Krishna centres and north-western monasteries reveal Buddhist regionality. Such sites must be dated phase by phase and do not by themselves prove every textual biography.

Buddhist expansion additionally used land and maritime routes, translation and adaptable monastic institutions to reach Sri Lanka, Central, East and Southeast Asia. Jainism formed denser India-centred regional and literary networks.

Therefore expansion was a system: doctrine attracted, institutions organised, patronage resourced, material sites anchored memory, and routes enabled movement. No one emperor, class or monument explains the outcome.

## 20-mark Question 2

**Why did Buddhism contract in much of India while Jainism retained durable regional communities? Give a multi-causal and regionally sensitive answer. Answer in 250-300 words.**

### Model answer

The contrast is real but cannot be explained by one invasion, one doctrinal change or a claim that one religion was inherently more practical.

Buddhist history in India was regionally uneven. Patronage shifted as dynasties and local elites supported changing religious institutions. Brahmanical and bhakti traditions expanded temples, pilgrimage and monastic forms while also absorbing Buddhist ethical and iconographic elements. Some large mahaviharas depended on concentrated landed patronage and scholarly networks; this produced intellectual strength but vulnerability when routes, revenues or political protection changed. Mahayana and Vajrayana were transformations, not simply decline. Warfare and attacks damaged centres such as Nalanda and Vikramashila, but these late events cannot explain centuries of earlier contraction. Buddhism persisted in Himalayan and eastern regions and flourished across Asia.

Jain continuity also involved change. A fourfold community joined ascetic authority to household support. Graded vows allowed participation without full renunciation. Regional courts, merchant and artisanal groups, pilgrimage centres, manuscript cultures and literature in Prakrit, Sanskrit, Kannada and other languages embedded Jain communities in western and southern India. Temples, image traditions and later non-image reforms show adaptation rather than stasis.

The comparison must be qualified. Buddhism's trans-Asian success is also continuity, while Jainism declined in some regions. Concentrated monasteries versus distributed lay networks is a useful contrast, not a universal law.

The outcome arose from changing patronage, institutional scale, regional economy, competition and accommodation, political violence, literary adaptation and geography. “Contraction,” “transformation” and “regional endurance” are more accurate than a single civilisational rise-and-fall story.

# REMEDIATION

## Diagnostic 1 - Founder simplification

**Misconception:** Mahavira founded Jainism from nothing.

**Repair:** Place him after Parshvanatha within the twenty-four-Tirthankara tradition. Use “decisive historical teacher and organiser of an older lineage.”

## Diagnostic 2 - Shared vocabulary collapse

**Misconception:** Because both teach karma and rebirth, their theories are identical.

```text
Jain: enduring jiva + material karma + samvara/nirjara
Buddhist: anatta + intentional karma + dependent cessation
```

## Diagnostic 3 - Anekantavada as indecision

**Misconception:** Jain many-sidedness says every belief is equally true.

**Repair:** Separate object complexity, partial standpoint and conditioned assertion. Ask “in what respect?” before accepting an apparent contradiction.

## Diagnostic 4 - Buddhism as pessimism

**Misconception:** Buddhism teaches only that life is misery.

**Repair:** Complete the physician structure: dukkha, causal origin, possible cessation and path. Define dukkha as conditioned unsatisfactoriness, not constant felt pain.

## Diagnostic 5 - Anatta as nothingness

**Misconception:** No permanent self means no person, responsibility or rebirth.

**Repair:** Use causal continuity without immutable identity. Reject both “exactly the same eternal entity” and “totally unrelated entity.”

## Diagnostic 6 - Council memorisation without ownership

**Misconception:** Four universally accepted Buddhist councils fixed one universal canon.

**Repair:** Attach place, purpose and transmitting tradition. Keep Kanishka-Kashmir and Aluvihara as different fourth-council memories.

## Diagnostic 7 - Ashoka-only spread

**Misconception:** Ashoka single-handedly converted Asia.

**Repair:** Add monastics, merchants, translators, local rulers, donors, pilgrimage and land and sea routes. Separate edicts from chronicles.

## Diagnostic 8 - One-cause decline

**Misconception:** Buddhism disappeared when Nalanda was destroyed.

**Repair:** Build a chronology of patronage, routes, institutional form, competition/absorption, internal transformation and late violence.

## Diagnostic 9 - Automatic equality

**Misconception:** Admission beyond hereditary elites abolished caste and patriarchy.

**Repair:** Distinguish spiritual access, institutional hierarchy and wider social structure. Replace “abolished” with challenged, widened or qualified.

## Diagnostic 10 - Art as universal code

**Misconception:** An empty seat always means one event.

**Repair:** Read wheel, tree, footprints, worshippers, inscription and narrative sequence together; keep detailed iconographic history with Art and Culture.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Register 1 - Source and chronology

- Buddhist and Jain teachings survive through later, school-specific transmission.
- Separate teacher horizon, oral recitation, compilation, redaction, manuscript and translation.
- Normative text reveals ideals and contested problems, not universal compliance.
- Inscriptions give firmer names, places and dates but remain selective.
- Archaeology proves institutional presence more securely than exact doctrine.
- Buddha and Mahavira belong broadly to the sixth-fifth centuries BCE; exact dates vary.
- Rival sources may corroborate a teacher while polemicising against doctrine.
- Modern relic exhibitions document current custody, not every ancient attribution.

## Register 2 - Context and Mahavira

- Shramana was a plural renunciant field; parivrajaka means wandering renunciant.
- Towns, routes, agrarian growth and states widened audiences without determining doctrine.
- Rishabhanatha: first Tirthankara in tradition.
- Parshvanatha: twenty-third and plausible earlier historical teacher.
- Mahavira/Vardhamana: twenty-fourth and decisive organiser.
- Nigantha Nataputta/Nayaputta is the Buddhist textual designation for Mahavira.
- Tirthankara means ford-maker.
- Exact biography and chronology require sectarian source labels.

## Register 3 - Jain ontology, path and knowledge

- Jiva is conscious, enduring and plural; ajiva includes matter, space, time and motion/rest media.
- Karma is subtle material bondage: asrava -> bandha -> samvara -> nirjara -> moksha.
- Four gatis: deva, manushya, naraki, tiryancha; yaksha is excluded.
- Triratna: right faith, right knowledge, right conduct.
- Five vows: ahimsa, satya, asteya, brahmacharya, aparigraha.
- Mahavratas are ascetic; anuvratas are graded for householders.
- Anekantavada = many-sided reality; nayavada = standpoints; syadvada = conditioned predication.
- Saptabhangi is mature systematisation, not automatically Mahavira's verbatim formulation.

## Register 4 - Jain community and endurance

- Fourfold community: monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen.
- Valabhi is associated with Shvetambara canon redaction.
- Digambaras maintain that the original canon was lost.
- Digambara-Shvetambara division was gradual and exceeds clothing.
- Sthanakavasi is a later non-image-worship Shvetambara reform.
- Kankali Tila shows Jain donors and images at Mathura.
- Udayagiri-Khandagiri and Hathigumpha illuminate Kalinga patronage.
- Regional courts, laity, pilgrimage and literature sustained continuity through change.

## Register 5 - Buddha and core path

- Siddhartha Gautama belonged to the Shakya setting.
- Shakyamuni and Tathagata refer to Buddha; Nayaputta to Mahavira.
- Lumbini-Bodh Gaya-Sarnath-Kushinagara forms the four-site life sequence.
- Lumbini pillar supports an early birthplace association.
- Middle Way rejects indulgence and disabling mortification.
- Four Truths: dukkha, origin, cessation, path.
- Eightfold Path: view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, concentration.
- Nirvana ends greed, hatred, delusion and conditioned rebirth; it is not annihilation.

## Register 6 - Conditionality and person

- Dependent origination means conditioned arising and cessation, not fate.
- Anicca means impermanence.
- Five aggregates: form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.
- Anatta denies a permanent independent self.
- Conventional personhood and ethical consequence remain.
- Buddhist karma centres on intention.
- Causal continuity is neither eternal identity nor total disconnection.
- Jain material karma and Buddhist intentional karma are not interchangeable.

## Register 7 - Sangha, society and gender

- Bhikkhu/bhikkhuni are ordained monk/nun; upasaka/upasika are layman/laywoman.
- Vinaya regulates ordination, possessions, residence and disputes.
- Different schools preserved different Vinayas.
- Mahapajapati Gotami anchors the nuns' order tradition.
- Therigatha preserves verses attributed to early nuns through later transmission.
- Garudhamma narratives are layered and debated.
- Buddhism challenged birth superiority and widened access.
- It did not instantly abolish caste, patriarchy or institutional hierarchy.

## Register 8 - Councils, schools and later forms

- First council: Rajagriha; second: Vaishali.
- Theravada third: Pataliputra, Ashoka and Moggaliputta Tissa.
- Northern fourth and Sri Lankan fourth are distinct memories.
- Theravada Tipitaka contains Vinaya, Sutta and Abhidhamma.
- Theravada is Sthavira-derived; Lokottaravada is Mahasanghika-linked.
- Sarvastivada mattered in north-western networks; Sanghabhuti wrote on its Vinaya.
- Mahayana emerged gradually; Maitreya is not exclusively Mahayana.
- Paramitas are Bodhisattva perfections.
- “Hinayana” is polemical; Vajrayana is later.

## Register 9 - Patronage, spread and change

- Ashoka was a major patron; dhamma was broader than Buddhism.
- Edicts, chronicles and pilgrim accounts must not be merged.
- Rulers, merchants, artisans, women and monastics all appear in patronage histories.
- Land, maritime and translation networks carried Buddhism.
- Buddhist contraction was gradual, regional and multicausal.
- Late attacks damaged institutions but do not explain the whole process.
- Jain continuity used ascetic-lay, regional, literary and pilgrimage networks.
- Neither religion should be reduced to merchant revolt or modern equality.

- 2019: Mahayana textbook features require historical qualification.
- 2020: Lokottaravada-Mahasanghika; paramitas-perfections; parivrajaka/upasaka terms.
- 2022: Parishishtaparvan and Trishashtilakshana Mahapurana are Jain.
