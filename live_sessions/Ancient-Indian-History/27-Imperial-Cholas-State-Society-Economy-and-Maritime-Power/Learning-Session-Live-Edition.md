## Lesson 1 - Which Cholas Are We Studying, and How Can We Know?
Progress: 1 / 16  |  Stage: Foundation  |  Subtopic: Scope, chronology, sources and boundaries

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Upinder Singh PDF pp. 1482-1493, 1565-1572 and 1742-1746 were locally read; the available R.S. Sharma ancient-history volumes do not provide an Imperial Chola chapter.
- **CA search:** `"site:pib.gov.in OR site:asi.nic.in OR site:culture.gov.in Chola temple bronze inscription March 2026 September 2026"`
- **CA found:** the May 2026 PIB repatriation report on Chola- and Vijayanagara-period temple bronzes is relevant to provenance and heritage ethics, not to reconstructing medieval state power.

### Visual first - keep names, periods and evidence apart

```text
SANGAM-AGE CHOLAS                IMPERIAL CHOLAS
early Tamilakam                  c. mid-9th to 13th century
Topic 18 owner                   Topic 27 owner
        \                           /
         same dynastic name, not one uninterrupted bureaucracy

RECONSTRUCTION:
inscription + archaeology + monument + foreign account + later text
                         -> comparison -> qualified claim
```

*A dynastic name can endure while the political formation, evidence and institutions change.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Imagine finding a royal inscription that says a king "conquered the world." The statement proves that the court wanted to publicise universal victory. It does not, by itself, draw the boundaries of actual administration. Chola history is unusually rich in inscriptions, but richness of record is not neutrality of record.

### 2. The chronological frame

[FACT] Vijayalaya established the later imperial line in the ninth century. Rajaraja I and Rajendra I mark its strongest expansion in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries. Later Cholas remained significant before losing imperial primacy in the thirteenth century.

[LIMIT] The early historical Cholas of Sangam literature cannot be joined to Vijayalaya's line as though one central government operated continuously across the intervening centuries.

### 3. Evidence classes and their limits

| Evidence | Strongest use | Hard limit |
|---|---|---|
| Royal inscription or *meikeerthi* | regnal sequence, titles, royal orders and claimed campaigns | eulogy is not a neutral territorial map |
| Temple inscription | endowments, personnel, dues, transactions and local bodies | record-rich temples overrepresent landed institutions |
| Copper plate | genealogy, grants and diplomatic-religious patronage | formula and legitimating ancestry need corroboration |
| Monument or bronze | patronage, labour, ritual use and craft capacity | artistic survival does not measure general prosperity |
| Sri Lankan or Chinese record | external view of campaigns, embassies and trade | genre, agenda and distance shape the account |
| Later Tamil literature | devotional memory, court culture and social ideals | composition date differs from the event remembered |

### 4. The central thesis

[INFERENCE] The Imperial Cholas were a Kaveri-centred early-medieval empire. Their power combined coercive kingship, agrarian surplus, surveys and dues, local corporate institutions, temple-centred integration, merchant networks and selective maritime force. None of these alone explains the whole formation.

### 5. Ownership firewall

- Topic 18: Sangam Cholas and early Tamilakam.
- Topic 23: Pallava and Chalukya trajectories before and alongside Chola rise.
- Topic 25: broad India-Asia cultural mechanisms and regional agency.
- Topic 26: subcontinent-wide transition and regionalisation debates.
- Art and Culture: formal Dravida vocabulary, sculptural iconography and bronze technique.
- Topic 27: Chola chronology, polity, society, economy, maritime power and historical patronage context.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Chola inscriptions are so numerous that a detailed administrative reconstruction is straightforward.

**Reply:** Quantity permits finer reconstruction, but the records cluster around royal, temple, landed and corporate interests. The under-recorded lives of labourers, mobile groups and dissenting communities require explicit caution.

### 7. UPSC application

Use this opening:

> The Imperial Cholas were not the Sangam polity continued unchanged, but a source-rich Kaveri-centred early-medieval formation whose inscriptions reveal both impressive state capacity and the unequal local institutions through which it operated.

### Mini recap

```text
Period -> Imperial Cholas begin with Vijayalaya, not Sangam continuity.
Evidence -> inscriptions are powerful but purpose-built.
Method -> compare source classes before mapping control.
Verdict -> source abundance supports precision, not certainty without limits.
```

### Revision notes

1. Distinguish Sangam-age and Imperial Cholas.
2. Date the Imperial phase broadly from the mid-ninth to thirteenth century.
3. Read conquest lists as claims requiring outcome checks.
4. Temple inscriptions illuminate institutions but privilege record-making elites.
5. Foreign accounts are corroborative, not automatically superior.
6. Monumental scale cannot stand for universal welfare.
7. Topic 27 owns the Chola combination, not every southern antecedent.
8. The best thesis links delta, court, locality, temple and sea.

## Lesson 2 - How Did a Regional Line Survive Expansion and Setback?
Progress: 2 / 16  |  Stage: Foundation  |  Subtopic: Vijayalaya to the recovery after Takkolam

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Upinder Singh PDF pp. 1482-1483 and the canonical chronology spine were read.
- **CA search:** the official Chola heritage search from Lesson 1 supplies no new political chronology.
- **CA found:** no current source changes the ruler sequence.

### Visual first - foundation was not a straight ascent

```text
Vijayalaya
Tanjavur foothold
      |
Aditya I -> Pallava eclipse -> Tondaimandalam
      |
Parantaka I -> Pandya expansion
      |
Takkolam, 949 -> Rashtrakuta setback
      |
Parantaka II / Sundara Chola + Uttama
      |
recovery platform for Rajaraja I
```

*Imperial power emerged through foundation, expansion, reversal and recovery.*

### 1. Vijayalaya and the Kaveri foothold

[FACT] Vijayalaya captured Tanjavur from the Muttaraiyar chiefs and established the territorial base of the imperial line in the lower Kaveri region.

[LIMIT] "Founder" identifies the later imperial line; it does not mean he created every institution later visible under Rajaraja I.

### 2. Aditya I and northern expansion

[FACT] Aditya I defeated the Pallava ruler Aparajita and extended Chola authority into Tondaimandalam. This mattered because the Cholas inherited and transformed a political, agrarian and temple landscape already shaped by the Pallavas.

### 3. Parantaka I and Takkolam

[FACT] Parantaka I expanded into Pandya country, but the Rashtrakuta victory at Takkolam in 949 severely checked Chola power. The defeat prevents a triumphalist chronology: the high empire was built after a major reversal.

### 4. Recovery

[FACT] The reigns associated with Parantaka II, also called Sundara Chola, and Uttama form a recovery bridge. Succession was not a frictionless ladder, but the political base survived long enough for Rajaraja I to consolidate it.

### 5. Why chronology is analytical

```text
setback tests institutions
-> survival preserves core resources and alliances
-> later ruler can reorganise and expand
```

[INFERENCE] Takkolam shows that the Kaveri core and dynastic network could outlast defeat. The Chola peak was not inevitable; it resulted from recovery and reorganisation.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Since Rajaraja I created the high empire, earlier rulers are minor prelims facts.

**Reply:** Without Vijayalaya's foothold, Aditya's northern expansion, Parantaka's southern ambition and the recovery after Takkolam, Rajaraja's consolidation appears causeless. The sequence explains resilience.

### 7. UPSC application

Periodise instead of listing:

> foundation under Vijayalaya -> northern and southern expansion -> Takkolam setback -> recovery -> imperial consolidation.

### Mini recap

```text
Base: Vijayalaya secures Tanjavur.
Expansion: Aditya I and Parantaka I widen the field.
Stress: Takkolam breaks linear-growth narratives.
Outcome: recovery makes Rajaraja's empire historically explicable.
```

### Revision notes

1. Vijayalaya is the founder of the later imperial line.
2. Aditya I is linked to the defeat of Pallava Aparajita.
3. Parantaka I expanded southward.
4. Takkolam in 949 was a major Rashtrakuta victory.
5. Recovery precedes Rajaraja I.
6. Tondaimandalam was inherited and transformed, not created from nothing.
7. Chronology should explain institutional resilience.
8. Avoid an uninterrupted victory narrative.

## Lesson 3 - What Made Rajaraja I and Rajendra I an Imperial Pair?
Progress: 3 / 16  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Consolidation, expansion, survey and imperial symbolism

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Upinder Singh PDF pp. 1482-1490 and 1570-1572 were locally read.
- **CA search:** UNESCO's official Great Living Chola Temples page was checked for monument-ruler links.
- **CA found:** living heritage status supports present significance, not an inference of uniform medieval prosperity.

### Visual first - two rulers, connected but different emphases

```text
RAJARAJA I (985-1014)
consolidation + survey + Sri Lanka + Brihadisvara
                    |
                    v
RAJENDRA I (1012/14-1044)
continued expansion + Ganga expedition + new capital + Srivijaya strike
```

*Rajaraja deepened the imperial platform; Rajendra extended and monumentalised it.*

### 1. Rajaraja I

[FACT] Rajaraja I expanded against Pandya and Kerala powers, established substantial Chola control in northern Sri Lanka, associated the state with a major land survey and built Brihadisvara at Tanjavur.

[INFERENCE] Survey and monument should be read together: one made resources more legible; the other made kingship publicly visible.

### 2. Rajendra I

[FACT] Rajendra I continued intervention in Sri Lanka and the Deccan, led the Ganga expedition, founded Gangaikondacholapuram and launched the c. 1025 campaign against Srivijaya-linked centres including Kadaram.

[LIMIT] Carrying Ganga water and adopting the Gangaikonda title converted victory into ritual-political memory. It did not create permanent Chola provinces across the march route.

### 3. The new capital

Gangaikondacholapuram linked:

```text
military claim -> sacred water -> royal title -> capital -> temple
```

It was not merely an administrative relocation. It made conquest part of dynastic landscape and ritual memory.

### 4. Later succession

[FACT] Rajadhiraja I, Rajendra II and Virarajendra continued warfare with Western Chalukyas and in Sri Lanka. Rajadhiraja's death at Koppam did not instantly end the polity. Kulottunga I later represented both Chola continuity and an Eastern Chalukya dynastic connection.

### 5. Achievement with limits

| Claim | Evidence | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| high state capacity | survey, orders, campaigns, officials | implementation still used local intermediaries |
| territorial expansion | Tondaimandalam, Pandya zones, northern Sri Lanka | intensity and durability varied |
| symbolic empire | Brihadisvara and Gangaikondacholapuram | monument is not a welfare census |
| maritime reach | Sri Lanka and Srivijaya expedition | permanent oceanic empire is unproven |

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Pairing the rulers hides Rajendra's independent achievements.

**Reply:** The pair is a causal device, not a denial of individuality. Rajendra inherited revenue, military and ideological resources, then redirected them through a new capital, Ganga claim and Southeast Asian expedition.

### 7. UPSC application

Use the pair as a transition:

> Rajaraja I made the Chola core more territorially, fiscally and monumentally legible; Rajendra I projected that consolidated capacity into new symbolic and maritime theatres.

### Mini recap

```text
Rajaraja: consolidate, survey, build, occupy.
Rajendra: extend, commemorate, relocate, strike overseas.
Continuity: both depend on the Kaveri-centred platform.
Limit: campaign geography is not automatically administrative geography.
```

### Revision notes

1. Rajaraja I: 985-1014.
2. Rajendra I: 1012/14-1044.
3. Brihadisvara at Tanjavur is linked to Rajaraja I.
4. Gangaikondacholapuram is linked to Rajendra I.
5. Ganga campaign had military and symbolic dimensions.
6. Srivijaya campaign is commonly dated c. 1025.
7. Kadaram is a named target.
8. Rajadhiraja's death did not end the dynasty.
9. Kulottunga I marks connection and consolidation.
10. Avoid converting targets into permanent provinces.

## Lesson 4 - Was the Chola Realm One Solid Territorial Block?
Progress: 4 / 16  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Political geography, Vengi and contested frontiers

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Upinder Singh PDF pp. 1483-1488 and the canonical political-geography dossier were read.
- **CA search:** no official current source changes the medieval territorial reconstruction.
- **CA found:** UNESCO evidence concerns three temple properties, not imperial boundaries.

### Visual first - zones of decreasing and changing control

```text
CORE
lower Kaveri, Tanjavur, Gangaikondacholapuram
        |
INTEGRATED / ANNEXED ZONES
Tondaimandalam and changing southern districts
        |
CONTESTED FRONTIERS
Pandya-Chera country, Western Chalukya borderlands
        |
DYNASTIC HINGE
Vengi and Eastern Chalukya marriage/succession
        |
CAMPAIGN / MARITIME REACH
Sri Lankan zones, Maldives, Ganga route, Srivijaya-linked targets
```

*Different zones require different verbs: rule, annex, contest, influence, raid or commemorate.*

### 1. Core and frontier

[FACT] The lower Kaveri delta was the political-fiscal core. Tondaimandalam was an important northern region gained after Pallava decline. Pandya, Kerala and Western Chalukya zones repeatedly shifted between campaign, tribute, occupation and resistance.

### 2. Vengi

[FACT] Vengi was a dynastic and strategic hinge because Eastern Chalukya succession and marriage connections intersected with Chola-Western Chalukya rivalry. It should not be reduced to a passive buffer.

### 3. Sri Lanka

[FACT] Chola occupation was stronger and more durable in the northern Rajarata zone than across the whole island. Resistance and eventual expulsion demonstrate the difference between landing, annexing and sustaining rule.

### 4. Map discipline

- Shade the Kaveri core most strongly.
- Use a different pattern for contested Pandya-Chera and Deccan frontiers.
- Show Vengi as a dynastic-strategic zone.
- Mark Sri Lanka in phases.
- Draw arrows, not provinces, for the Ganga and Srivijaya campaigns.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Graduated maps minimise genuine Chola imperial ambition.

**Reply:** Differentiation makes ambition more intelligible. It shows where the state extracted regularly, where it bargained through local institutions and where it could only strike or demand recognition.

### 6. UPSC application

Replace "the Chola empire stretched from the Ganga to Southeast Asia" with:

> Chola power radiated unevenly from the Kaveri core through annexed, contested, dynastic and expeditionary zones.

### Mini recap

```text
Core: direct fiscal and political density.
Frontier: repeated war and negotiated control.
Hinge: Vengi links dynasty and Deccan strategy.
Limit: arrows of campaign must not become solid provinces.
```

### Revision notes

1. Use graded territorial categories.
2. Kaveri is the core.
3. Tondaimandalam is a major integrated northern zone.
4. Pandya and Kerala regions remained contested.
5. Western Chalukya rivalry shaped the Deccan frontier.
6. Vengi connected marriage, succession and warfare.
7. Northern Sri Lanka was more durable than southern control.
8. The Ganga and Srivijaya events are expeditionary arcs.

## Lesson 5 - Centralised Empire, Segmentary State or Negotiated Formation?
Progress: 5 / 16  |  Stage: Advanced  |  Subtopic: Historiographical models and their limits

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST
- **Book context:** Upinder Singh PDF pp. 1565-1570 and the advanced dossier were locally read.
- **CA search:** historiographical models are not altered by the current heritage window.
- **CA found:** not relevant to this interpretive dependency.

### Visual first - each model sees something and misses something

| Model | What it explains | What it risks |
|---|---|---|
| Imperial-centralised, associated with K.A. Nilakanta Sastri | conquest, officials, hierarchy, survey, revenue and monuments | projects a uniform bureaucracy and romanticises empire |
| Segmentary, associated with Burton Stein | ritual sovereignty, locality strength and dilution away from the core | understates coercion, extraction, offices and sustained mobilisation |
| Inscription-led locality, linked to Karashima and Subbarayalu | taxes, offices, landholding and regional change | archive remains socially and geographically selective |
| Integrative/network synthesis | court, nadu, assemblies, temples, chiefs and merchants interact | can become vague unless unequal power is ranked |

### 1. Why the debate arose

Older narratives praised a strong, efficient and centralised Chola monarchy while also celebrating self-governing villages. Stein identified the tension: how could a tightly bureaucratic empire simultaneously depend on autonomous local institutions?

### 2. Stein's intervention

[DEBATE] Stein emphasised sacred kingship, a segmentary state and strong local corporate space. Effective control was strongest near the political core and thinner toward the periphery.

[LIMIT] Upinder Singh notes that Stein's alternative went too far when it denied substantial bureaucracy, revenue machinery and standing military capacity. Sustained campaigns required more than ritual supremacy and episodic plunder.

### 3. Inscription-led correction

[FACT] Research associated with Karashima, Subbarayalu, Matsui, Shanmugan and Heitzman identified administrative titles, tax terms and stronger royal revenue intervention, especially from around Rajaraja I. Upinder records a hierarchy of land-revenue officials reaching villages more systematically after c. 1000.

### 4. Balanced verdict

```text
royal force and orders
        +
local collection and implementation
        +
temple and corporate mediation
        +
regional variation
= unevenly integrated imperial formation
```

The state was neither a modern unitary bureaucracy nor an absent centre above independent village republics. Capacity varied by reign, region, institution and distance from the Kaveri core.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** An integrative model avoids choosing and therefore explains nothing.

**Reply:** It must be made testable. Ask who issued the order, who measured land, who collected dues, who recorded the transaction and who could resist. Integration describes a chain of unequal action, not harmony.

### 6. UPSC application

In a critical answer:

1. Name Sastri's achievement and overstatement.
2. Explain Stein's corrective.
3. Rebut Stein with survey, tax and office evidence.
4. Conclude with variable but real state capacity.

### Mini recap

```text
Sastri: sees imperial capacity.
Stein: restores locality and ritual sovereignty.
Epigraphy: restores offices, taxes and intervention.
Verdict: integration was real, mediated, unequal and regionally variable.
```

### Revision notes

1. Historiography is an argument about the same evidence.
2. Centralisation explains mobilisation but may flatten locality.
3. Segmentary theory explains diluted power but can understate the state.
4. Ritual and coercive power are not mutually exclusive.
5. Corporate villages do not prove peasant control of the empire.
6. Post-c. 1000 revenue evidence matters.
7. Karashima and Subbarayalu refine through inscriptions.
8. Use an integrative model only with named mechanisms.

[FACT] Shaiva patronage was especially prominent, but Vaishnava, Jain and Buddhist institutions persisted unevenly. Nagapattinam is a major Buddhist anchor.

Tamil and Sanskrit had overlapping functions. Sanskrit could carry genealogy and eulogy; Tamil increasingly recorded orders, gifts and local transactions. Bilingualism was layered, not a simple replacement.

### Visual first - decline was a causal web

```text

 + Hoysala and other intervention

 + altered regional and maritime networks
                    |

```

### 1. Gradual contraction

[FACT] Later Cholas faced stronger Pandyas, Hoysala intervention, persistent western and southern conflict and changing local power. The dynasty's decline culminated in the thirteenth century.

[LIMIT] Do not explain decline through one weak ruler, one invasion or trade collapse.

### 2. What survived

Kaveri irrigation, temples, Tamil literary and devotional traditions, merchant experience, local institutions and artistic memory outlasted imperial sovereignty. Institutional afterlife is not political continuity.

### 3. Final synthesis

```text
Kaveri surplus
-> fiscal and local articulation
-> military and monumental capacity
-> commerce and selective maritime force
-> uneven expansion
-> pressure on a mediated system
-> contraction with enduring regional legacies
```

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A multicausal explanation is merely a list.

**Reply:** Rank the interaction. Repeated wars raised fiscal and military strain; succession and changing intermediary relations reduced coordination; rising regional rivals converted strain into territorial contraction.

### 5. UPSC answer engine

1. **Locate:** Kaveri-centred early-medieval empire.
2. **Argue:** real central initiative through unequal intermediaries.
3. **Evidence:** survey, Uttaramerur, Brihadisvara, Ainnurruvar, Sri Lanka, Srivijaya.
4. **Qualify:** local restriction, source bias, uneven territory, uncertain permanent navy.
5. **Conclude:** regionally rooted, institutionally mediated and selectively maritime.


# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks / 150 words

**Question:** "The Chola sea rested on the Kaveri field." Explain.

**Model answer:** The statement identifies the agrarian foundation of Chola maritime power. Kaveri distributaries, tanks, canals and maintained sluices supported intensive wet-rice cultivation. Surveys under Rajaraja I and later Kulottunga I, differentiated land rights and dues in grain, cash and labour made part of this surplus available to the court, temples and local institutions. Ports, craft production, merchant bodies and overseas campaigns required food, labour, credit and fiscal backing from this hinterland. Rajendra I's c. 1025 expedition therefore reflected not an isolated seafaring culture but the outward projection of a resource-rich regional state. Yet ecology was not destiny: nattar, sabhas, villages, temples and officers maintained water and negotiated extraction. Thus the sea widened Chola influence, while the Kaveri field supplied its durable material engine.

## 15 marks / 250 words

**Question:** Critically examine the claim that Chola local administration represented village democracy.

**Model answer:** Chola inscriptions reveal sophisticated local corporate government, but "village democracy" is an anachronistic description if used without qualification. The *ur* served non-brahmadeya villages; the *sabha* was especially associated with brahmadeya settlements; *nagaram* had a merchant-market focus; and *nadu* linked agrarian localities. Uttaramerur records detailed eligibility, disqualification, *kudavolai* selection, specialised *variyams*, rotation and accountability. These features demonstrate rule-bound participation and considerable local capacity.

However, participation was not universal. Property, residence, learning, conduct, caste, gender and institutional membership shaped eligibility. A brahmadeya sabha cannot represent every settlement. Local bodies also worked within royal surveys, taxation and unequal landed relations; they could collect dues for the centre rather than exist outside the state.

The best interpretation is therefore "restricted corporate self-government." Chola local bodies were historically important mechanisms of administration and negotiation, but they operated within a stratified agrarian order and an expanding imperial framework. Their achievement lies in procedural sophistication, not in anticipating universal adult franchise.

## 20 marks / 300 words

**Question:** The Chola state was neither a centralised bureaucracy nor a loose ritual federation. Discuss with reference to agrarian institutions and maritime power.

**Model answer:** The Chola evidence supports substantial royal power and substantial local mediation, making either extreme inadequate.

The centralised-imperial model explains conquest, named officers, major surveys under Rajaraja I, reorganisation through *valanadus*, land-revenue vocabulary, military contingents and monumental patronage. Rajaraja's Sri Lankan intervention and Rajendra's Ganga and Srivijaya campaigns required resource mobilisation beyond ritual prestige.

Burton Stein's segmentary model nevertheless supplied a necessary correction. Authority was denser in the Kaveri core and more variable at the periphery. *Nadu*, *ur*, *sabha*, *nagaram*, temples and chiefs assessed, collected and recorded resources. Uttaramerur shows corporate procedure, while its restricted brahmadeya base warns against a uniform bureaucratic map.

Agrarian institutions joined the two levels. Irrigation and wet-rice surplus sustained taxation; differentiated land rights and grants connected rulers, Brahmanas, cultivators and temples; local bodies translated royal claims into locality practice. Merchant organisations and ports extended this resource system into the Bay of Bengal.

Maritime power also shows both capacity and limit. Chola forces occupied changing parts of Sri Lanka and struck Srivijaya-linked centres in 1025, proving sea-borne coercive coordination. Yet a permanent separately organised navy and Southeast Asian colonies are not established.

Thus the Chola state was an unevenly integrated empire: coercive and interventionist at the top, dependent on unequal intermediaries below, and capable of selective long-range projection without uniform control.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Scope and chronology

- Imperial Cholas: c. mid-ninth to thirteenth century.
- Not an uninterrupted Sangam bureaucracy.
- Vijayalaya -> Aditya I -> Parantaka I -> Takkolam -> recovery.
- Rajaraja I, 985-1014; Rajendra I, 1012/14-1044.
- Rajadhiraja and successors continued major wars.
- Kulottunga I, 1070-1122, joined Chola and Eastern Chalukya lines.
- Thirteenth-century decline was gradual.

## 2. Political geography

- Lower Kaveri delta: fiscal-political core.
- Tondaimandalam: important northern annexed region.
- Pandya, Kerala and Western Chalukya zones: contested.
- Vengi: dynastic and strategic hinge.
- Northern Sri Lanka: more durable occupation than the whole island.
- Ganga and Srivijaya routes: campaign arcs, not continuous provinces.

## 3. State and historiography

- Sastri: imperial-centralised narrative; strong on scale, weak on locality.
- Stein: segmentary state; strong corrective, but can understate capacity.
- Karashima/Subbarayalu: inscription-led taxes, offices and locality.
- Verdict: court + chiefs + nadu + ur/sabha/nagaram + temple + merchants.
- Capacity varied by reign, region and distance from core.

## 4. Administration and local bodies

- Mandalam: large division.
- Valanadu: intermediate grouping, prominent from Rajaraja's period.
- Nadu: agrarian locality.
- Ur: non-brahmadeya village body.
- Sabha: brahmadeya assembly.
- Nagaram: merchant-market body.
- Variyam: committee.
- Kudavolai: pot-ticket selection from eligible names.
- Uttaramerur: procedural sophistication plus elite restriction.

## 5. Agrarian economy

- Kaveri irrigation supported wet-rice intensification.
- Construction and maintenance had mixed agency.
- Rajaraja and Kulottunga surveys.
- Distinguish ownership, cultivation, revenue share and jurisdiction.
- Brahmadeya: Brahmana settlement/grant context.
- Devadana: temple-support assignment.
- Vellanvagai: non-brahmadeya peasant land context.
- Dues included grain, cash, food and labour obligations.
- No one empire-wide tax rate is secure.

## 6. Society and gender

- Vellalas were important but internally differentiated.
- Brahmadeya privileges shaped social geography.
- Artisans, merchants, service and labouring groups had varied rights.
- Kammana-cheri and parai-cheri can reveal segregation.
- Dependent labour existed; avoid false identity with Atlantic slavery.
- Sembiyan Mahadevi: major patron.
- Kundavai: named Brihadisvara donor.
- Female agency varied by property, caste, kinship and institution.
- Avoid projecting a timeless devadasi category.

## 7. Temple, culture and economy

- Temple functions: ritual, archive, land, employment, redistribution and patronage.
- Temple prominence did not mean total economic control.
- Brihadisvara: Rajaraja I.
- Gangaikondacholisvaram: Rajendra I.
- Airavatesvara: Rajaraja II.
- Shaiva prominence coexisted with Vaishnava, Jain and Buddhist survival.
- Tamil and Sanskrit had layered functions.
- Periyapuranam and Kamban are later-Chola cultural anchors.
- Bronzes were lost-wax ritual-processional images with earlier antecedents.

## 8. Trade and maritime power

- Ainnurruvar/Ayyavole, Manigramam, Anjuvannam and nagaram.
- Trade connected hinterland, crafts, temples, ports and overseas routes.
- Coin, produce, livestock, cloth, land shares and credit coexisted.
- Nagapattinam linked commerce, Buddhism and Srivijaya diplomacy.
- Sri Lanka: occupation varied; resistance endured.
- Rajendra I's c. 1025 Srivijaya/Kadaram strike.
- Maritime coercive capacity is secure; permanent modern navy is not.
- Merchant corporations were not royal naval departments.

## 9. Decline and answer spine

- Causes: repeated wars, succession pressures, Pandya resurgence, Hoysala intervention, changing local-fiscal relations and altered networks.
- Decline of sovereignty did not end irrigation, temples, commerce or Tamil culture.
- Introduction: Kaveri-centred, source-rich early-medieval empire.
- Body: delta -> institutions -> society -> temple/trade -> sea.
- Qualification: hierarchy, source bias, uneven control and uncertain naval organisation.
- Verdict: unevenly integrated agrarian empire with selective maritime projection.
