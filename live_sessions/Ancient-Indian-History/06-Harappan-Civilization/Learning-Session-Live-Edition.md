# The Harappan Civilization - Learning Session Live Edition

Imagine approaching a civilisation that built large cities, moved raw materials across mountains and coasts, maintained drains and reservoirs, marked objects with a recurring sign system, and yet left no securely readable royal proclamation. The temptation is to fill that silence with familiar kings, priests, castes and gods. The historian's harder task is to begin with what survives, ask what operation produced it, and stop where the evidence stops.

This session teaches the Harappan civilisation as a connected problem. First we establish chronology, evidence and geography. We then build the urban system from villages, labour, architecture and water; reconstruct production and exchange; test what signs, houses, burials and images can reveal; and finally explain regional transformation without a one-cause collapse story. Topic 05 owns the detailed Neolithic-Chalcolithic background. Topic 07 owns Aryan identity, migration, language and genetics debates. Indian Art and Culture owns detailed formal-aesthetic analysis; here objects are used as historical evidence.

The sequence is cumulative. A drain becomes evidence for governance only after its maintenance needs are understood; a seal becomes evidence for communication only after script limits are clear; a climatic proxy becomes historical explanation only after social response and regional variation are supplied.

The first discipline is simple: material practice is often visible; named institutions and meanings are much harder to recover.

### 1. Begin with the name

**Harappan civilisation** is generally the safest archaeological label because Harappa was the first type-site excavated and the cultural distribution extended far beyond the Indus valley. **Indus civilisation** remains common, but “Indus Valley” can shrink a network that also occupied the Ghaggar-Hakra region, Kutch, Saurashtra, Haryana, Rajasthan, western Uttar Pradesh, the Makran coast and a highland outpost at Shortugai.

“Sindhu-Sarasvati” introduces a further claim: that an archaeological distribution and a textual river identity can be joined. That possibility must be tested through palaeochannel history, settlement dates and textual chronology; it should not be built into the name as a settled conclusion.

### 2. Replace a rise-and-fall story with a process model

| Phase | Broad working range | Process | Typical tendency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early Harappan | c. 3300-2600 BCE | Regionalisation | Fortified or planned settlements, regional ceramics, growing craft and exchange |
| Mature Harappan | c. 2600-1900 BCE | Integration | Cities, shared signs, seals, graded weights, brick norms and wide networks |
| Late Harappan | c. 1900-1300 BCE, regionally variable | Localisation | Reduced urban traits, settlement shifts, regional pottery and diversified subsistence |

These dates are broad archaeological ranges. They vary with site sequence, sample and calibration. The phases describe changing systems, not three sealed peoples.

### 3. Discovery changed the history of India

Alexander Cunningham recorded a seal at Harappa in the nineteenth century without recognising its civilisational context. Daya Ram Sahni began excavation at Harappa in 1920-21, R. D. Banerji worked at Mohenjo-daro, and John Marshall formally announced the civilisation in 1924. The discovery pushed recognised urbanism in the subcontinent back to the third millennium BCE.

The history of research also matters. Early work privileged large monuments and comparisons with Mesopotamia. Later archaeology studied villages, households, craft debris, plants, animal bones, human remains and landscapes. Functional labels such as “granary,” “dockyard,” “citadel,” “assembly hall,” “Priest-King” and “Mother Goddess” are therefore hypotheses inherited from excavation history, not Harappan self-descriptions.

### 4. Use an evidence ladder

| Evidence | Strong claim it supports | Claim it cannot support alone |
|---|---|---|
| Walls, streets, drains | Construction, spatial organisation, maintenance | Exact constitution or office |
| Seeds, pollen, phytoliths | Plant presence and sometimes cultivation | Yield, ownership or universal diet |
| Animal bones | Species use, age profile, butchery | One complete economy or identity |
| Workshop debris | Production sequence and specialisation | Guild, caste or state factory |
| Weights and scales | Measurement conventions | Coinage or one imperial treasury |
| Signs on objects | Patterned communication | Secure language or translation |
| Burials and skeletons | Mortuary treatment, health, ancestry sample | Entire social system or civilisation-wide population |
| Images and figurines | Repeated representation and possible symbolism | Named deity or unchanged later faith |

✅ **Fact:** The sign system remains undeciphered and most inscriptions are short.

⚠️ **Inference rule:** Use `records` for direct observation, `indicates` for strong contextual inference, `suggests` for a plausible reading, and `cannot establish` when the evidentiary boundary is clear.

### Example and its limit

Shared brick proportions, seals and weights establish wide coordination during the Mature phase. They do not, by themselves, identify one emperor, one capital or one legal code.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A civilisation with cities, writing and standards must have had a centralised empire.

**Reply:** Coordination is certain, but several institutions can generate coordination: a central state, regional states, corporate elites, merchant networks or combinations of these. Without readable administrative records, the political form remains debated.

**Balanced assessment:** Begin with the secure system, then compare institutional models instead of choosing one by analogy.

### 1. Use directional anchors carefully

| Direction or domain | Useful anchor | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| West | Sutkagen-dor on the Makran coast | Boundary depends on phase and site definition |
| North | Manda in the Jammu region | Northern reach does not make every nearby find Mature Harappan |

| South | Malvan in southern Gujarat | Daimabad is better treated as Late Harappan/contact evidence, not a mechanical Mature extreme |
| Highland outpost | Shortugai in Afghanistan | Route node does not automatically mean annexed province |

| Ghaggar-Hakra | Kalibangan, Banawali, Rakhigarhi | Palaeochannel identity and Vedic river identity remain separate questions |
| Kutch/Gujarat | Dholavira, Lothal, Surkotada, Nageshwar | Strong architectural, coastal and craft variation |

The safest map sentence is: **a vast multi-ecological cultural zone linked river plains, semi-arid tracts, coasts, islands and highland routes.**

### 2. An urban civilisation contains many non-urban settlements

Large cities depended on smaller places. Farmers and herders supplied food and fibre. Quarry and mining zones supplied stone and metals. Coastal communities supplied shell and maritime access. Specialist centres converted materials into beads, seals, tools and ornaments. Towns and cities concentrated consumption, coordination and public works.

| Settlement role | Examples | Main contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Large urban centre | Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi | Dense habitation, public works, craft, exchange and coordination |
| Regional centre | Kalibangan, Banawali | Agricultural-craft integration and regional planning |
| Specialist settlement | Chanhudaro, Nageshwar | Bead, seal or shell production |
| Small settlement | Allahdino and many villages | Food, household craft and local exchange |
| Route/resource node | Shortugai, quarry and coastal sites | Procurement and movement |

### 3. Surplus is a relationship, not a pile of grain

Cities require food beyond what many urban residents directly produce. Yet a granary-shaped building does not prove a tax system. Surplus could move through tribute, redistribution, exchange, household obligations or mixed arrangements. The archaeological chain should be:

```text
rural production
      |
storage + transport + measurement
      |
urban consumption + craft support + public labour
      |
institution inferred, exact mechanism unresolved
```

### 4. Ghaggar-Hakra discipline

Dense settlement along now-dry or altered channels is archaeologically important. Geomorphology can reconstruct channel history; archaeology can date settlement; texts can preserve river names and meanings. These are three datasets. A physical channel is not automatically the Rigvedic Sarasvati, and a textual identification does not date every settlement beside it.

### Example and its limit

Shortugai's location near highland routes and its Harappan material culture support long-distance procurement and connectivity. They do not alone prove permanent military occupation by a single Harappan empire.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If sites shared seals, weights and artefacts, regional differences are secondary.

**Reply:** Shared conventions created integration, but differences in layout, crops, construction materials, water systems and artefact frequency shaped daily life. Integration and regionality are simultaneous, not competing descriptions.

**Balanced assessment:** Map the common network and the local adaptation in the same answer.

The best architectural answer shows both integration and adaptation.

### 1. What makes the settlements urban?

Urbanism involves more than a large population. Harappan cities combined dense habitation, differentiated spaces, specialised craft, exchange, public works, storage, water management and links with a productive hinterland. Their built form required planning, skilled construction and recurring maintenance.

Common tendencies include:

- streets and lanes organising blocks, though not a perfect grid at every site;
- courtyard-centred houses with controlled entrances;
- bathing floors, drains and wells in many domestic contexts;
- mud brick as a basic material and baked brick especially useful where water resistance mattered;
- commonly recurring Mature-phase brick proportions near `1:2:4`;
- raised, enclosed or fortified sectors;
- differentiated house sizes and workshop areas.

### 2. The house is an economic and social unit

A courtyard supplied light, ventilation and a work area. Side-lane entrances could protect privacy from major streets. Rooms, platforms, wells, bathrooms, hearths and craft debris reveal combinations of residence, production and storage.

But architecture does not transparently reveal family form. A large house might reflect wealth, an extended household, specialised work, rebuilding or several occupation phases. A private well indicates privileged access to water more securely than it establishes legal ownership.

### 3. “Citadel” is a location label

Archaeologists use **citadel** for a raised or enclosed sector. The word should not silently become “royal palace.” Mohenjo-daro and Harappa have familiar raised-western and lower-eastern divisions, but Dholavira used several enclosed sectors, Lothal had its own compact plan, and Chanhudaro lacked conventional fortification.

| Site | Architectural strength | Required qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Mohenjo-daro | Great Bath, wells, drains, courtyard houses | Grid is not perfectly uniform; named institutional labels are modern |
| Harappa | Multiple mounds, working platforms, large 12-unit structure | “Granary” is not confirmed by a Harappan text |
| Dholavira | Stone construction, several sectors, reservoirs | Corrects a universal two-part plan |
| Kalibangan | Fortified sectors and distinct wastewater arrangements | Not a smaller copy of Mohenjo-daro |
| Lothal | Compact settlement, drains, warehouse-like platform and basin | Basin function remains interpreted |
| Chanhudaro | Unfortified craft settlement | Urban function need not require a citadel |

### 4. Architecture stores labour

Every brick had to be prepared, moved and laid. Streets had to be aligned; walls repaired; drains cleared; wells lined; reservoirs desilted. Architecture therefore records **mobilised labour across time**. It establishes capacity for coordination, but not whether mobilisation came through tax, corvee, kin groups, neighbourhood bodies, merchants or rulers.

### 5. Exact 2025 Mains connection

The printed 2025 GS-I question asks:

> **Discuss the salient features of the Harappan architecture. (Answer in 150 words.)**

A high-scoring response should not become a monument list. Use:

```text
shared planning norms
-> domestic form
-> water and sanitation
-> public/special structures
-> named regional variation
-> function-label caution
```

### Example and its limit

The recurring `1:2:4` brick proportion supports modular knowledge and communication among builders. It does not prove that a single bureaucracy issued one building code to every site.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Regional variation weakens the claim that this was one civilisation.

**Reply:** Shared signs, weights, craft conventions and construction norms coexist with local materials and layouts. A system can coordinate standards while allowing adaptation.

**Balanced assessment:** Harappan architecture is best described as **standardised principles expressed through regional solutions**.

The historical fact is not merely that a drain existed, but that a system had to keep working.

### 1. Mohenjo-daro: distributed access and drainage

Mohenjo-daro is noted for numerous wells, including private and neighbourhood access; bathing floors drained through house channels into street systems. Covered drains, soak arrangements and inspection points separated liquid flow from solid waste. The system was sophisticated, but excavated streets do not prove that every lane or house functioned identically.

The Great Bath used carefully fitted baked bricks, gypsum mortar and bitumen waterproofing, with stairs and a controlled outlet. Its construction and water management are secure. Ceremonial bathing is plausible; a named cult or priestly corporation is not.

### 2. Dholavira: storage shaped the city

Dholavira stood in the semi-arid Kutch environment between the seasonal Manhar and Mansar streams. Dams and channels directed water into large stone-lined reservoirs and cisterns. Here water storage was not an attachment to the city; it was part of the settlement's spatial design.

### 3. Variation prevents one sanitation template

| Site | Water emphasis | Interpretive limit |
|---|---|---|
| Mohenjo-daro | Wells, bathing floors, street drains, Great Bath | Do not infer a named municipality |
| Dholavira | Dams, channels, reservoirs and stone-lined storage | Do not generalise this design to all sites |
| Kalibangan | House discharge into jars or troughs in parts of the lower town | Different drainage does not mean absence of planning |
| Lothal | Wells, drains and a major brick basin | “Dockyard” remains a leading interpretation, not a translated label |
| Harappa | Drains, bathing areas and possible central water feature | Fewer wells than Mohenjo-daro do not imply inferior urbanism |

### 4. From sanitation to institution

Drains clog. Wells need lining. Reservoirs silt. A working water system implies rules, labour, technical knowledge and repeated cooperation. This is evidence for civic capacity. It does not distinguish conclusively among palace command, council, corporate groups, neighbourhood organisation or mixed authority.

### Example and its limit

Dholavira's reservoir complex supports adaptation to seasonal water scarcity. It does not prove that environmental pressure alone created the city's political order.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Harappan drains prove an exceptionally hygienic society committed to public health.

**Reply:** The infrastructure demonstrates systematic wastewater management and maintenance. Motives may have included convenience, cleanliness, craft needs, flood control and ritual practice; modern public-health intentions should not be projected without evidence.

**Balanced assessment:** Describe function first, infer civic coordination second, and leave motive or constitution open.

- **Mains:** Use `infrastructure -> maintenance -> labour -> bounded institution`.

4. Dholavira integrated dams, channels and reservoirs.

Urban life rested on ecological diversity, not one universal cereal package.

### 1. Crop presence must be read regionally

Wheat and barley were important, especially in north-western and Indus settings. Millets became significant in some Gujarat and semi-arid contexts. Rice occurs at some sites and phases but should not be universalised. Pulses, sesame and mustard broadened the food and oil base. Cotton linked cultivation with textile production.

An excavated seed can establish presence when identification and context are secure. It does not reveal how much was consumed, who owned the field or whether the crop dominated the entire region.

### 2. Animals had multiple roles

Cattle were prominent, alongside sheep, goats, buffalo, pigs and other animals. Herds supplied meat, milk, traction, dung and hides in combinations that varied by age profile and context. Fish, shellfish and wild animals remained part of subsistence.

Horse/equid evidence is rare and disputed. A reported bone at Surkotada does not make the horse a characteristic Harappan economic animal.

### 3. How food reached cities

Rural producers could support urban residents and specialists only through storage, transport and social arrangements. Bullock carts, river movement and local routes connected fields, herds and settlements. Yet the pathway from crop to city cannot be reduced to one unverified tax system.

### 4. Scientific evidence can identify use, not the whole menu

Archaeobotany studies seeds and plant remains. Zooarchaeology studies animal remains, age profiles and butchery. Lipid residues can identify classes of fats absorbed into pottery. Stable isotopes can investigate diet or mobility when baselines are reliable.

Upinder Singh discusses lipid-residue research from Kotada Bhadli and from Rakhigarhi, Farmana and rural Haryana-western Uttar Pradesh sites. These studies strengthen evidence for dairy and varied vessel use. They remain samples from particular pots and sites, not a civilisation-wide recipe book.

### Example and its limit

Cotton fibres, textile impressions and spindle whorls support spinning and weaving. They do not show whether every household produced cloth or whether production was controlled by one institution.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Standardised cities must have depended on a standardised diet.

**Reply:** Urban standards in bricks and weights could connect populations whose crop and animal strategies differed by rainfall, soil, season and water access. Integration may rely on complementary diversity.

**Balanced assessment:** The Harappan food base was a regional portfolio that supported cities through exchange and risk spreading.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Avoid universal staples and horse-centred claims.
- **Mains:** Link food diversity to ecological spread, resilience and urban-rural dependence.
- **Science:** State sample, material tested, method and scale limit.
- **Transition:** Food and fibre now enter workshops and measurement systems.

### Revision notes

1. Wheat and barley were important but not the only crops.
2. Millets and rice had stronger roles in some regions.
3. Pulses and oilseeds widened the food system.
4. Cotton connected farming with textiles.
5. Cattle were prominent; herd use was multi-purpose.
6. Fish and wild resources continued within urban civilisation.
7. Horse evidence is rare and disputed.
8. Crop presence does not measure yield or dominance.
9. Cities depended on rural production and transport.

### Visual: follow the object backwards

```text
SOURCE -> RAW MATERIAL -> PREPARATION -> TECHNIQUE -> OBJECT -> USE/EXCHANGE -> DISCARD
 mine       copper/tin      alloying       casting     tool
 quarry     carnelian       heating        drilling    bead
 coast      shell           cutting        polishing   bangle
 field      cotton          spinning       weaving     cloth
```

An object is the end of a labour chain. Reconstructing that chain reveals more than naming the finished artefact.

### 1. Bronze Age does not mean metal replaced everything

Harappan technology combined copper and bronze with stone, shell, bone, clay, wood and fibres. Bronze is an alloy, usually copper with tin. Metal scarcity and recycling help explain why stone tools remained common. Iron was not the defining Mature Harappan technology.

The famous “Dancing Girl” from Mohenjo-daro is bronze and was made by the lost-wax process: model in wax, coat in clay, heat out the wax, pour molten metal, cool and finish. The modern name describes a pose; it does not provide the person's occupation or biography.

### 2. Craft specialisation is visible in debris

| Craft | Evidence | Inference | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bead-making | Rough-outs, drills, furnaces, finished and unfinished beads | Skilled multi-stage production | Workshop does not automatically mean state control |
| Shell work | Cut shell and waste at coastal/specialist sites | Resource procurement and specialist technique | Findspot may differ from source |
| Faience | Beads, bangles and small objects | Controlled high-temperature production | Exact workshop organisation varies |
| Metallurgy | Moulds, slag, tools, vessels and figurines | Alloying and casting knowledge | Metal object alone does not locate ore source |
| Textiles | Spindle whorls, impressions, fibres | Spinning and weaving | Domestic find does not prove exclusive household production |
| Pottery | Wheel-made forms, kilns, painted wares | Cooking, storage, craft and chronology | Pottery style is not ethnicity |

Chanhudaro and Lothal preserve strong bead-working evidence; Nageshwar is associated with shell specialisation. Long carnelian beads required procurement, heat treatment, shaping, drilling and polishing. Their labour cost may have made them valuable, but exact price and ownership are unknown.

### 3. Metrology made dispersed work comparable

Cubical stone weights occur in graded series. Smaller units often display binary progression, while higher values include decimal groupings. Shell and ivory scales show linear measurement. Brick proportions extend modular reasoning into construction.

These standards could support production, exchange and quality control. They do not prove metal coinage. Nor should one remembered “multiple of sixteen” replace the actual graded system and regional context.

### 4. Three models of standardisation

```text
central direction
       OR
corporate / merchant regulation
       OR
training + mobile specialists + dense exchange
       OR
some combination
```

Standardisation establishes shared conventions; the mechanism remains a historical question.

### Example and its limit

Concentrated bead waste at a workshop can reconstruct sequence and specialisation. It cannot by itself identify the worker's caste, freedom, gender or relationship to political authority.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Such accurate weights must have been coins.

**Reply:** A weight measures another substance; a coin carries socially guaranteed value as a medium. Harappan metrology is secure, while minted coinage is not.

**Balanced assessment:** Measurement increased comparability across the network without revealing one exclusive mode of exchange.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** “Dancing Girl” = bronze, not clay, gold or carnelian.
- **Mains:** Use `raw material -> technique -> labour -> distribution -> social limit`.
- **Trap:** Bronze Age allows extensive stone-tool use.
- **Transition:** Standards become useful when goods circulate.

### Revision notes

1. Harappan technology was copper-bronze based, not iron-centred.
2. Stone, shell, bone, clay and fibre remained important.
3. Dancing Girl is bronze and lost-wax cast.
4. Modern object names do not reveal ancient identity.
5. Chanhudaro and Lothal anchor bead production.
6. Nageshwar anchors shell specialisation.
7. Debris reveals operational sequence.
8. Cubical weights were graded, not coins.
9. Scales show linear measurement.
10. Standardisation can arise through several institutions.

```text
FARM / HERD / QUARRY / COAST
        |
        v

        |
        v
village <-> specialist centre <-> town <-> city
        |

        |
        v

```

Long-distance trade was the outer layer of an economy whose foundation was internal circulation.

### 1. Start inside the cultural zone

No city possessed every raw material. Copper, tin, chert, carnelian, lapis lazuli, shell, timber, food and fibre moved between ecological regions. Finished beads, pottery, seals, textiles and metal objects moved in return or through redistributive circuits.

Possible routes are reconstructed from:

- location of raw-material sources;
- geological provenance of artefacts;
- settlement distribution;
- workshop locations;
- finished and unfinished goods;
- cart tracks and models;
- boat depictions and coastal geography.

The route is stronger when material-source analysis joins distribution. A map line drawn between two finds is not enough.

### 2. Transport was mixed

Solid-wheeled cart models and tracks support wheeled land transport. Pack animals could move smaller loads. River craft linked settlements along waterways; coastal boats connected Gujarat, Sindh and Makran. Each mode had different costs and carrying capacities.

### 3. External evidence

Mesopotamian texts refer to **Meluhha**, conventionally connected with the Indus/Harappan region or a wider eastern trading sphere. **Dilmun** is associated with the Gulf, especially Bahrain, and **Magan/Makan** with the Oman-linked zone. Harappan or Harappan-related seals, beads and other objects outside the subcontinent, together with foreign materials within it, confirm contact.

| Evidence | Secure conclusion | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Mesopotamian references to Meluhha | An eastern trading sphere linked with Harappan exchange | Exact boundary and political identity uncertain |
| Harappan seals/objects abroad | Movement of objects and perhaps people | Does not quantify total trade |
| Gulf intermediary evidence | Multi-stage maritime circuits | Not direct Harappan rule |
| Shortugai | Highland procurement connectivity | Not automatic colony or province |
| Lothal/coastal sites | Maritime orientation | Basin function and route scale require caution |

### 4. Contact is not control

An imported bead proves movement. A cluster may show repeated exchange. Neither automatically proves colony, monopoly, merchant ethnicity or royal administration. Trade could involve direct voyages, intermediaries, itinerant merchants, gift exchange and several overlapping routes.

### 5. Why external trade should not explain everything

Earlier scholarship often treated Mesopotamian demand as the engine of Harappan urbanism. Internal trade among villages, towns, resource zones and cities was more extensive and indispensable. External contraction may have weakened some high-value networks, but it cannot alone explain all post-1900 BCE regional change.

### Example and its limit

Lapis lazuli links the Harappan network with highland sources and long routes. A lapis bead at a city does not identify who mined, transported, owned or exchanged it at every stage.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The word Meluhha proves the Harappans' own name and political unity.

**Reply:** It is an external Mesopotamian term. It supports contact with an Indus-related eastern zone, but its precise geography and internal political meaning remain debated.

**Balanced assessment:** External texts are strongest when combined with objects, routes and provenance, and weakest when made to speak for Harappan self-identity.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Distinguish Meluhha, Dilmun and Magan.
- **Mains:** Build economy from rural production to internal exchange before overseas trade.
- **Decline:** Trade contraction is one interacting cause, not a total explanation.
- **Transition:** Seals and signs now enter as instruments and symbols within these networks.

2. Raw-material provenance is stronger than proximity alone.

4. Harappan exchange operated without securely attested coins.
5. Meluhha is an external Mesopotamian term.
6. Dilmun is associated with the Gulf/Bahrain.
7. Magan is associated with the Oman-linked zone.
8. Contact does not prove colony or political control.
9. Shortugai supports procurement reach.
10. External trade contraction cannot explain every regional transition.

```text
INSCRIBED OBJECT
seal / sealing / tablet / pottery / signboard
               |
      signs + direction + sequence
               |
     statistical and contextual pattern
               |
   possible identity / office / commodity /
   ritual / ownership / administration
               |
      NO VERIFIED LANGUAGE OR READING
```

The script can be studied rigorously even though it cannot yet be read securely.

### 1. What is directly observable?

- inscriptions are generally short;
- signs occur on seals, sealings, tablets, pottery and other objects;
- several hundred signs are counted, depending on how variants are classified;
- many inscriptions appear to run right-to-left;
- signs recur in patterned positions and combinations;
- the Dholavira signboard placed large signs in a public architectural context.

These characteristics support an organised sign system. They do not settle whether every sign is logographic, syllabic, numerical or mixed.

### 2. Why decipherment remains difficult

A strong decipherment normally requires a substantial corpus, longer texts, known language, bilingual or parallel texts, and results that work consistently across inscriptions. Harappan inscriptions are short; no secure bilingual has been found; the underlying language is unknown; and proposed readings do not command scholarly consensus.

Claims for Proto-Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, Munda or other languages are hypotheses. Sign resemblance to Brahmi or another later script is not proof of descent.

### 3. Seals had images and contexts as well as signs

Steatite seals often carry animal or composite motifs and short sign sequences. Sealings can indicate that seals marked containers, doors, goods or transactions. Seals might relate to identity, office, authority, ownership, exchange or ritual. Their meaning could differ by context.

The widespread “unicorn” motif is culturally important, but neither the animal's name nor its exact symbolic meaning is known.

### 4. Administration without translation

Repeated seal forms, weights and movement of goods make administrative or commercial use plausible. But an administrative function does not reveal the text's wording. “Merchant's name,” “tax receipt” and “royal title” remain possible models, not translations.

### Example and its limit

The Dholavira signboard demonstrates large-scale public display of the sign system. It does not identify the place-name, ruler or message displayed.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the script is undeciphered, it contributes nothing to history.

**Reply:** Medium, distribution, direction, sign frequency and archaeological context reveal communication practices and social reach even without phonetic values.

**Balanced assessment:** Use the script as patterned material evidence while refusing claims that depend on a secure reading.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** “Undeciphered” does not mean random or absent.
- **Mains:** Link short texts to administrative possibilities and source limits.
- **Trap:** No king-list, law code or theology has been securely translated.
- **Transition:** The absence of readable titles makes polity and society a model-comparison problem.

We can argue that power existed without pretending to know its exact institutional form.

### 1. What social difference looks like archaeologically

Variation in house size and facilities, access to rare materials, craft concentrations, settlement hierarchy and mortuary treatment can indicate unequal access, occupation and status. Harappan society included farmers, herders, fishers, craftspeople, transporters, traders and people who constructed or maintained urban works.

These traces do not automatically reveal caste, slavery, matriarchy, property law or hereditary class. A social label needs repeated, independent evidence and often textual confirmation that is unavailable here.

### 2. Burials are selected performances

Harappan cemeteries occur at Harappa, Kalibangan, Lothal, Rakhigarhi and other sites. Extended burials, secondary or fractional treatment, symbolic burials and grave goods vary. A burial records how some dead were treated; it is not a census of the living.

Skeletal analysis can study age, sex estimation, trauma, disease, diet and biological relatedness. Interpretation depends on preservation, sample size, excavation context and representativeness. Cemetery R-37 at Harappa is important, but one cemetery cannot stand for all regions and centuries.

### 3. Why the political question remains open

**Centralised-state model:** wide standards, public works and long-lived integration may imply high-level direction.

**Regional-state or city-state model:** large regional centres and architectural variation may indicate several political units sharing cultural conventions.

**Corporate or heterarchical model:** limited royal display and strong collective standards may indicate authority distributed among mercantile, ritual, kin or civic groups.

No model resolves every feature. The absence of obvious royal monuments does not prove absence of rulers; the presence of standards does not prove one emperor.

### 4. Force and cooperation

Fortifications and some trauma remind us that the society was not demonstrably peaceful. Yet weapons and triumphal warfare imagery are less prominent than in some contemporary civilisations. The evidence does not support either a utopia without coercion or an empire defined by military display.

### 5. Labour is visible, legal status is not

Brick-making, construction, reservoir desilting, drain cleaning, quarrying, craft production and transport required workforces. Archaeology may reveal task scale, workshop concentration and bodily stress. It usually cannot determine whether workers were free, dependent, enslaved, seasonal or household-based.

### Example and its limit

Seals and weights found in a large Banawali lower-town house suggest affluent economic activity beyond a simple “citadel elite” zone. They do not reveal the occupant's name, office or legal status.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Without readable texts, all political reconstruction is speculation.

**Reply:** Public works, settlement hierarchy, standards and distribution are genuine evidence for organised power. The speculative step is not recognising authority; it is naming its exact form too confidently.

**Balanced assessment:** Harappan society was differentiated and strongly coordinated, while the scale and structure of rule remain unresolved.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** House forms do not establish common property or caste.
- **Mains:** Compare models against the same evidence.
- **Science:** Planned analysis at Rakhigarhi may refine individual health and mobility, not instantly define society.
- **Transition:** Religious interpretation faces the same observation-to-identity gap.

Naming is the last step, not the first.

### 1. The horned seated figure

A famous seal shows a horned seated figure with surrounding animals. The label **Pashupati** or “proto-Shiva” is a comparative interpretation. Posture, horns and animals are observable; the deity's name, theological identity and direct continuity are not.

### 2. Female figurines

Terracotta female figurines differ in form and context. Some may relate to household ritual, fertility, protection, play or ordinary representation. Calling all of them one “Mother Goddess” collapses variation and assumes a doctrine not preserved in text. Female divinity would not, in any case, prove matriarchy or high status for all women.

### 3. Trees, animals and composite beings

Pipal motifs, bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, unicorn-like figures and composite creatures suggest a rich symbolic world. Repetition can show cultural importance. It cannot disclose whether a motif was a deity, clan sign, story, office, commodity marker or protective emblem.

### 4. Fire, bathing and burial

Fire installations at Kalibangan and some other sites may have ritual significance, but domestic and industrial alternatives must be checked. The Great Bath may have hosted ceremonial bathing, yet its secure fact is specialised public water architecture. Burials and grave goods reveal mortuary choices, not one complete afterlife doctrine.

### 5. Continuity must be selective and testable

Similarities with later South Asian practices can generate questions: bathing, tree symbolism, bangles, craft techniques or selected motifs. A long time gap, changed contexts and unread language prevent automatic unbroken continuity. The strongest legacy claims concern transmitted skills, crops, settlement knowledge and regional populations; named theological identity is weaker.

### Example and its limit

A pipal image repeated on seals indicates cultural salience. It does not prove that the image carried the same meaning as later pipal worship.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Refusing names leaves only description and makes religion unknowable.

**Reply:** Context, repetition, spatial pattern and comparison can support ritual practice. What remains unavailable is the secure ancient name and doctrinal content.

**Balanced assessment:** Harappan evidence supports diverse ritual and symbolic practices, not a fully reconstructed pantheon or uniform state religion.

- **Mains:** Use `object -> context -> alternatives -> confidence`.
- **Boundary:** Detailed sculptural style belongs to Indian Art and Culture.
- **Transition:** The final lesson applies the same discipline to climate, science and decline.

### Visual 1: decline is a mechanism chain

```text
river/flood change + monsoon stress + exchange contraction +
resource pressure + institutional reorganisation
                           |
                  different local exposures
                           |
       adaptation / migration / contraction / continuity
                           |
large-city decline + reduced script/seals/standards +
regional settlement and crop change
                           |
             LATE HARAPPAN LOCALISATION
```

### Visual 2: scientific method has a scale limit

```text
QUESTION -> CONTEXT -> SAMPLE -> METHOD -> RESULT -> REPRESENTATIVENESS
                                        |
            date / diet / mobility / health / ancestry / material source
                                        |
            not automatic language / religion / caste / entire civilisation
```

The two visuals share one rule: a cause or sample must be matched to the scale of the conclusion.

### 1. What changed after c. 1900 BCE?

Across different regions and at different speeds:

- some large cities contracted or were abandoned;
- use of script, seals and some shared standards declined;
- long-distance exchange and specialised production changed;
- settlement distributions shifted;
- regional pottery traditions became more prominent;
- crop strategies diversified;
- smaller and rural communities continued.

This is better described as **deurbanisation, localisation and transformation** than sudden extinction.

### 2. Test causes through mechanisms

| Cause family | Possible mechanism | Why it cannot stand alone |
|---|---|---|
| River change or flood | Damages settlements or alters water access | Hydrological histories differ by region |
| Aridity or monsoon weakening | Raises crop and water risk | Social responses and proxy scales vary |
| Exchange contraction | Reduces raw-material flow and urban demand | Internal and rural production continued |
| Political-economic reorganisation | Weakens coordination and labour mobilisation | Institutions are not directly documented |
| Disease or local resource pressure | Adds demographic or health stress | Evidence remains sample-specific |
| Invasion | Could explain local conflict | No civilisation-wide destruction horizon supports one Aryan conquest |

A cause becomes historical explanation only when it has chronology, geography, mechanism, evidence and a plausible social response.

### 3. Why the old invasion story fails

Skeletons at Mohenjo-daro do not belong to one simultaneous massacre horizon. Different cities declined at different times and through different processes. Topic 07 examines migration and identity debates; Topic 06's secure conclusion is that no single military invasion explains the collapse of Harappan urban integration.

### 4. Science refines bounded questions

- **Radiocarbon dating:** refines the age of organic samples and associated contexts.
- **Archaeobotany and zooarchaeology:** reconstruct plant and animal use.
- **Residue analysis:** identifies preserved substances in vessels.
- **Stable isotopes:** can test diet, mobility or water exposure against baselines.
- **Osteology and palaeopathology:** study age, stress, disease and trauma.
- **Provenance analysis:** compares artefacts with possible material sources.
- **Ancient DNA:** investigates biological ancestry and relatedness where preservation and contamination control permit.

The 2019 Rakhigarhi genome came from one individual. It was important for population-history debate, but it cannot identify Harappan language, religion, caste or every regional population. Those broader migration and identity arguments belong to Topic 07.

### 5. Official June 2026 update

✅ **Fact:** PIB's 22 June 2026 release, “Human Skeletal Remains from Rakhigarhi Transferred to Anthropological Survey of India for Advanced Scientific Research,” reports that remains from ASI's 2025-26 excavation were transferred to AnSI's repository and laboratory in Kolkata.

✅ **Fact:** The report lists planned ancient-DNA, stable-isotope, osteological, palaeopathological and environmental investigations.

⚠️ **Inference:** The development may enlarge the sample base for health, diet, mobility and ancestry questions.

⚠️ **Hard limit:** Until analysed results are published with contexts and methods, no historical conclusion should be attributed to the transferred remains.

### 6. Continuity and discontinuity

| More plausible continuity questions | Clear major discontinuities |
|---|---|
| Crops, livestock and rural practices | Mature urban scale |
| Craft techniques and regional knowledge | Extensive sign use |
| Some pottery forms and symbols | Integrated seal and standard systems |
| Population continuity with later admixture | Particular long-distance networks |
| Water and settlement knowledge | Mature city institutions |

Legacy should therefore be framed as selective transmission and regional continuity, not one unchanged civilisational essence.

### Example and its limit

A palaeoclimate record showing reduced rainfall can establish environmental stress in its catchment and period. It cannot explain abandonment unless settlement chronology, water dependence and human response also align.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A multi-causal explanation avoids deciding what really caused decline.

**Reply:** Multi-causality is not a list of possibilities. It compares dated mechanisms across regions and ranks them. A single cause is weaker when it cannot explain different timings, ecologies and responses.

**Balanced assessment:** The Mature network transformed because interacting environmental, economic and institutional pressures were filtered through regional conditions.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Separate a planned scientific method from a published result.
- **Mains:** Write `cause -> mechanism -> regional evidence -> response -> limit`.
- **Boundary:** Leave the complete Aryan/genetics debate to Topic 07.
- **Final synthesis:** Harappan history is coordinated diversity followed by uneven localisation, not uniformity followed by extinction.

### Revision notes

1. Post-1900 BCE change was uneven.
2. Deurbanisation does not mean depopulation everywhere.
3. Script, seals and some standards declined.
4. Rural and regional communities continued.
5. No one flood, drought, river shift or invasion explains all sites.
6. Causes need mechanism and matched chronology.
7. Scientific results are sample- and context-bound.
8. One genome is not a civilisation-wide population.
9. June 2026 Rakhigarhi work was announced/planned, not completed.
10. Legacy combines selective continuity and major institutional discontinuity.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original Mains 1 - 10 marks

**Question:** Explain why Dholavira changes the conventional image of Harappan urban planning. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Model answer:**

Dholavira shows that Harappan planning was integrated but not uniform. Located on Khadir island in Kutch, it used extensive stone construction and several enclosed sectors rather than merely repeating the familiar raised-citadel and lower-town scheme. Its most distinctive feature was a water system in which dams and channels captured seasonal flows from the Manhar-Mansar setting and directed them into reservoirs and cisterns. The large signboard also suggests public display of the Harappan sign system. These features demonstrate measurement, labour mobilisation and civic planning shared with the wider civilisation, while adapting them to local material and hydrological conditions. Labels such as “stadium” remain interpretive, and the city's plan should not be universalised. Dholavira therefore replaces an identical-city model with “coordinated diversity”: common civilisational capacities expressed through a regionally specific urban solution.

**Marks logic:** Definition, four named features, comparison, inference and functional caution.

## Original Mains 2 - 10 marks

**Question:** Why does the undeciphered script make Harappan history both possible and limited? (Answer in 150 words.)

**Model answer:**

The Harappan sign system is not historically useless merely because it remains undeciphered. Short inscriptions on seals, sealings, tablets, pottery and other objects display recurring signs, patterned sequences and often right-to-left direction. Their distribution and contexts can illuminate communication, identity marking, exchange and possible administration. Dholavira's large signboard further demonstrates public display. Yet the corpus lacks long texts and a secure bilingual inscription, while the underlying language is unknown. Consequently, no proposed decipherment has produced an accepted king-list, law code, place-name system or theology. Images beside signs also cannot be translated independently. Harappan history is therefore strongest when writing is treated as material and contextual evidence: it proves an organised communication system, but it cannot yet name the institutions, persons or doctrines that used it.

**Marks logic:** Explains both evidentiary value and the exact boundary imposed by non-decipherment.

## Original Mains 3 - 15 marks

**Question:** Analyse how rural production, craft specialisation and exchange sustained Harappan urbanism. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer:**

Harappan cities were coordinating nodes within a larger rural and resource landscape. Farmers and herders supplied wheat, barley, regional millets and rice, pulses, oilseeds, cotton, livestock and labour. Fishing and wild resources diversified this base. Quarry, mining and coastal zones supplied chert, copper, tin, carnelian, lapis lazuli, shell and timber.

Specialist centres and urban workshops converted these inputs into beads, faience, seals, shell bangles, pottery, textiles and copper-bronze objects. Chanhudaro demonstrates concentrated bead and seal manufacture without a conventional citadel; Nageshwar specialised in shell; Lothal joined workshops, measurement and maritime orientation. Operational debris reveals stages of preparation, drilling, casting and finishing more securely than it reveals guilds or workers' legal status.

Internal circulation linked villages, towns, cities and resource nodes through carts, pack animals, river craft and coastal routes. Cubical weights, scales, seals and shared measures made dispersed production comparable. External connections extended toward Shortugai, the Gulf and Mesopotamia, where texts mention Meluhha, Dilmun and Magan.

However, imported objects prove contact rather than colonies, and standards prove coordination rather than one royal monopoly. Harappan urbanism was sustained by internal interdependence first and long-distance exchange second, through institutions whose exact balance of market, redistribution and direct procurement remains unresolved.

**Marks logic:** Builds a complete commodity chain and adds named sites, transport, standards and institutional limits.

## Original Mains 4 - 15 marks

**Question:** “Harappan society was differentiated, but its hierarchy cannot be read directly.” Discuss. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Model answer:**

Archaeology clearly indicates social and occupational differentiation in the Harappan world. Large cities coexisted with towns, specialist settlements and villages. Within settlements, houses varied in size, facilities and access to wells; rare stones, metal ornaments and jewellery were unevenly distributed; craft areas reveal specialised work. Seals and small weights in a large Banawali house suggest affluent economic activity, while workshops at Chanhudaro and Lothal demonstrate occupational concentration. Cemeteries add variation in body treatment and grave goods, and skeletal analysis can reveal health, diet, trauma and mobility.

Yet none of these observations names a social category. A large house may represent wealth, household size, work or rebuilding. Grave goods are selected mortuary offerings, not a transparent account of status in life. Workshop specialisation does not by itself establish caste, slavery or hereditary guilds. Even female figurines cannot prove matriarchy.

The safest reconstruction therefore triangulates independent evidence: domestic space, access to rare goods, labour organisation, settlement hierarchy and burial treatment. This supports unequal access, differentiated occupations and status distinctions. But the undeciphered script and limited samples prevent recovery of a complete class structure, property law or caste order. Harappan society was neither demonstrably egalitarian nor readable through modern social labels.

**Marks logic:** Uses multiple social datasets, tests each limitation and reaches a calibrated verdict.

## Original Mains 5 - 20 marks

**Question:** Examine the nature of Harappan political organisation through evidence of standardisation, urban works and regional variation. (Answer in 250-300 words.)

**Model answer:**

Harappan political organisation must be reconstructed without readable administrative texts, named rulers or an agreed palace. The secure starting point is strong coordination. Common signs, seals, graded weights, modular brick proportions, long-distance exchange and large public works persisted across a vast region. Streets, drains, wells and reservoirs also required mobilisation and recurring maintenance. These features make an absence-of-authority thesis implausible.

A centralised-state model explains wide standardisation and the capacity to coordinate labour. Yet it is weakened by the absence of securely identified royal tombs, monumental palaces, triumphal inscriptions or a known capital. A regional-state or city-state model fits major centres such as Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi and the pronounced variation in city plans, materials and water systems. It must still explain the circulation of shared standards across regions. Corporate or heterarchical models point to limited royal display and propose authority distributed among mercantile, ritual, kin and civic groups; however, the existence and composition of such bodies are not textually confirmed.

Fortifications caution against portraying the civilisation as inherently peaceful, while the limited prominence of weapons and warfare imagery cautions against a military-empire model. Labour is visible in brick-making, construction, craft and maintenance, but workers' legal status is not.

The evidence therefore supports durable institutions capable of integration without identifying their precise constitution. Harappan power was probably multi-scalar: strong enough to maintain standards and cities, yet regionally expressed and not securely reducible to one emperor, priesthood or merchant council.

**Marks logic:** Starts from secure facts, compares three models, includes force and labour, and separates coordination from constitution.

## Original Mains 6 - 20 marks

**Question:** Explain the decline of Mature Harappan urbanism as a regionally uneven transformation rather than a single civilisational collapse. (Answer in 250-300 words.)

**Model answer:**

After about 1900 BCE, the Harappan world experienced a weakening of Mature urban integration rather than simultaneous extinction. Major cities contracted or were abandoned at different times; the use of script, seals and some shared standards declined; specialised production and long-distance exchange changed; and settlement distributions shifted. At the same time, rural communities, crops, livestock, pottery traditions and regional populations continued.

No single cause explains this geography. River-course change, flooding or water loss could damage particular settlements, but hydrological histories differed. Monsoon weakening and aridity could raise agricultural risk, yet environmental proxies have local scales and social responses vary. Contraction of external and internal exchange may have reduced demand and raw-material circulation, but food production did not cease. Political-economic reorganisation may have weakened labour mobilisation and common institutions, although their exact form is unknown. Disease or conflict may have contributed locally, but no civilisation-wide destruction horizon supports an Aryan invasion explanation.

The causal method should therefore join date, place and mechanism: environmental or economic pressure altered resources; households and institutions responded through crop change, migration, settlement contraction or new networks; the resulting pattern differed by region. Late Harappan localisation included both continuity and discontinuity.

Thus “decline” should specify what declined: urban scale, integration, writing, standards or exchange. The better conclusion is a multi-causal, regionally mediated transformation from an integrated urban system into varied post-urban trajectories.

**Marks logic:** Defines the outcome, tests cause families through mechanisms, distinguishes continuity from discontinuity and rejects single-cause determinism.

- Script remains undeciphered.
- Strong evidence: building, craft, food, exchange and settlement.
- Weak access: named rulers, language, law and doctrine.

## Register 2 - Geography and settlement

- West: Sutkagen-dor; north: Manda; east: Alamgirpur; south: Malvan.
- Shortugai is a highland route/procurement outpost in Afghanistan.
- Indus core: Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.
- Ghaggar-Hakra zone: Kalibangan, Banawali, Rakhigarhi.
- Kutch/Gujarat: Dholavira, Lothal, Surkotada, Desalpur and coastal craft sites.
- Harappan geography included plains, semi-arid tracts, islands, coasts and highland routes.
- A mapped extreme must be qualified by phase and survey.
- Palaeochannel, settlement chronology and Sarasvati identification are separate propositions.
- Settlement hierarchy linked villages, craft centres, towns, cities and resource nodes.
- Urbanism depended on rural production.

## Register 3 - Architecture

- Shared grammar: streets, lanes, courtyards, drains, wells, brick modules and enclosed sectors.
- Plans were not identical or perfectly gridded.
- Mud brick was widespread; baked brick was important in water-facing structures.
- Mature brick proportions often approximated 1:2:4.
- Courtyard houses combined residence, work, light and ventilation.
- Side entrances may have controlled street access and privacy.
- “Citadel” means raised/enclosed sector, not proven palace.
- Dholavira used stone and multiple sectors.
- Chanhudaro lacked conventional fortification despite specialist urban function.
- Architecture records labour and maintenance, not a known constitution.
- Exact 2025 GS-I PYQ: salient features of Harappan architecture, 150 words.

## Register 4 - Water and sanitation

- Mohenjo-daro: numerous wells, bathing floors, house drains and street drains.
- Covered drains and soak arrangements required cleaning.
- Great Bath: fitted baked bricks, gypsum mortar, bitumen waterproofing, stairs and outlet.
- Ritual use is plausible; named cult is not known.
- Dholavira: Manhar-Mansar setting, dams, channels, cisterns and reservoirs.
- Kalibangan used distinct wastewater arrangements, including jars/troughs.
- Lothal had drains and a major brick basin.
- Water systems were regionally adapted.
- Maintenance proves civic coordination more securely than a specific government.
- Avoid projecting modern public-health motives as certainty.

## Register 5 - Site precision

- Harappa: type-site, Ravi region, Cemetery R-37, working platforms, crafts, seals and weights.
- Harappa's 12-unit structure is conventionally called a granary; exact function unknown.
- Mohenjo-daro: Great Bath, wells, drains, courtyard houses, workshops, Dancing Girl and male bust.
- Dholavira: Khadir island, stone architecture, sectors, reservoirs and signboard.
- Rakhigarhi: large Haryana complex, drains, crafts, cemetery and scientific potential.
- Kalibangan: ploughed-field marks, fortified sectors and fire installations.
- Lothal: bead/copper craft, measurement, basin and burials.
- Banawali: moat, curved plan, plough model, seals and weights.
- Chanhudaro: unfortified craft specialist settlement.
- Surkotada: fortified Kutch site; equid identification/significance disputed.
- Shortugai: highland procurement node, not automatically an imperial province.
- Desalpur: fortified Harappan site in Kutch.
- Sohgaura: later inscriptional site, not Harappan.

## Register 6 - Subsistence

- Wheat and barley were important.
- Millets and rice had stronger roles in some regions.
- Pulses, sesame and mustard broadened production.
- Cotton linked farming with textile manufacture.
- Cattle were prominent; sheep, goats, buffalo and pigs also occur.
- Fish and wild resources remained significant.
- Horse/equid evidence is rare and disputed.
- Crop presence does not establish dominance, yield or ownership.
- Animal bones require species, age, context and butchery analysis.
- Archaeobotany, zooarchaeology, residues and isotopes answer different questions.
- Urban food support required storage and movement, not necessarily one grain tax.

## Register 7 - Crafts and technology

- Bronze Age does not mean metal-only technology.
- Copper/bronze coexisted with stone, shell, bone, clay, wood and fibre.
- No iron-centred Mature Harappan system.
- Dancing Girl is bronze and produced by lost-wax casting.
- Chanhudaro and Lothal: bead-production evidence.
- Nageshwar: shell specialisation.
- Faience required controlled high-temperature production.
- Spindle whorls, fibres and impressions support spinning/weaving.
- Pottery supports use, chronology and regional comparison; it is not ethnicity.
- Operational chain: source -> preparation -> technique -> object -> use -> discard.
- Workshop specialisation does not prove caste or state ownership.

## Register 8 - Weights, measures and exchange

- Cubical weights occur in graded series.
- Smaller series show binary patterning; higher values include decimal groupings.
- Shell and ivory scales support linear measurement.
- Weights are not coins.
- Standards support comparability and coordination.
- Internal exchange linked farms, herds, mines, quarries, coasts, workshops and cities.
- Transport included carts, pack animals, river craft and coastal boats.
- Meluhha is an external Mesopotamian term linked conventionally with the Indus/eastern sphere.
- Dilmun is associated with the Gulf/Bahrain.
- Magan/Makan is associated with the Oman-linked zone.
- Imported objects prove contact before they prove volume, colony or control.
- External trade was important but did not replace the internal economy.

## Register 9 - Script and communication

- Harappan script remains undeciphered.
- Inscriptions are generally short.
- Signs occur on seals, sealings, tablets, pottery and other objects.
- Several hundred signs are counted depending on variant rules.
- Many inscriptions appear right-to-left.
- Dholavira's signboard demonstrates public display.
- Pattern, direction and context can be studied.
- No secure bilingual text is known.
- No accepted language, king-list, law code or doctrine has been recovered.
- A seal may mark identity, office, commodity, ownership, administration or ritual.
- Similarity to later scripts does not prove direct descent.

## Register 10 - Society and polity

- House-size and facility variation support unequal access.
- Rare materials, craft zones and settlement hierarchy support differentiation.
- Burials are selected mortuary treatments, not a census.
- Cemetery comparison requires sample and preservation control.
- Caste, slavery, matriarchy and property law are not directly established.
- Labour is visible in construction, maintenance, craft and transport.
- Workers' legal status is not directly known.
- Standards and public works demonstrate coordination.
- Central state, regional states and corporate elites are competing models.
- No secure king-list, palace, royal tomb or constitutional text survives.
- Fortifications prevent a simple peaceful-utopia claim.
- Limited military display prevents an automatic conquest-empire model.

## Register 11 - Religion and representation

- Horned seated figure: form is secure; Pashupati/proto-Shiva identification contested.
- Female figurines: varied forms and contexts; one Mother Goddess cult not established.
- Female divinity would not prove matriarchy.
- Pipal and animal motifs indicate symbolic importance without decoded meaning.
- Fire installations may be ritual, domestic or industrial depending on context.
- Great Bath may have had ceremonial use; secure fact is water architecture.
- No Harappan structure is conclusively identified as a monumental temple.
- Burials indicate mortuary practice, not one complete afterlife doctrine.
- Object -> context -> alternatives -> confidence is the correct method.
- Later similarity permits a continuity hypothesis, not automatic identity.

## Register 12 - Decline and transformation

- Post-1900 BCE change was regionally uneven.
- Large-city contraction, reduced script/seal use and changing standards mark deurbanisation.
- Settlement shifts and regional pottery mark localisation.
- Rural communities and subsistence continued.
- River change, flood, aridity, exchange contraction and institutional reorganisation can interact.
- Every cause needs matching chronology, geography and mechanism.
- No civilisation-wide Aryan invasion horizon explains decline.
- Mohenjo-daro skeletons do not form one simultaneous massacre.
- “Decline” should name the variable that declined.
- Transformation combines adaptation, migration, contraction and continuity.

## Register 13 - Scientific archaeology and current linkage

- Radiocarbon: chronology of organic samples and associated contexts.
- Archaeobotany: plant remains and use.
- Zooarchaeology: animal species, age, use and butchery.
- Lipid residues: substances processed in sampled vessels.
- Isotopes: diet or mobility against local baselines.
- Osteology/palaeopathology: body, stress, disease and trauma.
- Provenance: material-source comparison.
- Ancient DNA: biological ancestry and relatedness, not language or religion.
- The 2019 Rakhigarhi result represents one sampled individual.
- PIB, 22 June 2026: ASI transferred Rakhigarhi remains to AnSI for advanced study.
- Planned methods are not completed results.
- Sample -> method -> result -> representativeness must be stated.
