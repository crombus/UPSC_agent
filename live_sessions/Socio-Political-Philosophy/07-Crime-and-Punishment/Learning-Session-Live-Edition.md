> **UPSC Philosophy Optional · Paper II · Socio-Political Philosophy**
>
> **Syllabus:** “Crime and Punishment : Corruption, Mass Violence, Genocide, Capital Punishment.”
>
> **Learning promise:** The session begins with the conditions under which blame can fairly be
> attributed, then separates rival justifications of punishment, and only afterward applies them to
> corruption, mass violence, genocide and capital punishment. Every major position is taught through
> a visual, a plain-language entry, its argument and presuppositions, an example with limits, its
> strongest objection and reply, and its exact UPSC use.

## Lesson 1 — From wrongdoing to responsible agency

Progress: 1/10 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Crime, criminalisation and responsibility

### Visual: six gates before coercive blame

```text
GATE 1          GATE 2          GATE 3          GATE 4          GATE 5          GATE 6
Public rule  -> Conduct      -> Fault        -> Causation    -> Capacity     -> Fair process
in advance     or duty-        attributable    linked to       to understand   + impartial
               based omission  to the agent    the wrong       and control      authority

Failure at any gate can defeat, reduce or redirect punishment.
```

*The visual shows why “something bad happened” is not yet a complete ground for punishing a
particular person.*

### 1. Legal crime and moral wrong are not coextensive

In ordinary conversation, “crime” often means anything gravely wrong. Philosophy must be more
careful.

- A **legal crime** is conduct prohibited and punishable under a valid criminal rule.
- A **moral wrong** violates a justified ethical norm.
- A **sociological account** asks how institutions and power classify conduct as deviant.

The three questions overlap but do not collapse. A valid prohibition may be morally unjust, while
some serious moral failures may fall outside criminal law. Therefore legal validity cannot alone
justify coercive blame.

### 2. When may a wrong be criminalised?

A defensible criminalisation claim normally needs more than disapproval. The state must show:

1. **harm or public wrong** serious enough to concern citizens collectively;
2. **rights-consistency**, so that protection does not destroy the status it claims to defend;
3. **fair notice**, because secret, retrospective or indeterminate rules cannot guide agency;
4. **culpability**, because accidental involvement is not equivalent to blameworthy action;
5. **proportionality**, because criminal law is a severe form of public condemnation.

**Strong objection:** Harm is contested, and public morality may itself be oppressive.

**Reply:** The objection supports a stricter test, not the abandonment of every criminal rule.
Coercion should track demonstrable public wrong, attributable responsibility and equal rights,
rather than moral dislike alone.

**Residual problem:** Reasonable citizens may still disagree about where a private wrong becomes a
public wrong. Philosophy supplies criteria, not an automatic list.

### 3. Conduct, omission and the limited use of *actus reus*

The expression **external act or culpable omission (*actus reus*)** is used here only as a
philosophical prerequisite. Punishment should attach to attributable conduct, not to an unwanted
thought or social identity.

An omission becomes relevant only where an agent had a duty or responsibility, knew or could
reasonably know what was required, and possessed sufficient capacity to act.

**Bounded Indian hypothetical:** A duty-bound public official knowingly suppresses an emergency
warning that the office is responsible for issuing. The omission matters because an assigned duty,
knowledge and avoidable non-performance connect the agent to the public wrong. The example does not
state any legal offence or outcome.

### 4. Fault, causation and the limited use of *mens rea*

The **fault element (*mens rea*)** grades answerability through such categories as intention,
knowledge, recklessness and negligence. These terms do not imply that every legal offence has an
identical mental requirement. Their philosophical role is narrower: punishment should correspond to
the agent’s attributable culpability.

```text
INTENTION  -> result or conduct is aimed at
KNOWLEDGE  -> relevant circumstance or consequence is known
RECKLESSNESS -> unjustified risk is consciously taken
NEGLIGENCE -> required care is culpably absent
ACCIDENT   -> harmful result without adequate fault
```

Causal contribution is also insufficient by itself. A person may be part of a causal chain without
the knowledge, control or duty required for blame. Conversely, an official may be blameworthy for an
omission even without a dramatic physical act.

### 5. Justification and excuse

| Question | Justification | Excuse |
|---|---|---|
| Was the act wrongful in the circumstances? | says **no** | says **yes** |
| What happens to blame? | blame is defeated because the act was permissible | blame is reduced or defeated because agency was impaired |
| Safe illustration | necessary and proportionate defence against an unjust threat | serious duress or incapacity reducing control |
| Common error | treating every beneficial result as justified | treating mitigation as proof of innocence |

This distinction protects two separate judgments: what conduct was permissible and how fully the
agent can be blamed.

### 6. Free will, determinism and responsibility

| Position | Responsibility claim | Penal attraction | Main difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Libertarian freedom** | genuine alternatives are required for desert | strongly protects authorship | hard to reconcile with complete causal explanation |
| **Hard determinism** | basic desert is undermined | redirects response toward protection and rehabilitation | may treat persons as risks rather than answerable agents |
| **Compatibilism** | reasons-responsive understanding and control can coexist with causation | supports graduated responsibility | the threshold of adequate control remains contestable |

The practical standard used here is compatibilist. It asks whether the conduct issued from an
adequately functioning capacity to recognise reasons and guide action, or whether coercion,
incapacity or seriously diminished control defeated that capacity. Causal explanation may therefore
change the degree or kind of response while leaving meaningful differences among purposive,
risk-taking, careless and accidental conduct.

### 7. Social causes without erased agency

Poverty, exclusion, trauma, distorted incentives or violent socialisation may shape conduct. Two
opposite mistakes follow:

```text
STRUCTURE EXPLAINS EVERYTHING             STRUCTURE EXPLAINS NOTHING
          |                                          |
agency disappears; persons                 unequal opportunity and coercive
become mere effects                         conditions disappear
          \                                          /
           \                                        /
            --> GRADED RESPONSIBILITY <--
       ask knowledge, control, options, pressure and contribution
```

The better approach distinguishes causal explanation from moral exculpation. Structure may reduce
control, alter available options or weaken the state’s standing to punish, but it does not
automatically cancel agency.

### 8. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A reasons-responsive test simply hides the free-will problem. If every response has
causes, the offender never truly originates action.

**Reply:** Public responsibility need not solve the entire metaphysics of causation. It can track
whether the person understood reasons, could deliberate, and possessed ordinary control relative to
coercion, incapacity and accident.

**Residual:** Compatibilism secures a workable practice of graduated responsibility, but disagreement
continues over whether this grounds ultimate desert or only fair social accountability.

### UPSC application

- **Use:** open punishment answers by separating wrong, attribution and sanction.
- **PYQ link:** the 2023 juvenile question requires fault and developmental control, not harm alone.
- **High-value distinction:** causal contribution ≠ moral responsibility.
- **Trap:** *actus reus* and *mens rea* are philosophical prerequisites here, not an invitation to
  write a criminal-law essay.

### Revision notes

1. Legal crime and moral wrong overlap but are not identical.
2. Criminalisation requires a public justification, not moral dislike.
3. Punish conduct or a duty-based omission, never thought or identity alone.
4. Fault grades responsibility through intention, knowledge, recklessness and negligence.
5. Causation does not by itself establish blame.
6. Justification defends the act; excuse concedes wrong but mitigates the actor’s blame.
7. Fair notice, reliable proof, impartial authority and equal application are moral conditions.
8. Compatibilism grounds responsibility in reasons-responsive understanding and control.
9. Deterministic explanation may mitigate without producing automatic acquittal.
10. Structural causation and personal agency must be assessed together.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** State the six gates before punishment.

**Application:** A teenager joins a destructive crowd after sustained peer pressure but understands
that serious harm may occur. Which facts matter before assigning punishment?

**Model route:** Identify conduct, knowledge or recklessness, causal contribution, maturity,
susceptibility to influence, available alternatives, degree of control and procedural reliability.
The harm must be named fully, but gravity alone does not establish adult-equivalent culpability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Explaining pressure is not the same as declaring the agent innocent.

## Lesson 2 — The architecture of punishment

Progress: 2/10 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Aim, liability, severity and proportionality

### Visual: one word, three different questions

```text
                       "WHY PUNISH?"
                              |
          +-------------------+-------------------+
          |                   |                   |
       Q1 AIM              Q2 LIABILITY        Q3 AMOUNT
 Why have a penal       Whom may the state   How severe may
 institution?           burden?              the response be?
          |                   |                   |
 prevention /           guilt + fair proof   ordinal ranking +
 protection / repair    + attributable act   cardinal ceiling
```

*The visual prevents the most common error: treating every penal theory as an answer to the same
question.*

### 1. Punishment is more than imposed hardship

Punishment is an authoritative response to attributed wrongdoing. It normally includes:

- an imposed burden or deprivation;
- a claim that the person is answerable for a public wrong;
- condemnation or censure;
- an institutional authority purporting to act in the name of a political community.

A tax, fee or quarantine may burden a person without condemning culpable wrongdoing. This is why the
meaning, addressee and authority of punishment matter alongside its physical severity.

### 2. Hart’s three-question grid

H. L. A. Hart’s distinction between the **general justifying aim**, the **distribution of
punishment**, and its **amount** clarifies what each theory can and cannot do.

| Question | What must be justified? | Strong candidate | Characteristic failure if used alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1: aim | existence of the institution | prevention, protection or repair | pure desert may not explain why the state should inflict suffering |
| Q2: liability | who may be punished | guilt and attributable culpability | pure utility may select an innocent person if doing so helps |
| Q3: amount | permissible severity | proportionality and humane ceiling | unconstrained prevention can escalate without limit |

**Argument for a mixed view:**

1. Social protection can justify maintaining a penal institution.
2. Benefit alone cannot decide who may be burdened.
3. Guilt and culpability constrain liability.
4. Proportionality and rights constrain severity.
5. Therefore a theory may be consequentialist about institutional aim while deontological about
   individual application.

**Presupposition:** The aim of an institution and the principles controlling its use can be
coherently separated. A pure retributivist may reject that assumption.

### 3. Rival theories at a glance

| Theory | Core claim | Direction | What it protects | Principal danger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Retributive** | culpable wrongdoing deserves proportionate censure | backward-looking | guilt, answerability, equality | revenge or sanctified suffering |
| **Deterrent** | sanction reduces future offending | forward-looking | future security | instrumentalising the offender |
| **Preventive/incapacitative** | restraint blocks further harm | forward-looking | immediate protection | indefinite control based on dangerousness |
| **Reformative/rehabilitative** | capacities and conditions for lawful agency can change | forward-looking | dignity and reintegration | paternalism or compulsory treatment |
| **Expressive/denunciatory** | public response condemns the violated norm | public/backward | meaning and victim recognition | censure does not itself justify hard treatment |
| **Communicative** | the state addresses the offender as an answerable member | relational | continued civic standing | coerced “dialogue” and doubtful state standing |
| **Restorative** | affected parties address harm, needs and obligations | relational | victim participation and repair | unequal bargaining or privatisation of public wrong |

No entry in the table is self-sufficient. A strong answer asks which question a theory answers and
which constraint it still needs.

### 4. Three justificatory logics

```text
DEONTOLOGICAL          CONSEQUENTIALIST          RELATIONAL
What is owed because   What response best        What form of censure,
of culpable agency?    prevents future harm?     address and repair is fitting?
       |                       |                         |
desert, rights,        deterrence, restraint,    expression, communication,
proportionality        rehabilitation            restoration
```

- **Deontology** refuses to trade the innocent or the excessively punished person for aggregate
  benefit.
- **Consequentialism** insists that state-inflicted suffering needs a future-protective purpose.
- **Relational approaches** ask whether condemnation can recognise victims and still address the
  offender as a member capable of response.

### 5. Ordinal and cardinal proportionality

**Ordinal proportionality** ranks penalties relative to the seriousness of offences. More serious
wrongdoing normally warrants a more serious response.

**Cardinal proportionality** asks how severe the punishment scale may be in the first place. A
perfectly ordered ladder can still be inhuman if every rung is excessive.

**Example with limit:** Suppose two offences are ranked correctly, with the more serious receiving
the heavier sanction. That establishes ordinal fit only. It does not prove that either sanction is
within a morally permissible cardinal ceiling.

### 6. Juveniles: offence-centred and offender-centred judgment

For a juvenile, a proportionate judgment must combine:

| Offence-centred facts | Offender-centred facts |
|---|---|
| gravity and nature of harm | maturity and understanding |
| intention and causal contribution | control and susceptibility to influence |
| victim impact | capacity for reform |
| public need to name the wrong | reliability and safeguards of the process |

The nature of the crime is relevant but not conclusive. Equal concern for victims requires full
recognition of harm; equal justice for the accused requires individualised blameworthiness.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A mixed theory is an unstable compromise. If prevention justifies the institution,
why should desert block useful punishment?

**Reply:** Because institutional benefit does not erase the separateness of persons. The innocent
cannot fairly be used as tools, and the guilty remain rights-holders. Desert operates as a
side-constraint rather than an instruction to maximise suffering.

**Residual:** The theory must still explain why desert sets exactly the proposed upper limit and how
relational aims such as repair fit within it.

### UPSC application

- **Use:** begin any broad justification answer with Q1 aim → Q2 liability → Q3 amount.
- **PYQ link:** the 2026 deontology/consequentialism question tests rival logics; the 2023 juvenile
  question tests both forms of proportionality.
- **Trap:** a theory strong at one question may be weak at another.
- **Marks move:** state the theory’s gain, its characteristic objection, the reply and the residual.

### Revision notes

1. Punishment combines burden, attribution, condemnation and authority.
2. Hart separates institutional aim, liability and severity.
3. Prevention may justify the institution without determining who may be punished.
4. Guilt protects the innocent; proportionality protects against excess.
5. Deontology, consequentialism and relational theory ask different normative questions.
6. Expressive meaning is not yet a complete defence of hard treatment.
7. Incapacitation requires necessity and review, not dangerousness alone.
8. Ordinal proportionality ranks; cardinal proportionality limits the whole scale.
9. Juvenile punishment requires both offence- and offender-centred assessment.
10. A mixed theory needs a principled account of how its parts cohere.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** Which question does the innocent-person objection target most directly?

**Application:** A fictional district proposes punishing one visibly unpopular person to reassure
the public after unsolved offences. Diagnose the proposal.

**Model route:** The proposal may claim a preventive Q1 benefit, but it fails Q2 because liability
requires proof of guilt and attributable conduct. It also corrupts punishment’s expressive meaning
by attaching condemnation to the innocent.

**Misconception to avoid:** A socially useful display is not punishment justly distributed.

## Lesson 3 — Retribution, deterrence and incapacitation

Progress: 3/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Desert, prevention and public protection

### Visual: backward and forward justifications

```text
PAST CULPABLE WRONG                                FUTURE RISK OF HARM
        |                                                  |
        v                                                  v
RETRIBUTION                                      DETERRENCE / INCAPACITATION
"answer because you did it"                     "respond so harm is less likely"
        |                                                  |
guilt + desert + proportion                     expected effect + necessity
        |                                                  |
risk: suffering becomes an end                  risk: person becomes a means
```

### 1. Kantian retribution

Kantian retribution treats the offender as a responsible rational agent. Punishment must answer to
the crime committed; it may not be imposed merely to generate social utility.

**Argument:**

1. A culpable offender acts on a maxim incompatible with equal right.
2. Attributable agency makes the offender answerable.
3. Public punishment repudiates the wrongful maxim.
4. A proportionate response therefore recognises rather than bypasses agency.

The non-instrumental point is crucial: punishing a person only to frighten others treats that person
as a device.

### 2. Hegelian restoration of right

For Hegel, crime negates right by asserting a wrongful principle. Punishment negates that negation
and restores the public order of right. The response is not private retaliation; it is an
institutional denial of the offender’s wrongful claim.

**Gain:** The theory explains why censure must publicly reaffirm a norm.

**Limit:** The abstract idea of restoring right does not independently calculate the proper
severity of a sanction.

### 3. Retribution and human rights

Retribution is not inherently hostile to rights. A limiting form can insist:

- no punishment without guilt;
- no punishment without fair trial and reliable proof;
- no penalty beyond proportionate desert;
- no instrumental sacrifice of one person for social benefit.

The danger begins when “desert” is used to justify degrading treatment, disregard unequal defence,
or close every path to reform.

**Strong objection:** Suffering does not undo the original suffering, and desert does not explain
why the state should add pain.

**Reply:** Retribution can function as a principle of liability and upper limit, not a command to
maximise pain. It names answerability and publicly affirms the violated right.

**Residual:** Even deserved hard treatment still needs a humane institutional purpose. Irreversible
execution faces a separate dignity and fallibility test.

### 4. Deterrence

Deterrence justifies a sanction by its expected reduction of future offending.

- **Specific deterrence** aims to discourage the punished person.
- **General deterrence** aims to discourage others.

Its argument depends on four propositions:

1. relevant agents notice and respond to expected costs;
2. sanctions are sufficiently known and credible;
3. the preventive gain exceeds direct and collateral harm;
4. less harmful alternatives are inadequate.

Benthamite reasoning asks whether punishment prevents a greater evil. Beccarian restraint stresses
calibration and the importance of credible, timely enforcement rather than theatrical severity.

### 5. The innocent-person and excessive-severity objections

If aggregate prevention is the only value, punishing an innocent person could be permitted when it
produces enough reassurance or fear. The same logic can escalate severity without a ceiling.

**Rule-utilitarian reply:** Stable legality, accurate proof and due process protect public trust and
produce better long-term consequences.

**Residual:** Critics argue that the innocent have rights that constrain utility independently, not
merely because rights usually work well.

### 6. Incapacitation

Incapacitation protects the public by restricting an offender’s opportunity to cause further harm.
Its moral test is **necessity**, not vengeance.

| Legitimate question | Dangerous substitute |
|---|---|
| What restraint is necessary to address a demonstrated risk? | How dangerous does this person seem? |
| Is the restraint proportionate and reviewable? | Can confinement be extended indefinitely? |
| Is a less restrictive protection feasible? | Is maximum restriction politically reassuring? |

Dangerousness alone can produce preventive detention without fair limits. Public protection must
remain connected to proved wrongdoing, reliable risk assessment, proportionality and review.

### 7. Example with limit

**Hypothetical:** A person convicted after fair process is restrained to prevent a demonstrated and
continuing risk. The illustration shows the protective logic of incapacitation.

**Limit:** It does not prove that every severe penalty is necessary, that risk prediction is
infallible, or that execution is needed when secure custody can also prevent reoffending.

### 8. Synthesis

```text
RETRIBUTION contributes: guilt + answerability + upper limit
DETERRENCE contributes: future-protective purpose + empirical burden
INCAPACITATION contributes: immediate safety + necessity test

NONE ALONE contributes all of:
fair procedure + dignity + victim recognition + repair + reintegration
```

### UPSC application

- **2020 retribution/human-rights:** distinguish limiting retribution from degrading or excessive
  penalties.
- **2024 death-penalty deterrence:** demand comparative evidence over a feasible alternative.
- **2026 capital punishment:** explain deontological desert separately from consequential
  prevention before criticising either.
- **Trap:** severity does not prove deterrent effectiveness.

### Revision notes

1. Kant rejects punishment imposed merely as a means to social benefit.
2. Hegel reads punishment as public restoration of violated right.
3. Retribution differs from revenge through legality, impartiality and proportionality.
4. Limiting retribution protects the innocent and caps severity.
5. Desert still needs a public and humane institutional purpose.
6. General deterrence addresses others; specific deterrence addresses the offender.
7. Deterrence is empirical and comparative, not implied by harshness.
8. Rule-utilitarian safeguards answer but do not eliminate the rights objection.
9. Incapacitation must satisfy necessity, proportionality and review.
10. Secure restraint and execution are not equivalent protective claims.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** Why is the innocent-person objection aimed at deterrence rather than retribution?

**Application:** A government claims that the harshest available penalty is justified because “the
public must learn a lesson.” What must be added before this becomes an argument?

**Model route:** Specify general deterrence, show credible comparative preventive effect, establish
guilt and fair procedure, rule out less harmful alternatives, and satisfy proportionality and
rights. Without these steps, the statement is rhetoric.

**Misconception to avoid:** Public anger is not evidence of either desert or deterrent effect.

## Lesson 4 — Reform, censure, communication and repair

Progress: 4/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Dignity, victims, reintegration and disciplinary power

```text
    |                        |                         |                      |

Feinberg                Duff                       victim/offender/        dignity +
condemnation            answer + penance           community process      reintegration
    \___________________________ rights and proportionality ___________________________/
                                      |
                                      v
                         FOUCAULT'S WARNING:
           "reform" can also become surveillance, normalisation and control
```

### 1. Reformative or rehabilitative punishment

Reformative theory treats the offender as capable of moral development and social reintegration.
It may address ignorance, addiction, trauma, exclusion or distorted incentives without denying
responsibility.

**Dignity argument:**

1. A person is not reducible to the worst act committed.
2. Responsible capacities can be impaired, developed or rebuilt.
3. A humane state should reduce future wrongdoing through capability rather than degradation.
4. Punishment should preserve a route back to equal membership.

**Objection:** Compulsory treatment can pathologise nonconformity and become indefinite.

**Reply:** Rehabilitation must remain proportionate, reviewable, rights-bounded and consensual
where consent is possible.

**Residual:** The state must distinguish restoring capacity from remoulding a person into an
officially preferred personality.

### 2. Retribution and reform need not be opposites

Retribution preserves answerability: the offender is not merely a patient. Reform preserves
futurity: the offender is not permanently frozen in a past act.

```text
DESERT WITHOUT REFORM             REFORM WITHOUT DESERT
person frozen as offender         person treated only as defective patient
          \                                /
           \                              /
       ACCOUNTABLE REINTEGRATION
 guilt and proportion set limits; humane change shapes the response
```

### 3. Feinberg: punishment as expressive condemnation

Joel Feinberg distinguishes punishment from a mere penalty through its **expressive function**.
Both may impose hardship, but punishment communicates authoritative condemnation of wrongdoing.

This explains:

- why wrongful conviction injures reputation and civic standing even after compensation;
- why degrading forms of punishment matter beyond their numerical severity;
- why acquittal is a vindication, not simply release;
- why victims may seek public recognition of the wrong, not only material repayment.

**Objection:** If condemnation is the point, a declaration should suffice.

**Reply:** Defenders say a proportionate burden makes censure serious rather than cheap.

**Residual:** Hard treatment then becomes a means of making expression credible and still needs
independent justification.

### 4. Duff: communication, secular penance and standing

R. A. Duff develops censure into **communication**. Punishment is addressed to the offender as a
responsible member of the political community and seeks acknowledgement, reform and reconciliation.
Hard treatment functions as **secular penance**: a burden focused on the wrong and on a possible
responsible response.

| Expressive account | Communicative account |
|---|---|
| society speaks about the wrong | the polity addresses the wrongdoer |
| offender is object of judgment | offender remains a participant capable of answering |
| central aim is condemnation | central aim includes repentance, reform and reconciliation |

The decisive presupposition is the state’s **standing to punish**. A political order that has
systematically denied a person the conditions of citizenship weakens its authority to demand
penance.

**Objection:** A burden backed by force is a strained form of invitation.

**Reply:** Communication need not compel repentance, and refusal should bring no added penalty.

**Residual:** The coercive setting continues to limit the dialogical ideal.

### 5. Foucault: genealogy, not justification

Michel Foucault’s *Discipline and Punish* analyses the movement from spectacular bodily punishment
to continuous discipline. Modern institutions observe, classify, timetable, train and normalise.
**Panopticism** names asymmetrical possible visibility that induces subjects to regulate themselves.

Foucault’s use here is strictly critical:

1. humanitarian language may coexist with intensified control;
2. reform can shift the target from the body to conduct, habits and identity;
3. knowledge about offenders can grow together with power over them.

**Compulsory caveat:** This is a genealogy of penal power, not a theory of just desert, liability or
severity. It cannot replace retribution, deterrence or rights as a justification.

**Strong objection:** If power forms every subject and norm, Foucault appears to lack a standard
from which to criticise power.

**Reply:** The genealogy can reveal contingent practices and open them to resistance.

**Residual:** The absence of an explicit normative criterion remains a real limitation.

### 6. Restorative justice

Restorative justice views crime as a violation of persons and relationships and invites victim,
offender and community to address harms, needs and obligations.

| Axis | Conventional/retributive emphasis | Restorative emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| central question | what rule was broken and what is deserved? | who was harmed and what repair is owed? |
| victim | witness for the state | participant with voice and needs |
| offender | object of adjudication | accountable participant expected to acknowledge and repair |
| outcome | proportionate sanction | restitution, apology, repair, changed conduct, reintegration |
| public dimension | represented by authority | must remain present for serious public wrongs |

**Restitution** is not identical with restoration. Returning value or compensating loss is one
possible outcome; restorative justice is the broader process of acknowledgement, participation,
obligation and reintegration.

### 7. Braithwaite: reintegrative versus stigmatising shaming

- **Stigmatising shaming** condemns the person, severs social bonds and offers no route back.
- **Reintegrative shaming** condemns the act while preserving respect for the person and ending in
  reacceptance.

The theory assumes that inclusion is valued and that the community is willing to reaccept. That
assumption may fail for already-excluded persons or unequal communities.

### 8. Victims and the limits of restoration

Victims’ claims include recognition, safety, participation and repair. They do not automatically
dictate the maximum penalty.

**Strong objections:**

1. Different victim preferences can produce unequal outcomes.
2. Face-to-face processes can reproduce power imbalance or re-traumatise.
3. Treating crime as private harm can erase its public character.

**Replies:**

1. Operate within proportionate upper and lower limits.
2. Require genuine voluntariness, safety, skilled facilitation and screening.
3. Combine restoration with authoritative public condemnation for grave wrongs.

**Residual:** These replies show why restorative justice is conditionally appropriate rather than a
universal substitute for punishment.

### UPSC application

- **2021 reform/dignity:** show both the route back to membership and the paternalism objection.
- **Social justice answers:** use Duff’s standing argument precisely, not as a vague claim that
  deprivation excuses all crime.
- **Juvenile answers:** reintegration and reform carry special weight but remain bounded by public
  recognition of harm.
- **Trap:** Foucault belongs in the critical paragraph, never as the justificatory theory.

### Revision notes

1. Reform recognises capacity for change and social return.
2. Rehabilitation must be proportionate, reviewable and rights-bounded.
3. Retribution protects answerability; reform protects futurity.
4. Feinberg locates punishment’s distinctiveness in authoritative condemnation.
5. Expression alone does not justify hard treatment.
6. Duff treats punishment as communication to a continuing member.
7. Standing to punish depends partly on the justice of the political order.
8. Foucault diagnoses discipline and panopticism but does not justify punishment.
9. Restitution is an outcome; restoration is a participatory process.
10. Reintegrative shaming condemns conduct without fixing a permanent deviant identity.
11. Victim participation must not override voluntariness, equality and public justice.
12. Restoration supplements rather than universally replaces authoritative condemnation.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** Give one sentence each on expression, communication, restoration and reform.

**Application:** A school proposes a restorative meeting after serious vandalism. State four
conditions that make the proposal defensible.

**Model route:** Responsibility must be admitted or fairly established; participation must be
voluntary and safe; power imbalance must be screened; repair must occur within proportionate
limits; and the public wrong should still be named. This is a bounded illustration, not a legal
claim.

**Misconception to avoid:** Restorative justice is not simply “forgive and forget.”

## Lesson 5 — Corruption as a betrayal of entrusted office

Progress: 5/10 | Stage: Application | Subtopic: Public trust and conflicting normative standards

### Visual: when legitimate loyalty becomes corrupt partiality

```text
PRIVATE DOMAIN                              ENTRUSTED PUBLIC ROLE
care for family/friends                     same criteria for similarly placed citizens
special concern may be proper               office exists for a public purpose
           |                                             |
           +---------------- role boundary --------------+
                              |
            use public power to favour the private relation
                              |
                              v
              CONFLICT OF INTEREST / RECUSAL QUESTION
                              |
               concealed or improper private advantage
                              |
                              v
                         CORRUPTION
```

### 1. Definition

**Corruption** is the misuse of entrusted public or institutional power through partial, concealed
or improperly exchanged advantage, thereby diverting an office from its justified purpose.

Bribery is one form, not the whole category. Favouritism, nepotism, embezzlement, regulatory
capture and abuse of discretion can also violate entrusted purpose.

### 2. Why corruption is morally wrong

| Wrong | How it arises |
|---|---|
| **breach of trust** | an entrusted role is used for an alien purpose |
| **violation of impartiality** | similarly placed persons face different criteria |
| **rule-of-law injury** | public rules are displaced by private access |
| **agency injury** | citizens become clients or supplicants rather than equals |
| **institutional injury** | repeated misuse changes expectations and makes honesty costly |

The wrong exists even where a precise monetary loss is hard to calculate. A public role is a
fiduciary relation: its holder must use power for the purpose that justifies entrusting it.

### 3. Particularistic and universalistic standards

**Particularistic norms** give special moral weight to family, friendship, caste or patronage.
**Universalistic norms** require a public official to apply the same justified criteria to similarly
placed persons.

The tension is genuine because private loyalty is not always immoral.

**Argument:** The decisive unit is the role. Friendship or kinship can rightly guide private care,
but authority attached to an office is held on behalf of persons beyond that relationship. Once an
office-holder changes its criteria for an insider, the private bond determines the use of power
whose beneficiaries and burdens are public. The same loyalty that is legitimate in one domain thus
becomes corrupt when it displaces the office’s impartial standard.

The remedy is not emotional detachment. It is **role differentiation**, disclosure of conflict and
recusal where impartial judgment is compromised.

### 4. Example with limit

**Bounded Indian hypothetical:** A member of a public recruitment panel discovers that a close
relative is an applicant. Care for the relative is legitimate in private life. Using the office to
alter criteria is corrupt; disclosure and recusal preserve both the relationship and public
impartiality.

**Limit:** Recusal addresses a conflict in one decision. It does not by itself solve opaque
institutional design or systemic patronage.

### 5. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Universal public rules can be insensitive to context and may disguise the interests
of dominant groups.

**Reply:** Public impartiality need not mean blind sameness. Criteria may recognise relevant
difference, but they must be publicly defensible and applied consistently rather than secretly
altered for insiders.

**Residual:** Disagreement remains over which differences are relevant. Transparency, reason-giving
and review are needed because no rule applies itself.

### 6. What the 2025 PYQ is really asking

The question is not whether family and friendship should disappear. It asks whether corruption
reveals a conflict between two kinds of norm.

```text
CONCEDE: particular duties can be morally genuine
        |
DEFINE: public office carries universal role-obligations
        |
SHOW: private loyalty corrupts when exercised through entrusted power
        |
QUALIFY: public criteria may recognise relevant difference
        |
VERDICT: disclose, recuse and give reviewable reasons
```

### UPSC application

- **2025 Q1(a):** agree with qualification; the conflict is role-specific, not a war between all
  private loyalty and all universal norms.
- **2018 remedies:** disclosure, recusal and transparent criteria directly answer the role problem.
- **Trap:** do not call every form of partiality corrupt.
- **Answer line:** “The moral fault lies not in caring particularly, but in spending public power as
  if it were private property.”

### Revision notes

1. Corruption is misuse of entrusted power, not bribery alone.
2. It breaches trust, impartiality, equal citizenship and the rule of public reasons.
3. Particular obligations can be morally legitimate outside entrusted office.
4. Universalistic standards require publicly defensible criteria in role-governed decisions.
5. Corruption arises when private loyalty displaces the purpose of office.
6. Disclosure and recusal preserve role boundaries.
7. Impartiality may recognise relevant differences; it does not require blind sameness.
8. Public reason-giving and review protect against disguised partiality.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** Define corruption without using the word “bribe.”

**Application:** Why is helping a friend not always corrupt but manipulating a public tender for the
same friend corrupt?

**Model route:** The first may occur within a legitimate private relationship. The second uses
entrusted public power under universal criteria to confer concealed particular advantage, thereby
injuring unrepresented citizens and the purpose of office.

**Misconception to avoid:** Universalism does not require citizens to stop having special personal
relationships.

## Lesson 6 — Corruption: ethical choice, systemic ecology and remedies

Progress: 6/10 | Stage: Application | Subtopic: Causation, regimes, institutions and education

### Visual: the dual diagnosis

```text
                    CORRUPT PRACTICE
                           |
             +-------------+-------------+
             |                           |
        AGENT SIDE                   SYSTEM SIDE
 greed / loyalty / fear          opacity / monopoly /
 weak integrity / choice         discretion / impunity
             |                           |
 moral formation, refusal        transparency, audit,
 disclosure, professional ethos  review, fair enforcement
             \                           /
              \                         /
               ---- COMPLEMENTARITY ----
 Structure changes opportunity and cost;
 education changes willingness, judgment and norms.
```

### 1. Ethical or systemic?

An **ethical diagnosis** locates corruption in choice, character and practical judgment. A
**systemic diagnosis** locates it in opportunities, incentives and institutional equilibrium.

The strongest position is conjunctive: corruption is an ethical act within a systemic ecology.
Structure influences what is easy, rewarded or concealed; it does not commit the act by itself.
Character matters; heroic integrity cannot substitute for sound institutions.

### 2. Principal–agent and collective-action patterns

Two systemic patterns should be distinguished.

| Pattern | Problem | Why a single honest person may not be enough |
|---|---|---|
| **principal–agent** | entrusted officials pursue private advantage because monitoring is weak | stronger oversight can alter detection and sanction |
| **collective-action equilibrium** | many expect everyone else to misuse office, so honesty appears futile or costly | isolated integrity may be punished unless norms change together |

Klitgaard’s formulation—monopoly plus discretion minus accountability—helps identify opportunity.
It does not explain every corrupt choice, because officials still interpret rules and can resist
pressure.

### 3. How regimes condition corruption

No regime type guarantees clean government.

- Autocratic arrangements may concentrate discretion and weaken scrutiny.
- Democracies may expose wrongdoing more readily yet face patronage, campaign-finance or capture
  pressures.
- More observed cases may indicate more underlying abuse, better discovery, or both.

The proper object of comparison is not a label but a mechanism:

```text
Who can scrutinise? -> Can reasons be challenged? -> Are sanctions credible?
       |                        |                         |
information               contestability             accountability
```

**Verdict:** Regimes condition the form, visibility and incentive structure of corruption; they do
not make every individual act inevitable.

### 4. India-facing institutional illustrations

- The **Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988**, amended in **2018**, is an enacted criminal-law
  framework.
- The **Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013** is an enacted institutional-accountability framework.

These are dated legal facts. They do not prove eradication, implementation quality, institutional
independence or any measured effect.

Normatively defensible measures include:

1. transparent selection, procurement and allocation;
2. disclosure and recusal for conflicts of interest;
3. independent audit and accessible grievance channels;
4. credible, fair sanctions under due process;
5. protection for reporting and reasoned review;
6. oversight of the overseer to reduce capture and retaliatory use.

### 5. Value-based education: the six mechanisms

Value-based education forms judgment, character and practical competence around publicly
defensible values such as honesty, impartiality, accountability, dignity and public service.

| Mechanism | What it changes |
|---|---|
| **moral cognition** | recognises conflict of interest and normalised misuse |
| **moral motivation** | supports integrity beyond fear of detection |
| **practical competence** | rehearses disclosure, recusal, refusal and response to pressure |
| **norm correction** | challenges the belief that “everyone is corrupt” |
| **civic agency** | teaches use of transparency, audit and grievance mechanisms |
| **professional identity** | makes integrity part of what it means to hold a role |

The underlying argument is Aristotelian in form: dispositions can be built through habituation and
practice. The **National Education Policy, 2020** may be cited only as a dated policy document that
refers to ethical reasoning and constitutional values, not as evidence of results.

The United Nations Convention against Corruption (**2003**) supplies a dated anchor for
public-sector integrity training and public education, but a UPSC answer should keep the mechanism
central rather than become a treaty recital.

### 6. Strong objections and replies

**Knowledge–action gap:** Knowing the rule does not ensure courageous conduct.

**Reply:** Use dilemma practice, protected refusal, disclosure and institutional support rather
than slogan-based teaching.

**Residual:** Education cannot guarantee courage under retaliation.

**Hidden curriculum:** An institution that teaches integrity while practising opaque admission,
grading or procurement teaches hypocrisy.

**Reply:** The institution’s own procedures must model its stated values.

**Residual:** Institutional reform may therefore be prior to the institution’s educational role.

**Indoctrination:** “Values” can become partisan or sectarian conformity.

**Reply:** Use public-reason values and critical deliberation that permits disagreement.

**Residual:** The boundary between civic formation and imposed comprehensive morality remains
contested.

**Individualisation error:** Education can conceal monopoly, discretion and impunity.

**Reply:** Education is necessary but not sufficient; it changes moral demand while institutional
reform changes opportunity and incentive.

### 7. Why “eradicate” needs a qualified answer

No proposal can honestly guarantee eradication. The defensible standard is whether reforms reduce
hidden discretion, increase justified detection, protect fair process and make ethical refusal
practicable.

### UPSC application

- **2018 Q1(e):** organise measures as prevention → detection → accountability → ethical capacity.
- **2020 Q4(c):** assess mechanisms of scrutiny, visibility and incentive; do not label a current
  regime.
- **2022 Q1(c):** reject the false choice between ethical and systemic explanations.
- **2026 Q1(e):** answer “how” through the six mechanisms and conclude with complementarity.
- **Trap:** statutes and policies are illustrations, not proof of effect.

### Revision notes

1. Corruption is chosen by agents within an incentive structure.
2. Ethical diagnosis without systems becomes moralism.
3. Systemic diagnosis without agency becomes exculpation.
4. Principal–agent and collective-action failures require different remedies.
5. Regimes affect scrutiny, challenge, sanctions and visibility.
6. Observed corruption is not identical with underlying prevalence.
7. India-facing legal references must be classified and bounded.
8. Transparency, recusal, audit, grievance and fair enforcement target opportunity and impunity.
9. Education targets judgment, willingness, skill, norms, civic agency and role identity.
10. Hidden curriculum and the knowledge–action gap are the strongest practical objections.
11. Public-reason values answer, but do not dissolve, the indoctrination objection.
12. Education supplies moral demand; institutions alter opportunity and incentive.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** State the ethical/systemic verdict in one sentence.

**Application:** A department adds an integrity lecture but retains opaque procurement and
retaliates against internal reporting. Diagnose the failure.

**Model route:** The lecture targets willingness while the institution preserves opportunity,
impunity and a corrupt hidden curriculum. Practical competence and moral motivation will be
undermined unless transparency, protection, audit and review accompany education.

**Misconception to avoid:** Teaching values is not a substitute for making honest conduct feasible.

## Lesson 7 — Structural harm, mass violence and differentiated responsibility

Progress: 7/10 | Stage: Application | Subtopic: Scale, mechanisms and graded agency

### Visual: from widespread harm to differentiated responsibility

```text
LARGE-SCALE HARM
      |
      +--> Was there direct or organised violence?
      |        |
      |        +--> dehumanisation / propaganda / obedience / bureaucracy
      |
      +--> Was harm produced through institutional diversion?
               |
               +--> call "mass violence" only if a credible harm-chain is shown;
                    otherwise use "structural harm" or "systemic injustice"
      |
      v
WHO DID WHAT, KNEW WHAT, COULD DO WHAT?
leaders -> planners -> direct actors -> enablers -> bystanders -> resisters
      |
      v
guilt remains individual; political and organisational responsibility may be collective
```

### 1. Is corruption a form of mass violence?

Ordinary corruption is not direct physical violence. Large-scale corruption can nevertheless
produce structurally violent effects where diversion of essential resources foreseeably exposes
many people to serious deprivation, danger or exclusion.

A defensible analogy needs four links:

1. entrusted power is diverted at scale;
2. an essential service or protection is impaired;
3. widespread serious harm is foreseeable;
4. a credible causal connection is shown rather than assumed.

**For the analogy:** diffuse victims, cumulative harm and institutional concealment can make severe
injury socially invisible.

**Against the analogy:** the language of violence may erase differences in physical agency,
intention and remedy.

**Verdict:** Use “mass violence” only as a qualified analogy with a demonstrated harm-chain.
Otherwise write **structural harm** or **systemic injustice**. Never infer genocide from corruption.

### 2. Mass violence

Mass violence is large-scale violence against populations produced through organised coercion,
collective mobilisation or cascading participation. It is broader than genocide.

| Mechanism | Moral effect |
|---|---|
| **dehumanisation** | presents persons as contaminants, threats or non-persons |
| **moral exclusion** | withdraws ordinary restraints from an out-group |
| **diffusion of responsibility** | lets each participant deny ownership of the whole |
| **obedience and bureaucracy** | recasts harmful action as routine role-performance |
| **polarisation and propaganda** | suppresses ambiguity, dissent and shared standing |
| **security dilemma** | presents pre-emption as unavoidable self-defence |

These mechanisms explain participation. They do not automatically excuse it.

### 3. Arendt and the banality of evil

Hannah Arendt’s account of the **banality of evil** shows how grave wrongdoing can be carried by
clichés, careerism, bureaucratic routine and failure of independent judgment rather than by a
mythically monstrous psychology.

**Presupposition:** Responsibility includes a duty to judge one’s role rather than treat orders as
self-validating.

**Objection:** “Banality” may understate ideology, hatred and initiative.

**Reply:** The thesis need not deny ideological commitment. It explains how ordinary role-obedience
can become a vehicle for extraordinary wrongdoing.

**Residual:** Psychology and degree of guilt must be assessed separately; ordinary appearance does
not imply diminished responsibility.

### 4. Five different forms of responsibility

| Type | Core claim | Holder | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| **criminal guilt** | this person planned, ordered, incited or committed the act | individual | requires personal proof |
| **organisational liability** | a decision-capable body is responsible for what it authorised | institution | needs genuine procedures and agency |
| **political responsibility** | a polity owes reform, memory or reparation for what institutions did in its name | citizens/institutions | does not create inherited guilt |
| **responsibility for omission** | a person failed to act despite knowledge, capacity and acceptable cost | individual by role | must be graded by risk and ability |
| **inherited guilt** | membership or ancestry itself makes a person guilty | rejected | reproduces indiscriminate logic |

The controlling formula is: **guilt is individual; responsibility admits collective forms**.

### 5. Role-differentiated responsibility

Leaders, planners, direct perpetrators, enablers, bystanders and resisters do not bear identical
responsibility. Assess:

- contribution;
- knowledge;
- authority;
- capacity to refuse or prevent;
- risk attached to resistance;
- benefit received;
- later duties of truth, repair and institutional reform.

### 6. Religious identity and violence: three controls

1. **Doctrine versus mobilisation:** A tradition’s texts are not identical with a movement’s
   political organisation. Explanation must examine leadership, incentives, impunity and grievance.
2. **Communal violence, pogrom and genocide:** Reciprocal conflict, a one-sided attack with official
   acquiescence, and destruction of a group as such have different structures and evidentiary tests.
3. **Identity as target versus identity as motive:** Victims may be selected by an identity marker
   while the organisers pursue power, territory, labour or resources.

No community, country, party, government, movement or episode is classified in this session.
Where application is requested, use criteria and evidence requirements.

### 7. Prevention

Philosophical prevention protects personhood through:

- independent information;
- protection of dissent;
- universal civic standing;
- accountability for command;
- refusal of dehumanising classifications;
- early challenge to propaganda and polarisation;
- memory and education joined to institutional checks.

Moral exhortation without contestable institutions is insufficient.

### 8. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Collective concepts invite guilt by association.

**Reply:** Differentiate criminal guilt from organisational and political responsibility. The
latter can ground duties of reform and reparation without declaring every member culpable.

**Residual:** Political responsibility remains difficult where citizens lacked effective voice or
faced severe coercion. Duties must be graded rather than presumed identical.

### UPSC application

- **2019 corruption/mass violence:** define violence’s criteria before testing the analogy.
- **Future mass-violence stems:** explain mechanisms, then allocate responsibility by role.
- **Trap:** bureaucracy diffuses action but does not erase judgment.
- **Trap:** never infer guilt from community membership.

### Revision notes

1. Corruption becomes analogous to mass violence only through a shown harm-chain.
2. Structural harm is often the more accurate term.
3. Mass violence is broader than genocide.
4. Dehumanisation and moral exclusion withdraw ordinary restraints.
5. Bureaucracy fragments action but not necessarily responsibility.
6. Arendt explains thoughtlessness without excusing grave wrongdoing.
7. Criminal guilt requires personal proof.
8. Organisational and political responsibility can be collective.
9. Inherited guilt must be rejected.
10. Roles differ by knowledge, contribution, capacity and risk.
11. Doctrine, mobilisation, target identity and motive must be separated.
12. Prevention joins civic equality, information, dissent and command accountability.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** Complete the formula: guilt is ___; responsibility admits ___.

**Application:** A clerk processes a small part of a harmful bureaucratic chain and claims, “I did
not design the policy.” What must be examined?

**Model route:** Examine knowledge, role, causal contribution, authority, capacity to refuse,
foreseeability and cost of resistance. Limited role may reduce responsibility but does not
automatically erase it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Distributed action does not mean ownerless action.

## Lesson 8 — Genocide and the destruction of a group as such

Progress: 8/10 | Stage: Application | Subtopic: Specific intent, group continuity and evidence

### Visual: scale is not the defining test

```text
WIDESPREAD OR LARGE-SCALE HARM
              |
              +--> MASS VIOLENCE
              |    scale + collective pattern
              |
              +--> CRIMES AGAINST HUMANITY
              |    widespread/systematic attack on civilians
              |
              +--> GENOCIDE
                   one or more specified acts
                   + intent to destroy, wholly or partly,
                   + a national, ethnical, racial or religious group
                   + as such

Numbers alone never supply the special intent.
```

### 1. Convention definition

Under the **Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (1948)**,
genocide consists of specified acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a
national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such.

The four protected categories must not be expanded in a legal-definition answer. Political groups
are not included in that Convention definition. This legal limit is not moral permission; other
grave wrongs may fall under other categories.

### 2. Lemkin’s broader conception

Raphael Lemkin coined **genocide** in *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe* (**1944**). His conception was
not simply a synonym for mass killing. It concerned a coordinated attack on the foundations of a
group’s continued life—institutions, culture, language, religion, economic existence, security and
dignity.

**Argument for distinctiveness:**

1. Some crimes harm persons because they are members of a targeted group.
2. A group persists through institutions, shared practices, transmission and memory.
3. An attack may aim at ending that shared life as such.
4. The wrong therefore exceeds the sum of separately counted individual injuries.
5. A distinct concept is needed to name group-directed destructive purpose.

The Convention adopted a narrower legal definition than Lemkin’s broader conception. Do not claim
that it reproduces his account in full.

### 3. Genocide, mass violence, crimes against humanity and war crime

| Category | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| **mass violence** | scale and collective pattern; no single fixed special-intent test |
| **genocide** | specified acts plus intent to destroy a protected group as such |
| **crimes against humanity** | specified acts within a widespread or systematic attack on civilians |
| **war crime** | serious violation connected to armed conflict |

The comparison is conceptual. It does not classify any event or actor.

### 4. Why group destruction is a distinctive moral wrong

Genocide attacks:

- persons as bearers of an identity marked for elimination;
- intergenerational continuity;
- language, memory and shared institutions;
- human plurality itself, by claiming authority to decide which group may continue to exist.

**Individualist objection:** Every concrete injury is suffered by an individual, so “group harm”
adds no distinct moral subject.

**Reply:** The loss of a form of shared life affects survivors, future members and the human world
in ways not captured by adding isolated injuries.

**Residual:** The moral status of groups must still be argued; group protection must never justify
sacrificing dissenting individuals within the group.

### 5. Specific intent and evidentiary discipline

Genocide requires a group-directed destructive purpose. Scale, horror or outrage alone is
insufficient.

Intent may sometimes be inferred from an organised pattern and context, but inference is not
invention. A strong answer states both:

1. direct evidence of intent may be difficult;
2. evidentiary difficulty does not justify abandoning the defining element.

### 6. Obedience, denial and responsibility

“Following orders” does not automatically excuse a manifest wrong. Leaders, commanders, planners,
direct actors, propagandists and enabling officials raise different questions of knowledge,
authority and contribution.

Denial can continue injury to memory, testimony and survivor standing. Yet criminal guilt remains
individual and cannot be inherited by later members of a population.

### 7. Strongest objections and replies

**Protected-group limitation:** The Convention omits political and many social groups.

**Reply:** Acknowledge the legal limitation and use other categories for wider moral condemnation;
do not rewrite the treaty through rhetoric.

**Proof problem:** The special-intent requirement can make conviction difficult.

**Reply:** Patterns and context may support inference under appropriate standards, but outrage
cannot replace evidence.

**Concept inflation:** Calling every mass atrocity genocide appears to express moral seriousness.

**Reply:** Inflation weakens the concept and obscures the different responsibility and prevention
questions attached to other grave crimes.

### UPSC application

- **Literal syllabus duty:** Genocide has no primary-owned question in the held 2018–2026 cycle,
  but it remains independently compulsory.
- **Answer route:** definition → protected groups and special intent → Lemkin/Convention distinction
  → distinctive wrong → evidence problem → qualified conclusion.
- **Trap:** not every massacre or mass violence is genocide.
- **Trap:** do not name an actual case, community or government in this session.

### Revision notes

1. Genocide requires specified acts and special intent.
2. The protected groups are national, ethnical, racial and religious.
3. Scale alone does not establish genocide.
4. Lemkin coined the term in 1944.
5. Lemkin’s conception included destruction of a group’s institutional and cultural foundations.
6. The 1948 Convention is narrower than Lemkin’s broader idea.
7. Political groups are outside the Convention’s protected list.
8. Genocide differs from mass violence and crimes against humanity by its group-destruction intent.
9. The distinctive wrong concerns shared life and intergenerational continuity.
10. Intent may be inferred from evidence but must never be invented from outrage.
11. Orders do not automatically excuse manifest wrongdoing.
12. Memory and reparation do not imply inherited criminal guilt.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** What element distinguishes genocide from mass violence?

**Application:** Why is “very large death toll” an insufficient definition?

**Model route:** Numbers establish scale, not the required intent to destroy a protected group as
such. A correct answer identifies the protected-group object, specified acts and special purpose,
then separates moral gravity from legal classification.

**Misconception to avoid:** A narrower legal definition does not imply that excluded atrocities are
morally acceptable.

## Lesson 9 — Capital punishment as the hardest penal test

```text
  desert      deterrence  security  public censure
```

### 1. The philosophical issue

Capital punishment is the state’s intentional execution of a convicted offender. Its finality
eliminates the person whose agency, reform and rights ground penal theory. The burden is therefore
not merely to justify punishment, but to justify an irreversible killing after the immediate threat
has been placed under custody.

### 2. The retributivist case

The retributivist argues that exceptionally grave culpable killing may deserve the gravest
proportionate censure. Kantian non-instrumentality avoids making the offender a mere deterrent
example; Hegelian public justice distinguishes legal response from private revenge.

**Argument:**

1. the offence represents the most serious attack on equal right;
2. guilt has been reliably established;
3. proportionate desert requires a response matching gravity;
4. execution is claimed to be that response.

**Characteristic difficulty:** Desert may establish liability to severe punishment without proving
that the state may kill. Dignity, fallibility and the cardinal ceiling remain independent tests.

### 3. The deterrence case

The consequentialist claim is not “death is severe.” It is:

1. potential offenders respond to the expected sanction;
2. execution prevents more grave harm than a feasible alternative;
3. the additional benefit exceeds all direct and collateral costs;
4. no less final measure achieves the same protection.

This is a comparative empirical burden. Philosophical severity alone establishes no measured
deterrent effect, so an answer must not fabricate one.

**Characteristic difficulty:** Even proven extra prevention would still face rights,
proportionality and innocent-person constraints.

### 4. Incapacitation and necessity

Execution permanently prevents reoffending. Secure custody can also incapacitate. Therefore public
safety alone does not establish the necessity of killing.

The defender must show why a less final protective alternative is inadequate. Without that added
step, incapacitation supports restraint but not execution.

### 5. Public affirmation and victims

A grave wrong demands public recognition. Punishment may affirm the equal value of victims and
condemn the violation.

**Objection:** Rejecting execution may appear to minimise the victim’s life.

**Reply:** Full recognition, proportionate censure, safety and repair do not logically require the
irreversible penalty. The state may affirm victim standing through other severe and public
responses.

**Residual:** An abolitionist account must avoid speaking only about the offender; victims’
recognition and security remain central.

### 6. The principal objections

| Objection | Philosophical force |
|---|---|
| **fallibility and irreversibility** | wrongful execution cannot be repaired |
| **dignity** | deliberate killing of a restrained person may contradict equal human standing |
| **unequal administration** | unequal defence, mitigation and discretion undermine equal treatment |
| **deterrence uncertainty** | utilitarian case fails without comparative evidence |
| **foreclosure of reform** | execution removes every possibility of transformation |
| **added process cruelty** | prolonged uncertainty may impose suffering beyond the formal sentence |

### 7. Kantian tension

Kantian retribution is associated with death for murder, yet the Kantian principle of persons as
ends sharpens concern about proof, non-instrumentality and equal treatment.

A Kantian abolitionist cannot merely utter “dignity.” The argument must explain why equal respect
forbids execution even when guilt and desert are conceded—typically through finality, fallibility,
the availability of protection without killing, or a cardinal limit on what the state may do.

### 8. Indian legal illustrations, correctly classified

- ***Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980)** is a Supreme Court **judgment** associated with the
  “rarest of rare” framework.
- **Law Commission of India Report No. 262 (2015)** is an advisory **report**, not a judgment or
  legislation. It recommended abolition for ordinary crimes while excluding specified
  terrorism-related and war contexts from that recommendation.

These illustrate a legal compromise. Neither document proves moral legitimacy or deterrent effect.

### UPSC application

- **2022 moral justifications:** present retribution, deterrence, incapacitation and public
  affirmation, then test each.
- **2026 Explain:** keep desert/proportionality and aggregate prevention analytically separate.
- **Trap:** a penalty can be expressive without execution being necessary for expression.
- **Trap:** permanent incapacitation does not prove that only death can protect.

### Revision notes

1. Capital punishment is irreversible state execution after conviction.
2. Retribution argues from exceptional guilt and proportionate desert.
3. Desert does not automatically establish a permission to kill.
4. Deterrence needs comparative evidence over a feasible alternative.
5. Severity alone does not establish prevention.
6. Even effective deterrence remains subject to rights and proportionality.
7. Secure custody weakens the simple incapacitation argument for execution.
8. Victims’ claims to recognition and safety are morally central.
9. Expression of condemnation does not logically entail death.
10. Fallibility becomes uniquely grave where correction is impossible.
11. Unequal defence and discretion threaten equal proportionality.
12. *Bachan Singh* is a judgment; Report 262 is advisory.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** Give the characteristic difficulty of retribution and deterrence in one line each.

**Application:** A speaker says, “Execution is justified because it permanently incapacitates.”
What is missing?

**Model route:** The speaker must compare secure custody, establish why killing is necessary rather
than merely effective, and satisfy dignity, fallibility, proportionality and equal-process
constraints.

**Misconception to avoid:** A unique final effect is not the same as a uniquely necessary effect.

### 1. Is the right to life absolute?

A right can be fundamental without being exceptionless. Proportionate defence against an ongoing
unjust threat is structurally different from execution after custody and adjudication.

**Argument against equivalence:**

1. defence responds to a present threat;
2. execution occurs after control is established;
3. less final means of incapacitation are available for comparison;
4. therefore an exception for immediate defence does not automatically justify execution.

The strongest abolitionist argument is not “every right has no exceptions.” It is that deliberate,
irreversible killing after control fails necessity under fallibility and feasible alternative
protection.

### 2. Capital punishment and procedural social justice

An abstractly neutral penalty can be unjust in administration where:

- access to competent defence is unequal;
- mitigation is unevenly discovered or presented;
- fact-finding and discretion vary;
- social disadvantage affects the quality of participation;
- irreversible error cannot be repaired.

This is not a claim about any named present case. It is a philosophical condition: equal culpability
must receive equal and reliable treatment if proportional desert is to be meaningful.

### 3. Capital punishment and substantive social justice

Substantive justice includes both:

- recognition and security for victims;
- the moral standing and equal rights of the accused.

**Objection:** Severe public wrong demands the highest available censure.

**Reply:** Censure, safety and victim recognition may be achieved through less final penalties.

**Residual:** A defender may still claim that only death expresses proportionate desert. The debate
then turns on the cardinal ceiling and the state’s authority to extinguish life.

**Conditional verdict:** Capital punishment weakens social justice where reliable, equal and
consistent administration cannot be secured. The retributive argument remains distinct and must be
answered rather than assumed away.

### 4. Effective deterrence: empirical and moral questions

```text
EMPIRICAL QUESTION                         MORAL QUESTION
Does execution prevent more harm           Even if it does, may the state use it?
than secure long-term custody?              rights / innocence / dignity / equality
          |                                           |
          +---------------- neither answers the other -+
```

To accept execution as an effective deterrent, one must show extra prevention over a feasible
alternative and explain offender perception, certainty of detection and collateral effects. To
accept it as morally permissible, one must additionally pass independent rights and proportionality
tests.

Uncertain deterrence weakens the utilitarian case; it does not by itself refute retributive desert.

### 5. Juvenile proportionality revisited

The 2023 question combines two judgments:

1. the nature and gravity of the offence must be considered;
2. juvenile culpability is individualised through maturity, understanding, control,
   susceptibility and reform capacity.

The right verdict is neither “ignore the crime” nor “treat the juvenile as an adult because the
harm is grave.” Ordinal seriousness must be integrated with blameworthiness and a humane cardinal
ceiling.

|---|---|---|
| **Explain / elucidate** | reconstruct doctrine, premises and contrast | giving a policy vote instead |

### 7. Marks-sensitive answer architecture

#### 10 marks: four moves

1. Define the operative term.
2. State one central distinction or argument.
3. Use one named anchor or bounded Indian illustration.
4. Give a qualified verdict.

#### 15 marks: six moves

1. Decode the directive.
2. State the relevant theory in its strongest form.
3. Present the strongest rival on the same question.
4. Add one comparison or mechanism table.
5. Work one objection → reply → residual.
6. Separate moral validity, implementation and empirical effect.

#### 20 marks: eight moves

1. Identify the central clash.
2. Separate aim, liability and amount.
3. State rival theories charitably.
4. Apply culpability, rights, dignity and proportionality.
5. Develop two objection–reply chains.
6. Add one precisely used critical refinement.
7. Use one accurately classified Indian illustration where relevant.
8. End with a conditional verdict naming its controlling principle.

### 8. Whole-topic verdict

A defensible penal philosophy is neither punishment without responsibility nor compassion without
answerability. It treats prevention as a legitimate institutional aim, guilt as a condition of
liability, proportionality as a limit, dignity as a constraint on form, victim standing as a reason
for public recognition, and restoration as a route to repair where safety and equality permit.
Capital punishment is the hardest test because fallibility and unequal process become irreversible.

### UPSC application

- **2025 right to life:** distinguish fundamental from exceptionless, then use custody and
  necessity.
- **2019 social justice:** connect unequal process to proportionality and state standing.
- **2024 deterrence:** keep empirical effectiveness apart from moral permissibility.
- **2023 juvenile:** combine gravity, developmental agency and reform.
- **Trap:** do not answer a 20-marker with a catalogue of theories.

### Revision notes

1. Fundamental rights need not be exceptionless.
2. Defensive force against a present threat differs from execution after custody.
3. Non-absoluteness of life does not prove a death-penalty exception.
4. Procedural inequality undermines proportionate desert.
5. Substantive justice includes victims and the accused.
6. Deterrent effectiveness is comparative and empirical.
7. Moral permissibility remains independent of proven effectiveness.
8. Juvenile culpability cannot be inferred from harm alone.
9. Directive words determine answer structure.
10. A qualified verdict names conditions, not merely preference.

### Retrieval and application drill

**Retrieval:** Why does self-defence not settle the capital-punishment question?

**Application:** Construct a one-sentence verdict on the juvenile proportionality question.

**Model route:** The nature of the crime must inform ordinal seriousness and victim recognition,
but juvenile punishment must also reflect maturity, understanding, control, susceptibility, reform
capacity, fair process and a humane cardinal ceiling.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Proportionate to crime” does not mean “determined by harm alone.”

## A1. Can a mixed theory remain coherent?

The Core mixed position makes prevention the institution’s aim, guilt the condition of liability,
and proportionality the limit of severity. Its attraction is clear, but three coherence questions
remain.

| Question | Advanced pressure | Defensible reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Why should desert constrain utility? | the components may look borrowed rather than integrated | persons remain separate rights-holders even inside beneficial institutions | the exact cardinal ceiling is still disputed |
| Why burden the guilty if repair needs less hardship? | desert may become unnecessary once prevention and restoration work | authoritative censure can recognise agency and the public wrong | expression still does not automatically require pain |
| Can restoration vary within equal justice? | victim preference may make like cases unlike | restorative outcomes operate inside proportionate bands | this concedes an outer retributive structure |

## A2. Structural explanation, mitigation and state standing

Three claims that are often merged should be kept apart:

1. **Causal claim:** social conditions helped produce the conduct.
2. **Responsibility claim:** those conditions reduced knowledge, control or available options.
3. **Standing claim:** the state’s own injustice weakens its authority to demand penance.

The first does not entail the second; the second does not entail complete excuse; the third does not
declare the act permissible.

```text
CAUSE? -> MITIGATION? -> STANDING?
 yes       not always      independent question
```

**Gain:** This triad allows social causes to matter without erasing agency or victims.

**Cost:** Each judgment is fact-sensitive; a slogan about poverty or exclusion cannot do the work.

**Selective use:** Best in reform, social-justice or Duff-based answers. Avoid it in a narrow
10-marker unless the stem expressly asks about structure.

## A3. Irreversibility creates an epistemic asymmetry

All punishment can be mistaken, but reversible sanctions permit some correction. Execution does
not. Finality therefore changes the moral weight of uncertainty.

**Argument:**

1. A just punishment must correspond to actual guilt and proportionate desert.
2. Institutions are fallible and may distribute legal capacity unequally.
3. Irreversible punishment makes later correction impossible.
4. The penalty can therefore defeat the very proportionality invoked to justify it.

**Objection:** Every institution acts under uncertainty; demanding certainty can paralyse justice.

**Reply:** The argument does not demand infallibility for every action. It says that the allowable
risk depends partly on reversibility and available alternatives.

**Residual:** A retributivist may still hold that extreme desert outweighs residual error risk. The
debate then turns on the state’s permissible cardinal ceiling, not simply on proof standards.

**Selective use:** Add this refinement to social-justice, right-to-life or fallibility paragraphs.
Do not misstate it as an empirical claim that a named process is erroneous.

## Advanced traps

- **Trap:** a mixed theory automatically reconciles every aim.
  **Repair:** identify why its constraints are principled and where tension remains.
- **Trap:** social causation equals excuse.
  **Repair:** ask separately about control, mitigation and state standing.
- **Trap:** reversibility makes non-capital punishment harmless.
  **Repair:** it changes corrective possibility; it does not erase other injustice.
- **Trap:** restorative flexibility is always humane.
  **Repair:** voluntariness, equality, public condemnation and proportionate bands remain.
- **Trap:** one advanced paragraph may replace the Core theory.
  **Repair:** define and argue the Core first.

### Check 2

### Check 3

**Model answer:** It removes the possibility of correction and thereby raises the acceptable burden

# VERIFIED AND SOLVED PRIMARY-OWNED PYQS

> The wording and marks below follow the verified 2018–2025 bank and the 2026 supplement. Each
> model obeys the directive and stays inside this topic’s ownership.

## 2018 Q1(e) — 10 marks

**Question:** What measures do you suggest to eradicate corruption in Indian democracy?

### Demand

“Measures” requires cause-linked reforms, not a list of bodies. “Indian democracy” requires bounded
institutional illustrations and democratic controls. “Eradicate” should be qualified as an
aspiration.

### Model answer

Corruption is the misuse of entrusted power for concealed or partial advantage. In a democracy it
injures equal citizenship because public rules are displaced by private access. Measures should
therefore target both opportunity and willingness. Transparent procurement and recruitment reduce
hidden discretion; disclosure and recusal protect the boundary between private loyalty and public
office; independent audit, accessible grievance channels and reviewable decisions improve
detectability. Credible sanctions must follow due process so that anti-corruption power does not
become retaliatory.

The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, amended in 2018, and the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013,
are enacted frameworks, but their existence is not evidence of success. Oversight of the overseer,
protection for reporting and value-based professional formation are also necessary. The objection
that additional bodies merely create additional discretion is met by transparency, reason-giving
and external review.

Thus eradication cannot be promised. The realistic democratic aim is to reduce opportunities,
correct corrupt norms and make fair accountability credible without sacrificing due process.

## 2019 Q1(d) — 10 marks

**Question:** Is corruption not a form of mass violence? Discuss.

### Demand

Define the test for mass violence, apply it to corruption, identify the disanalogy and give a
qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Corruption is not ordinarily direct physical violence; it is misuse of entrusted power. Yet
large-scale corruption can become analogous to mass violence when a demonstrable causal chain
links diverted public protection or essential services to foreseeable, widespread and serious
harm. Its victims may be diffuse, the injuries cumulative and the agency hidden within institutions.

The analogy nevertheless has limits. Physical mass violence normally involves more direct coercive
agency, while ordinary bribery may lack an intention to attack a population. Loose usage can
inflate the concept and conceal differences in responsibility and remedy. Corruption must also not
be called genocide, whose defining element is special intent to destroy a protected group.

Therefore corruption may be described as mass violence only analogically where scale,
foreseeability and a credible harm-chain are established. Where they are not, “structural harm” or
“systemic injustice” is more precise.

## 2019 Q4(c) — 15 marks

**Question:** Does capital punishment weaken the doctrine of social justice? Discuss.

### Demand

Assess both procedural equality and substantive victim/offender claims; do not assume abolition or
retention.

### Model answer

Social justice requires that like culpability receive like treatment under fair institutions and
that both victims and accused persons retain equal moral standing. Capital punishment can weaken
this doctrine because its abstract neutrality may conceal unequal access to defence, mitigation,
fact-finding and appellate resources. Where discretion and social disadvantage affect outcomes,
irreversible punishment magnifies rather than corrects inequality. Fallibility also defeats
proportionate desert if an error cannot be repaired.

A further concern is state standing. A political order that has denied some citizens the effective
conditions of participation weakens its authority to demand irreversible penance. Yet the opposing
argument is serious: grave wrongdoing demands public condemnation, victim recognition and a
proportionate response. Abolition cannot mean indifference to those claims.

The reply is that safety, severe censure and victim recognition may be secured through less final
sanctions. *Bachan Singh* (1980) is a Supreme Court judgment articulating a rarest-of-rare
limitation, while Law Commission Report No. 262 (2015) is advisory; neither settles philosophy.

Capital punishment weakens social justice wherever equal and reliable administration cannot be
secured. The remaining retributive claim depends on whether execution lies within a humane cardinal
ceiling despite finality.

## 2020 Q3(c) — 15 marks

**Question:** Do you think that retributive theory of punishment is against human rights? Discuss.

### Demand

Distinguish limiting retribution from degrading or maximal retribution; show both rights-protective
and rights-endangering aspects.

### Model answer

Retributivism holds that punishment is justified by culpable wrongdoing and must be proportionate
to desert. Properly limited, it is not inherently against human rights. Kantian retribution rejects
punishing an innocent person merely to deter others and treats the offender as an answerable agent.
Guilt, fair trial and a ceiling fixed by culpability protect persons from arbitrary or excessive
utility.

The human-rights objection arises when desert is treated as permission for degradation, unequal
process or permanent moral exclusion. Suffering does not undo the original harm, and guilt alone
does not establish why the state may impose every severe form of hard treatment. Execution is the
sharpest case because irreversibility, procedural fallibility and the loss of every reformative
future require an additional dignity test.

A defender can reply that public, impartial censure differs from revenge and that respect includes
holding rational agents answerable. This reply succeeds only if punishment remains humane,
proportionate and rights-bounded.

Thus retribution is compatible with human rights as a principle of liability and limit, but not as
a blanket licence for whatever suffering is said to be deserved.

## 2020 Q4(c) — 15 marks

**Question:** How far do the social and political regimes condition corrupt practices in a
nation-state? Discuss.

### Demand

“How far” requires mechanism, degree and limit. Distinguish actual prevalence from visibility.

### Model answer

Social and political regimes condition corruption by shaping information, discretion, contestation
and sanction. An autocratic arrangement may conceal misuse where scrutiny is weak and authority is
concentrated. A democracy may expose more cases through media, opposition and audit, yet can also
create patronage, campaign-finance or capture incentives. Consequently, a higher number of observed
cases need not prove a higher underlying prevalence; it may indicate stronger discovery.

Social norms also matter. Where favouritism is treated as ordinary loyalty or where everyone
expects misuse, corruption can become a collective-action equilibrium in which isolated honesty is
costly. Institutions alter opportunity and expected cost, but they do not erase agency. A corrupt
act remains a choice by an office-holder.

The strongest objection is that regime-level explanation excuses individuals. It does not:
monopoly, opacity and impunity explain why wrongdoing becomes easy or normal, while culpability
still depends on knowledge, control and choice.

Regimes therefore condition the form, visibility and incentive structure of corruption, not its
inevitability. Comparison should focus on scrutiny, review and fair enforcement rather than attach
moral labels to regime names.

## 2021 Q2(b) — 15 marks

**Question:** Explain the reformative theory of punishment and discuss whether this is in tune with
human dignity.

### Demand

First explain the theory; then assess dignity through agency, paternalism, victims and limits.

### Model answer

The reformative theory treats the offender as capable of moral development and social
reintegration. Rather than making suffering an end, it seeks to rebuild lawful agency by addressing
ignorance, addiction, trauma, exclusion or distorted incentives. Its central claim is that a person
is more than the worst act committed and should retain a route back to membership.

This is broadly in tune with dignity. It avoids degrading treatment, recognises future autonomy and
reduces crime through capability rather than fear alone. It can also complement retribution:
desert preserves answerability and fixes an upper limit, while reform shapes humane treatment within
that limit.

However, compulsory rehabilitation may become paternalistic, pathologise dissent or continue
indefinitely in the name of treatment. Foucault’s disciplinary analysis warns that reformative
language can conceal surveillance and normalisation. Victims may also disappear if the focus rests
only on the offender.

The reply is to require proportionality, review, consent where possible, victim recognition and a
clear distinction between restoring capacity and enforcing conformity. Reformative punishment is
therefore dignity-compatible when it treats the offender as an accountable rights-holder with a
future, not as an object to be remade.

## 2022 Q1(c) — 10 marks

**Question:** Is corruption a systemic issue or an ethical issue? Give your critical comments.

### Demand

Reject the false binary and explain how agency and structure interact.

### Model answer

Corruption is an ethical act within a systemic ecology. The ethical diagnosis identifies greed,
partiality, fear and dishonest choice; it preserves personal responsibility and supports moral
education and professional integrity. The systemic diagnosis identifies opacity, monopoly,
discretion, impunity and corrupt equilibrium; it explains why honest conduct may become costly and
supports transparency, audit and fair enforcement.

Either account alone fails. Moralism blames character while leaving opportunities untouched.
Structuralism risks converting influence into excuse. Klitgaard’s monopoly–discretion–
accountability formulation identifies institutional opportunity, but no rule applies itself:
office-holders still interpret, resist or exploit it.

Thus institutions should reduce opportunity and impunity, while education and professional norms
strengthen judgment and willingness. Structure conditions action but does not perform it; agency
selects among structured possibilities. Corruption is therefore neither only systemic nor only
ethical.

## 2022 Q1(e) — 10 marks

**Question:** What are the moral justifications of capital punishment? Discuss.

### Demand

Present the principal justifications and test their moral adequacy rather than merely list pros and
cons.

### Model answer

Capital punishment is morally defended on four grounds. **Retribution** claims that exceptionally
grave culpable killing deserves the gravest proportionate censure. **Deterrence** claims that
execution prevents future harm more effectively than alternatives. **Incapacitation** points to
permanent prevention of reoffending. **Public affirmation** argues that the penalty recognises the
equal value of victims and condemns the wrong.

Each ground faces a limit. Desert does not by itself prove that the state may kill; deterrence
requires comparative evidence that cannot be inferred from severity; secure custody can also
incapacitate; and public condemnation does not logically require irreversible punishment.
Fallibility, unequal administration, dignity and foreclosure of reform intensify these objections.

Therefore the moral case is strongest only if guilt is certain, execution uniquely necessary,
proportionate and equally administered. Since each condition is independently demanding, the
justifications establish a serious debate but not an automatic permission to execute.

## 2023 Q4(a) — 20 marks

**Question:** “Severity of punishment should be proportionate to the seriousness of the crime.” Do
you agree that while punishing a juvenile, the nature of the crime should be considered? Justify
your answer.

### Demand

Take a qualified position, explain both forms of proportionality, and integrate victim harm with
developing agency.

### Model answer

The nature of a juvenile’s crime must be considered, but it cannot alone determine punishment.
Proportionality has two dimensions. **Ordinal proportionality** ranks penalties according to the
seriousness of wrongs; grave harm must therefore be publicly named and victims’ claims cannot be
minimised. **Cardinal proportionality** asks how severe the entire scale may be and prevents an
otherwise well-ranked system from becoming inhuman.

Responsibility also differs from harm. A just response must examine intention, knowledge,
recklessness, causal contribution and fair procedure. In a juvenile’s case, maturity,
understanding, reasons-responsive control, susceptibility to influence and capacity for reform are
especially relevant. Developing agency can reduce culpability even where the outcome is severe.

The objection is that mitigation on account of age trivialises the victim’s injury. The reply is
to separate condemnation of the act from assessment of the actor. The full gravity of harm can be
recognised while responsibility is individualised. Retributive censure may set an upper limit;
reformative and reintegrative aims acquire special weight because the offender’s capacities are
still developing.

The offence-centred view is therefore necessary but insufficient. Punishment should integrate the
nature of the crime with blameworthiness, maturity, control, procedural reliability and a humane
cardinal ceiling. Equal respect for victims does not justify ignoring differences in agency.

## 2024 Q3(b) — 15 marks

**Question:** On what grounds would you accept or reject the idea of capital punishment as an
effective deterrent? Discuss.

### Demand

Assess the empirical conditions of deterrence and then separate effectiveness from moral
permissibility.

### Model answer

To accept capital punishment as an effective deterrent, one must establish more than its severity.
General deterrence requires potential offenders to know and credibly perceive the sanction, respond
to expected cost, and be deterred by execution more than by a feasible alternative such as secure
long-term custody. Certainty of detection and prompt, consistent enforcement may matter more than
maximum severity.

The case should be rejected if no comparative additional effect is shown, if relevant offending is
not responsive to calculation, or if collateral harms outweigh the claimed prevention. The
empirical issue remains contested here; therefore no measured result should be invented.

Even demonstrated effectiveness would not settle moral permission. Punishing the innocent,
disproportionate severity, unequal administration, irreversibility and dignity remain independent
constraints. Conversely, uncertain deterrence refutes only the consequential argument, not a
separate retributive claim.

I would therefore reject severity as proof and place the burden on the defender to establish
comparative prevention over less final alternatives. Acceptance, if any, must remain independently
rights-bounded.

## 2025 Q1(a) — 10 marks

**Question:** “Corrupt practices reveal an inherent tension between particularistic and
universalistic normative standards.” Do you agree with this statement? Give reasons and
justification for your answer.

### Demand

Agree or disagree with qualification. Preserve legitimate personal loyalty while defending the
public role.

### Model answer

I agree, provided the tension is located within entrusted roles. Particularistic standards give
special weight to family, friendship or community; such loyalties can be morally genuine in private
life. Universalistic standards require an office-holder to apply publicly justified criteria to
similarly placed citizens.

Corruption occurs when a legitimate private loyalty governs a decision whose purpose is public. An
official who alters recruitment or procurement criteria for an associate spends entrusted power as
private property and imposes costs on unrepresented citizens. The wrong lies not in affection but
in crossing the role boundary.

Universalism should not mean blind sameness: relevant differences may be recognised through public,
consistent reasons. Disclosure, recusal and review reconcile personal relationships with impartial
office.

Thus corrupt practice does reveal a particularism–universalism tension, but the solution is not to
abolish loyalty. It is to prevent private obligation from displacing universal public criteria.

## 2025 Q3(a) — 20 marks

**Question:** Can one’s right to life be absolute? Answer with reference to the idea of Capital
Punishment.

### Demand

Distinguish fundamental from exceptionless; compare ongoing defence with execution after custody;
then test necessity, alternatives, fallibility and equality.

### Model answer

A right to life can be fundamental without being exceptionless. Proportionate force in defence
against an ongoing unjust threat shows that life may be limited under necessity. But capital
punishment is structurally different: it is intentional state killing after custody, conviction and
control, when alternative incapacitation can be considered. Therefore non-absoluteness does not
itself establish a death-penalty exception.

The retributivist argues that exceptionally grave culpable killing deserves the highest
proportionate response. This respects agency by punishing for the crime rather than merely using the
offender. The deterrent argues that execution may prevent future harm, while incapacitation and
public affirmation support security and victim recognition.

Each claim faces a separate burden. Desert must show that death lies within a humane cardinal
ceiling; deterrence needs additional effect over secure custody; incapacitation needs necessity;
expression does not logically require killing. Fallibility and unequal access to defence are
especially serious because execution cannot be corrected. Foreclosure of reform also conflicts
with the idea that a person exceeds one act.

*Bachan Singh* (1980) is a judgment associated with the rarest-of-rare framework; Law Commission
Report No. 262 (2015) is advisory. Neither decides the moral issue.

The right to life is not absolute in the sense of never admitting defensive limitation. Yet
custodial execution is justified only if necessity, proportionality, reliable equality and the
absence of less final protection are shown. Under fallibility, that burden is exceptionally hard to
meet.

## 2026 Q1(e) — 10 marks

**Question:** How does value based education address the problem of corruption?

### Demand

“How” requires mechanisms. Define corruption and education, show their causal connection and state
the institutional limit.

### Model answer

Corruption is misuse of entrusted power; value-based education forms judgment, character and
practical competence around public values. It addresses the agent and norm components of
corruption in six ways: moral cognition identifies conflicts of interest; moral motivation supports
integrity beyond fear; dilemma practice builds skills of disclosure, recusal and refusal; norm
correction challenges the belief that everyone is corrupt; civic agency teaches use of transparency
and grievance mechanisms; professional identity links office to public service.

The knowledge–action gap and hidden curriculum are serious objections. A learner may know the rule
but fear retaliation, while an opaque school teaches the opposite of its stated values.

Therefore education must be practical, critically deliberative and modelled by transparent
institutions. It creates moral demand and willingness; audit, protection and fair enforcement alter
opportunity and incentive. Education is necessary for integrity but insufficient without systemic
reform.

## 2026 Q3(a) — 20 marks

**Question:** Retributivism presents a deontological justification for capital punishment, whereas
deterrence presents a consequential justification for it. Explain.

### Demand

The directive is “Explain,” not “Should India retain execution?” Keep the two logics separate,
apply them to death and state each characteristic difficulty.

### Model answer

Retributivism is deontological because it asks what response is owed to a culpable agent in view of
the wrong committed. Kantian reasoning forbids using a person merely as an instrument of social
benefit; punishment must answer to guilt and proportionate desert. Applied to capital punishment,
the argument is that exceptionally grave culpable killing may deserve the gravest public censure.
Hegelian language adds that punishment restores the order of right violated by crime.

Its strength is that it protects the innocent, takes agency seriously and limits punishment by
desert. Its characteristic difficulty is that deserved severity does not by itself prove that the
state may kill. Dignity, procedural certainty, unequal administration and the cardinal ceiling
remain.

Deterrence is consequential because it assesses punishment by expected future harm reduction.
Execution is justified only if potential offenders respond to it, if it prevents more grave crime
than secure custody, and if the additional benefit exceeds costs. General deterrence addresses
others. Execution cannot operate as specific deterrence in the ordinary sense because the offender
has no future conduct to regulate; its individual preventive effect is incapacitative.

Its strength is supplying a public protective purpose. Its characteristic difficulties are
empirical uncertainty and the danger of treating the offender as a means. Even proven effect would
remain constrained by innocence, proportionality and rights.

Thus retribution moves from culpable past act to desert, while deterrence moves from expected future
consequence to prevention. Capital punishment is justified by neither label alone: each logic must
meet the independent demands exposed by irreversibility.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** Why is causal contribution insufficient for criminal responsibility? Answer in about
150 words.

### Model answer

Causal contribution answers whether an act or omission helped produce an outcome; criminal
responsibility asks whether that contribution is fairly attributable to an agent. The latter needs
additional conditions: conduct or a duty-based omission, an appropriate fault element, knowledge,
reasons-responsive control, fair notice and reliable proof.

A person may enter a causal chain accidentally or without the capacity to understand the relevant
risk. Conversely, a duty-bound official may be responsible for knowingly failing to act even
without a dramatic physical intervention. The distinction between justification and excuse also
matters: an act may be permissible in the circumstances, or wrongful but less blameworthy because
agency was impaired.

Social pressure and structural disadvantage can further reduce options without automatically
erasing choice. Therefore responsibility is a normative judgment about attributable agency, not a
mere scientific statement that “but for” the person, the outcome would not have occurred. Causation
is necessary in many cases, but blame requires fault, control and fair institutional attribution.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** “A just penal theory must recognise victims without treating offenders as
rightless.” Discuss. Answer in about 220 words.

### Model answer

Victims rightly demand that the wrong be named, safety restored and their standing publicly
affirmed. Feinberg’s expressive account explains why compensation alone is inadequate: punishment
also conveys authoritative condemnation. Restorative justice adds participation, acknowledgement
and repair.

Yet victim recognition does not make the offender rightless. Retribution itself can protect rights
when guilt is proved, the innocent are excluded and severity is capped by proportional desert.
Duff’s communicative view goes further: punishment addresses the offender as a continuing member
capable of answering, reform and reconciliation. A person is accountable without being reducible to
one act.

The tension becomes acute when victim preference is used to determine severity. Different
preferences can produce unequal outcomes, and face-to-face processes can reproduce domination. The
reply is a combined model: public law establishes guilt and proportionate limits; censure recognises
the victim; communication preserves the offender’s agency; restoration operates only where
participation is voluntary and safe; reform retains a route back to membership.

Foucault’s warning remains relevant: even humane language can mask expanded control. A just system
must therefore judge techniques as well as declared aims.

Victim standing and offender rights are not rival absolutes. Justice requires public recognition
of harm within legality, dignity, proportionality and equal process.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** Can punishment be justified in a society where social structures contribute to
crime? Discuss with reference to responsibility, reform and state standing. Answer in about
300 words.

### Model answer

Structural contribution complicates punishment but does not make it impossible. The first task is
to separate three questions. Causal explanation asks how poverty, exclusion, coercive socialisation
or distorted incentives shaped conduct. Responsibility asks whether the accused understood reasons,
possessed adequate control and had realistic alternatives. State standing asks whether a polity
that denied conditions of citizenship may legitimately demand penance.

At the level of liability, compatibilism permits graded responsibility without proving
metaphysical freedom. Intention, knowledge, recklessness, negligence and accident remain distinct;
structure may mitigate control without converting every harmful act into innocence. Victims’
injuries must still be named.

At the level of penal aim, a mixed theory is preferable. Prevention can justify the institution,
but guilt restricts whom the state may burden and proportionality restricts amount. Reformative
punishment respects dignity because it addresses impaired capacities and retains a path to
membership. Retribution prevents reform from treating the offender merely as a defective patient.

Duff’s standing objection supplies the deepest qualification. A state that has systematically
excluded an offender weakens its authority to demand communicative penance. This does not justify
the original crime; it requires institutional self-criticism, fairer conditions and less
sanctimonious punishment. Foucault adds that “rehabilitation” can intensify surveillance, so
reviewability and rights remain essential.

The objection is that structural explanation excuses wrongdoing and abandons victims. The reply is
graded attribution: identify knowledge, contribution, options and pressure, while preserving public
censure and repair.

Punishment is therefore justified only conditionally—where it is lawful, individually attributed,
proportionate, reform-oriented and imposed by institutions willing to remedy their own contribution
to injustice. Structure qualifies agency and state standing; it does not erase responsibility.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Crime, criminalisation and attribution

1. **Legal crime:** conduct prohibited and punishable by valid law.
2. **Moral wrong:** conduct violating a justified ethical norm; the two classes overlap but differ.
3. Criminalisation needs harm/public wrong, rights-consistency, fair notice, culpability and
   proportionality.
4. **External act or culpable omission (*actus reus*)** is used only as an attribution prerequisite.
5. Omission requires a relevant duty, knowledge and adequate capacity.
6. **Fault element (*mens rea*)** grades responsibility through intention, knowledge, recklessness
   and negligence; it is not one uniform legal formula.
7. Causation is necessary in many cases but insufficient for responsibility.
8. **Justification** defends conduct; **excuse** concedes wrong but reduces blame.
9. Fair punishment requires legality, reliable proof, impartial authority, equality and due process.
10. Do not punish thoughts, identities or causal proximity alone.

## 2. Freedom, structure and responsibility

1. Libertarianism grounds desert in alternative possibilities.
2. Hard determinism challenges basic desert and favours protection/rehabilitation.
3. Compatibilism uses reasons-responsive understanding and control.
4. Penal responsibility need not establish metaphysical exemption from causation.
5. Structural causes may reduce options, knowledge or control without automatically erasing agency.
6. Separate causal explanation, mitigation, excuse and state standing.
7. Social disadvantage is neither irrelevant nor a universal acquittal.

## 3. The three questions and penal theories

1. **Q1 aim:** Why maintain a penal institution?
2. **Q2 liability:** Whom may it burden?
3. **Q3 amount:** How much may it impose?
4. Prevention can justify Q1; guilt controls Q2; proportionality controls Q3.
5. **Retribution:** backward-looking desert and proportionate censure.
6. **Deterrence:** future harm reduction; general and specific.
7. **Incapacitation:** protection by restricting opportunity; necessity and review required.
8. **Reform:** rebuilding capacity and reintegration within rights.
9. **Expression:** public condemnation distinguishes punishment from a mere penalty.
10. **Communication:** address to the offender as a responsible member.
11. **Restoration:** victim–offender–community process of acknowledgement, repair and reintegration.
12. Mixed theory: prevention as aim; guilt, rights and proportionality as constraints.

## 4. Retribution, deterrence and proportionality

1. Kant rejects punishment imposed merely as a means to utility.
2. Hegel treats punishment as negation of the crime’s negation of right.
3. Retribution differs from revenge through public law, impartiality and proportion.
4. Limiting retribution protects the innocent and caps severity.
5. Desert still needs a humane public purpose.
6. Deterrence requires known, credible sanctions and comparative preventive benefit.
7. Severity alone does not establish deterrent effect.
8. Rule-utilitarian safeguards protect trust but do not dissolve independent rights.
9. **Ordinal proportionality:** rank penalties by seriousness.
10. **Cardinal proportionality:** set a humane ceiling for the whole scale.
11. Correct ranking can coexist with an excessive system.

## 5. Reform, censure, communication and restoration

1. Reform treats persons as capable of change and social return.
2. Paternalism, forced treatment and indefinite control are the main objections.
3. Reform must be proportionate, reviewable and consensual where possible.
4. Retribution preserves answerability; reform preserves futurity.
5. Feinberg: punishment conveys authoritative condemnation.
6. Wrongful conviction causes expressive as well as material injury.
7. Duff: punishment is communication and secular penance.
8. State standing weakens where the polity has denied conditions of citizenship.
9. Foucault: modern discipline observes, classifies and normalises.
10. Foucault is a genealogy, not a justification of punishment.
11. Restitution is repayment; restorative justice is a broader process.
12. Braithwaite: condemn the act and preserve the person’s route back.
13. Restoration requires voluntariness, safety, equality and public recognition of grave wrong.

## 6. Juveniles

1. Nature and gravity of the offence must be considered.
2. Harm alone does not fix culpability.
3. Assess maturity, understanding, control, influence and reform capacity.
4. Recognise victims without presuming adult-equivalent desert.
5. Combine ordinal seriousness with a humane cardinal ceiling.
6. Juvenile status mitigates contextually; it does not automatically erase responsibility.

## 7. Corruption: concept and normative conflict

1. Corruption is misuse of entrusted power through partial, concealed or improper advantage.
2. It includes bribery, favouritism, nepotism, embezzlement, capture and abuse of discretion.
3. It violates trust, impartiality, equal citizenship and the rule of public reasons.
4. Particular loyalties can be legitimate in private life.
5. Universal public standards govern entrusted role-decisions.
6. Corruption arises when private loyalty displaces public purpose.
7. Disclosure, recusal, transparent criteria and review preserve role boundaries.
8. Universalism allows publicly justified relevant difference; it is not blind sameness.

## 8. Corruption: agency, systems, regimes and remedies

1. Settled verdict: an ethical act within a systemic ecology.
2. Ethical causes: greed, fear, partiality, weak integrity and choice.
3. Systemic causes: opacity, monopoly, discretion, impunity and equilibrium.
4. Principal–agent failure differs from a collective-action equilibrium.
5. Regimes condition scrutiny, challenge, sanction, visibility and incentive.
6. Observed cases are not identical with underlying prevalence.
7. India illustrations: Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, amended 2018; Lokpal and Lokayuktas
   Act, 2013—both enacted frameworks, not proof of outcomes.
8. Measures: transparent allocation, disclosure, recusal, independent audit, grievance, fair
   enforcement, reporting protection and oversight of overseers.
9. “Eradication” is aspirational; reduce opportunity, impunity and corrupt norms.

## 9. Value-based education and corruption

1. Value-based education forms judgment, character and practical competence around public values.
2. Six mechanisms: moral cognition, motivation, practical competence, norm correction, civic
   agency and professional identity.
3. Aristotelian basis: dispositions grow through habituation and practice.
4. Education targets willingness and norms; institutions target opportunity and incentive.
5. Knowledge–action gap: knowing does not guarantee courage.
6. Hidden curriculum: corrupt institutional practice defeats stated teaching.
7. Indoctrination risk: use public-reason values and critical deliberation.
8. Individualisation error: never substitute education for audit, protection and enforcement.
9. NEP 2020 is only a dated policy illustration, not evidence of effect.

## 10. Corruption and mass violence

1. Ordinary corruption is not direct physical violence.
2. Qualified analogy requires entrusted-power diversion, essential loss, foreseeable widespread
   harm and a credible causal chain.
3. Without that chain, use “structural harm” or “systemic injustice.”
4. Never infer genocide from corruption.
5. The analogy illuminates diffuse victims but risks conceptual inflation.

## 11. Mass violence and responsibility

1. Mass violence is organised or cascading large-scale violence against populations.
2. Mechanisms: dehumanisation, moral exclusion, diffusion, obedience, bureaucracy, polarisation,
   propaganda and security dilemma.
3. Arendt’s banality thesis concerns thoughtlessness and routinised role-performance.
4. Banality does not mean innocence or absence of ideology.
5. Criminal guilt is individual.
6. Organisational liability and political responsibility can be collective.
7. Political responsibility may ground memory, reform and reparation.
8. Responsibility for omission depends on knowledge, capacity and risk.
9. Inherited guilt is rejected.
10. Grade leaders, planners, direct actors, enablers, bystanders and resisters differently.
11. Separate religious doctrine from political mobilisation.
12. Distinguish reciprocal communal conflict, pogrom and genocide by structure and evidence.
13. Separate identity as target from identity as motive.
14. Prevention protects information, dissent, universal standing and command accountability.

## 12. Genocide

1. Convention definition: specified acts plus intent to destroy, wholly or partly, a protected group
   as such.
2. Protected groups: national, ethnical, racial and religious.
3. Political groups are outside the Convention list.
4. Lemkin coined genocide in *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe* (1944).
5. Lemkin’s conception included destruction of institutional, cultural and generational life.
6. The 1948 Convention is narrower than Lemkin’s broader conception.
7. Mass violence turns on scale/pattern; genocide adds special group-destruction intent.
8. Crimes against humanity involve widespread or systematic attack on civilians.
9. Group destruction attacks shared life, memory and plurality beyond aggregate individual harm.
10. Intent may be inferred from evidence but never invented from outrage.
11. Orders do not automatically excuse manifest wrong.
12. Denial can injure memory and survivor standing; guilt is not inherited.
13. No actual episode or actor should be classified without evidence.

## 13. Capital punishment

1. Capital punishment is intentional state execution of a convicted offender.
2. Finality raises a burden beyond punishment in general.
3. Retributive case: exceptional culpability may deserve gravest censure.
4. Retributive limit: desert does not automatically authorise killing.
5. Deterrent case: execution must add prevention over secure custody.
6. Deterrent limit: effect is empirical; severity proves nothing.
7. Incapacitation can be achieved without execution.
8. Public affirmation recognises victims but does not logically require death.
9. Objections: fallibility, irreversibility, dignity, unequal process, reform foreclosure and added
   process suffering.
10. Kantian tension: desert supports execution; persons-as-ends heightens proof and dignity demands.
11. Fundamental does not mean exceptionless.
12. Immediate defence differs from execution after custody.
13. Non-absoluteness of life does not prove a death-penalty exception.
14. Necessity must compare alternatives after control is established.
15. Procedural social justice concerns defence, mitigation, fact-finding and discretion.
16. Substantive social justice includes victim recognition and offender standing.
17. *Bachan Singh* (1980) is a Supreme Court judgment.
18. Law Commission Report No. 262 (2015) is advisory.
19. Neither legal illustration proves morality or deterrence.

## 14. Directive and answer routes

1. **Explain:** doctrine → premises → distinction → application.
2. **Discuss:** theory → rival → objection → reply → verdict.
3. **Critically examine:** the objection–reply–residual structure is central.
4. **How far:** state degree, mechanism and limit.
5. **Is X a form of Y?:** define Y’s necessary criteria before applying them.
6. **Do you agree?:** take a qualified position at the outset.
7. **Measures:** link every remedy to a diagnosed cause.
8. **10 marks:** one distinction, one argument, one anchor, one verdict.
9. **15 marks:** strongest theory and rival plus one worked objection.

## 16. Final answer checklist

- Define the exact operative term before taking a position.
- State whether the issue concerns aim, liability or amount.
- Attribute conduct through fault, causation, knowledge and control.
- Name the theory’s presupposition, not only its conclusion.
- Use one bounded example and state its limit.
- Present the strongest objection rather than a weak caricature.
- Give a reply and identify the unresolved residual.
- Keep victims, offender rights, public safety and fair process visible.
- Classify Indian illustrations as statute, judgment, report or policy.
- Never fabricate deterrent effects, implementation success or current legal outcomes.
- Never assign guilt to a group by identity.
- End with a conditional verdict naming the principle that controls it.
