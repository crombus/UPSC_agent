### Visual: the three relations and the quantity held fixed

```text
Two quantities are linked in a question
                 |
   Which quantity stays fixed?
                 |
   +-------------+-----------------------------+
   |                                           |
 The RATIO stays fixed                    The PRODUCT stays fixed
 y/x is constant (direct variation)       x times y is constant (inverse variation)
 double x, double y                       double x, halve y
 part = total times share                 rate times time = work; time = 1/rate
   |                                           |
 Fails when the base is not the total     Fails when the product is not really fixed
```

### Plain intuition

A ratio gives the shape of a comparison, not its size. The ratio 3 : 5 says the two parts are in that shape. The total gives the size. The learner's first habit is to ask: is the question giving me a shape, a size, or a product that must not change?

Most CSAT mistakes in this lesson come from a silent switch of the fixed quantity. A candidate who assumes the ratio is fixed when the product is fixed will double the wrong number. The fix is to name the fixed quantity before writing any multiplication.

### The rule: name the fixed quantity

- **Direct variation.** If y is directly proportional to x, the ratio y/x is constant. Doubling x doubles y.
- **Inverse variation.** If y is inversely proportional to x, the product xy is constant. Doubling x halves y.
- **Condition.** Both hold only when every other factor is fixed. If the crowding of a machine rises as more machines are added, the output does not scale as the simple rule says.

**Worked example.** Eight identical workers make 240 units at a fixed rate. Twelve workers make 240 times 12/8 = 360 units, a direct variation. Eight identical workers finish a job in 15 days; twelve finish it in 8 times 15/12 = 10 days, an inverse variation with equal productivity.

### Shortcut 1: the ratio split, and the part-of-total test

- **Shortcut.** A part of a total with ratio parts is total times own parts over the sum of parts. Amount of 4,800 shared 3 : 5 gives the smaller share 4,800 times 3/8 = 1,800.
- **It fails** when the ratio compares one quantity with another quantity, not with the total. "A is 25% more than B" gives A : B = 1.25 : 1 = 5 : 4. It does not give A's share of the combined total.
- **Three parts.** An amount of 3,300 shared in the ratio 2 : 3 : 6 has 11 parts, so one part is 300 and the shares are 600, 900 and 1,800. Check: 600 + 900 + 1,800 = 3,300. The method is the same as for two parts; add the parts in the denominator and nothing else.
- **Counterexample.** Let B = 80, so A = 100. A's share of the combined total is 100/180 = 55.6%. The tempting answer 125% is a ratio to B, not a share of the total.

### Shortcut 2: constant spending after a price change (Advanced form)

- **Shortcut.** If the price rises by r (as a fraction) and spending must not change, the quantity must fall by r/(1+r). A 25% price rise needs a cut of 0.25/1.25 = 20%.
- **It fails for a price fall.** If the price falls by r, the quantity must rise by r/(1-r). A 25% fall needs a rise of 0.25/0.75 = 33 1/3%. Using the rise formula for a fall gives 0.25/1.25 = 20%, which is wrong. A 20% fall needs a rise of 25%, not 16.7%.
- **The cut is not the same percentage as the rise.** Cutting the quantity by the same 25% after a 25% price rise leaves spending at 1.25 times 0.75 = 0.9375 of the original, a fall. The cut must be r/(1+r), which is 20%.
- **It fails when expenditure is not price times quantity.** Slab tariffs and bulk discounts change the cost of the next unit. Compute the bill directly.

**Worked example.** A price falls by 20%. Spending is price times quantity, so the new quantity is 1/0.8 = 1.25 times the old one, a rise of 25%.

### Shortcut 3: work rates add, time does not

- **Shortcut.** A worker who finishes a job alone in T days does 1/T of the job per day. Rates add. Two workers alone in 12 and 18 days together do 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 per day, so they take 36/5 = 7.2 days.
- **It fails when the workers interfere** with each other or when one leaves partway through. Suppose A alone takes 10 days and B alone takes 15 days, but when they work together they block each other and take 8 days. Their rates do not add, and the naive answer of 6 days is wrong.
- **Wages follow the same logic.** If each unit of work earns the same pay, earnings are in the ratio of work done. If the pay rate differs by person, multiply work by pay per unit first.

### Conservation under transfer

Money-transfer items use one invariant: the total does not change when money passes between holders. Check the total after every transfer. It fails when a fee or a loss is deducted in the transfer.

**Worked example.** P holds 50, Q holds 80, R holds 20 and S holds 10, a total of 160. P pays 15 to Q, Q pays 25 to R, and R gives 5 to S. The holdings become 35, 70, 40 and 15. Check: 35 + 70 + 40 + 15 = 160, so the total is unchanged. Any statement about who is richest can be checked against the unchanged total.

A related error is an interchanged count on a bill. Suppose a black pair costs 30, a brown pair costs 10, and the intended order is 5 black and 3 brown. The correct bill is 150 + 30 = 180. If the counts are swapped, the bill is 90 + 50 = 140. The gap, 40, equals the price gap times the count gap, (30 - 10) times (5 - 3).

**Further worked checks.**

- Chain 2 : 3 and 4 : 5 by making B common: A : B = 8 : 12 and B : C = 12 : 15, so A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15.
- Fifteen machines make 900 parts in 6 hours. Ten identical machines in 9 hours make 900 times (10/15) times (9/6) = 900 parts, because output is proportional to machines and to time.
- An 80 per cent price rise needs a cut of 0.8/1.8 = 44 4/9 per cent in consumption.

### Part E - Two relations and one unknown: crossovers, allocations and common prices

Many commercial questions give two or three relations between the same unknowns. The learner's first question is how many independent relations the stem supplies. Two relations fix two unknowns; a third unknown needs a third relation, or a limit such as "not negative" or "whole number".

```text
Two firms, one number of units n
   firm X:  charge_X(n)  = 100 + 3n   for n up to 20;  fixed at 160 after that
   firm Y:  charge_Y(n)  = 5n
          step 1: write each charge as a function of n
          step 2: set them equal and solve for n
          step 3: check that the root lies in the range where that formula holds
```

**Crossover with a cap.** Set the uncapped formulas equal: 100 + 3n = 5n gives n = 50. But X's formula stops at 20 units, so n = 50 is outside the range where the first formula describes X. Above 20 units X charges a fixed 160. The fares then match where Y's charge reaches 160, that is 5n = 160, n = 32. Check: at n = 32, X charges 160 and Y charges 160.

**Failure condition.** Accept the first root without checking its range and the answer is 50, which the stated schedule never produces. Solve each formula only on the interval where it applies, and read the capped value where a cap exists.

**Common prices from two baskets.** Asha bought 4 pens and 2 erasers for 44. Bina bought 2 pens and 5 erasers for 34. Write the first relation as pen = (44 minus 2 erasers)/4 and substitute in the second: 2 times (44 minus 2e)/4 + 5e = 34, so 22 - e + 5e = 34, and e = 3. Then pen = (44 - 6)/4 = 9.5. Check: 4 times 9.5 + 2 times 3 = 44 and 2 times 9.5 + 5 times 3 = 34.

**Failure condition.** If the second relation is a multiple of the first, the two relations are the same equation. Two baskets of 2 pens and 1 eraser for 22 would give exactly half of Asha's relation, and the price of one pen could not be fixed. The stem then needs a third relation.

**Conditional allocation.** Three people P, Q and R hold 190 in total. If P gives 20 to Q, they hold equal amounts. Q holds 30 more than R. Write p + q + r = 190, p - 20 = q + 20, and q = r + 30. So p = q + 40 and r = q - 30. Substitute: (q + 40) + q + (q - 30) = 190, so 3q = 180 and q = 60. Then p = 100 and r = 30. Check: 100 + 60 + 30 = 190; after the gift P and Q both hold 80; Q is 30 above R.

**Common count from a chain.** If a = b + 6 and a + b = 22, then 2b + 6 = 22, b = 8 and a = 14. Each step uses one relation and one unknown at a time. A chain of relations is solved from the end whose unknown is already isolated.

**Non-negative solutions of a combination.** Make 500 with exactly 20 notes of 50, 20 and 10, using at least one note of each value. Let a, b and c be the counts of the 50, 20 and 10 notes. Then a + b + c = 20 and 5a + 2b + c = 50. Subtracting gives 4a + b = 30, so b = 30 - 4a, and c = 20 - a - b = 3a - 10. Requiring b at least 1 gives a at most 7; requiring c at least 1 gives a at least 4. Four combinations exist: (4, 14, 2), (5, 10, 5), (6, 6, 8) and (7, 2, 11). Check (4, 14, 2): 4 + 14 + 2 = 20 and 200 + 280 + 20 = 500.

**Failure condition.** A statement that one combination is the only one is false whenever a range of values survives. A value of a = 3 gives c = -1, which is impossible; the non-negativity limit is the test that removes it.

### Part F - Equal totals, linked counts and proportional weights

**Equal totals.** If 4 bags weigh the same as 6 boxes and one box weighs 12 kg, then the 6 boxes weigh 72 kg, so one bag weighs 72/4 = 18 kg. The shared total is the fixed quantity; the count of items and the weight per item are in inverse proportion when the total is held fixed.

**Linked counts.** Suppose 40 per cent of men and 25 per cent of women are married, and every married man is married to a married woman. The number of married men equals the number of married women. Write 0.4 times M = 0.25 times W, where M and W are the numbers of men and women. Then M : W = 0.25 : 0.4 = 5 : 8.

**Failure condition.** Assuming that the numbers of men and women are equal is the usual error. The linking count is the married pairs, not the population, and it does not fix the population ratio on its own.

**Proportional weights.** If five cups weigh the same as three bowls and one bowl weighs 500 g, the three bowls weigh 1,500 g and one cup weighs 300 g. Read the weights through the shared total first, then divide by the count of the item asked for.

### Part G - Equivalences, conversions and the magnitude check

**Capacity equivalence.** A hall holds 8 adults or 12 children. Six adults are seated. Six adults use 6/8 = 3/4 of the hall, so 1/4 remains. The remaining quarter holds 1/4 of 12 = 3 children. Equivalence means the same space holds different counts of different people, so compare fractions of the capacity, not counts of people.

**Failure condition.** Subtracting the six adults from twelve children, 12 - 6 = 6, treats one adult as one child. An adult uses 12/8 = 1.5 children's worth of space, so the subtraction is wrong whenever the two groups are not equal in size.

**Unit conversion.** One litre of a liquid weighs 1.2 kg, so one cubic centimetre weighs 1.2 g. How many cubic centimetres weigh 6 g? 6/1.2 = 5 cubic centimetres. Keep the chain of units explicit: 1 litre = 1,000 cubic centimetres = 1,000,000 cubic millimetres, and 1 kilogram = 1,000 grams.

**Failure condition.** Dividing the mass by the density but reading the answer in litres gives 0.005 and is 1,000 times too small. The unit of the answer must be the unit the question asks for.

**Verify by magnitude.** Before choosing, check the size of the answer against what the stem allows. A discount answer must be smaller than the price. A share must be no larger than the total. A joint completion time must be less than the faster worker's time alone. A combined average must lie between the two group averages. An answer that breaks one of these limits is wrong before any arithmetic is checked.

### Objection and reply

**The wrong method** is to average two times in a joint-work problem. Two workers with times of 12 and 18 days appear to average 15 days together. The reply is that the rates add: together they finish faster than either alone, so the answer must be below 12 days. The average of the times is not a time for joint work.

### Near-neighbour pairs

- **Ratio versus part of total.** "A : B = 3 : 5" says A is 3/5 of B. "A is 3/8 of the total" is a different claim, because the total is 8 parts. Both can be true together, but neither implies the other.
- **Direct versus inverse.** "More workers, same time, more output" is direct variation in workers and output. "More workers, same job, less time" is inverse variation. Name the held quantity before deciding.

### Retrieval before you read on

Without a calculator, split 4,800 in the ratio 3 : 5, then say which quantity is held fixed in "twelve workers finish in 10 days". Then name the one check that catches a price fall being treated as a rise. Finally, say why 50 is a wrong crossover for the capped firm in Part E even though it solves the uncapped equation.

**Answers.** 1,800. The job is the fixed quantity: workers times days, so the job takes the same worker-days whatever the crew size. A price fall needs a quantity rise of r/(1 minus r), not r/(1 plus r). The capped crossover is 32 units; 50 solves the uncapped equation outside the range where that formula holds.

### Revision points

- A ratio gives a shape. The total gives the size.
- Name the fixed quantity before you multiply: a ratio for direct variation, a product for inverse variation.
- A ratio to B is not a share of A plus B. "25% more" is 1.25 of B.
- A price rise r needs a quantity fall of r/(1+r). A price fall r needs a quantity rise of r/(1-r).
- Work rates add. Times do not add or average.
- Joint work with interference breaks the rates-add rule; check for blocking before you add.
- Money transfers conserve the total. Check the total after every transfer.
- An interchanged count changes a bill by the price gap times the count gap.
- Ratio parts share one total only when the stem gives the parts of that total.
- Two relations fix two unknowns. Solve each relation only on the range where its formula holds.
- A common price or a combination count is found by elimination; reject values that are negative or fractional when the stem counts items.
- Equal totals fix the total, not the count: divide the shared total by the count the question asks about.
- Capacity equivalences compare fractions of one capacity. Do not subtract counts of different people.
- Verify by magnitude: a discount is smaller than the price, a share is at most the total, and a combined average lies between the group averages.

### Practice

**MCQ 1**

Section X has 40 per cent more members than section Y. Section Y has 150 members. What percentage of the combined membership belongs to section X?

- A. 58 1/3%
- B. 16 2/3%
- C. 41 2/3%
- D. 28 4/7%

**MCQ 1 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** X has 210 members, so the combined membership is 360. X's share is 210/360 = 7/12 = 58 1/3%, on the combined total, not section Y.
- **B — Incorrect:** Takes the difference between the two sections, 60 members, as a share of the combined total: 60/360 = 16 2/3%. The gap between two groups is not a share of the whole.
- **C — Incorrect:** Reports section Y's share, 150/360 = 41 2/3%. The two sections have been swapped, so this is the smaller group's share, not the larger group's.
- **D — Incorrect:** Divides the gap of 60 by X's own size, 210, giving 28 4/7%. The base is X rather than the combined total, so the figure answers a different question.


**MCQ 2**

The price of a good falls by 30 per cent. By what percentage must the quantity bought rise so that total spending stays the same?

- A. 21.0%
- B. 42.9%
- C. 23.1%
- D. 233.3%

**MCQ 2 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Multiplies the two factors, 0.3 times 0.7 = 0.21. The quantity change is a ratio to the new price, not a product of the two changes.
- **B — Correct:** Spending is price times quantity. The new price is 0.7 of the old, so the quantity must be 10/7 of the old, a rise of 3/7, which is 42.9 per cent.
- **C — Incorrect:** Applies the rise formula, r divided by (1 plus r), to a fall: 0.3/1.3 = 23.1 per cent. That formula belongs to a price rise and gives too small a change here.
- **D — Incorrect:** Inverts the ratio of the prices, 0.7/0.3 = 233.3 per cent. The rise is measured from the smaller price left after the fall, so dividing the new price by the fall reverses the base.


**MCQ 3**

Worker A can finish a job alone in 9 days and worker B alone in 18 days. Working together, how many days will they take?

- A. 4.50 days
- B. 7.20 days
- C. 6.00 days
- D. 6.75 days

**MCQ 3 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Doubles the faster worker's rate to 2/9 of the job a day, which gives 4.50 days. Rates add; B's rate is not a second copy of A's rate.
- **B — Incorrect:** Misreads the second worker's time as 36 days: 1/9 + 1/36 = 5/36, giving 7.20 days. B alone takes 18 days, so B's rate is 1/18 of the job a day.
- **C — Correct:** Work rates add. A does 1/9 of the job a day and B does 1/18, so together 2/18 + 1/18 = 1/6 a day. The time is 6.00 days, below the faster worker's 9.
- **D — Incorrect:** Averages the two times, 13.5, and halves the result again to 6.75. Joint work does not average times; the rates combine.


**MCQ 4**

Taxi firm X charges a fixed fee of 40 and then 12 per kilometre. Firm Y charges 16 per kilometre with no fee. At what distance do the two fares match?

- A. 5.0 km
- B. 2.5 km
- C. 40.0 km
- D. 10.0 km

**MCQ 4 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Halves the fixed fee, using 20 in place of 40 and dividing by the rate gap of 4. The fixed fee must be used as it is stated.
- **B — Incorrect:** Divides the fixed fee by Y's rate, 40/16 = 2.5. The divisor must be the difference in the per-kilometre rates, not one firm's rate.
- **C — Incorrect:** Reads the fixed fee as the distance itself. A fee is a sum of money and cannot be a distance; the fares match where the two totals are equal.
- **D — Correct:** Set 40 + 12d = 16d. Then 4d = 40 and d = 10 km. Check: X charges 40 + 120 = 160 and Y charges 160.

### One percentage, one base

```text
p% of a base B      part = B times p/100
change from Old     New = Old times (1 + p/100)      base = Old
chain of changes    final = start times m1 times m2 times m3
                    each m multiplies the RUNNING value, not the original
gap between         52 - 40 = 12 percentage points   (a gap in points)
two percentages     12 / 40 = 30 per cent            (a relative change; base = starting rate)
```

### The idea in one sentence

A percentage is a fraction written per hundred, and a fraction always has a denominator. Most errors in this lesson are errors about the denominator: the candidate reads "per cent of what?" too late. Ask first what the base is, then write one multiplier. The arithmetic afterwards is short.

The second intuition is that changes compound. A price that rises and then falls is a new price, so the fall applies to the risen price, not to the old one. The multiplier view makes this automatic.

### The rule: name the base, then multiply

- **Percentage change.** For a change of p per cent, the new value is the old times (1 + p/100). A fall uses (1 - p/100).
- **Successive changes multiply.** A rise of 20 per cent, a fall of 20 per cent and a rise of 10 per cent give a net factor 1.20 times 0.80 times 1.10 = 1.056. The net change is +5.6 per cent.
- **Percentage points** are the gap between two percentages. A relative change is that gap divided by the starting percentage.

### Shortcut 1: chain multipliers on the running value

- **Shortcut.** For a sequence of percentage changes applied one after another to the same quantity, the final value is the start times the product of the multipliers.
- **It fails** when a change is applied to a different number than the running value. Multiplier chaining is not safe in every case: two changes of 20 per cent that both refer to the original quantity do not chain. The condition is therefore stated here, not assumed.
- **Counterexample.** Start with 100. Add 20 per cent of the original (to 120) and then take away 20 per cent of the original (to 100). The net change is 0 per cent. Chaining the multipliers gives 1.2 times 0.8 = 0.96, a fall of 4 per cent. The two methods disagree because the second change does not act on the running value.
- **Worked example.** A price rises 20 per cent, then falls 20 per cent of the new price. Start at 100: 120, then 120 times 0.8 = 96. The net change is minus 4 per cent, and the chain is correct here because each change acts on the running value.

**General rule for a change and its reversal.** Apply +a per cent and then -a per cent to the same running value. The factors are (1 + a/100) and (1 - a/100), so the product is 1 - a squared/10,000 and the net result is a fall of a squared divided by 100 per cent of the starting value. For +10 then -10 the fall is 10 squared/100 = 1 per cent, which matches 1.10 times 0.90 = 0.99. For +40 then -40 the factors give 1.40 times 0.60 = 0.84, a fall of 16 per cent = 40 squared/100. The fall is never zero, because the second change is taken on a larger or smaller base.

**General rule for two changes.** Two successive changes, in per cent, give a net change that adds a cross-term. For two rises a and b the net change is a + b + ab/100. For a rise a followed by a fall b the net change is a - b - ab/100. For a rise of 20 then a fall of 5, the net change is 20 - 5 - 1 = +14 per cent, and 1.20 times 0.95 = 1.14 confirms it. The product term is always a cross-effect and is never dropped.

**Order does not change a product of factors.** Changes that multiply the same running value give the same answer in any order: a 10 per cent rise then a 20 per cent fall gives 1.10 times 0.80 = 0.88, and a 20 per cent fall then a 10 per cent rise gives 0.80 times 1.10 = 0.88. The order matters only when a change refers to a different quantity, such as a fixed amount added after a percentage, or a percentage taken of the original rather than of the running value.

**Failure condition.** Adding a and -a to get zero is wrong, and so is reading the squared term as a fraction of the original value. A squared rate is a fall in the product of the two factors, so the square of the rate is what appears, divided by 100 per cent.


### Shortcut 2: successive discounts, and the sign of the product term

- **Shortcut.** Two successive discounts d1 and d2 (in per cent) equal one discount of d1 + d2 - d1 times d2 / 100. For 20 per cent then 10 per cent, the single equivalent is 20 + 10 - 2 = 28 per cent.
- **It fails** when the second discount is taken on the original price instead of on the reduced price. A flat 30 per cent off the marked price is not the same as 20 per cent followed by 10 per cent.
- **Sign error.** The product term is subtracted. Adding it gives 32 per cent and a wrong answer.
- **Worked example.** A marked price of 1,000 with discounts of 20 per cent and then 10 per cent on the reduced price: 1,000 times 0.80 times 0.90 = 720, the same as one discount of 28 per cent. A flat 30 per cent gives 700, which is wrong.

### Shortcut 3: percentage points versus relative change

- **Shortcut.** Gap in points = new percentage minus old percentage. Relative change = gap divided by the starting percentage.
- **It fails** when the stem asks for one and you compute the other. A rise from 40 to 52 per cent is 12 percentage points and a relative rise of 30 per cent. The relative figure has base 40, not 52 and not 60.
- **Worked example.** A pass rate moves from 40 to 52 per cent. The gap is 12 points. Relative change: 12/40 = 30 per cent. Dividing by 60 gives 20 per cent, which uses a base that is not the starting rate.

### Part B - Find the base before you multiply

Four situations change the base while the words of the stem look ordinary. A stem may give a part and the percentage it is of, and ask for the whole. A stem may increase a total and ask for a share of the increased total. A stem may give a derived quantity, such as a saving, and ask for its change. A stem may compare two quantities with "more than" and expect the base to be the quantity named after "than".

**Reconstructing the whole from a part.** A candidate needs 50 per cent to pass and scores 35, failing by 10 marks. The pass mark is 35 + 10 = 45, and the pass mark is 50 per cent of the maximum, so the maximum is 45/0.5 = 90. Check: 50 per cent of 90 is 45. Failure condition: taking 50 per cent of the score, 17.5, as the pass mark gives a wrong maximum of 35.

Another case: a buyer has 40 per cent of the price of a phone and needs 900 more. The 900 is the other 60 per cent, so the price is 900/0.6 = 1,500. The unknown is the base; the known amount is the part that is missing.

**Electorate from a margin.** Suppose 75 per cent of the voters turn out, and the winner gets 55 per cent of the votes cast and wins by 900 votes. The margin is 55 minus 45 = 10 per cent of the votes cast, so votes cast = 900/0.10 = 9,000. The voters on the list are 9,000/0.75 = 12,000. Failure condition: the turnout is a percentage of the list, not of the votes cast, so it is the last division, not the first.

**The total changes, so the base changes.** Block A weighs 20 kg. Placing block B on it increases the weight by 50 per cent of A, so B weighs 10 kg and the combined weight is 30 kg. Removing B reduces the weight by 10/30 = 33 1/3 per cent of the combined weight. Failure condition: 10/20 = 50 per cent is the change relative to A alone, not to the weight the question measures.

**A derived quantity has its own base.** Suppose a person saves 10 per cent of a salary S, so the saving is 0.1S. If the salary rises by 12 per cent to 1.12S, and expenditure rises by 10 per cent from 0.9S to 0.99S, the new saving is 1.12S minus 0.99S = 0.13S. The saving rose from 0.1S to 0.13S, a rise of 30 per cent of the old saving. Failure condition: reporting the change against the salary, or as the salary's 12 per cent rise, reads a derived quantity against the wrong base.

**"More than" uses the quantity after "than" as its base.** If P is 20 per cent lower than Q, and Q is 20 per cent lower than R, then P = 0.8 times 0.8 times R = 0.64R. R is more than P by 1/0.64 minus 1 = 56.25 per cent of P. Check: R = P divided by 0.64 = 1.5625 times P, so R is 56.25 per cent more than P. Failure condition: taking 36 per cent of R, the gap, or 56.25 per cent of R, uses a base that is not the quantity named after "than".

**Changes that refer to the original value do not chain.** The Shortcut 1 rule applies to changes on the running value. The base reconstruction in this part is a separate rule: find the base the stem gives, then apply the multiplier to that base only.

### Shortcut 5: overlap, bounds and subgroup share

Overlap is a set idea expressed with percentages. This lesson uses three identities.

- **Union.** Share in A or B = share in A + share in B - share in both.
- **Neither.** Share in neither = 100 - union.
- **Bounds.** The share in both can be no larger than the smaller of the two shares, and no smaller than the sum of the two shares minus 100 (or zero, if that sum is negative). Between those two limits lie all the overlaps the figures allow. A question that asks for the smallest or largest possible overlap uses one bound or the other.

**Worked bound.** Seventy per cent of the students take mathematics and 60 per cent take physics, and both shares are of the same class. Only 30 per cent take no mathematics, so at most 30 per cent of the physics students can be outside mathematics. The overlap is therefore at least 60 - 30 = 30 per cent, which is 70 + 60 - 100. The overlap is at most the smaller share, so it is at most 60 per cent. Every overlap from 30 to 60 per cent is possible. Check: an overlap of 45 per cent gives a union of 70 + 60 - 45 = 85 per cent and a neither-share of 15 per cent, both inside the limits.

**Failure condition.** The bounds hold only when both shares are of the same whole. If 70 per cent of the boys and 60 per cent of the girls are quoted, the two shares sit on different bases, and 70 + 60 - 100 is not a bound on any overlap. A bound is also a limit, not a value: a statement that the overlap is exactly 30 per cent is false unless the stem fixes it.
- **Subgroup share.** The share of a subgroup that has a feature is the count with the feature divided by the subgroup size. This is a ratio inside a named subgroup, not a share of the whole population.
- **It fails** when two percentages have different bases. "Thirty per cent of Indian students are girls" does not mean thirty per cent of all students are girls. If Indian students are 60 per cent of all students, girls are 0.30 times 0.60 = 18 per cent of all students.
- **Scope.** This lesson teaches the percentage form, the bounds and the subgroup bridge. Counts of three overlapping groups are outside this lesson.

**A rate on a changing base (Advanced).** A scheme is approved by 30 per cent of 2,000 respondents in year one and by 36 per cent of 2,500 in year two. Three different percentages are true answers to three different questions.

- The approval rate rises by 6 percentage points.
- The relative rise in the approval rate is 20 per cent, since 6 divided by 30 is 20 per cent.
- The number of approvers rises from 600 to 900, a rise of 50 per cent.

Underline whether the stem asks about the rate or the count before computing. A pass rate moving from 48 to 60 per cent is 12 points and a relative rise of 25 per cent.

### Shortcut 6: the smart base

- **Shortcut.** When a stem gives several percentages of the same total, pick a base that makes each one a whole number. For fifths, quarters and thirds, the base 60 works: 1/5 of 60 is 12, 1/4 is 15 and 1/3 is 20.
- **It fails** when the question asks for an absolute number that the chosen base does not give. Convert back to the stated total before you answer, and use the smart base only for ratios and percentages.

### Part D - The operation-swap error

A number should be divided by a factor but is multiplied by it instead, or the reverse. The question then asks for the percentage change in the result caused by the slip. The base of that change must be the value the stem treats as the reference. The wording of the stem decides which value that is, so read it first and name the base before computing.

**Worked example.** A value x should be divided by 5 but is multiplied by 5. Take x = 20. The correct result is 4 and the mistaken result is 100. The change from the correct result to the mistaken one is (100 - 4)/4 = 24, that is +2,400 per cent. The change from the mistaken result back to the correct one is (4 - 100)/100 = -96 per cent. Both figures are true; they answer different questions.

**General form.** If the correct result is x divided by k and the mistaken result is kx, then the change from correct to mistaken is (kx - x/k)/(x/k) = k squared minus 1, always taken as a percentage of the correct result. The change from mistaken back to correct is (1/k squared) minus 1, a fall. For k = 5 the first is 24 times, or +2,400 per cent, and the second is 1/25 - 1 = -96 per cent. The size of the error grows with the square of the factor, because the mistake changes both the magnitude and the direction of the operation.

**Failure condition.** Choosing the mistaken result as the base for the change reverses the sign and changes the size. The usual error is to report the change from the mistaken result, -96 per cent, when the question asks for the change the mistake caused, which is +2,400 per cent from the correct result. The first step is therefore to name the base: the value the stem treats as correct.

### The tempting shortcut and its reply

**The tempting shortcut** adds the percentages. "Up 20, down 20, up 10 is up 10." The reply is that each change acts on a different running value, so the factors multiply: 1.056, a rise of 5.6 per cent. The same reply covers successive discounts. Adding 20 and 10 to get 30 ignores that the second discount is taken on a smaller price.

### Pairs that get confused

- **Percent of versus percent more than.** "B is 25 per cent of A" means B = 0.25A. "B is 25 per cent more than A" means B = 1.25A. The base is A in both cases, but the multiplier differs.
- **Percentage point versus percent change.** The gap in points and the relative change answer different questions, and both can be right. Read which one the stem asks for.
- **Percent of a percent.** 30 per cent of 60 per cent is 18 per cent of the whole, because the bases compose by multiplication.

### Where the marks come from

Percentage items are the largest group among the linked questions for this lesson: fifteen percentage items, seven successive-change items and five overlap items, for 2013 to 2026. Most need the base named and one multiplier. Base reconstruction, base-of-comparison and operation-swap items are taught in Part B and Part D. Successive-change items need the running-value check and, where two changes are given, the general rule. Overlap items use the union, bounds and subgroup identities. Use the table only as a fast reference when an option needs an exact fraction.


### Quick retrieval

Without a calculator, write the net factor for +25 per cent then -20 per cent. Then say whether a flat 30 per cent off a marked price of 500 is the same as 25 per cent then 10 per cent off the reduced price. **Answers.** 1.25 times 0.80 = 1.00, so there is no net change. A flat 30 per cent off 500 gives 350; 25 then 10 per cent gives 500 times 0.75 times 0.90 = 337.5, so they are not the same.

### Revision points

- A percentage needs a base. Name it before you multiply.
- Successive changes multiply. A chain is valid only when each change acts on the running value.
- Two discounts: d1 + d2 - d1 d2/100. The product term is subtracted.
- Percentage points are gaps; relative change divides the gap by the starting value.
- Use exact fractions when options are close; 1/3 of 300 is 100, not 99.9.
- Overlap: union = A + B - both; neither = 100 minus union; subgroup share divides by the subgroup, not the whole.
- Overlap bounds: the overlap is at most the smaller share and at least the sum of the shares minus 100.
- A rate on a changing base has three answers: points, relative change and change in count.
- A figure stated to one decimal is not the exact share of a count.

**MCQ 5**

A price rises by 20 per cent, then rises by 10 per cent, then falls by 5 per cent. What is the net percentage change in the price?

- A. +25.4%
- B. +25.0%
- C. +8.3%
- D. +4.5%

**MCQ 5 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** Successive changes multiply: 1.20 times 1.10 times 0.95 = 1.254, a net rise of 25.4%.
- **B — Incorrect:** Adds the three signed changes, 20 plus 10 minus 5 = 25. Successive percentage changes do not add, because each change acts on a different price.
- **C — Incorrect:** Averages the three changes, 25/3 = 8.3 per cent. An average of changes is not the composite change, and it ignores that the later changes act on a larger price.
- **D — Incorrect:** Drops the first rise and applies only the last two changes, 1.10 times 0.95 = 1.045. The first change still acts on the starting price, so it cannot be ignored.


**MCQ 6**

A school's pass rate rises from 45 per cent to 54 per cent. Which statement is correct?

- A. It rises by 9 per cent, a relative rise of 9 per cent.
- B. It rises by 9 percentage points, a relative rise of 20 per cent.
- C. It rises by 9 percentage points, a relative rise of 16 2/3 per cent of the starting rate.
- D. It rises by 9 percentage points, a relative rise of 45 per cent.

**MCQ 6 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Reports the gap of 9 as a percentage change. A gap between two percentages is measured in points, so the first figure has the wrong unit, and the relative figure repeats the gap.
- **B — Correct:** The gap is 54 minus 45 = 9 percentage points. The relative change is 9 divided by the starting rate of 45, which is 20 per cent. Both figures answer different questions, and both are true.
- **C — Incorrect:** Divides the gap by the new rate, 9/54 = 16 2/3 per cent, and reports it as a relative rise on the starting rate. The relative change must use the starting rate as its base, which gives 20 per cent.
- **D — Incorrect:** Uses the starting rate itself, 45 per cent, as the relative rise. The relative change is the gap divided by the starting rate, not the starting rate.


**MCQ 7**

70 per cent of students play cricket, 40 per cent play football and 25 per cent play both. What percentage of students play neither game?

- A. 30%
- B. 85%
- C. 15%
- D. 25%

**MCQ 7 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Subtracts only the cricket share, 100 minus 70. It ignores football and the students who play both, so the result is too large.
- **B — Incorrect:** Reports the union, 70 plus 40 minus 25 = 85%, as the answer. The question asks for the complement of the union, which is everything outside both groups.
- **C — Correct:** The union is 70 plus 40 minus 25 = 85. Neither is the complement of the union, 100 minus 85 = 15.
- **D — Incorrect:** Treats the overlap, 25%, as the share who play neither game. The overlap is part of the union, not its complement.


**MCQ 8**

A marked price of 2,400 is reduced by 25 per cent and then by a further 20 per cent of the reduced price. What is the final price?

- A. 1,152
- B. 1,800
- C. 1,200
- D. 1,440

**MCQ 8 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Reads the first discount as 40 per cent, giving 2,400 times 0.60 times 0.80 = 1,152. The first discount is 25 per cent.
- **B — Incorrect:** Applies only the first discount, 2,400 times 0.75 = 1,800, and stops. The second discount is part of the stem and must act on the reduced price.
- **C — Incorrect:** Uses the formula d1 plus d2 plus d1 times d2 to get 50 per cent off. The product term is subtracted, not added, so this overstates the reduction.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The price chain

```text
Cost price CP ----markup on CP----> Marked price MP ----discount on MP----> Selling price SP
     |                                                                           |
     +---------------------- profit or loss % is measured on CP -----------------+

Default bases:  markup = % of CP     discount = % of MP     profit and loss = % of CP
Change a base only when the stem states it.
```

### Why the base matters

A shopkeeper marks up a price to leave room for bargaining, then gives a discount off that marked price. The customer pays the discounted figure. The shop's gain or loss is always set against what the goods cost. So the three bases are different things: the cost, the list price, and the price actually paid.

Most profit questions go wrong at the base, not in the arithmetic. The question is whether the percentage is measured from the cost, the marked price or the selling price.

### The rule: state the base before the multiplier

- **Markup** is a percentage of cost price. MP = CP times (1 + markup).
- **Discount** is a percentage of marked price. SP = MP times (1 - discount).
- **Profit or loss** is a percentage of cost price. Profit % = (SP - CP)/CP times 100.
- **The default is cost price for profit and loss, and marked price for discount.** A stem that says otherwise states the base, and the base it states governs.

### Shortcut 1: the net multiplier for markup then discount

- **Shortcut.** With markup on cost and discount on marked price, SP = CP times (1 + m) times (1 - d). Profit % = [(1 + m)(1 - d) - 1] times 100.
- **Worked example.** Markup 40 per cent and discount 25 per cent: 1.40 times 0.75 = 1.05, a profit of 5 per cent.
- **It fails when the discount is taken on cost.** Suppose the same 25 per cent is taken off the cost of 400 instead of the marked price of 560. The discount is 100, so the price becomes 560 - 100 = 460 and the profit is 60, or 15 per cent. The multiplier method gives 5 per cent. Read the base of the discount first.

### Shortcut 2: loss recovery and the cost of a sale

- **Shortcut.** When a sale is at a loss of l per cent on cost, cost = SP divided by (1 - l/100). An article sold for 480 at a 20 per cent loss on cost had cost 480/0.8 = 600.
- **It fails when the loss is measured on selling price.** If the loss is 20 per cent of the selling price, then CP - SP = 0.20 times SP, so CP = 1.20 times SP. The same 480 now implies a cost of 576, not 600.
- **Check.** Recompute the loss from the answer you find. The loss must equal the stated percentage of the base the stem names.

### Shortcut 3: equal selling prices, opposite results

- **Shortcut.** If two identical articles are each sold at the same price, one at +x per cent profit and one at -x per cent loss, the net result is a loss of x squared divided by 100 per cent of the total cost.
- **Why.** Each cost is different, so the percentages sit on different bases. Working the two costs out and summing them gives the true base.
- **It fails** when the two selling prices differ, or when the two profit percentages are not equal and opposite. Then compute each cost separately.
- **Worked example.** Two articles each sold at 960: one at 20 per cent profit (cost 800) and one at 20 per cent loss (cost 1,200). Total cost 2,000, total sale 1,920, loss 80, which is 4 per cent of 2,000. The formula gives 20 squared divided by 100 = 4.

### Part B - Two discounts after a markup

A marked article with more than one discount is a chain of three multipliers: a markup on cost, then each discount on the running marked price. Two cases show the chain.

**Core case.** Cost 500, markup 20 per cent, then discounts of 10 per cent and 5 per cent. The marked price is 500 times 1.20 = 600. The first discount leaves 540. The second discount is taken on 540, the running price, not on 600 and not on cost. The selling price is 540 times 0.95 = 513, so the profit is 13, which is 13/500 = 2.6 per cent of cost. Check: 600 times 0.90 times 0.95 = 513.

**Advanced check.** Markup 25 per cent, then discounts of 10 per cent and 4 per cent. The multiplier is 1.25 times 0.90 times 0.96 = 1.08, a profit of 8 per cent on cost. The two discounts multiply on the marked price; adding them to 14 per cent gives 1.25 times 0.86 = 1.075, a wrong 7.5 per cent.

**Failure condition.** Subtracting the total discount, 15 per cent of 600, from the marked price leaves 510 and a profit of 2 per cent. Discounts applied one after another act on the running price, so their percentages cannot be summed against the marked price.

### Part C - The same cost, two selling prices (Advanced)

One article is sold at a percentage profit and, on the same cost, at the same percentage loss. The unknown is the cost. Let the cost be C and the percentage be x. Then C times (1 + x) is the higher selling price and C times (1 - x) is the lower one.

Adding the two selling prices gives 2C, so the cost is the arithmetic mean of the selling prices: C = (high + low)/2. Subtracting gives 2Cx, so x = (high - low)/(high + low).

**Worked example.** An article is sold for 300 at a profit and for 180 at the same percentage loss, on the same cost. The cost is (300 + 180)/2 = 240, and x = 120/480 = 25 per cent. Check: 240 times 1.25 = 300 and 240 times 0.75 = 180.

**Failure condition 1.** The formula assumes one cost. If the two sales are of different articles or different costs, each cost must be found separately, as in Shortcut 3.

**Failure condition 2.** Measuring the percentage on the higher selling price gives x = (300 - 180)/300 = 40 per cent and a cost of 300/1.4 = 214 2/7. That cost contradicts the second sale, because 214 2/7 times 0.6 is about 128.6, not 180. Check the base before choosing an answer that uses a percentage of a selling price.

**Failure condition 3.** The harmonic mean of the two selling prices, 2 times 300 times 180 divided by 480 = 225, is not the cost. Two equal, opposite percentages on one cost give the arithmetic mean, not the harmonic mean.


### Price-quantity links back to Lesson 1

A price hike and a fall in quantity bought are a constant-spending problem. Use the rule from Lesson 1: a price rise of r needs a quantity cut of r/(1+r), and a price fall of r needs a quantity rise of r/(1-r). Items of this kind use the Lesson 1 rule and are not taught again here.

### Worked checks

- **(a) A chain of markup and two discounts.** A cost of 800 is marked up by 25 per cent. The marked price is 800 times 1.25 = 1,000. A discount of 10 per cent is then taken on the marked price, leaving 900. A further 20 per cent of the reduced price is taken, leaving 720. The selling price is 720, a loss of 10 per cent on the cost of 800.
- **(b) Loss and a higher price.** An article sold at a 20 per cent loss would have made a 10 per cent gain if its selling price were 300 higher. At a 20 per cent loss the selling price is 0.8C, and 0.8C + 300 = 1.1C gives C = 1,000.

### The wrong base, and why

**The wrong method** takes discount on cost. It is attractive because "a 25 per cent discount" sounds like it applies to what the shopkeeper paid. The reply is the base rule: the discount is printed on the marked price, so the multiplier must act on the marked price. Changing the base changes the answer from 5 per cent to 15 per cent.

**A second wrong method** measures profit on selling price. A profit of 20 on a selling price of 420 is 4.8 per cent, while the profit on cost of 400 is 5 per cent. The stem names the base; it is almost always cost.

### Three near-neighbour pairs

- **Markup on cost versus margin on selling price.** A markup of 25 per cent on cost of 100 gives a selling price of 125. The margin on the selling price is 25/125 = 20 per cent. Both are true and they differ, because the bases differ.
- **Profit per cent versus loss per cent.** A profit of 20 per cent and a loss of 20 per cent on the same cost do not cancel. Their effects act on different costs.
- **Discount on marked price versus discount on cost.** These are different amounts. Only the first is the usual base of a discount.

### Exam application

Five linked questions for 2013 to 2026 use this method, all typed as profit, loss and discount: the equal-selling-price shape (Shortcut 3), a discount with a stated gain on one article (Shortcut 1), a resale profit and loss, a loss recovery from a selling price (Shortcut 2), and the same-cost reconstruction in Part C. Each uses a base check first and then one multiplier or one division. Check the base in the first line of the stem, then choose the multiplier.


### Mental-maths check

A cost of 800 is marked up by 15 per cent and then discounted by 10 per cent off the marked price. Give the profit percentage on cost. Then say which base changes the answer if the discount is taken on cost instead. **Answers.** The marked price is 800 times 1.15 = 920; the 10 per cent discount is 92, leaving 828, a profit of 3.5 per cent on cost. Taken on cost, the discount is 80 and leaves 840, a profit of 5 per cent.

### Revision points

- Markup: percentage of cost. Discount: percentage of marked price. Profit and loss: percentage of cost, by default.
- Net profit multiplier: (1 + m)(1 - d) - 1, valid only with the bases stated above.
- Loss recovery: cost = SP divided by (1 - loss on cost). A loss on selling price changes the cost.
- Two equal sales at plus and minus x per cent lose x squared divided by 100 per cent of the total cost.
- A profit percentage on selling price is not the usual profit percentage.
- A discount applied after a markup acts on the marked price, not on the cost.
- Two discounts applied one after another are multiplied, not added.
- Changing the base of one percentage changes the answer, so label every base before you multiply.

### Part A - Sum, weights, corrections and transfers

### Visual: what an average preserves

```text
mean  m  of n values      sum S = m times n           (keep the SUM, not the mean)
          |
  change one value x -> y         S changes by y - x          m changes by (y - x)/n
  add one value x                  m' = m + (x - m)/(n + 1)
  remove one value x               m' = m - (x - m)/(n - 1)
  groups with sizes n1, n2         m = (n1 m1 + n2 m2)/(n1 + n2)   (weight by size)
  ordered list of n values         median = middle value (odd n) or mean of middle two (even n)
```

### What an average keeps

An average is a sum spread evenly over a count. The sum is the thing that does not lie: if the total is known, the average follows. Most errors come from treating the average as if it were the data, and then averaging averages without the counts.

**What an average hides.** Two groups can share an average of 50 and differ widely: marks of 45, 50 and 55 average 50, and so do marks of 20, 50 and 80. Failure condition: a mean cannot tell these sets apart, so a question about spread, the lowest value or the middle value needs the list itself.

Two averages with different group sizes are not equal weights. A class of 20 and a class of 60 do not contribute equally to a combined mean. The weight is the size.

### The rule: work with the sum

- **Mean.** Mean = sum divided by count. Sum = mean times count.
- **Weighted mean.** Combined mean = (sum of weight times value) divided by (sum of weights). Equal weights give the plain mean; unequal weights do not.
- **Correction of a recorded value.** When one value r should have been c, in a group of n values, the mean changes by (c - r)/n. Recompute the sum, not the mean, if several values are wrong.

**Worked example.** The mean of 20 marks is recorded as 48, but one entry 35 should have been 53. The correction is (53 - 35)/20 = 0.9, so the correct mean is 48.9.

**Several errors.** Twenty marks average 48, a total of 960. One mark 35 should be 53 (+18) and another 40 should be 30 (-10). The total becomes 960 + 8 = 968, so the correct mean is 968/20 = 48.4. Failure condition: a wrong sign on one error (reading 30 as the value that raised the total) changes the total by the wrong amount, and the mean with it.

### Shortcut 1: the weighted mean, not the plain mean

- **Shortcut.** Combined mean = (n1 times m1 + n2 times m2)/(n1 + n2).
- **Worked example.** Thirty boys average 62 and twenty girls average 72. The combined mean is (30 times 62 + 20 times 72)/50 = 3,300/50 = 66. The plain mean of 62 and 72 is 67, which is wrong because the groups differ in size.
- **It fails** when the weights are not the counts. For a mix at different prices, the weights are the quantities bought, not the number of purchases.

### Lever rule: the overall average fixes the group sizes

When two group averages and the combined average are known, the sizes of the groups are fixed by the distances. The size of the high group to the size of the low group equals (combined - low) : (high - combined). It is a distance rule for two averages, derived here from the sum of each group and needs no later method.

**Worked example.** Girls average 80 marks and boys average 60 in one test, and the class average is 72. The ratio of girls to boys is (72 - 60) : (80 - 72) = 12 : 8 = 3 : 2. Check: (3 times 80 + 2 times 60)/5 = 72. The same ratio then predicts the class average for a second test in which girls average 50 and boys average 30: (3 times 50 + 2 times 30)/5 = 42.

**Failure condition.** The rule holds only when the combined average lies between the two group averages. If the stated combined average is outside that interval, no group sizes can produce it, and the stem contains an error or a different group.


### Shortcut 2: add or remove one value

- **Shortcut.** Adding x to n values gives m' = m + (x - m)/(n + 1). Removing x from n values gives m' = m - (x - m)/(n - 1).
- **Worked example.** Four numbers average 20. Add 40: the new mean is 20 + (40 - 20)/5 = 24. Check: 80 + 40 = 120, and 120/5 = 24.

**Removing a large value.** Five numbers average 27, a total of 135. Remove 35: the total is 100 and the mean of the four is 25. The removed value is above the mean, so the mean falls. Failure condition: the removed value must be the value that is actually removed; removing a value and replacing it are different operations, and the replacement rule of the next paragraph applies to replacement.
- **It fails** when the value is replaced rather than added or removed, and when the values are weighted. Replacement uses the correction in the previous section, and weights use Shortcut 1.

### Invariance under swaps and transfers

An average does not change when members move between groups in a way that keeps both the total and the count of the whole group. A class of 30 students averages 50. One student from group A (average 40) and two students from group B (average 60) move into group C. The groups change, but the class still has 30 students and the same total, so the class average is still 50. The group averages change; the class average does not.

**Failure condition.** A move that changes the count, such as a student who leaves the class, or the total, such as a corrected mark, does change the class average. Then the add-or-remove rule above applies.

**Transfer between two groups.** Class B has 30 students averaging 26. Four students averaging 20 transfer from class A to class B. Class B's new average is (30 times 26 + 4 times 20)/34 = 860/34 = 25.3, a fall. The direction depends only on the moved students' average compared with the receiving group's average. Check with moved students averaging 30: (780 + 120)/34 = 26.5, a rise.

**Failure condition.** The highest and lowest scores of a class limit the moved students' average, but they do not by themselves fix whether the receiving average falls or rises. The comparison with the receiving group's average does that.


### Missing-group and missing-member averages

- **(a) A missing group.** A class of 50 averages 60, and its 30 boys average 56. The girls' total is 50 times 60 minus 30 times 56 = 1,320, so the 20 girls average 1,320/20 = 66.
- **(b) A missing member.** Thirty students average 12 years. With a teacher included, 31 people average 13. The teacher's age is 31 times 13 minus 30 times 12 = 43.
- **(c) A weighted price.** Three kilograms of rice at 40 per kilogram and two kilograms at 50 average (3 times 40 + 2 times 50)/5 = 44 per kilogram, a weighted mean with the kilograms as weights.
- **(d) A shared member.** Group P, Q and R averages 30 (sum 90), and group Q, S and T averages 36 (sum 108). Q belongs to both groups and is counted once, so the five distinct people P, Q, R, S and T total 90 + 108 - Q = 198 - Q. Their average therefore depends on Q's score, and the two group averages alone do not fix it. Add a sixth person U whose score equals Q's: the six people total 198 and average 33. Failure condition: counting Q twice gives a wrong total, and a five-person average cannot be stated from the two group averages alone.

### Part B - Arithmetic progressions and spacing (Advanced)

### Shortcut 3: the arithmetic progression (Advanced)

An arithmetic progression (AP) has a constant difference. Three facts cover most progression items.

- **Mean of an AP.** The mean of any AP is (first + last)/2. For an odd number of consecutive integers the mean is the middle term; for five consecutive odd numbers from 35 to 43 the mean is 39.
- **Even count.** With an even number of terms the mean is the average of the middle pair, not one of its members. Fourteen even numbers from 10 to 36 have mean (10 + 36)/2 = 23, the average of 22 and 24.
- **Number of terms and spacing.** n terms from a to l with difference d have n = (l - a)/d + 1 terms. Points spaced along a line have one fewer gap than points. Sixteen lamps 30 metres apart span 15 gaps, so the first to last distance is 15 times 30 = 450 metres.
- **It fails** when the terms are not in progression. A set {1, 2, 2, 3, 10} has no constant difference, so the midpoint rule does not give its mean.

**The sum of a progression.** The sum of n terms of an arithmetic progression is n times (first plus last)/2. Take six rows of seats that shrink by three seats each row, starting from twenty seats in the first row: the rows are 20, 17, 14, 11, 8 and 5, and their sum is 6 times (20 plus 5)/2 = 75. Check: 20 + 17 + 14 + 11 + 8 + 5 = 75.

**Solving for the number of rows.** When the total, the first term and the step are known but the count is not, write the sum as n/2 times (2 times first + (n minus 1) times step) and solve for n. Rows that start at 7 seats and grow by 2 each row total 55 seats: n/2 times (14 + 2(n minus 1)) = 55 simplifies to n times (n + 6) = 55, so n squared + 6n - 55 = 0, which factorises as (n + 11)(n - 5) = 0. Only n = 5 is a possible count, since a row count cannot be negative. Check: 7 + 9 + 11 + 13 + 15 = 55. If the equation gives a non-integer root, the stated data cannot describe whole rows.

**Failure condition.** Multiplying the number of rows by the first term alone, 6 times 20 = 120, ignores the decrease. Using the number of rows as the last term, or using n instead of n minus 1 when finding the last term, counts one row too many. The last term is first plus (n minus 1) times the step.


### Part C - Median, mode and skew (Advanced statistics)

### Shortcut 4: the median and the mode

- **Median.** Sort the values. For an odd count the median is the middle value. For an even count it is the mean of the two middle values. Sorted values 2, 4, 9, 11 have median (4 + 9)/2 = 6.5.
- **It fails** when the values are not sorted, and it does not equal the mean in general. For 2, 4, 9, 11, 30 the median is 9 and the mean is 56/5 = 11.2.
- **Mode.** The most frequent value. A set can have no mode or several modes.
- **Skew.** In a symmetric distribution mean, median and mode coincide. A long right tail usually pulls the mean above the median, with the mode lowest: mean greater than median greater than mode. A long left tail gives mean less than median less than mode.
- **The empirical relation is a heuristic only.** The rule mode is about 3 times median minus 2 times mean holds roughly for moderately skewed unimodal data. It fails badly on {1, 2, 2, 3, 10}: the mean is 3.6, the median is 2 and the mode is 2, but 3 times 2 minus 2 times 3.6 = minus 1.2. Use the skew direction, not the formula, to decide the ordering.


### A tempting average and its reply

**The wrong method** averages two group averages with equal weight. It is quick, and it is wrong whenever the groups differ in size. The reply is the sum: total marks of each group, added, divided by total count. A second wrong method takes the mean when the question asks for the median; the reply is to sort first.

### Mean, median and weights compared

- **Mean versus median.** The mean moves with every value; the median moves only with the middle values. A single extreme value changes the mean and leaves the median almost unchanged.
- **Median versus mode.** The median is a position; the mode is a frequency. A data set can have a mode far from its centre.
- **Plain mean versus weighted mean.** The plain mean treats each group as one unit. The weighted mean treats each member as one unit.

### How averages are examined

Twenty-one linked questions for 2013 to 2026 use this lesson's methods: sixteen averages (sums, weights, corrections, add and remove, transfers and invariance), one median-and-mode item (Part C), and four progression or sum items (Part B). Each uses the sum, the weight, the correction or the add-remove rule, and the progression items use the progression mean or the spacing count. The statistics ordering is taught in Part C because the routed item needs it.


### Check before you answer

Five numbers average 36. Remove one value of 50. What is the new mean? Then give the median of 8, 2, 13, 5, 10, 6 without writing the list in order first, and say why it is not the mean. **Answers.** The sum is 180; removing 50 leaves 130 over four, a mean of 32.5. Sorted, the six values are 2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13, so the median is (6 + 8)/2 = 7, while the mean is 44/6 = 7.33. The median uses the middle pair, and the mean uses every value.

### Revision points

- Keep the sum, not the mean. Mean = sum/count.
- Combined averages are weighted by size, not by the number of groups.
- Adding x to n values: m + (x - m)/(n + 1). Removing: m - (x - m)/(n - 1).
- A recorded value corrected by (c - r)/n changes the mean by that amount.
- Mean of an AP = (first + last)/2; an even count means the middle pair is averaged.
- Median: sort first. An even count averages the middle two.
- Skew: a long right tail gives mean > median > mode; the relation 3 median minus 2 mean is a heuristic only.
- A missing group or a missing member is found from the total: the total of the whole, minus the known part.
- Two averages of unequal groups are never averaged with equal weight.
- Weights are the counts or quantities, never the number of groups.
- A lever rule: the distances from the combined average give the ratio of group sizes.
- Invariance: moving members between groups keeps the average when the total and the count of the whole are unchanged.
- A shared member is counted once in the total of the union of the groups.

**MCQ 12**

Class X has 40 students with an average of 50. Class Y has 60 students with an average of 65. What is the combined average of the 100 students?

- A. 57.5
- B. 52.0
- C. 60.0
- D. 59.0

**MCQ 12 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Takes the plain mean of 50 and 65, ignoring that the classes differ in size. The larger class must count more heavily in the combined average.
- **B — Incorrect:** Uses the class sizes, 40 and 60, as if they were the averages, with the same weights: (40 times 40 plus 60 times 60)/100 = 52. Sizes are counts, not scores.
- **C — Incorrect:** Reads the larger class's head count, 60, as the combined average. A combined average lies strictly between the two class averages, and a count is not a score.
- **D — Correct:** Weighted average: (40 times 50 plus 60 times 65)/100 = (2,000 + 3,900)/100 = 59. Each student counts once.


**MCQ 13**

The mean of 20 marks is recorded as 26. One mark was entered as 57, but it should have been 37. What is the correct mean?

- A. 25.00
- B. 24.00
- C. 25.95
- D. 23.81

**MCQ 13 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The correction is (37 minus 57)/20 = minus 1, so the correct mean is 26 minus 1 = 25. The total falls from 520 to 500.
- **B — Incorrect:** Divides the error of 20 by 10 instead of by 20, giving a correction of minus 2 and a mean of 24. The error is shared over all twenty marks.
- **C — Incorrect:** Adds the correction to the total instead of to the mean: 520 minus 1 = 519, and 519/20 = 25.95. The correction changes the mean by one twentieth of its size.
- **D — Incorrect:** Divides the corrected total, 500, by 21 instead of by 20, giving 23.81 to two places (23 17/21). The count of marks is still twenty.


**MCQ 14**

Twelve consecutive odd numbers start at 7. What is their mean?

- A. 14.5
- B. 18.0
- C. 12.0
- D. 19.0

**MCQ 14 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Halves only the last term, 29/2 = 14.5. The mean of a progression uses both ends, so one end cannot stand for the whole set.
- **B — Correct:** The last term is 7 + 2 times 11 = 29. For an arithmetic progression the mean is (first plus last)/2 = (7 plus 29)/2 = 18.
- **C — Incorrect:** Reads the number of terms, 12, as the mean. The mean lies between the first and last terms, 7 and 29, and a count of terms is not a value in the list.
- **D — Incorrect:** Takes the seventh term as the middle of the list, 19. The count here is twelve, which is even, so no single term sits in the middle; the middle pair is the sixth and seventh terms.

The marks of eight students are 15, 4, 22, 11, 7, 18, 6 and 14. What is the median?

- A. 11.0
- B. 12.125
- C. 12.5
- D. 13.0

**MCQ 15 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Takes the fourth value of the list alone. With an even count the median is the average of the middle pair, not one member of it, so the sorted order must be used first.
- **B — Incorrect:** Computes the mean, 97/8 = 12.125. The mean is not the median and is pulled by extreme values, so it is the wrong measure of the centre here.
- **C — Correct:** Sorted, the marks are 4, 6, 7, 11, 14, 15, 18, 22. With an even count the median is the average of the two middle values, (11 plus 14)/2 = 12.5.

### Two conserved quantities

```text
MIXTURE      two prices or strengths a and b  ->  a target m between them
             alligation: cheaper : dearer = (b - m) : (m - a)
             conserved: the pure component; volumes add

PARTNERSHIP  money times time for each partner  ->  profit shared in that ratio
             equal time: the capital ratio decides; equal capital: the time ratio decides

TWO VESSELS  pure fraction in each vessel, weighted by the volume taken from it
             conserved: the pure quantity moved, not the fraction
```

### Why one quantity stays fixed

A mixture takes its price or strength from its ingredients. The target lies somewhere between them, and the closer the target is to one ingredient, the more of the other ingredient is needed. A partnership shares profit in proportion to money multiplied by time, because money that stays in for longer earns more of the profit. In both problems, name the quantity that does not change before writing any equation: the pure component in a mixture, and money times time in a partnership.

### Part A - Alligation and its endpoints (Core)

**The rule.** To mix two prices a and b (with a below b) to reach a target m between them, the ratio of the cheaper to the dearer quantity is (b - m) : (m - a). The cheaper ingredient is used in the ratio of the distance from the target to the dearer price.

**Worked example.** Alloys at 60 and 90 per kilogram are mixed to make a target of 70. The ratio is (90 - 70) : (70 - 60) = 20 : 10 = 2 : 1. Check: 2 kilograms at 60 and 1 kilogram at 90 cost 2 times 60 + 90 = 210, and 210/3 = 70 per kilogram.

**A second worked example.** Rice at 30 and at 40 per kilogram is mixed to a price of 34: the ratio is (40 - 34) : (34 - 30) = 6 : 4 = 3 : 2. Check: 3 times 30 + 2 times 40 = 170, and 170/5 = 34.

**Endpoints.** If the target equals one endpoint, the other ingredient has zero quantity. A target of 60 needs only the 60 alloy; a target of 90 needs only the 90 alloy. The formula gives a zero term at that endpoint, and the zero is the correct answer.

**A zero-cost ingredient.** Water costs nothing and honey costs 1 per unit. A mixture sold at honey's price of 1 gives a 20 per cent gain on its cost only if its cost is 1/1.2 = 5/6 per unit. Alligation gives water : honey = (1 - 5/6) : (5/6 - 0) = 1 : 5. Water is one sixth of the mixture, and by quantity water is 20 per cent of the honey.

**Failure condition 1.** If the target lies outside the two prices, one term is negative. A negative part means no mixture of these two ingredients can reach the target. Take signs seriously: the absolute values of a negative ratio do not give an answer.

**Failure condition 2.** The alligation ratio is the ratio of the ingredients, not the fraction of the pure substance in the final mixture. The final pure fraction is the weighted average of the two fractions with those quantities.

### Part B - Partnership: capital times time (Core, with an Advanced salary case)

**The rule.** Profit is shared in the ratio of capital times time, provided the capital is fixed for the whole period.

**Worked example.** A invests 5,000 for 12 months and B invests 4,000 for 9 months. The products are 60,000 and 36,000, a ratio of 5 : 3. If the profit is 2,400, B takes 2,400 times 3/8 = 900. Check: A takes 1,500 and B takes 900.

**Equal time and equal capital.** When two partners have the same time, the capital ratio decides. When they have the same capital, the time ratio decides. Compare the products before choosing which ratio to use.

**Changing capital (Core).** If a partner adds or withdraws capital during the period, add the capital times months for each stretch. A partner who has 3,000 for four months and then 4,000 for eight months has 3,000 times 4 + 4,000 times 8 = 44,000 capital-months.

**Salary first (Advanced).** If a partner is paid a salary before the rest is shared, deduct the salary from the profit first. With a profit of 2,200 and a salary of 200 to A, the remaining 2,000 is shared 2 : 3, so A gets 800 and 200 + 800 = 1,000 in all.

**Failure condition.** The product uses only the opening capital when the capital changes during the period, and the salary is treated as profit when it is paid first. Both errors give a wrong ratio.

### Part C - Two vessels, conservation and exchanges

**Conservation of the pure component.** A volume drawn from a mixture takes away the same fraction of each component. Track the pure quantity in each vessel after every move.

**Worked exchange (Core).** Container X holds 60 ml of milk and container Y holds 60 ml of water. Move 15 ml of milk from X to Y and mix. X holds 45 ml of milk. Y holds 60 ml of water and 15 ml of milk, a total of 75 ml. Move 15 ml of Y's mixture back to X. The milk in that 15 ml is 15 times 15/75 = 3 ml, and the water is 12 ml. X now holds 48 ml of milk and 12 ml of water: total 60 ml and a milk fraction of 0.8. Check: 45 + 3 = 48 ml of milk.

**Failure condition.** Treating the returned 15 ml as pure milk ignores the water that comes back and gives the wrong milk total.

**Two vessels with unequal volumes (Advanced).** When the two vessels are mixed in equal volumes, the milk fraction is the plain average of the two fractions. When the volumes taken differ, weight each fraction by the volume taken. Vessel one holds milk to water in 3 : 1 and vessel two in 5 : 2. Equal volumes give a milk fraction of (3/4 + 5/7)/2 = 41/56. Taking 20 litres from the first and 10 litres from the second gives 20 times 3/4 + 10 times 5/7 = 22.14 litres of milk in 30 litres, a fraction of 0.738. Using the equal-volume figure, 0.732, is wrong here.

**Concentration.** A combined concentration is the total pure quantity divided by the total volume, never the average of the two concentrations unless the volumes are equal.

### Two wrong methods

**The tempting wrong method** averages two mixture fractions as if the volumes were equal, or averages two capital shares without the time. The reply is the conserved quantity: the pure substance in a mixture, and money times time in a partnership. Averaging fractions is right only when the two volumes are equal; averaging capital shares is right only when the periods are equal.

**A second wrong method** takes the alligation ratio as the composition of the final mixture. The reply is the arithmetic of the pure quantity: the final fraction is the weighted average of the two fractions, using the quantities actually mixed.

### Which quantity is conserved?

- **Alligation ratio versus the component ratio of the final mix.** The alligation ratio tells how much of each input to take; the final pure fraction depends on the input fractions and is not the same ratio.
- **Equal volumes versus unequal volumes.** Equal volumes give the plain average of the fractions; unequal volumes need the weighted average.
- **Partnership by capital versus by time.** Equal time means the capital ratio decides; equal capital means the time ratio decides.

### Linked questions

Eight linked questions for 2013 to 2026 use these methods: six mixture items (alligation, dilution, concentration and exchanges between containers) and two partnership items (2024 and 2026). The mixture items use the conserved pure quantity or the alligation ratio, and the partnership items use the capital-time product.

### Revision points

- Alligation: cheaper : dearer = (b - m) : (m - a), valid only when a is at most m and m is at most b.
- A target equal to an endpoint leaves zero of the other ingredient.
- A zero-cost ingredient is an ordinary endpoint with price zero.
- Partnership: capital times months summed over periods; equal time gives the capital ratio; equal capital gives the time ratio.
- A salary is deducted before the rest is shared.
- Exchanges conserve the pure component, not the fraction; track the milk and the water separately.
- Two vessels: weight the fractions by the volumes taken, never by equal weights unless the volumes are equal.

**MCQ 16**

Alloy X is 20 per cent copper and alloy Y is 60 per cent copper. In what ratio, X to Y by weight, should they be mixed to get an alloy that is 50 per cent copper?

- A. 3 : 1
- B. 1 : 1
- C. 3 : 4
- D. 1 : 3

**MCQ 16 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Inverts the alligation ratio, giving more of X than Y. The target is nearer to Y's 60 per cent, so Y must take the larger share.
- **B — Incorrect:** Assumes equal quantities, which would give 40 per cent copper, not 50.
- **C — Incorrect:** Divides the cheaper alloy's gap, 50 minus 20 = 30, by the full range from 20 to 60, 40, giving 3 : 4. The two gaps must be compared with each other, not with the range.
- **D — Correct:** Alligation gives X : Y = (60 minus 50) : (50 minus 20) = 10 : 30 = 1 : 3. Check: (20 + 3 times 60)/4 = 50.


**MCQ 17**

Partners A and B invest 10,000 and 4,000. A's money stays in for 6 months and B's for 12 months. The total profit is 3,780. What is B's share?

- A. 1,680
- B. 1,080
- C. 2,520
- D. 1,890

**MCQ 17 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** Capital times time: A has 60,000 (10,000 times 6) and B has 48,000 (4,000 times 12). The ratio is 5 : 4, so B takes 3,780 times 4/9 = 1,680.
- **B — Incorrect:** Shares the profit by capital alone, 10,000 : 4,000 = 5 : 2, which gives B 3,780 times 2/7 = 1,080. Time is ignored, although B's money stays in twice as long as A's.
- **C — Incorrect:** Shares the profit by time alone, 6 : 12 = 1 : 2, which gives B 2,520. Capital is ignored, although B's capital is smaller.
- **D — Incorrect:** Splits the profit equally, giving B 1,890. Equal shares are right only when the capital and the time products are equal.


**MCQ 18**

Two containers, X and Y, hold 100 ml of milk in X and 100 ml of water in Y. 20 ml of milk moves from X to Y and is mixed. Then 20 ml of the mixture moves back from Y to X. How many millilitres of milk are now in X?

- A. 80.0 ml
- B. 83.3 ml
- C. 84.0 ml
- D. 90.0 ml

**MCQ 18 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Ignores the milk that returns. The 20 ml mixture carries milk back, so X does not stay at 80.0 ml.
- **B — Correct:** After the first move Y holds milk in 120 ml, a fraction of 1/6. The 20 ml returned carries 20/6 = 3 1/3 ml of milk, so X holds 83 1/3 ml, or 83.3 ml.
- **C — Incorrect:** Uses Y's milk fraction with the wrong volume: 20 times 20/100 = 4 ml, giving 84.0 ml. The fraction is 20/100 instead of 20/120; Y's volume after the first move is 120 ml, not 100.
- **D — Incorrect:** Assumes that Y is half milk after the first move and returns half of the 20 ml as milk, giving 90.0 ml. Y is 1/6 milk, not 1/2.

### The balance over time

```text
SIMPLE        SI = P x R x T / 100           interest on the original principal (linear in T)
COMPOUND      A = P x (1 + R/100)^T          interest on the running balance (geometric in T)
CHANGING R    A = P x (1 + R1/100) x (1 + R2/100) ...   one factor for each year's rate
WITHDRAWAL    balance -> add or take out at the stated time -> then compound the new balance
INSTALMENTS   balance x (1 + r) - payment, year by year, until the balance is zero
```

### How a balance moves

Simple interest is paid on the same base every year, so it grows in a straight line. Compound interest is paid on everything already earned, so each year's interest is larger than the last. The two agree after one period and separate after that. Every interest problem then reduces to one question: what is the balance at each time point, and what changes it at that time point? A payment, a withdrawal or a change of rate all change the balance at a stated moment, and the order of those moments decides the answer.

### Part A - Simple and compound interest (Core)

**The rule.** Simple interest is SI = P times R times T/100. Compound interest is A = P times (1 + R/100) to the power T, and CI = A minus P. For k compoundings a year, use A = P times (1 + R/(100k)) to the power kT.

**Worked example (two years).** A sum of 10,000 at 10 per cent a year, compounded annually: A = 10,000 times 1.1 squared = 12,100, so CI = 2,100. Simple interest on the same terms is 10,000 times 10 times 2/100 = 2,000. The difference is 100, which equals P times (R/100) squared = 10,000 times 0.01.

**The square formula is for two years only.** CI minus SI = P times (R/100) squared holds only for exactly two years, annual compounding and a constant rate. Over three years at 10 per cent, CI = 10,000 times 1.331 minus 10,000 = 3,310 and SI = 3,000. The difference is 310, not 100, so the two-year formula cannot be used for three years. For two years compounded half-yearly at 10 per cent the difference is 155.06, again not 100.

**Failure condition.** Using the two-year square outside its conditions gives a confident but wrong gap. Compute both CI and SI directly when the period, the frequency or the rate differs from the stated two-year annual case.

### Part B - Changing rates and withdrawals (Core)

**A rate that changes between years.** A rate that is not constant must be split into years. A deposit of 10,000 earns 10 per cent in year one and 20 per cent in year two, compounded annually. Year one gives 10,000 times 1.10 = 11,000, and year two gives 11,000 times 1.20 = 13,200. Check: 10,000 times 1.10 times 1.20 = 13,200. The single-formula shortcut would use one rate. Using the average rate of 15 per cent gives 13,225, which is wrong, because the rates do not act on the same balance.

**Failure condition.** The formula A = P(1 + R/100) to the power T assumes one constant rate. When the rate changes, multiply one factor per year. An average of the rates is not a substitute, because the second year's rate acts on a larger balance.

**Withdrawal at the end of a year.** A deposit of 10,000 at 10 per cent a year, with 1,100 withdrawn at the end of year one: after year one the balance is 11,000 minus 1,100 = 9,900; after year two it is 9,900 times 1.10 = 10,890.

**Withdrawal at the beginning of a year.** The same 1,100 withdrawn at the start of year one leaves 10,000 minus 1,100 = 8,900 to compound. After two years it is 8,900 times 1.1 times 1.1 = 10,769. The earlier withdrawal leaves less to compound, so it reduces the final balance by more.

**Failure condition.** Compounding the withdrawn amount as if it stayed in the account, or subtracting the withdrawal after the whole period, reverses the timing and gives the wrong balance. Write a line for each time point: the balance, the cash flow, and the result.

### Part C - Instalments with and without interest (Core)

**Without interest.** Equal instalments are proportional to the count. If 9 equal instalments have cleared 45 per cent of a loan, the total is 9/0.45 = 20 instalments, and 11 remain. Check: 9 is 45 per cent of 20.

**With interest on the balance.** Each year the balance grows by the rate and then falls by the instalment. For a loan of 2,100 at 10 per cent cleared by two equal annual instalments E: after year one the balance is 2,310 minus E; after year two it is (2,310 minus E) times 1.1 minus E = 0. So 2,541 = 2.1E and E = 1,210. Check: after year one the balance is 1,100 and after year two 1,100 times 1.1 minus 1,210 = 0.

**Unequal instalments.** Use the same balance line for each year. A loan of 10,000 at 10 per cent is cleared by a first payment of 4,000 at the end of year one and a second payment at the end of year two. After year one the balance is 11,000 minus 4,000 = 7,000. Year two adds 10 per cent, so the second payment is 7,000 times 1.1 = 7,700.

**Failure condition.** Dividing the principal by the number of instalments ignores interest. For the 2,100 loan, 2,100/2 = 1,050 leaves interest unpaid. A balance that does not reach zero at the last instalment shows that the method is wrong.

### Part D - Non-annual compounding and the effective annual rate (Core)

**The rule.** For k compoundings a year, each sub-period earns R/k per cent. Half-yearly at 8 per cent gives two sub-periods of 4 per cent: 1.04 squared = 1.0816, an effective annual rate of 8.16 per cent.

**Comparing rates.** Two schemes at the same nominal rate, one compounded annually and one half-yearly, are not equal. The half-yearly scheme has the higher effective rate, because interest is earned on interest earlier. Compare effective annual rates, not nominal rates, when the frequencies differ.

**Failure condition.** Reading the nominal rate as the effective rate gives 8 per cent for a half-yearly 8 per cent scheme, which understates the year's growth.

### The balance line as the reply

**The tempting wrong method** takes the average of the rates for a changing-rate balance, or divides the principal by the number of instalments. The reply is the balance line: each year's rate acts on that year's balance, and each instalment reduces the balance that the next year's interest acts on.

**A second wrong method** uses the two-year square for a three-year or half-yearly case. The reply is to compute CI and SI directly when the period, the frequency or the rate departs from the two-year annual constant-rate case.

### Rates compared

- **Simple versus compound interest.** The two agree after one period and separate after that. The gap grows with the number of periods, which is why the two-year shortcut fails for three years.
- **Nominal rate versus effective rate.** The nominal rate is the stated rate; the effective annual rate is what the balance grows by in a year once sub-period compounding is counted.
- **Withdrawal before versus after compounding.** A withdrawal at the start of a year reduces the amount that compounds; a withdrawal at the end of the year is taken after that year's interest.

### Linked questions

Three linked questions for 2013 to 2026 use these methods: one instalment item without interest (2014), one instalment item with interest and unequal payments (2018), and one effective-rate comparison (2023). Compound interest and simple interest appear in the lesson's worked examples and in the instalment method.

### Revision points

- Simple interest is on the principal; compound interest is on the running balance.
- CI minus SI = P times (R/100) squared only for exactly two years, annual compounding and a constant rate.
- A changing rate is split into one factor per year.
- A withdrawal or payment is applied at its stated time, before or after that year's interest.
- Instalments without interest are proportional to the count.
- Instalments with interest: balance times (1 + r) minus instalment, each year, to zero at the end.
- Effective annual rate: compound the sub-period rate over the number of sub-periods, then subtract 1.

**MCQ 19**

A sum of 12,000 is invested at 5 per cent a year, compounded annually for two years. What is the compound interest minus the simple interest?

- A. 600
- B. 1,200
- C. 30
- D. 60

**MCQ 19 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Takes one year's simple interest, 600, as if the difference were a single year's interest. Two years of compounding are involved.
- **B — Incorrect:** Reports the simple interest over two years, 12,000 times 5 times 2/100 = 1,200. The question asks for the gap between compound and simple interest.
- **C — Correct:** Compound interest is 12,000 times (1.05 squared minus 1) = 1,230 and simple interest is 1,200. The difference is 30, which equals 12,000 times (0.05) squared.
- **D — Incorrect:** Multiplies the principal by 0.1 and by 0.05, giving 60. The square term P times (R/100) squared needs the same rate in both factors, so one factor cannot use a different rate.


**MCQ 20**

A loan is repaid in six equal instalments with no interest. After two instalments have been paid, what percentage of the loan remains?

- A. 33 1/3%
- B. 16 2/3%
- C. 83 1/3%
- D. 66 2/3%

**MCQ 20 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Reads the share paid, 2/6 = 33 1/3 per cent, as the share still owed. The question asks for what remains after the two payments.
- **B — Incorrect:** Reads one instalment, 1/6 = 16 2/3 per cent, as the share left. Two instalments have been paid, not one.
- **C — Incorrect:** Counts only one payment, which gives 5/6 = 83 1/3 per cent remaining. The second instalment also reduces the loan, so the count is off by one.
- **D — Correct:** With no interest, instalments are proportional to their count. Six equal instalments make the loan, two are paid, so 4/6 = 66 2/3 per cent remains.


**MCQ 21**

A loan of 2,200 carries 20 per cent interest a year on the outstanding balance. It is cleared by two equal instalments paid at the end of each year. What is each instalment?

- A. 1,440
- B. 1,100
- C. 1,320
- D. 1,584

**MCQ 21 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** After year one the balance is 2,200 times 1.2 minus E = 2,640 minus E. After year two (2,640 minus E) times 1.2 minus E = 0, so 2.2E = 3,168 and E = 1,440.
- **B — Incorrect:** Divides the principal by two, 1,100, and ignores interest. Interest on the balance is unpaid, so the balance would remain.
- **C — Incorrect:** Divides the balance after one year's interest, 2,640, by two. This ignores that the second year carries its own interest on what remains.
- **D — Incorrect:** Takes the amount owed after two years with no repayment, 3,168, and halves it. Instalments are paid during the term, so each repayment reduces the balance before the next year's interest.


**MCQ 22**

A deposit earns 20 per cent a year, compounded half-yearly. By how many percentage points does its effective annual rate exceed the stated 20 per cent?

- A. 2 percentage points
- B. 1 percentage point
- C. 4 16/21 percentage points
- D. 21 percentage points

**MCQ 22 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Adds a second half-year of 10 per cent of the nominal rate to the rate itself, giving 2 points. The second half-year's interest is earned on the balance, not added to the rate.
- **B — Correct:** Each half-year earns 10 per cent, so the annual factor is 1.1 squared = 1.21, an effective rate of 21 per cent. The gap is 21 minus 20 = 1 percentage point.
- **C — Incorrect:** Divides the gap by the effective rate, 1/21 = 4 16/21. That is a relative change on the wrong base, not a gap in points.
- **D — Incorrect:** Reads the effective rate of 21 as the gap. The gap is the difference from 20, not the effective rate itself.

### Visual: one timeline for ages and one balance per round for replacement

```text
AGES          past ----- now ----- future
              the gap between two people is fixed
              each age moves by the SAME number of years
              write both ages at the same time point, then solve

REPLACEMENT   vessel V with pure substance
              round 1: draw x, mix, refill with water     -> pure left = V (1 - x/V)
              round 2: draw x of the new mixture, refill  -> pure left = V (1 - x/V) squared
              conditions: same x each round; mixed before each draw; total restored
```

### One timeline, one repeated step

Both topics are built on one idea: a fixed reference point moves forward or backward for everyone at the same rate, or a fixed fraction of the mixture is removed each round. An age question is a timeline with one shift applied to both people. A replacement question is a sequence of identical steps, each acting on what was left after the last one. In both, the error comes from moving one side and not the other, or from treating a diluted mixture as if it were still pure.

### Part A - Ages on one timeline (Core; shifted ratios and added members: Advanced)

**The rule.** Let the present ages be the unknowns and write one equation for each time point. Add or subtract the same number of years to both sides of the equation for that time point.

**Worked example (ratio of ages).** A father is four times his son's age now and a mother is three times the son's age. The father and mother together are 42 years. Let the son be s: 4s + 3s = 42, so s = 6, the father is 24 and the mother is 18. Check: 24 + 18 = 42, and 24 is four times 6.

**Worked example (a shift in time).** A father is five times his son's age now. In six years he will be three times the son's age at that time. With the son at s: 5s + 6 = 3(s + 6), so 2s = 12, s = 6 and the father is 30. Check: in six years the father is 36 and the son is 12, and 36 = 3 times 12.

**Failure condition.** Adding six years to only one side gives 5s = 3(s + 6), so s = 9 and the father 45. The second person's age did not move forward in time, so the answer describes a different timeline. Write both sides at the same time point.

**Ratio and a shift.** If the ratio of two present ages is 3 : 5 and it becomes 2 : 3 in six years, write the ages as 3x and 5x. Then (3x + 6)/(5x + 6) = 2/3 gives 9x + 18 = 10x + 12, so x = 6. The ages are 18 and 30, and in six years they are 24 and 36, a ratio of 2 : 3.

**Ages with an added member.** Four people now have a total age of 92, an average of 23. A newborn joins, and the average of the five people becomes 19. The total is then 5 times 19 = 95, so the newborn is 95 minus 92 = 3 years old. Failure condition: dividing the new total by four, or by the old count, uses the wrong number of people.

### Part B - Repeated replacement (Advanced)

**The rule.** If a vessel holds V litres of pure substance, and each round removes the same volume x, mixes well, and refills with the other liquid, the pure substance left after n rounds is V times (1 - x/V) to the power n.

**Worked example (Method 1 numbers).** From 40 litres of milk, 8 litres are removed and replaced with water, and this is repeated three times. The milk left is 40 times (1 - 8/40) cubed = 40 times 0.8 cubed = 20.48 litres. Each round removes 80 per cent of the previous round's milk, because the vessel is mixed and refilled.

**Three conditions.** The formula is valid only when the same volume is drawn in every round, the mixture is mixed before each draw, and the total volume is restored by refilling. The volume removed each round is the same x, and the pure fraction left is multiplied by (1 - x/V) each round.

**Failure condition.** Removing the same 8 litres three times without refilling leaves 40 minus 24 = 16 litres of milk in a shrinking vessel, which is not the formula's answer. Removing the milk and never refilling changes the volume, so the factor 1 - x/V no longer applies.

**Repeated replacement in two different vessels.** When the drawn volume and the refill are not the same, or a different liquid is poured in each round, track the pure quantity of each liquid line by line, as in Lesson 5, Part C. The formula is a shortcut for the single case of identical rounds only.

### Timeline errors

**The tempting wrong method** shifts only one person's age in a timeline equation, or divides a new total by the old count when a member joins. The reply is the timeline: both people move together, and the count is the number of people after the change.

**A second wrong method** applies the replacement formula to a vessel that is not refilled, or to rounds that differ. The reply is the three conditions: the same volume, mixed before each draw, and the total restored after each round.

### Shift and ratio

- **Shift of the present versus shift of the past.** "Six years ago" and "in six years" move the same person in opposite directions; the equation must move both sides by the same amount.
- **Ratio of ages now versus ratio of ages later.** The present ages are the unknowns; the later ratio is written from them with the shift added to both.
- **Repeated replacement versus repeated withdrawal.** A replacement keeps the total volume and changes the mixture; a withdrawal alone shrinks the volume and does not follow the replacement formula.

### Linked questions

Five linked questions for 2013 to 2026 use these methods: three ages items (2015, 2016 and 2024) and two repeated-replacement items (2020 and 2026). The ages items use one timeline and the added-member count; the replacement items use the pure-quantity line for each round.

### Revision points

- Ages: one timeline; write both ages at the same time point; both sides move by the same years.
- A ratio of ages with a shift in time: write the ages as multiples of one unknown and solve the shifted ratio.
- An added member changes the count as well as the total; use the new count.
- Replacement: pure left = V times (1 - x/V) to the power n, valid only when the same volume is drawn, mixed and refilled.
- Without refilling, the vessel shrinks and the formula does not apply.
- For unlike rounds, track the pure quantity line by line.

### Bridge to the cumulative set

Each lesson has isolated one base, one fixed quantity or one conserved quantity. A real commercial question seldom asks for only one. The six cumulative items join two methods from different lessons, so that the reader must choose which quantity is conserved and which base applies before calculating.

**MCQ 23**

A mother is three times her daughter's age now. In eight years she will be twice her daughter's age at that time. What is the mother's present age?

- A. 48
- B. 32
- C. 24
- D. 12

**MCQ 23 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Gets 48 by taking three times a daughter's age of 16, which comes from shifting the time on one side only. The mother's age and the daughter's age both move forward in time.
- **B — Incorrect:** Adds the eight years to the mother's present age, giving 32. The eight years are a future shift, not an addition to the present age, and the daughter's age must also be used.
- **C — Correct:** Let the daughter be d. Then the mother is 3d, and in eight years 3d + 8 = 2(d + 8), so d = 8 and the mother is 24. Check: in eight years the mother is 32 and the daughter 16.
- **D — Incorrect:** Uses the wrong equation 3d + 4 = 2(d + 4), which shifts by four years instead of eight. It gives a daughter of 4 and a mother of 12.


**MCQ 24**

A tank holds 60 litres of liquid A. Twelve litres are drawn off and replaced with liquid B, and the tank is mixed. Then twelve litres of the mixture are drawn off and replaced with liquid B. What is the ratio of liquid A to liquid B now?

- A. 1 : 1
- B. 4 : 1
- C. 3 : 2
- D. 16 : 9

**MCQ 24 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Assumes two draws of twelve litres from a sixty-litre tank leave A and B equal, a ratio of 1 : 1. Twenty-four litres removed and replaced cannot turn 60 litres of A into an even split: the tank still holds 38.4 litres of A and 21.6 of B.
- **B — Incorrect:** Stops after one round: A = 48 and B = 12, a ratio of 4 : 1. The second round must be applied to the mixture left after the first.
- **C — Incorrect:** Treats the twelve litres as pure liquid A both times: A = 36 and B = 24, a ratio of 3 : 2. The mixture is not pure after the first draw.
- **D — Correct:** After one round A is 48 litres. The second draw takes 12 times 48/60 = 9.6 litres of A, leaving 38.4 litres of A and 21.6 of B. The ratio is 38.4 : 21.6 = 16 : 9.


**MCQ 25**

The ratio of two people's present ages is 5 : 7. In fourteen years the ratio will be 6 : 7. What is the elder person's present age?

- A. 14
- B. 28
- C. 10
- D. 2

**MCQ 25 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** Let the ages be 5x and 7x. Then (5x + 14)/(7x + 14) = 6/7 gives 35x + 98 = 42x + 84, so x = 2 and the elder is 14. Check: 24 and 28 are in the ratio 6 : 7.
- **B — Incorrect:** Gives the elder person's age in fourteen years, 14 + 14 = 28. The question asks for the present age, so the fourteen years must not be added to it.
- **C — Incorrect:** Reports the younger person's present age, 5 times 2 = 10. The question asks for the elder.
- **D — Incorrect:** Reports the common multiplier x = 2 as the age. The multiplier is not an age; the ages are 5x and 7x.

# CUMULATIVE MCQS

These six items combine methods from different lessons, and numbering continues from the lesson items. Item 26 joins a capital-time share with a salary taken first. Item 27 joins a ratio with a percentage change on a changed total. Item 28 joins a sum-based average with a time shift. Item 29 joins a percentage with the exact count it implies. Item 30 joins a subgroup share with the complement of a percentage. Item 31 joins a price rise with constant spending. No item repeats a lesson item.

**MCQ 26**

Partners A and B invest 3,000 and 7,000 for the whole year. Of an annual profit of 4,600, A first takes a salary of 700. The remaining profit is shared in the ratio of capital. What is A's total?

- A. 1,170
- B. 1,870
- C. 1,380
- D. 700

**MCQ 26 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Shares only the remaining 3,900, giving A 1,170, and leaves out the salary. A's total includes the salary paid first.
- **B — Correct:** Remove the salary first: 4,600 minus 700 = 3,900. Shared 3 : 7, A takes 1,170; add the salary back for 1,870.
- **C — Incorrect:** Shares the whole 4,600 in the capital ratio, 4,600 times 3/10 = 1,380, without removing the salary first.
- **D — Incorrect:** Counts only the salary, 700, and ignores A's share of the remaining profit. A is a partner as well as a salaried person.


**MCQ 27**

The amounts held by X and Y are in the ratio 3 : 5. Y's amount is then increased by 20 per cent, while X's stays the same. What percentage of the new total does X now hold?

- A. 37 1/2%
- B. 45%
- C. 33 1/3%
- D. 66 2/3%

**MCQ 27 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Keeps the old total of 8 after Y has risen, giving 3/8 = 37 1/2%. The total must include the increase in Y.
- **B — Incorrect:** Raises X's old share of 37 1/2 per cent by 20 per cent to get 45. X's amount did not change, so its share changes only through the total.
- **C — Correct:** Take X as 3 and Y as 5. Y becomes 6, so the total is 9 and X's share is 3/9 = 1/3 = 33 1/3%.
- **D — Incorrect:** Reads Y's new share, 6/9 = 66 2/3%, as X's share. The two shares add to 100 per cent, so X's share is the complement of Y's.


**MCQ 28**

Three friends have an average age of 20 years. A fourth friend, aged 32, joins the group. Five years later, what is the average age of the three original friends?

- A. 18.75
- B. 23.00
- C. 21.67
- D. 25.00

**MCQ 28 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Adds 15 years to the total of 60 and divides by four, 75/4 = 18.75. The count is three, because the newcomer is not one of the original group.
- **B — Incorrect:** Gives the four-person average at the time of joining, (60 plus 32)/4 = 23. It includes the newcomer and ignores the five years that have passed.
- **C — Incorrect:** Adds the five years once to the total, (60 plus 5)/3 = 21.67, which is 21 2/3. Each of the three has aged five years, so the total rises by 15, not 5.
- **D — Correct:** The three original friends total 60 years at joining. Five years later each has aged 5, so the total is 75 and the average is 25. The newcomer does not enter this group.


**MCQ 29**

A school has 700 students. The ratio of students who play sport to those who do not is 2 : 5. A report gives the sport share as 28.6 per cent. What is the exact number of students who play sport?

- A. 200
- B. 196
- C. 280
- D. 210

**MCQ 29 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The sport share is 2/7 of 700, which is exactly 200 students. The report's 28.6 per cent is a rounded figure, and the ratio fixes the count exactly.
- **B — Incorrect:** Reads the report's figure as 28 per cent, a truncation, which gives 196 students.
- **C — Incorrect:** Reads the ratio 2 : 5 as the fraction 2/5, which gives 280 students. The ratio compares players with non-players, so the players are 2 of every 7.
- **D — Incorrect:** Rounds 28.6 up to 30 per cent, which gives 210 students. A rounded share of a count is not an exact count of people.


**MCQ 30**

70 per cent of the students in a class are from India, and 40 per cent are girls. Of the Indian students, 25 per cent are girls. What percentage of the foreign students are boys?

- A. 17.5%
- B. 25.0%
- C. 22.5%
- D. 7.5%

**MCQ 30 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Reports the Indian girls' share of all students, 17.5 per cent, as the answer. That is a share of the whole class, not of the foreign students.
- **B — Correct:** Indian girls are 0.25 times 70 = 17.5 per cent of all students. Foreign girls are 40 minus 17.5 = 22.5 per cent. Foreign students are 30 per cent, so foreign boys are 7.5 per cent of all students, which is 7.5/30 = 25 per cent of the foreign group.
- **C — Incorrect:** Reports the foreign girls' share, 22.5 per cent of all students, as the answer. The question asks for boys among the foreign group.
- **D — Incorrect:** Reports the foreign boys as a share of the whole class, 7.5 per cent. The base must be the foreign group, which is 30 per cent of the class.


**MCQ 31**

The price of an item rises by 150 per cent. By what percentage must the quantity bought fall so that the total spending is unchanged?

- A. 33 1/3%
- B. 66 2/3%
- C. 60%
- D. 40%

**MCQ 31 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Reads a 150 per cent rise as a price factor of 1.5, so it takes the quantity as 1/1.5 = 2/3 of the old and reports a fall of one third. A rise of 150 per cent makes the price 2.5 times the old, not 1.5 times.
- **B — Incorrect:** Reads the rise as a factor of 1.5 as well, then reports the resulting quantity factor 2/3, which is 66 2/3 per cent, as the fall. That is a share kept, not a share cut, and the price factor is 2.5 in any case.
- **C — Correct:** Spending is price times quantity. The price is 2.5 times the old, so the quantity must be 1/2.5 = 2/5 of the old, a fall of 3/5 = 60 per cent.
- **D — Incorrect:** Reads the share that remains, 2/5 = 40 per cent, as the fall. The fall is what is cut from the old quantity, which is three fifths.

## Three-pass control routine

1. **Pass one.** Read the stem once and name the base, the fixed quantity or the conserved quantity. If the relation is named within 15 seconds and the budget allows, solve it now. Skip anything that needs a second relation.
2. **Pass two.** Return to skipped items. Write the one equation or the one multiplier, then check it by the second route in the lesson: reverse the change, recompute the total, or test the sign.
3. **Pass three.** Items still open get elimination only. Eliminate any option that breaks the base, the sign, the range or the feasibility test, then choose only if the remaining options leave a positive expected score (see the rubric below).

## Marks-per-minute rubric

- Each correct item is worth 2.5 marks; each wrong item loses one-third of that, or 5/6 of a mark. These are the paper's stated marks.
- A blind guess among four options has an expected value of 2.5 times 1/4 minus 5/6 times 3/4 = 0. Guess blind only when no reasoning is possible and the time is spent.
- After one option is eliminated, a guess among three has an expected value of 2.5 times 1/3 minus 5/6 times 2/3 = 5/18, positive. Among two, it is 5/6.
- **Marks per minute.** At a steady pace of 1.5 minutes an item, a set of eight items gives 12 minutes. A fully correct set of eight earns 20 marks on the practice scale (eight times 2.5). The practice score is a count of correct items against the set size.
- **Qualifying bar.** The stated minimum is 33 per cent of Paper II, which is 66 of 200 marks. This is a qualifying bar for the paper. It is not a target for a practice set.
- **Personal target.** For practice sets this session uses seven correct items out of eight within 12 minutes. This is your personal target, not an official cut-off. It is a learning goal for the lesson sequence, and a set missed below it is re-run after remediation.

# MASTER METHOD MAPS

Each map starts from the question the stem asks and ends at one relation. Use the first map that matches the stem's first noun.

```text
MAP 1 - WHICH BASE? (percentages and profit)
  stem says "of what?"
     |
     +-- of the total or whole ............ base = total; part = total times p/100
     +-- of the starting value (change) ... base = starting value; multiplier 1 +/- p/100
     +-- of the cost (profit, loss) ....... base = cost price; discount is on marked price
     +-- of a named subgroup .............. base = subgroup size; ratio inside the subgroup
     +-- the total of a changed whole ..... base = the new total, not the first part
     +-- a derived quantity (saving) ...... base = the old value of that quantity
     +-- two rates, changing base ......... name the rate or the count before computing
     +-- "more than" a named quantity ..... base = the quantity after "than"
```

```text
MAP 2 - CHAIN OR ADD? (successive changes and discounts)
  two or more changes to the same quantity
     |
     +-- each change acts on the value left by the last one -> MULTIPLY factors
     |        one discount d1 then d2 -> single d1 + d2 - d1 d2/100
     |        rise a then fall a -> net fall a squared/100 per cent
     +-- each change refers to the original value ........... ADD on the original base
     |        check: final = original plus the sum; reverse each change to confirm
```

```text
MAP 3 - WHICH AVERAGE? (averages and medians)
  given a list or groups
     |
     +-- groups with counts ................... weight each mean by its count
     +-- combined average known, groups sought  lever: sizes = distances to the combined average
     +-- one value added or removed ........... m + (x - m)/(n + 1) or m - (x - m)/(n - 1)
     +-- one value recorded wrongly ........... m + (c - r)/n
     +-- members moved, total and count kept .. the average does not change
     +-- consecutive terms with a difference .. mean = (first + last)/2; odd count: middle term
     +-- ordered values for a middle ........... sort first; even count: average middle two
     +-- skew question ......................... order mean, median, mode from the tail side
```

```text
MAP 4 - WHICH MIXTURE? (mixtures, alloys, replacement)
  two prices or two vessels, or repeated draws
     |
     +-- target price between two prices ...... alligation: (b - m) : (m - a); check a <= m <= b
     +-- target equal to one endpoint ......... the other ingredient has zero quantity
     +-- zero-cost ingredient ................. an endpoint with price zero
     +-- target outside the two prices ........ no mixture exists; stop
     +-- same volume drawn, mixed, refilled .. pure left = V times (1 - x/V) to the power n
     +-- conditions missing .................... track the pure quantity line by line
     +-- two vessels, unequal volumes ......... weight each fraction by the volume taken
     +-- containers exchanging liquid ......... track each liquid; conservation, not fraction
```

```text
MAP 5 - WHICH TIMELINE? (ages, partnership, interest, instalments)
  people, partners, a loan or a deposit
     |
     +-- ages, years earlier or later ......... one timeline; both sides move by the same years
     +-- ages in a ratio that changes ......... write as multiples of one unknown; solve the shifted ratio
     +-- an added member changes the count .... use the new count in the average
     +-- partners with different capital ...... sum capital times months for each partner
     +-- salary or commission to one partner .. deduct it first; share the rest
     +-- compound interest over n years ....... balance times (1 + R/100) each year
     +-- rate changes between years ........... one factor per year, not an average rate
     +-- withdrawal or payment ................. apply at the stated time, then compound
     +-- CI minus SI ........................... P (R/100) squared only for two years, annual
     +-- equal instalments without interest ... count proportion (paid count / total count)
     +-- equal instalments with interest ....... balance times (1 + r) minus instalment, each year
```

```text
MAP 6 - HOW MANY RELATIONS? (linear commercial items)
  unknowns named in the stem
     |
     +-- two unknowns, two relations .......... solve by elimination; check each range
     +-- a formula with a cap or a stop ....... solve on each interval; use the capped value
     +-- relations proportional to each other . no unique answer; a third relation is needed
     +-- combination with a total ............. eliminate one count; require every count >= 0
     +-- equal totals of different groups ..... fix each group's total; divide by its count
     +-- linked counts (married pairs) ........ equate the linked counts; then compare groups
```

```text
MAP 7 - WHAT IS THE REFERENCE? (operation errors and reversals)
  a value is computed the wrong way round
     |
     +-- divided instead of multiplied ........ reference = the correct result
     +-- change from the mistake to the truth . the reverse change, not the mistake's size
     +-- a reversal of a percentage change .... the reversed change uses the other base
```

## Revision recall by subtopic

- **Ratio and fixed quantity.** A ratio is a shape; the total gives size. Name the held quantity before multiplying.
- **Constant spending.** A rise r needs a cut r/(1+r); a fall r needs a rise r/(1-r). Spending is price times quantity.
- **Two relations.** Solve each relation on its own range; a cap or a stop changes the formula; proportional relations give no unique answer.
- **Joint work.** Rates add. Times never add or average. Interference breaks the rule.
- **Transfers and counts.** Totals are conserved through transfers; an interchanged count changes a bill by the price gap times the count gap.
- **Percentage base.** Name the base first. Successive changes multiply; a chain is valid only on the running value.
- **Two discounts and rises then falls.** d1 + d2 - d1 d2/100; a rise a then a fall a gives a net fall of a squared over 100.
- **Points and relative change.** Points are gaps; relative change divides the gap by the starting value.
- **Base reconstruction.** The whole is the part divided by the percentage; a changed total is the new base.
- **Overlap.** Union = A + B - both; neither = 100 minus union; bounds limit the overlap; subgroup share divides by the subgroup.
- **Markup, discount and profit.** Markup on cost; discount on marked price; profit and loss on cost unless stated; net multiplier (1 + m)(1 - d) - 1.
- **Same cost, two selling prices.** Cost is the mean of the two selling prices; the percentage is their difference over their sum.
- **Averages.** Keep the sum; weight by counts; lever rule for group sizes; add or remove one value by the (x - m)/(n ± 1) rule; correct by (c - r)/n; moves within a whole leave its average unchanged.
- **Progressions and median.** AP mean is (first + last)/2; sort before taking the median; even count averages the middle pair.
- **Skew.** Long right tail gives mean > median > mode; the 3 median minus 2 mean relation is only a heuristic.
- **Alligation.** Cheaper : dearer = (b - m) : (m - a), valid only when the target lies between the prices; an endpoint gives a zero term.
- **Two vessels and exchanges.** Track the pure quantity; weight fractions by the volumes taken.
- **Partnership.** Capital times months; salary first; equal time gives the capital ratio.
- **Interest.** Simple on principal, compound on running balance; CI minus SI = P (R/100) squared for two years only; a changing rate has one factor per year; a withdrawal is applied at its stated time.
- **Instalments.** Proportional without interest; with interest, balance times (1 + r) minus instalment, each year to zero.
- **Ages.** One timeline; both sides move by the same years; added members change the count.
- **Replacement.** Pure left = V times (1 - x/V) to the power n, under the three conditions.
