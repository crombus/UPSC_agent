# Interpersonal and Communication Skills - Learning Session Live Edition

**Paper:** CSAT (Civil Services Preliminary Examination, Paper II) | **Format:** four-option objective items only; there is no Mains component in this paper | **Practice in this session:** multiple choice, answered in a separate block after each item

Communication questions in this paper look simple until you read them closely. A sentence says that a notice was sent, that a colleague agreed, or that a form was filled in, and the item asks whether communication has actually happened. The answer depends on a discipline that this session builds step by step: name the purpose, follow the meaning to the person who must receive it, check what they understood, and only then judge whether a response is good. Every lesson gives you a visual, a plain explanation, a method with its failure condition, examples with their limits, the strongest wrong approach and its reply, and a short set of original items that test the method rather than the memory of a term.

Work through the lessons in order. Each one depends on the lessons before it. Attempt each item before you open its answer block, and treat a wrong answer as information about which step of the method you skipped.

Your personal target is a learning goal, not an official cut-off. The timed-set section explains how to use it.

## Lesson 1 - The communication cycle: sender, encoding, channel, noise, decoding and feedback

Progress: 1/9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: The communication cycle

### Visual: the loop a message must complete

```text
SENDER has a purpose
   |
   v
ENCODE   turn the meaning into words, figures, signs or gestures
   |
   v
CHANNEL  speech, letter, notice, form, screen ........ NOISE can enter here
   |                                                   (physical, semantic, psychological,
   v                                                    organisational, technological)
RECEIVER -> DECODE  rebuild a meaning from what arrived
   |
   v
FEEDBACK  "I understood this" / "What do you mean?" / an action that shows the meaning
   |
   +--> a gap in the feedback sends the sender back to ENCODE
```

Read the diagram as a loop, not as a straight line. The arrow after decoding does not end the job. The feedback arrow tells the sender whether the job was done. Most failures in this topic happen between encoding and decoding, which is the stretch the sender cannot see directly. That is why the cycle keeps returning to the sender through feedback.

A second diagram shows what each link asks.

```text
LINK        WHAT HAPPENS                              THE QUESTION TO ASK
Sender      starts the exchange with a purpose        Who is speaking, and with what authority?
Encoding    meaning is turned into signs              Would the receiver read these signs as I intend?
Channel     the signs travel                          Does this medium suit the purpose and urgency?
Noise       interference on the way                   What could change the meaning in transit?
Decoding    the receiver rebuilds a meaning           What did the receiver actually take from it?
Feedback    the receiver reports or acts              How will I know the meaning arrived?
```

### Plain intuition

Start with an ordinary case. You send a friend the message "Bring the file tomorrow." The friend replies "OK" and forgets. Did you communicate?

You did send a signal, and the signal arrived. But your friend does not hold the meaning you intended. Which file? Which tomorrow? Which place? The cycle asks whether the meaning moved, not whether the words moved. A message that travels and is misunderstood has been transmitted but not communicated.

The idea you need is a split between two things that feel the same from the sender's side. **Transmission** means the message has left. **Communication** means the intended meaning has reached the receiver and is understood well enough for the purpose the message serves. Transmission costs almost nothing. Communication has to be checked. Paper questions in this area usually show a sender who has done the first part and ask what is still missing. What is missing is nearly always decoding or feedback.

### Doctrine: the six links and the success test

**Definition.** Communication is an exchange in which the receiver grasps the intended meaning well enough for the purpose of the message. Two terms carry the definition.

- The **purpose** is what the message is for: to inform, consult, persuade, instruct, warn, negotiate or record. Every later choice depends on it.
- The **success test** is the phrase "well enough for the purpose." An instruction needs enough understanding for the receiver to act correctly. A notification needs enough understanding for the receiver to know that something has changed. The test is set by the purpose, not by the sender's satisfaction.

**Definition and implication.** The definition sets the standard. The implication is practical: if a message's purpose depends on action, the sender must plan a point at which understanding is shown. A message without such a point may still be transmitted, but nothing in it tells the sender whether the purpose was met.

### Part A (Core): the six links in prose

The **sender** is the person who starts the exchange, and the sender's standing matters. A message from a person with authority to set a procedure carries binding force. The same words from a peer carry a request. Authority tells the receiver how much weight the message carries, but it does not guarantee that the words were understood.

**Encoding** is the step where a meaning becomes signs. A decision becomes a sentence, a figure becomes a table, a warning becomes a bold notice. Encoding fails when the sender uses signs that the receiver cannot read the same way. The sender has the meaning in mind and tends to read their own text as clear. The check is to read the message as the receiver would, with only the receiver's knowledge.

The **channel** carries the signs. It may be spoken, written, posted, digital or on a form. The channel does not create meaning, but it changes what can go wrong. A telephone call allows a question at once; a notice cannot be questioned. Channel choice is treated in full below, but the cycle needs the idea now: the channel is a link, and it can add or remove a chance for feedback.

**Noise** is any interference that changes the meaning between encoding and decoding. The word is broader than loud background sound. Noise can be physical (the room is loud), semantic (a term has a different meaning for the receiver), psychological (the receiver is afraid), organisational (two instructions contradict each other), cultural (a norm changes how a request is heard) or technological (the file will not open). Eight barrier types are treated in full below. Here the point is that noise can enter at any link, and the sender usually does not feel it.

**Decoding** is the receiver's rebuilding of meaning from what arrived. It is the receiver's act, and the sender cannot perform it for them. A receiver may decode a message correctly, partly, or wrongly, and each case produces different behaviour. Decoding is visible only through what the receiver does or says afterwards.

**Feedback** is the return signal. It confirms, corrects or exposes a misunderstanding. Feedback can be a question, a restatement, a correct action or a visible error. It is the only link through which the sender learns the state of the other links. This is why the cycle is a loop.

### Part B (Core): the eight-question checklist

Before any important message, the sender can run eight questions. They appear here in the order the exam often rewards.

| Element | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Sender | Who initiates the communication, and with what authority? |
| Purpose | Is the message meant to inform, consult, persuade, instruct, warn, negotiate or record? |
| Message | Is it accurate, relevant, complete and understandable? |
| Channel | Which medium fits the purpose, urgency, complexity and need for a record? |
| Receiver | What language, knowledge, interest, vulnerability or accessibility need matters? |
| Feedback | How will understanding or compliance be confirmed? |
| Context | Which institutional, cultural, emotional or time constraint affects the meaning? |
| Noise | Which physical, semantic, psychological, organisational or technological barrier could interfere? |

The checklist does not ask you to write a long plan. It asks you to notice the one element that is weak. Most messages are accurate and relevant; the weak point is usually the receiver, the feedback or the noise. A message can pass every test except the last, and it still fails.

### Part C (Core): sending is not communicating

The core distinction of this lesson is that information can be transmitted one way. Effective communication normally needs interpretation and feedback. A bulletin on a noticeboard transmits information in one direction, and it may be enough when the purpose is only to notify. A bulletin that asks staff to change a procedure needs more: a way for the sender to see whether staff understood the change.

A useful test is to ask what the sender would be able to say, one week after the message, about what the receiver did with it. If the honest answer is "I sent it," then the cycle has not closed.

### The five-check before a message matters

1. **Name the purpose in one word.** Inform, instruct, consult, persuade, warn, negotiate or record.
2. **State what the receiver must understand or do.** Write it as an observable result.
3. **Choose a channel that fits.** Test it against urgency, complexity, sensitivity and the need for a record.
4. **Name the likeliest noise.** Which link is weakest for this receiver?
5. **Decide how feedback will be confirmed.** Name the question, the action or the reply that will show understanding.

**Failure condition.** If the purpose cannot be named in one word, the message is probably mixed. It may inform and instruct at the same time, and the receiver will not know which governs. Split it into two messages, or decide which purpose controls and state it. If step 5 has no answer, the communication is not finished, only sent.

### Examples, with their limits

**Example 1: the circular with the table.** A district health officer sends a circular with a dosage table to fifty clinics. Each clinic replies by ticking a register that says "received." The register confirms transmission. It does not confirm decoding. A question such as "State the dose for the youngest group" would test decoding, and a wrong answer would show where the table was misread.

*Limit of the example:* the register is not worthless. It proves that the message reached the clinic, and for a notice whose purpose is only notification it may be enough. The example bites only when the purpose is action.

**Example 2: the meeting that nodded.** A manager says, "Let us become more efficient," and the room nods. Each person heard a different plan: fewer meetings, longer hours, fewer reports, a new software tool. The encoding was vague, and the nodding was not feedback. It showed politeness or a wish to close the item.

*Limit of the example:* nodding is not always empty. In some settings a nod is the only acceptable reply. The lesson is that a nod does not show which meaning was received, so a follow-up question is needed when the purpose depends on it.

**Example 3: the email with the noisy word.** A sender writes "escalate the file to the review desk," meaning forward it for information. The receiver reads "escalate" as a formal referral with a deadline and reports it. The channel carried the words perfectly. The noise was semantic: the word meant two things to two readers.

### A tempting wrong turn, and why it fails

**Wrong approach:** "The message was delivered, so the responsibility has ended with me."

**Reply:** The cycle makes the sender responsible for the meaning that arrives, not only for the signal. The success test is what the receiver understood for the purpose. Delivery is the first stage of the job. It is not the last. This reply does not blame the receiver for every failure. If a receiver misreads a clear message, the decoding failure is real, but the sender still has to check, because the check is the only way the failure can be found.

**A second wrong approach:** "If the message is not understood, add more words." **Reply:** More words raise the volume of the signal and can increase the noise. Information overload is a barrier of its own. The remedy depends on the barrier, and a diagnosis comes before any remedy, as the barrier discussion below shows.

### UPSC application

Items in this area ask why an instruction was not followed, what a sender should do next, or which step failed. The best answer is usually the option that adds a check of understanding, such as a question that tests the receiver's reading or a confirmation that is specific to the purpose. Weaker options repeat the message louder, treat transmission as proof, or blame the medium without evidence that the medium caused the failure. When the stem says the message was delivered without distortion, the medium is cleared. When it also says the encoding was clear, the failure lies at decoding. Feedback is the check that would have exposed the failure; it is the failed link only when the stem shows that a check was made and ignored.

### Revision points

1. Communication is the receiver grasping the intended meaning well enough for the purpose. Transmission is only the message leaving.
2. The cycle is a loop: sender, encoding, channel, noise, receiver, decoding and feedback, with feedback returning to the sender.
3. The sender owns the meaning that arrives. The receiver owns their own decoding.
4. Authority tells the receiver how much weight a message carries. It does not prove understanding.
5. Encoding fails when the sender reads their own text as clear.
6. The channel is a link that can add or remove a chance for feedback.
7. Noise can enter at any link and may be physical, semantic, psychological, organisational, cultural or technological.
8. Receipt is not understanding. A register, an acknowledgement or a "noted" reply shows arrival.
9. Feedback is the only link that shows the state of the others. Use a question or an action that tests the meaning.
10. Before any important message, name the purpose in one word, state what the receiver must understand, and decide how feedback will confirm it.

### Practice

**MCQ 1**

A district officer emails a revised circular to all field staff and records the task as complete once the email has been sent. Which description of the communication fits the cycle?

- A. Incomplete until feedback shows that staff had understood the revised procedure.
- B. Complete, because the full text left the officer's account in the intended wording.
- C. Complete, because a sent email is a written record that the staff have received it.
- D. Incomplete, because email is an unsuitable channel for procedural revisions.

**MCQ 1 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The success test is understanding for the purpose. A revised procedure is complete only when feedback shows that staff decoded it, for example through a question answered correctly or an action taken under the revision.
- **B — Incorrect:** This treats transmission as communication. Leaving the account intact shows only that the signs were sent, which is the first stage of the cycle, not its success test.
- **C — Incorrect:** This treats a record of delivery as proof of decoding. A sent email shows arrival at most; it does not show what each reader took from the change.
- **D — Incorrect:** This judges the channel rather than the gap. Email is not unsuitable for revisions; the gap is the missing check of understanding, which a feedback step closes on the same channel.

**MCQ 2**

A sender encodes a clear instruction, and the channel delivers it without distortion. The receiver reads the word 'escalate' as 'set aside for now', because that is how the receiver's unit uses the term, and acts on that reading. Which link of the cycle has failed?

- A. The channel, because the instruction travelled by a medium the receiver did not expect.
- B. Decoding, because a shared word carried a different meaning for the receiver's unit.
- C. The sender's authority, because the instruction came from someone without power over the procedure.
- D. Encoding, because the sender should have avoided the word 'escalate' altogether.

**MCQ 2 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** The stem says the channel delivered the instruction without distortion. The medium is cleared by the case itself.
- **B — Correct:** Encoding was clear and transmission was intact, so the meaning changed where the receiver rebuilt it. That is decoding, disturbed here by a term with a different sense for the receiver.
- **C — Incorrect:** Authority decides whether an instruction binds. The stem gives no reason to doubt the sender's standing, and the failure concerns meaning, not power.
- **D — Incorrect:** The stem says the instruction was clear as encoded, so the sign was not faulty. The fault lies in how the receiver read the sign, not in how the sender produced it.

**MCQ 3**

A supervisor sends a set of instructions to a team through the usual route. Two days later, three items are done in a way the instructions did not allow. The route delivered every message, and the team was present when the instructions were given. Which step of the cycle was not checked?

- A. The supervisor's authority to issue these instructions to this particular team.
- B. The choice of the usual route as the channel for the instructions.
- C. Whether a reply showed that the team had read the instructions as intended.
- D. Whether the instructions were entered in the office register on the day they were issued.

**MCQ 3 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Authority decides whether an instruction binds. The case shows no question about who issued the instructions, so binding force is not the step that failed.
- **B — Incorrect:** The stem says the route delivered every message. The channel was therefore not the unchecked step.
- **C — Correct:** Delivery and presence do not show decoding. The unchecked step is a test that required a reply revealing how the team read the instructions, such as a restatement or a correct action.
- **D — Incorrect:** A register entry shows that instructions were issued and filed. It cannot reveal misreading, so filing would not have exposed the failure.

### Visual: the channel decision as a grid

```text
                    FAST / URGENT            RECORD NEEDED           SENSITIVE / PRIVATE
ORAL                 strong (immediate        weak (memory          good for tone, weak
                     check possible)          fades)                for proof
WRITTEN              slow feedback            strong (durable,      good when the text
```

Read the grid by asking the purpose first. The columns are the questions the channel must answer. A form is a tool; its fit depends on which column you are in.

### Plain intuition

Think of a channel as a road. A fast road is not better than a slow one if you are carrying something that must be checked at each turn. A written note is a road that leaves a trace, but it is slow to ask questions along. A conversation is quick to correct and hard to prove afterwards. Most communication problems in this area come from choosing the road by habit rather than by the load.

The second idea is that a message has more than one channel at once. Words travel through the spoken or written form. Tone, posture, distance and timing travel through other routes. Two channels can disagree, and the receiver has to decide which to trust. That disagreement is where many misreadings start.

### Part A (Core): the forms and their traps

The seven forms each have a strength and a trap. Learn the pairs together, because an exam item usually tests the trade-off, not the definition.

| Form | Strength | Limitation or trap |
|---|---|---|
| Oral | Fast clarification and immediate feedback | Memory and record may be weak |
| Written | Precision, consistency and a durable record | Feedback is slow, and wording can be misread |
| Non-verbal | Conveys attention, confidence and emotion | Depends on culture and context; cannot override clear words automatically |
| Visual | Simplifies patterns, procedures and warnings | A bad scale, label or symbol can distort the meaning |
| Digital | Speed, reach and searchability | Access, privacy, overload, impersonation and loss of context |
| Formal | Authority, accountability and traceability | Can become rigid or heavy with jargon |
| Informal | Speed and relationship-building | Rumour, exclusion and weak accountability |

Two of these pairs need care. **Formal and informal** describe the relationship and the authority behind a message. They do not describe the medium. A formal message can be spoken in a corridor if it is an official instruction given with authority, and an informal message can be a written note between colleagues. **Oral and written** describe the medium. Mixing the two pairs produces a common error: assuming that a written message must be formal, or that spoken messages must be informal.

### Part B (Core): verbal and non-verbal cues in full prose

A verbal cue is the content of what is said or written, and also its wording, pace and tone. A verbal message carries the literal meaning, but it also tells the receiver how much urgency, respect or doubt the sender attaches to it. Consider the sentence "Please send the report when you have a moment." The words ask for the report. A quick tone makes the request urgent. A slow, careful tone makes it considerate. The literal content is the same, and the receiver's response may differ.

A non-verbal cue is everything else a sender communicates without words: eye contact, posture, facial expression, gesture, distance, timing and silence. Non-verbal cues convey attention, confidence and emotion. A sender who turns away while saying "I am listening" has given two signals, and the receiver usually trusts the cue that is harder to fake. This is an analytical judgement, not a rule: it explains why receivers feel that the body contradicts the words, but it does not justify reading every gesture as a truth.

Two limits matter for the exam. First, non-verbal cues are **culture- and context-sensitive**. Eye contact that signals honesty in one setting can signal challenge in another, and silence can mean thought, assent, disagreement or fear. Second, a non-verbal cue **cannot override clear words automatically**. If a supervisor says "Your report is accepted" while looking at the clock, the receiver has to resolve the mismatch. The right response is a question, not a conclusion about the supervisor's real attitude.

The working rule for the exam is to **align** the words, the tone and the context. When they agree, the message is stronger. When they disagree, the message is not yet clear, and the sender should say the intended meaning in plain words and confirm it.

*Example, with its limit.* A senior officer tells a junior, in a warm tone and with a smile, "Take your time and send the draft by the end of the week." The next day the junior sends the draft on Friday evening and is told it was late. The warm tone conveyed reassurance, the words set a deadline, and the context (the week's workload) was never stated. The lesson is not that the officer was deceptive. It is that a message needs its deadline in words, because the tone can be read as "no hurry."

*Limit of the example:* we cannot tell from the case whether the officer meant the deadline firmly. The point is that the receiver could not know, and the cure is to put the deadline in words.

### Part C (Core): channel criteria

A channel is chosen by six criteria, read together: **purpose, urgency, complexity, sensitivity, need for a record and receiver accessibility**. The paper rewards the choice that answers the criteria, not the channel that is most familiar.

Work through the criteria as a short list. Is the message urgent, so that a delay is a harm? Is it complex, so that a spoken explanation may lose detail? Is it sensitive, so that an open medium is unsafe? Must there be a record, so that a later dispute can be settled from the document? Can the receiver use the channel, given their language, disability, connectivity or literacy? The answers pull in different directions, and the best channel is the one that meets the most important criterion without breaking the others. Often a two-part message works: a spoken briefing for urgency, followed by a written summary for the record.

*Example, with its limit.* An urgent but complex change to a payment procedure is best given in a short meeting for questions, followed by a written note that sets out the steps. The meeting handles urgency and feedback; the note handles complexity and record. The limit: the note alone would be slower to correct, and the meeting alone would leave no trail.

### Part D (Core): firm and open, formal and informal

**Firm** communication gives a clear instruction, a boundary or a decision. **Open-ended** communication invites a response or exploration. The two are two different intentions, and each can be right. A firm message closes a question that has been decided. An open message keeps a question open so that the receiver can contribute. Neither pair is automatically better. The rule is to match the stated relationship and purpose. A decision that has been taken should be firm, and a consultation that has not yet produced its inputs should be open.

*Example.* A department head decides the date of a training session and tells the team the date is fixed. That is firm, and the team needs the boundary. The same head, asking the team what hours would suit them, is open. Using a firm tone for the consultation would signal that the answer does not matter; using an open tone for the decision would invite debate on a point that is closed.

*Limit.* Firm can be rude, and open can be evasive. The test is whether the form of the message fits the intention behind it. A firm message needs a reason the receiver can see, and an open message needs a stated limit on what is open.

### Part E (Core): purpose-to-tool matching

Choosing a tool is a matching task. The tool is the medium together with the audience it reaches. A tool does not have one purpose forever: the same medium can serve different purposes when it is framed differently. The taught principle is to match the form to the purpose and the audience, and to judge a medium by how it works for them rather than by its name.

*Analytical inference (a working aid, not a rule from the sources):* a practical habit is to ask two questions of any medium: who must act on the message, and who must be able to see that it arrived. The answers usually settle the choice. The habit is drawn from the channel criteria in Part C; the exam rewards the purpose match itself.

*Example, with its limit.* A town office must warn residents of a road closure that starts tomorrow morning. The purpose is warning and the urgency is high. Few residents read email, but many pass the notice board at the bus stop and listen to the community radio. A notice on both, with the same wording, reaches them in time. The limit: a board reaches only the people who pass it, so the message must also go where people already look, and the wording must not change between the two routes.

**Audience and form.** Who receives a message is part of its form. Some messages are written once for a defined group; others can be shaped to the person who receives them. The general principle is to name the audience first, and then choose the form that reaches it with the text the purpose needs.

### Checking the channel before sending

1. Name the purpose in one word.
2. Name who must receive the message, and whether they need the same text or a text shaped to one person.
3. Name what the receiver must do or understand, and whether a record is needed.
4. Choose the form whose reach, timing and record fit those answers, and say why.

**Failure condition.** The check fails when the purpose is mixed or the audience is unknown. Then split the message, or find out who the audience is before choosing. A familiar medium that does not reach the audience in time is not a fit.

### The shortcut that looks like good practice

**Wrong approach:** "Formal means written, and informal means spoken, so I can pick the channel from the relationship alone."

**Reply:** Formality belongs to authority and relationship, and the medium is a separate choice. A formal instruction can be given orally to a team that will also receive it in writing. An informal exchange can be written. The purpose and the audience decide the channel, under the habit described in Part E; the relationship decides the tone and the level of formality. Picking the channel by formality alone skips the purpose, which is the first question.

**A second wrong approach:** "Written communication is always better because it is precise." **Reply:** Precision is a strength of writing, but writing has the trap of slow feedback and misreadable wording. A written message that cannot be questioned can fail as badly as an unclear speech. The reply is not to avoid writing but to pair it with a way to confirm understanding when the purpose needs it.

### UPSC application

Channel and purpose items often present a relationship, a tool or a message, and ask which pairing fits. Start with the purpose and urgency. Then check the match between medium, audience and purpose. Eliminate options that break the stated purpose or relationship before comparing the rest. Matching items on tools and purposes reward the discipline of stating the purpose in one word first. Items that pair a relationship with a register reward the reading of the relationship's authority, and the firm or open intent of the message.

### Revision points

1. Forms are oral, written, non-verbal, visual, digital, formal and informal. Each has a strength and a trap.
2. Formal and informal describe relationship and authority. Oral and written describe medium. Do not mix the two pairs.
3. A verbal cue is content plus wording, pace and tone. A non-verbal cue is gesture, posture, distance, timing and silence.
4. Non-verbal cues are culture- and context-sensitive, and they cannot override clear words automatically.
5. When words and cues disagree, ask a question and state the intended meaning in plain words.
6. Choose a channel by purpose, urgency, complexity, sensitivity, need for a record and receiver accessibility.
7. Firm communication closes a decided matter. Open-ended communication keeps an undecided matter open for response.
8. Match a tool to the purpose and the audience, and judge it by how it works for them.
9. The same tool can serve more than one purpose. Frame it, and test it again.
10. A two-part message, with a briefing for urgency and a written summary for the record, often beats a single channel.
11. Do not choose a channel by habit. Choose it by the load the message carries.

### Practice

**MCQ 4**

A department must tell all staff about a leave rule that takes effect next month, and must later be able to show that each officer received the same text. Which channel meets both needs?

- A. A verbal announcement at the morning briefing, with the text kept only in the speaker's notes.
- B. A volunteer's summary posted in an informal group chat for anyone who wishes to read it.
- C. A telephone call to the three senior officers, who will pass the rule on to their own teams.
- D. A written circular to all officers, with a dated delivery record kept on file.

**MCQ 4 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** A spoken announcement reaches the people present but leaves no shared text. The stem requires the same text to be shown later, which speech cannot provide.
- **B — Incorrect:** A volunteer's summary is a paraphrase by someone else. It breaks the requirement that each officer receive the same text, and an informal group keeps no reliable record.
- **C — Incorrect:** The call reaches three officers, so the audience requirement fails. Relaying the rule through them adds a further layer of paraphrase and no record.
- **D — Correct:** A written circular reaches every officer with the same text, and a dated delivery record shows later what each officer received. The channel meets the audience, the sameness of text and the record together.

**MCQ 5**

A head of office wants a junior officer to walk through how a file was processed, stage by stage. Which mode is most suitable?

- A. An open question that invites the officer to describe the process at each step in full.
- B. A firm, closed instruction that names the one reply the officer may give.
- C. A formal written order that lists the steps and bars comment on them.
- D. An informal aside that asks whether the file was handled acceptably.

**MCQ 5 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The head needs the officer's account of each step, which an open question draws out. The open form keeps the matter open for the officer's response, which is what the purpose requires.
- **B — Incorrect:** A closed instruction limits the reply to one permitted answer, so the account of each step cannot come out. It is firm where the purpose calls for openness.
- **C — Incorrect:** A formal order that bars comment shuts the exchange the head needs. Formality is not the defect; the form blocks the response the purpose requires.
- **D — Incorrect:** A question about acceptability invites a yes or no and does not draw out the steps. The aside is informal, but the purpose is an account, which a yes-or-no answer cannot give.

## Lesson 3 - Listening and feedback: the HEAR chain, question types, paraphrase and the properties of feedback

Progress: 3/9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Listening and feedback

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the HEAR chain as a loop with a check at the end

```text
HEAR -> ATTEND -> CLARIFY -> PARAPHRASE -> RESPOND -> CONFIRM
  |        |          |            |            |          |
  |        |          |            |            |          +-- did the speaker agree
  |        |          |            |            |              the meaning is right?
  |        |          |            |            +-- answer, decide or act
  |        |          |            +-- restate the meaning in your own words
  |        |          +-- ask for what is missing or ambiguous
  |        +-- give the speaker full attention; hold judgement
  +-- take in the words and the concern
```

The chain is a sequence, but not a straight line. If the speaker says the paraphrase is wrong, the listener returns to CLARIFY. The chain only moves forward when the speaker has confirmed the meaning. The arrow back is the most important part of the diagram.

### Plain intuition

Listening is an active task. Hearing is what the ear does. Listening is what the mind does with what it hears: holding the meaning, sorting facts from concerns, noticing what is missing and checking the result with the speaker. Most listening failures are not failures of attention but of judgement. The listener decides early what the speaker means, then hears only what fits.

Here is a useful distinction. **Understanding** is the accurate grasp of what the speaker means. **Agreement** is whether the listener accepts the claim. The two are separate. A listener can understand a complaint exactly and still disagree with it, lawfully and respectfully. Listening does not require agreement. It requires that the listener first be right about what was said.

### Doctrine: the chain and its tools

**Definition.** Active listening gives attention, avoids premature judgement and identifies both the facts and the concerns in what is said. It is the listener's job to find both, and to say back what they have found.

Each step of the HEAR chain has a function that the next step depends on.

- **HEAR** is taking in the words and the concern behind them. It is the stage at which the listener notes what is said without answering it.
- **ATTEND** is giving the speaker full attention, so that the listener's own reply does not interrupt the message.
- **CLARIFY** asks for what is missing or ambiguous. A clarifying question asks for the missing piece without putting words into the speaker's mouth.
- **PARAPHRASE** restates the understood meaning in the listener's words, so that the speaker can correct it.
- **RESPOND** answers, decides or acts on the meaning that has now been confirmed.
- **CONFIRM** checks that the speaker agrees the meaning was right before the matter is closed.

### Part A (Core): the HEAR chain step by step

**HEAR and ATTEND.** These two steps set the conditions for everything after them. A listener who prepares a reply while the speaker is still talking has stopped listening, even while nodding. The test is whether the listener can name the speaker's main concern without looking at their own notes. If not, the listener is still composing the answer.

**CLARIFY.** This step handles missing or ambiguous information. Suppose a colleague says, "The file has been with them for a while." The listener does not know who "them" is or how long "a while" means. A clarifying question, such as "With which desk, and since when?", gets the missing piece. A poor clarifying question would supply the answer: "Do you mean the review desk, which is always slow?" That question puts the listener's assumption into the speaker's mouth.

**PARAPHRASE.** The paraphrase is the most powerful step, because it is a test. The listener restates the meaning: "So the file has been with the review desk since last month, and you want it moved this week." If the speaker says "yes," the meaning is confirmed. If the speaker says "not quite, it has been at the registry," the listener has discovered an error before acting on it. The paraphrase should be the listener's words, not a copy of the speaker's, and it should not add the listener's own view.

**RESPOND and CONFIRM.** Only after the meaning is confirmed should the listener answer. The confirmation closes the loop. "Does that match what you need?" is a good final check.

### Part B (Core): question types

The two most useful question types are the open question and the closed question.

- An **open question** invites explanation. "How did the delay happen?" asks the speaker to describe a sequence. It is useful for exploring a problem and for finding the facts the listener did not know to ask for.
- A **closed question** obtains a specific fact or a confirmation. "How many files are pending in the section?" asks for a number. "Is the file with the review desk?" asks for a yes or no. Closed questions are useful for a report, for a check, or for ending a vague exchange.

Two further kinds of question are often misused. A **leading question** pushes the speaker towards a chosen answer. A **loaded question** presupposes something that the speaker has not accepted. Both damage listening, because they tell the speaker what the listener expects. The same two defects reappear in written surveys. In conversation, the same defects change the speaker's answer.

*Example, with its limit.* A supervisor asks a subordinate, "Why did you miss the deadline?" That is a closed-form question in a loaded frame, because it assumes the deadline was missed and asks for a reason. An open question would be, "What happened with the deadline?" A closed question would be, "Was the report sent by Friday?" The first closed question is useful if the purpose is to establish a fact. The limit: a closed question gets the fact but may miss the context that explains it, so the supervisor may need an open follow-up.

### Part C (Core): paraphrase in practice

The paraphrase protects against two errors. It protects against the listener's assumption, because a wrong meaning is caught early. It also protects the speaker, because the speaker learns that the listener has tried to understand. A useful pattern is to name the facts and the concern separately: "The facts are that the file was moved on the eleventh, and your concern is that the move cost a week." The speaker can then confirm each part.

*Example, with its limit.* A complainant says, "This is the third time I have come here." A paraphrase of the facts alone would be, "You have visited three times." A paraphrase of the concern would be, "You are frustrated that the problem has not been settled." Both are needed. The limit: a paraphrase of the concern can slip into interpretation. Keep it tied to what the speaker said, and let the speaker correct it.

### Part D (Core): feedback and its properties

**Feedback** is information that returns to the person whose conduct or message it concerns. Four properties are standard. Feedback should be **timely**, given close enough to the event that the person can act on it. It should be **specific**, naming the thing that happened. It should be **behaviour-focused**, describing what was done and not what kind of person the receiver is. It should be **actionable**, pointing to a change the receiver can make. The properties work together, and a feedback statement that lacks one of them is weaker.

Feedback is also **two-way**. The receiver should be able to respond, to explain the context, and to ask for clarification. Two-way feedback is what turns a one-sided judgement into a shared correction.

*Example, with its limit.* A manager tells a team member, "Your last two reports had wrong totals. Please check each total against the source sheet before you send." That feedback is timely if given the same week, specific because it names the reports and the error, behaviour-focused because it names the checking step, and actionable because it gives the step. The limit: if the team member explains that the source sheet was itself wrong, the feedback must be two-way, and the manager must check the sheet before blaming the checking.

### Working the listening chain

1. Hear the whole statement before judging any part of it.
2. Clarify the one thing that is missing or ambiguous.
3. Paraphrase the facts and the concern separately, and let the speaker correct you.
4. Respond only after the speaker confirms the meaning.
5. Confirm at the end that the speaker agrees the meaning was right.

**Failure condition.** The chain fails when the paraphrase adds the listener's own view, or when the listener responds before confirmation. Then return to step 2 and ask again.

### The reply that feels like listening

**Wrong approach:** "If I agree with the speaker, I have listened well. If I disagree, I have not."

**Reply:** That confuses listening with agreement. The trap is that a listener may understand accurately and still disagree lawfully and respectfully. The test of listening is whether the paraphrase is confirmed, not whether the listener accepts the claim. A listener who disagrees should say so after the meaning is confirmed, and should give reasons.

**A second wrong approach:** "Feedback is only useful when it is negative, so tell people what they did wrong." **Reply:** Feedback is useful when it is specific and actionable, whether it praises or corrects. A vague criticism ("you are careless") gives the receiver nothing to do. Specific praise or correction, given in time, changes behaviour more reliably than a general mood.

### UPSC application

Items on listening and feedback often describe a short exchange and ask what the listener should do next, or which response shows good listening. The best response in these items completes the chain in order: it clarifies or paraphrases before it decides, and it confirms before it acts. Watch for responses that answer before the meaning is confirmed, that supply the listener's own assumption as a question, or that treat listening as agreement. Feedback items reward the four properties. When two responses both give feedback, prefer the one that is specific, behaviour-focused and actionable, and given in time.

### Revision points

1. HEAR -> ATTEND -> CLARIFY -> PARAPHRASE -> RESPOND -> CONFIRM is the listening chain. Move forward only when the speaker has confirmed the meaning.
2. Hearing is what the ear does. Listening is holding the meaning and checking it.
3. Understanding is an accurate grasp of the speaker's meaning. Agreement is acceptance of the claim. They are separate.
4. ATTEND means the reply is not being composed while the speaker is still talking.
5. CLARIFY asks for what is missing without supplying the answer yourself.
6. An open question invites explanation. A closed question gets a fact or a confirmation.
7. A leading question pushes the speaker. A loaded question presupposes an unaccepted claim.
8. PARAPHRASE is a test. The speaker's correction is the signal that the meaning is wrong.
9. Paraphrase the facts and the concern separately, and keep the paraphrase tied to what was said.
10. Feedback is timely, specific, behaviour-focused and actionable.
11. Feedback is two-way. The receiver can explain the context and ask for clarification.
12. A listener can understand exactly and still disagree lawfully.

## Lesson 4 - Barriers: eight types, diagnosis before remedy, and communication under complexity

Progress: 4/9 | Stage: Core, with an Advanced part | Subtopic: Barriers and the correct remedy

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: eight barriers, one question each

```text
WHERE DOES THE MEANING GET LOST?

  before the words reach the ear or eye
     PHYSICAL ............ the room, the line, the distance
     TECHNOLOGICAL ....... the file, the platform, the connection

  in the words themselves
     SEMANTIC ............ the term means something else to the receiver
     INFORMATION OVERLOAD  too many items; the important one is buried

  in the people and the structure
     PSYCHOLOGICAL ....... fear, anger, prejudice, defensiveness
     ORGANISATIONAL ...... conflicting orders, long chains, information silos
     CULTURAL / SOCIAL ... different norms, status effects, exclusion
     SELECTIVE PERCEPTION  the receiver hears only what confirms a belief
```

Each barrier sits at a different point in the cycle from Lesson 1. A physical barrier lies in the channel. A semantic barrier lies in decoding. A psychological barrier lies in the receiver's state. An organisational barrier lies in the structure around both parties. Knowing where a barrier sits tells you what will not fix it.

### Plain intuition

A barrier is anything that stops a meaning from arriving intact. The cure depends on the barrier. A person who cannot hear you in a loud room needs a quieter place. A person who cannot follow the word "remit" needs a plain sentence. A person who is afraid of their manager needs a safe place to speak. Giving the wrong cure makes the situation worse, because it spends effort on a problem that is not there.

Think of a doctor. A fever is a symptom, and a doctor who prescribes a painkiller without finding the cause has not treated the patient. The same discipline applies to communication. The visible symptom is that the message failed. The question is which barrier caused the failure.

### Doctrine: the barrier types and the rule of diagnosis

**Definition.** A communication barrier is a condition that interferes with the meaning between sender and receiver. Eight types are standard. Each one has a diagnostic feature, an example and a better remedy.

**The rule.** Diagnose the barrier before selecting the response. Public blame cannot fix an ambiguous instruction. More technical detail cannot fix a language mismatch. Louder repetition cannot fix a fear that stops the receiver from asking. The response must be matched to the barrier, not to the frustration of the sender.

### Part A (Core): the eight barriers

**Physical.** The interference is in the environment: noise, distance, poor acoustics, a crowded room, a line that cuts out. *Remedy:* change the place or the channel, then repeat and confirm.

**Semantic.** The interference is in the words: jargon, ambiguous terms, a translation that fails, or a term used in a sense the receiver does not share. *Remedy:* plain language, examples and a shared definition of the key terms.

**Psychological.** The interference is in the receiver's state: fear, anger, prejudice or defensiveness. The receiver may hear the words and still not take them in. *Remedy:* psychological safety, a neutral tone and a private place for clarification.

**Organisational.** The interference is in the structure: two instructions that contradict each other, a hierarchy so long that a message is retold at each level, or information kept within one unit. *Remedy:* a single authoritative instruction, clear role boundaries and a defined path for escalation.

**Cultural or social.** The interference is in the norms that shape how a request is heard: different expectations of directness, the effect of status, or the exclusion of a group from the conversation. *Remedy:* respectful adaptation of style, avoidance of stereotypes, and inclusion of the affected voices.

**Technological.** The interference is in the tools: poor connectivity, a file format the receiver cannot open, or a platform that fails. *Remedy:* an alternative channel, an accessible format and a backup route.

**Information overload.** The interference is in the quantity: too many messages, or irrelevant detail that hides the item that matters. *Remedy:* prioritise, structure the message, and summarise the action points at the top.

**Selective perception.** The interference is in the receiver's filter: the receiver hears only what confirms an existing belief. *Remedy:* ask for evidence, paraphrase the other side's view, and invite information that might contradict the receiver's expectation.

A note on labels. Papers and books name these families with different words. Some use cognitive, affective or cultural labels for families that overlap the list above. The fact pattern in the question controls the answer, not the label. When a label is unfamiliar, describe the fact pattern in your own words, then choose the barrier whose diagnostic feature the pattern shows.

### Part B (Core): diagnosis before remedy

The diagnosis is a short set of questions, asked in order. First, is the problem in the environment or the channel? If yes, the barrier is physical or technological, and the remedy changes the place or the medium. Second, is the problem in the words? If the receiver cannot restate the term, the barrier is semantic. Third, is the receiver silent or defensive? That points to psychological interference. Fourth, do two sources of authority disagree? That is organisational. Fifth, is the receiver overwhelmed or selectively hearing? Those are information overload and selective perception.

The order matters. A remedy that is correct for the wrong barrier fails, and it also makes the sender feel the matter has been handled. Use the diagnostic feature before the remedy.

*Example, with its limit.* A leave policy is circulated as a dense legal paragraph. Most staff ask the same question in the corridor afterwards. The diagnostic feature is the words: the staff can hear the paragraph but cannot say what it requires. The barrier is semantic, and the remedy is a plain-language version with worked examples. The limit: part of the confusion might come from two different policies being in force, which would make the barrier organisational as well. The diagnosis must check for that.

### Part C (Advanced): communication under complexity

*This part is Advanced. It extends the diagnosis to situations where several barriers act at once. It is not required to answer the Core diagnosis above, but it is taught in full here.*

Real administrative communication often has several failures at the same time: uncertain facts, unequal power, strong emotion, time pressure, multiple audiences and a need for a record. A single barrier diagnosis is then not enough. A layered response is more useful:

```text
uncertain facts + unequal power + strong emotion + time pressure
            + multiple audiences + need for a record
        |
        v
1. stabilise the immediate safety or service
2. separate verified facts from allegations
3. identify the audiences and their information needs
4. choose the channel and the spokesperson
5. say what is known, what is unknown and what is being done
6. create feedback and correction loops
7. document decisions, and update them when the facts change
```

The sequence is practical. Safety or service comes first, because a communication cannot wait for a perfect account while harm continues. Separating facts from allegations comes next, because a message that repeats an unverified claim spreads it. The audiences and the spokesperson decide who speaks and to whom. The known-unknown-action statement is the core of honest communication under uncertainty, and the feedback loop and the record keep the message correctable.

*Example, with its limit.* A water supply fails in one ward. Residents are frightened, a rumour says the water is contaminated, and two departments are unsure who is responsible. The first step is to make the supply safe or to issue an interim advisory. The second is to say what has been tested and what has not. The third is to speak through one authorised channel. The limit: the sequence assumes an authority exists to act. Where it does not, the first step becomes an escalation, and the message must say so.

**Failure condition.** The sequence fails when a later step is run before the first one is secured, for example when a public statement goes out before safety or service is protected, or when an unverified allegation is repeated as fact. Then return to the step that was skipped before any further message is sent.

### Working order for diagnosis

1. **Name the symptom** in one line: what did not arrive?
2. **Ask the five diagnostic questions** in order: environment or channel; words; receiver's state; structure; receiver's filter.
3. **Name the barrier** whose feature the facts show.
4. **Choose the remedy** for that barrier, and check it does not treat a different barrier.
5. **Use the ordered sequence** only when two or more barriers appear at once.

**Where it breaks.** The method fails when the facts do not show a feature of any single barrier. Then the first step is to collect the missing facts, usually by a question to the receiver. Repeating the message does not collect facts.

### The remedy that feels obvious

**Wrong approach:** "The message was not understood, so I will repeat it more loudly and more firmly."

**Reply:** Loudness addresses a physical barrier and nothing else. A semantic barrier needs plainer words; a psychological barrier needs safety; an organisational barrier needs one authority. Repetition with more force may also raise the receiver's defensiveness, which creates a psychological barrier where none existed. Diagnose first.

**A second wrong approach:** "The receiver does not understand the technical term, so I will explain it with more technical detail." **Reply:** More technical detail deepens a language mismatch. The remedy for a semantic barrier is a shared definition in plain words, followed by an example.

**A third wrong approach:** "The team's confusion is a discipline problem, so I will blame the person who asked the question." **Reply:** Public blame cannot fix an ambiguous instruction. The question is evidence that the instruction was unclear, and the remedy is to restate it in one clear form.

### UPSC application

Barrier items often describe a situation and ask which barrier is present, or which remedy fits. The discipline is to name the diagnostic feature first. A remedy that matches a different feature is usually the distractor. Watch for options that offer a remedy for the wrong barrier, such as a technical explanation for a language problem, or a public correction for a private fear. Items that describe several failures at once reward the Advanced sequence: safety, facts, audience, channel, honest status, feedback and record, in that order.

### Revision points

1. A barrier is any condition that stops a meaning arriving intact.
2. The eight barriers are physical, semantic, psychological, organisational, cultural or social, technological, information overload and selective perception.
3. Each barrier has a diagnostic feature. Name the feature before the remedy.
4. Physical and technological barriers lie in the environment and the medium. Semantic barriers lie in the words.
5. Psychological barriers lie in the receiver's state. Organisational barriers lie in the structure.
6. Information overload is a quantity problem. Selective perception is a filter problem.
7. Louder repetition fixes only physical interference.
8. Technical detail deepens a language mismatch.
9. Public blame cannot fix an ambiguous instruction.
10. Labels vary by source. Read the fact pattern, not the label alone.
11. When several barriers act at once, use the ordered sequence: safety, facts, audience, channel, honest status, feedback, record.
12. Where facts are unknown, say so, and collect them before deciding the remedy.

The middle position is not a compromise between two weak styles. It is a different style with a different purpose: to say what is true and what must change, without giving up respect for the other person.

### Plain intuition

Imagine a meeting where one colleague keeps talking over the others. You can say nothing and let the meeting drift. You can shout that the colleague is rude and never listens. You can say calmly that you need to finish your point, and ask the group to hear each speaker in turn. The first silences you. The second wins a short argument and loses the working relationship. The third gets what the meeting needs.

The eight interpersonal skills are the conduct that lets a correct message land. A message that is accurate but delivered with contempt will not be heard, and a message that is kind but evasive will not be acted on. The skills keep the substance and the respect together.

### Doctrine: what an interpersonal skill is

**Definition.** An interpersonal skill is a repeatable pattern of conduct that helps two people work toward a legitimate shared purpose. It is judged by its effect on the purpose and on the relationship, not by how pleasant it feels in the moment.

The standard list names eight skills. Each is taught in prose below with an example and a limit, because each is easy to misread as a slogan.

### Part A (Core): the eight skills

**Empathy** is the ability to understand another person's perspective and constraints, without giving up law or objectivity. An officer who understands that a clerk is covering two desks can plan the work fairly. Empathy does not mean accepting a wrong. If a clerk has broken a rule, the officer can understand why the rule was hard to keep and still correct the breach. *Limit:* empathy that becomes agreement with a wrong has stopped being empathy and started being surrender.

**Respect** is the preservation of another person's dignity. It means no discrimination and no humiliation, including in private remarks that the other person may later hear. A complaint about a report can be firm and still respect the author. *Limit:* respect does not require agreement. A respectful refusal is still a refusal.

**Assertiveness** is stating facts, needs and limits clearly, without aggression and without passivity. It is taught in full in Part B below, because it is the centre of the style spectrum.

**Emotional regulation** is preventing anger, fear or ego from controlling the response. A regulated person notices the emotion, decides what the purpose requires, and then responds. *Limit:* regulation is not suppression. A person who hides distress so completely that the other side cannot respond has not regulated; they have withheld information.

**Cooperation** is coordinating roles and information toward a legitimate shared objective. A cooperative colleague shares what the others need to finish a task, and does not hoard information to gain an advantage. *Limit:* cooperation toward an unlawful objective is not a virtue; the shared objective must be legitimate.

**Trustworthiness** is keeping commitments, protecting justified confidentiality and correcting one's own errors. A trustworthy officer who learns that an earlier figure was wrong says so, and tells the people affected. *Limit:* confidentiality is not absolute. Where the law or an immediate safety risk requires disclosure, trust is kept by disclosing through the proper route, not by silence.

**Adaptability** is adjusting the language or the channel while keeping the substance. The same decision can be explained in one way to an expert and in another to a citizen with limited literacy, without changing what the decision is. *Limit:* adaptation that changes the facts to make them easier is distortion, not adaptability.

**Persuasion** is using reasons, evidence and credible framing to change a view, rather than coercion or deception. A persuasive officer gives the reason that the other person can check. *Limit:* persuasion is not manipulation. Deception and coercion are warning signs, and a method that works only because the other person does not know the facts is not persuasion.

### Part B (Core): passive, aggressive and assertive styles

The three styles are best learned as patterns with predictable results.

**Passive** conduct avoids stating a legitimate concern. It can preserve short-term calm, but it leaves the problem in place, and the person who held the concern usually grows resentful. A passive officer who accepts a deadline they know is impossible has protected the meeting and failed the work.

**Aggressive** conduct threatens, humiliates or dominates. It may win a point, but it damages dignity, trust and cooperation, so the next exchange becomes harder. An aggressive correction tends to produce defensiveness, and the receiver then stops listening, which is a psychological barrier created by the sender.

**Assertive** conduct states the issue, the evidence, the boundary and the next step respectfully. It protects both the purpose and the relationship. Its structure is simple: say what happened, say what it means for the work, say what you need, and say what happens next.

*Example, with its limit.* A colleague has missed three shared deadlines. The passive response says nothing and absorbs the extra work. The aggressive response says the colleague is lazy and unreliable. The assertive response says: "Three of our shared deadlines in the last month were missed after the second day. I need the figures by Tuesday noon to finish the summary. Can we agree a check on Monday?" The limit: an assertive statement is only as good as its facts. If the figures are wrong, the statement must be corrected, not defended.

### Part C (Advanced): the difficult-conversation structure

*This part is Advanced. It gives a structure for conversations where the other person may be defensive, emotional or unwilling. It is taught in full, and the Core styles above are enough to answer the basic questions.*

A difficult conversation is easier to hold when it follows a set order:

```text
OBSERVATION (specific, verifiable)
     -> IMPACT (why it matters to the work or the people)
     -> PERSPECTIVE (invite the other person to explain)
     -> REQUIREMENT / BOUNDARY (what must change, or what is not acceptable)
     -> AGREED ACTION + REVIEW (what each person will do, and when it is checked)
```

Each step has a function. The observation keeps the conversation on facts. The impact explains why the matter matters, without attacking the person. The perspective step is essential, because it accepts that the other person may hold information you lack. The requirement states the boundary clearly. The action and review make the conversation end with a commitment that can be checked.

Avoid the following, because each makes the conversation harder to repair:

- attributing a motive without evidence, such as "you did that to spite me";
- global labels, such as "you are always careless";
- public humiliation;
- false promises that cannot be kept;
- threats outside lawful authority;
- debate while the other person cannot engage safely.

*Example, with its limit.* A supervisor must raise a repeated lateness problem with a team member who is anxious about home responsibilities. The observation names the dates of the late arrivals. The impact explains the effect on the rota. The perspective step asks what is happening at home. The boundary is the arrival time required. The action is a rota change, reviewed in two weeks. The limit: if the home situation is serious, the supervisor must also use the proper welfare route, and the difficult conversation cannot replace it.

**Failure condition.** The structure fails when the observation contains a motive or a label, or when the conversation jumps from the observation to the requirement without the perspective step. Then the other person has no chance to supply the information the speaker lacks, and the conversation becomes a verdict.

### The assertive statement, step by step

1. **State the observation** in a sentence that could be checked by a third person.
2. **State the impact** on the purpose, the work or the people.
3. **Ask for the other person's account** before deciding the cause.
4. **State the boundary or requirement** plainly.
5. **Agree the next step and a review point.**

**When it fails.** The method fails when the observation contains a motive or a label. "You were late three times" can be checked. "You do not care about the team" cannot. When the observation fails the check, rewrite it before speaking.

### The style that passes for confidence

**Wrong approach:** "Assertive means being firm and loud, so the louder I am, the more assertive I am."

**Reply:** Assertiveness is measured by respect, evidence and clarity. Volume and force belong to the aggressive style. A calm statement of a fact and a boundary is assertive even when spoken quietly, and a loud statement that attacks the person is aggressive even when it is accurate.

**A second wrong approach:** "Empathy means I should let the person off, because they are under pressure." **Reply:** Empathy means understanding the pressure. It does not mean accepting a wrong. The assertive response keeps both: it understands the constraint and still states the breach and the required correction.

### UPSC application

Items on interpersonal skills often describe a reaction and ask which style it shows, or which response is the best. Name the style by its pattern: does the response avoid the concern, attack the person, or state the issue with evidence and a boundary? For best-response items, the answer usually keeps the substance and the respect together, and avoids both silence and humiliation. Watch for options that offer empathy as a reason to accept a breach, and for persuasion options that rely on a fact the other person has not been told.

### Revision points

1. The eight interpersonal skills are empathy, respect, assertiveness, emotional regulation, cooperation, trustworthiness, adaptability and persuasion.
2. Empathy understands another person's constraints without accepting a wrong.
3. Respect preserves dignity; a respectful refusal is still a refusal.
4. Emotional regulation is not suppression of the information the other side needs.
5. Cooperation must be toward a legitimate objective.
6. Trustworthiness keeps commitments and corrects one's own errors. Confidentiality is not absolute.
7. Adaptability changes the language or channel, not the substance.
8. Persuasion gives checkable reasons. Deception and coercion are warning signs.
9. Passive conduct avoids a legitimate concern. Aggressive conduct threatens or humiliates. Assertive conduct states the issue, evidence, boundary and next step.
10. The difficult-conversation structure is: observation, impact, perspective, requirement, agreed action and review.
11. An observation must be checkable. A motive or a label cannot be checked and should be removed.
12. Ask for the other person's account before settling on the cause.

- A. Let the interruptions continue, and avoid objecting so that the working relationship is not strained by a dispute over the meeting.
- B. Tell the colleague that the interruptions show disrespect, and leave them out of future discussions.
- C. Describe the problem to the group after the colleague leaves, and avoid naming anyone.
- D. Say you need to finish the point, state how the interruptions affect the timetable, and ask that speakers be heard in turn.

**MCQ 12 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Letting the interruptions continue is the passive style. It keeps calm and leaves the problem unresolved.
- **B — Incorrect:** Excluding the colleague and calling them disrespectful is aggressive. It attacks the person and closes the chance of a shared fix.
- **C — Incorrect:** Complaining to the group after the colleague leaves avoids stating the concern to the colleague. It is indirect and does not address the interruptions where they happen.
- **D — Correct:** It states the need, names the effect on the work, and proposes a rule the group can check. It is respectful and clear, which is the assertive pattern.

**MCQ 13**

A junior officer gives an opinion that contains a factual error the senior officer knows to be wrong. Which combination of skills best supports a respectful correction?

- A. Respect and assertiveness used together to state the fact with evidence and invite a check.
- B. Empathy used to accept the error for now, since the officer's confidence matters more than accuracy.
- C. Emotional regulation used to delay the correction until a larger mistake can be shown.
- D. Persuasion used to win agreement by presenting the correction as a favour the senior officer is owed.

**MCQ 13 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** Respect protects the officer's dignity, assertiveness states the fact with its evidence, and the invitation to check together keeps the correction joint. The three fit the stem together.
- **B — Incorrect:** Empathy that accepts an error has become surrender. The correction is still needed, and the officer's confidence does not outweigh an accurate figure.
- **C — Incorrect:** Delaying a correction so that a bigger error can be shown is not regulation. It lets the error spread.
- **D — Incorrect:** Persuasion that rests on a personal favour gives the officer no reason to check. Persuasion must give reasons, not leverage.

**MCQ 14**

A colleague shares a sensitive personal matter in confidence. Later a manager asks about it in a meeting. Which response is most consistent with trustworthiness and proportionate handling of confidentiality?

- A. Mention the matter briefly in the meeting, since the manager has authority over the team.
- B. Decline to discuss the personal matter in the meeting and offer only the work-related facts through the proper channel.
- C. Share the full details with the team, since that lets the whole team support the colleague equally.
- D. Answer the manager's question in full, since information requested by an authority must be given.

**MCQ 14 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Authority does not make a confidence disclosable. Mentioning the matter because the manager has power ignores the reason it was shared.


The PAUSE chain is a method for ordinary disputes. The stop rules are a reminder that some disputes cannot be settled by talking. The two are used together: the chain for the ordinary case, the stop rules when the case is not ordinary.

### Plain intuition

Two people want the same room at three o'clock. One says, "I must have the room." The other says, "I need the room too." The positions clash, and each person is ready to argue for the room. Now ask a different question: why does each person need the room at that time? One needs to see a visitor who can only come at three. The other needs a quiet place to read a file that is due tomorrow. These are different needs, and some of them can be met in other ways: a different room, a different time, a shared meeting.

The difference between a **position** and an **interest** is the key to most ordinary disputes. A position is what a person says they want. An interest is why they want it. Positions compete. Interests can often be met together.

### Doctrine: position, interest and the chain

**Definition.** A position is a stated demand. An interest is the underlying need or concern that the demand serves. Negotiation that stays at the level of positions tends to produce a winner and a loser. Negotiation that reaches the level of interests can produce options that meet both.

**The chain.** The PAUSE chain in the visual is a sequence for any ordinary dispute. Each step has a function. Establishing the facts first stops the argument from being about assumptions. Hearing each side and identifying interests moves the talk from demands to needs. Separating the person from the problem keeps the attack on the issue, not on the individual. Identifying the constraints marks what cannot be traded. Generating options finds more than one route to each interest. Agreeing responsibility, timeline and review turns the agreement into work that can be checked. Documentation follows where accountability needs it.

### Part A (Core): position versus interest

The question that separates the two is "why?" Asked gently, it reveals the need beneath the demand. "Why do you need that date?" "What would the meeting let you do?" "What worries you about the other option?" Each answer is an interest. The listener then tests whether another arrangement meets the same interest.

*Example, with its limit.* A dispute over a shared vehicle is stated as "I must have the car on Thursday." The interest is that a patient must be taken to a clinic on Thursday morning. Once that is known, the options widen: the other colleague might bring the patient, or the vehicle might be booked for a different hour. The limit: the question must be asked without suspicion. A person who feels cross-examined will defend the position harder, and then the interest stays hidden.

### Part B (Core): compromise limits and escalation

Compromise is useful, but it is not automatically correct. The standard rule is explicit: unlawful, discriminatory or unsafe conduct cannot be legitimised by splitting the difference. A compromise between a lawful entitlement and an unlawful demand gives the unlawful demand a share it should not have. A compromise that leaves a safety hazard in place leaves the hazard in place.

The same logic governs escalation. The stop rules in the visual apply before the chain is started, not after it has failed. Where safety is at risk, serious illegality is alleged or evidence must be preserved, the matter goes to the authority at once. Where the matter is outside the power of the people in the room, the chain can record the facts and pass them on.

The "talk privately first" habit is useful for an ordinary misunderstanding. It is not a rule that overrides mandatory reporting, urgent protection or a serious conflict of interest. Using it there delays the protection the stop rules require.

*Example, with its limit.* A contractor and a site engineer disagree about whether a finished wall meets a fixed specification. A compromise that accepts a half-met specification would be a bad outcome if the wall is structural. The correct move is to test the wall against the specification, not to split the difference. The limit: where the specification is a matter of judgement rather than law, a compromise may be reasonable, and the chain's options step can find it.

**Fair process before coercive escalation.** The default for an ordinary dispute is a fair process: each side is heard, the facts are checked, and problem-solving comes before any coercive step, such as a sanction, a formal threat or a public demand. This default is why the chain starts with facts and interests rather than with an ultimatum. It gives way when a stop rule applies.

*Example, with its limit.* A supervisor suspects a colleague took leave without approval and considers an immediate disciplinary notice. A fair process first asks the colleague for the leave record, checks the register and hears the explanation. Only if the record is missing and no explanation is given does a formal step become proportionate. The limit: if the absence coincided with a safety-critical duty, the stop rule applies and the supervisor must act at once, while still recording the facts.

**Failure condition.** The default fails when the facts are already established and the conduct is serious. Then waiting for a fair hearing becomes an excuse for inaction, and the matter must move to the proper process without delay.

### Part C (Core): preparation, the lawful alternative and objective criteria

Four preparations are standard for a negotiation: interests, options, objective criteria and a lawful alternative. The **BATNA**, or best alternative to a negotiated agreement, is the course a negotiator takes if no agreement is reached. It must be lawful and available. Knowing it protects the negotiator from accepting a deal worse than the alternative only because they feel pressed.

**Objective criteria** are standards that both sides can accept on their merits: law, evidence, precedent, cost or a published rule. They shift the argument from who wins to what the standard requires. A dispute about a fee is easier to settle by the schedule that applies than by the mood of either party.

*Example, with its limit.* A tenant and a landlord disagree about a repair. The tenant's BATNA is to report the repair to the housing authority, a lawful route with a stated process. That alternative sets a standard: the landlord's offer must be at least as good as the authority's likely outcome. The objective criterion is the repair schedule in the agreed lease. The limit: the BATNA must be real. A threat the negotiator would not carry out is not an alternative, and the other side will notice.

### Part D (Advanced, A4): reservation point, the zone of agreement, and integrative and distributive bargaining

*This part is Advanced. It extends the negotiation vocabulary to the point where a deal is judged and to the kinds of bargaining.*

The **reservation point** is the boundary beyond which an agreement is worse than the alternative. It is the negotiator's minimum. The **zone of possible agreement** is the overlap, if any, between the acceptable ranges of the two sides. If the ranges do not overlap, no agreement is possible without changing one side's limits or its alternatives.

**Integrative** negotiation trades across differing interests to create mutual value. Two sides who want different things from a shared budget can often each get what they need by agreeing on sequence, scope or shared costs. **Distributive** negotiation divides a fixed value. Each gain for one side is a loss for the other, so the risk of positional conflict is higher.

The exam-safe response seeks interests and objective criteria. It never bargains away mandatory law, safety or equal treatment. A negotiation that can be made integrative should be, but not at the price of a legal requirement.

*Example, with its limit.* Two offices share a fixed training budget. A distributive split halves it and each office is unhappy. An integrative approach asks what each office needs the money for. One needs equipment, the other needs trainers. A shared trainer for equipment training meets both interests. The limit: integrative solutions depend on the interests being genuinely compatible. Where the interests are truly opposed over a fixed value, the distributive split remains the honest outcome, and it should be stated as such.

**Failure condition.** The distinctions fail when interests that are not genuinely compatible are treated as if they were, or when a fixed legal requirement is treated as one of the tradeable interests. Then the integrative label is false, and the honest outcome is a distributive split, stated as such.

### Part E (Advanced, A5): mediation and de-escalation

*This part is Advanced. It describes a neutral facilitator's role. It is taught in full because mediation is a common answer in administrative disputes.*

A neutral facilitator:

1. sets respectful ground rules;
2. lets each account be given without interruption;
3. reframes accusations into issues and interests;
4. identifies the common facts and the disputed facts;
5. tests options for fairness and feasibility;
6. records the agreement and a review date.

Mediation is unsuitable where urgent protection is needed, where there is a severe imbalance of power, where coercion is present, or where a mandatory legal process makes voluntary bargaining unsafe or improper. In those cases the stop rules apply, and the matter goes to the proper authority. A mediator who continues to mediate in those cases has confused a useful tool with a required one.

*Example, with its limit.* Two staff members who work well together have a dispute about credit for a joint report. A mediator asks each to describe the work and the concern, identifies the disputed fact (who drafted which section), and proposes a written attribution with a review. The limit: if one staff member is being harassed by the other, mediation is unsuitable, and the complaint must go through the proper process.

**Failure condition.** Mediation fails when one party cannot speak safely, when the facilitator takes sides, or when a stop rule applies and the matter is still sent to mediation. Then the facilitator must stop and refer the matter to the proper authority.

### Running the chain and the stop rules

1. **Check the stop rules** before anything else: safety, serious illegality, evidence and authority.
2. **Establish the facts**, and mark which are assumed.
3. **Hear each side**, and ask why for each position.
4. **Separate the person from the problem** in every statement you make.
5. **Mark the lawful and non-negotiable constraints.**
6. **Generate at least two options** for each interest.
7. **Agree responsibility, timeline and review**, and document it where accountability needs a record.

**Where the chain stops.** The chain fails when an interest cannot be identified, when one side refuses to engage, or when a stop rule applies. In those cases the chain stops and the matter is escalated or recorded, not forced to an agreement.

### The compromise that looks neutral

**Wrong approach:** "Split the difference, and the dispute is over. Compromise always resolves conflict."

**Reply:** Compromise is only correct where the midpoint is lawful, fair and safe. The standard rule is that unlawful, discriminatory or unsafe conduct cannot be legitimised by splitting the difference. Before splitting, check the constraints. A midpoint between a legal requirement and a breach is a breach.

**A second wrong approach:** "Mediation is always the best route, so the complaint should go to a mediator." **Reply:** Mediation is unsuitable where urgent protection, serious power imbalance, coercion or a mandatory legal process applies. The stop rules come first.

**A third wrong approach:** "Negotiation means bargaining away whatever the other side asks, to keep the peace." **Reply:** Negotiation seeks interests and objective criteria. It never gives away a legal requirement, safety or equal treatment.

### UPSC application

Conflict and negotiation items often present a dispute and ask for the best next step, or for the reason a proposed settlement is wrong. The answer usually follows the chain: facts, interests, constraints, options, responsibility and review. Watch for options that split a legal or safety matter, that promise a result before the facts are known, or that use a mediator where a stop rule applies. Items about BATNA or objective criteria reward the recognition of a lawful alternative and a standard both sides can accept.

### Revision points

1. A position is what a person demands. An interest is why they want it.
2. Ask why to move from positions to interests. Then test whether another arrangement meets the same interest.
3. The PAUSE chain runs facts, interests, separation of person and problem, constraints, options, responsibility and review, and documentation where needed.
4. The stop rules are safety, serious illegality, evidence preservation and matters beyond the people in the room. They apply before the chain.
5. Compromise is not automatically correct. Unlawful, discriminatory or unsafe conduct cannot be split.
6. "Talk privately first" is a default for ordinary misunderstanding, not an override of mandatory reporting or urgent protection.
7. The BATNA is the lawful, available alternative to a negotiated agreement.
8. Objective criteria are standards both sides can accept on their merits, such as law, evidence, precedent, cost or a published rule.
9. The reservation point is the boundary below which an agreement is worse than the alternative. The zone of possible agreement is the overlap of acceptable ranges.
10. Integrative bargaining creates value across differing interests. Distributive bargaining divides a fixed value.
11. A neutral facilitator sets ground rules, lets each account be given, reframes into issues, identifies facts, tests options and records the agreement.
12. Mediation is unsuitable where urgent protection, serious power imbalance, coercion or a mandatory legal process applies.

**MCQ 15**

Two staff members both claim the same meeting room at three o'clock. One states a position: 'I must have the room.' Which question asks about the interest behind that position?

- A. Who booked the room first, so that the booking rule can be applied mechanically.
- B. Which of the two has more seniority on the office calendar.
- C. Why the room is needed at that time, so that other feasible ways of meeting that need can be considered.
- D. Whether the other staff member will accept a smaller room without further argument.

**MCQ 15 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Applying the booking rule answers the position and stops there. It does not ask what the room is for.
- **B — Incorrect:** Seniority is a status question. It does not reveal the need, and it turns the dispute into a contest of rank.
- **C — Correct:** It asks why, which surfaces the need. Once the need is known, other feasible ways of meeting it can be weighed.
- **D — Incorrect:** Asking whether the other person will accept a smaller room is a concession question. It moves towards settlement before the interest is known.

**MCQ 16**

A fee for a service is fixed by law at a set amount. One party proposes splitting the difference between the legal amount and the amount demanded. What is the correct reply?

- A. Split the difference, since agreement between the parties settles a dispute.
- B. Accept the midpoint now, since the difference is small and the matter would otherwise stay open.
- C. Treat the midpoint as acceptable once both parties have signed a statement of agreement.
- D. Refuse to split a legally fixed amount, because an agreement between parties cannot alter it.

**MCQ 16 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Agreement between parties does not settle a matter that the law fixes. A midpoint on a legal amount is a breach, not a compromise.
- **B — Incorrect:** Accepting an unlawful midpoint because the difference seems small makes the breach the starting point. The reply must be right at the outset.
- **C — Incorrect:** A signed statement does not make an unlawful amount lawful, because the parties cannot override the fixed amount by agreement.
- **D — Correct:** A fixed legal requirement is a constraint, and the chain treats constraints as not negotiable. Splitting it would give the breach a share it should not have.

**MCQ 17**

A negotiator has a lawful route to a slower forum. Before talks begin, the negotiator asks what that route does for the bargaining position.

- A. It sets a standard that a proposed agreement must meet.
- B. It reveals the other party's interests, so there is little need to ask about them.
- C. It requires the negotiator to accept a proposal the other party makes before the deadline.
- D. It settles the outcome of the talks in advance, since the forum's ruling is known.

**MCQ 17 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The lawful alternative is the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. It gives a standard: a proposal worth accepting must do better than the route the negotiator can take instead.
- **B — Incorrect:** The alternative says nothing about the other party's interests. Those still have to be asked, because they shape which agreement is possible.
- **C — Incorrect:** An alternative gives the negotiator the option to refuse an agreement worse than it. It does not require acceptance of any proposal, so it is not an obligation.
- **D — Incorrect:** The alternative is not a ruling. It sets the standard for judging offers and does not fix the outcome of the talks.

**MCQ 18**

Two departments disagree about dividing a fixed training budget. Which approach is integrative rather than distributive?

- A. Divide the budget in proportion to each department's stated request, without examining what the requests are for.
- B. Identify what each department needs the money for, and find a shared programme that meets both.
- C. Give the larger share to the department with the stronger senior sponsor, to avoid a prolonged stalemate.
- D. Halve each request, and let each department carry its unmet part into the next budget.

**MCQ 18 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Dividing by stated requests is a fixed-value split. It does not examine the interests, so it is distributive.
- **B — Correct:** It identifies the interests and seeks a package that creates value across them. That is integrative bargaining.
- **C — Incorrect:** Allocating by sponsorship divides a fixed value by influence. It ignores the purposes and remains distributive.
- **D — Incorrect:** Halving the requests and carrying the rest forward splits the value again. It creates nothing new for either department.

## Lesson 7 - Public and administrative dealing: accessibility, procedural fairness, documented follow-up, the six situations, and the Advanced stakeholder and crisis parts

### Visual: one test, six situations

```text

                    |
```

The flow is the test that the six situations in this lesson share. Each situation fails at one of its questions, and the right response fixes that question first.

### Plain intuition

Public communication is measured by one thing: whether the person receiving it can act on it. An accurate notice that a citizen cannot read is a failure. A decision that is explained only to the office is a failure. A promise of an update that never comes is a failure of trust, even when the decision itself was right. Public dealing turns the cycle from Lesson 1 outward, to people who did not choose the sender and who may have little time, little literacy or little power.

### Doctrine: clarity, accessibility, accuracy, timeliness, dignity, accountability and feedback

**Definition.** Good public communication combines clarity, accessibility, accuracy, timeliness, dignity, accountability and feedback. Each word names a test. These are treated together, because a message that satisfies one and fails another is still a weak message.

- **Clarity:** the meaning is plain enough for the reader to act on.
- **Accessibility:** the reader can reach the message through their language, format and channel.
- **Accuracy:** the facts stated are true, and uncertain facts are marked as uncertain.
- **Timeliness:** the message arrives in time for the reader to act on it.
- **Dignity:** the reader is treated as a person with a right to be informed and heard.
- **Accountability:** the decision has a stated basis and an identifiable authority.
- **Feedback:** the reader can respond, and the sender can see whether the message worked.

### Part A (Core): accessibility and plain language

Accessibility is often misread as a matter of format. It is more basic. A citizen with limited digital access cannot use a portal, however clear the portal's instructions. A citizen with limited literacy cannot use a dense form, however complete it is. A citizen who speaks a different language cannot use a notice printed only in English. Accessibility asks, for each reader, what route is actually open to them.

Plain language means short sentences, familiar words, the steps in order, and the documents named. It does not mean simplifying the content. A plain-language notice keeps every legal condition and states it in words the reader can use.

*Example, with its limit.* A water-supply change is announced in a dense paragraph in one language, with a telephone number at the end. A plain version gives the date, the three steps the reader must take, the document needed, and a local place where help is available in the local language. The limit: a plain version cannot replace a legal text when the legal text is the binding instrument. Both may be needed, with the plain version as the reader's route into the legal one.

### Part B (Core): procedural fairness

Procedural fairness is the fairness of the process by which a decision is reached, separate from the outcome. The analytical idea is that a person affected by a decision should know the rule, be told the reasons, have a chance to respond where the law allows, and receive the same treatment as others in the same position. A decision that is right on its merits can still be unfair if the affected person was never told what to do.

This is an analytical point drawn from the standard requirement of procedural fairness. It is not a claim about one particular statute. The practical test is simple: could the affected person describe the rule, the reason, the chance to respond and the outcome? If not, the process needs a repair.

*Example, with its limit.* A licence renewal is refused without a stated reason. The refusal may be correct, but the applicant cannot respond because they do not know what was missing. The repair is to state the reason and the missing document, and to allow the applicant to supply it. The limit: where the law forbids disclosure of a reason for security or privacy reasons, the process must still say what the applicant can do next, and the restriction must be the law's, not the office's convenience.

### Part C (Core): the six situations

Six situations recur in public dealing. Each has a response architecture: a sequence that starts from the question the situation fails at.

**1. A citizen does not understand a procedure.** The response is to use plain language, explain the steps and the documents, provide an accessible written reference, and confirm that the citizen understands. The confirmation is the step most often skipped. A citizen who nods may still not know what to bring.

**2. A colleague receives conflicting instructions.** The response is to identify the controlling authority, issue one corrected instruction, and remove the obsolete versions. The fault is a structural one. Blaming the colleague, or accepting both instructions indefinitely, leaves the conflict in place.

**3. Stakeholders oppose a proposal.** The response is to hear the interests, disclose the constraints, explain the evidence and record the unresolved objections. The opposition is information about the proposal, and an objection that is recorded and answered is more useful than one that is ignored.

**4. A rumour or misinformation spreads.** The response is to verify the facts, respond quickly through credible channels, and correct the error without repeating unnecessary detail. Repeating a rumour in order to deny it can spread it further, so the correction states the facts and not the false claim in full.

**5. A sensitive personal matter arises.** The response is to use privacy, disclose the minimum necessary, and use an appropriate secure channel. The question is what the affected person needs to know and what the public needs to know. In most cases the answer is less than the whole.

**6. A service failure occurs.** The response is to acknowledge the failure, explain the known facts without speculation, give a remedy or timeline, and provide an update. An apology without a timeline is not a remedy, and a timeline without an update is a promise that is not kept.

*Example, with its limit.* A citizen's pension payment is delayed, and the office does not know the cause. The response acknowledges the delay, states that the cause is being checked, gives a date for the next update, and provides the route for an urgent case. The limit: the office must not guess the cause. A guessed cause, later shown false, is worse than an honest "not yet known."

### Part D (Core): documented follow-up

A follow-up is documented when the commitment is written, dated and closed. The record needs three entries: what was promised, the date by which it will be done, and the outcome. A follow-up that is promised orally and never recorded is invisible when the citizen returns. Documentation is not a substitute for communication, though. A file note that the citizen never received does not count as having told them.

*Example, with its limit.* A complaint about a delayed certificate is acknowledged, and the officer promises a reply in seven days. The documented follow-up records the complaint, the promise and the date. On the date, the officer gives an update, even if the certificate is not yet ready, and records the final outcome when it comes. The limit: an update that says only "still in progress" is weak. It should say what remains and when the next update will come.

### Part E (Advanced, A2): the stakeholder communication matrix

*This part is Advanced. It helps plan communication when several groups are affected differently. It is taught in full.*

A stakeholder matrix asks six questions of each group:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Power | Can the stakeholder authorise, block or materially alter the action? |
| Interest | How directly are they affected? |
| Vulnerability | Do they face language, disability, income, gender, geographic or digital barriers? |
| Information need | Do they need decision detail, an operational instruction, a warning, a chance to be consulted, or reassurance? |
| Channel | What is accessible, credible, timely and recordable for them? |
| Feedback | How can their objections, misunderstanding or implementation failure return to the decision-maker? |

A high-power stakeholder does not erase the need to hear a highly affected group with little power. The matrix exists to prevent exactly that error: the decision-maker hears the people with authority and misses the people the decision actually touches.

*Example, with its limit.* A new bus timetable affects office workers, students and elderly residents with no smartphone. The office workers have power through their employer; the students have interest; the elderly residents have high vulnerability and no digital channel. The communication must reach the elderly residents through a printed timetable at the stop and a helpline, even though they have the least power. The limit: the matrix does not decide whose view prevails. It decides who must be informed and how.

**Failure condition.** The matrix fails when a group is left out because it has little power, or when its information need is assumed rather than asked. Then the communication reaches the decision-maker and misses the people the decision affects most.

### Part F (Advanced, A6): risk, crisis and misinformation communication

*This part is Advanced. It covers communication during a fast-moving risk, where a delay or an error has larger costs. It is taught in full.*

Risk communication has its own discipline:

- communicate early enough to support protective action;
- distinguish confirmed fact, current assessment and uncertainty;
- give specific action instructions;
- use one credible source of updates;
- correct visibly and promptly;
- avoid both false reassurance and unsupported alarm;
- adapt the format for language, disability and connectivity constraints.

The three categories in the second item are the core of honest crisis communication. A confirmed fact is what has been checked. A current assessment is a judgement that may change. An uncertainty is what is not yet known. Mixing the three produces either false reassurance, which says all is well when it is not known, or unsupported alarm, which says the worst when it is not confirmed.

Trust is preserved more by transparent correction than by pretending uncertainty never existed. A message that said "the water is safe" and was later corrected, with the correction made visible and quick, damages trust less than a message that was never corrected.

*Example, with its limit.* A rumour spreads that a bridge is unsafe. The office checks the inspection record, finds the bridge is safe for light vehicles, and restricts heavy vehicles pending a further check. The message states the confirmed fact (light vehicles may cross), the restriction and its reason (heavy vehicles are restricted pending a check), the uncertainty (the check result is due on a date), and one channel for updates. The limit: if the inspection record is itself out of date, the message must say so, and the restriction must follow the record's age, not the rumour.

**Failure condition.** The approach fails when a message states an uncertain fact as confirmed, or when it waits for certainty before warning. Then the people at risk act on a rumour, or lose the time the warning was meant to protect.

### Part G (Core): consensus is not proof

A group that agrees has produced agreement, not evidence. Consensus is not proof of correctness or legality. A room of officials who share an error will agree on it, and a plan that every stakeholder accepts can still breach a legal requirement, because the requirement does not depend on who agrees with it.

*Example, with its limit.* A department consults three senior officers, who all endorse a plan to waive a documentation step for applicants. The endorsement is unanimous, but the step is required by a rule that none of them has checked. The test is the rule, the facts and the affected applicants, not the agreement. The limit: agreement among the affected people is still valuable evidence of acceptability, and it should be sought. It just cannot replace the check against the rule.

**Failure condition.** The reasoning fails when no one checks the rule or the facts before the consensus is recorded. A recorded consensus that was never tested against evidence is a risk, not a finding.

### Four checks for any public message

1. **Check comprehension:** can the affected person understand the message in their language and format?
2. **Check reach:** can they take the next step through an open route, with documents named?
3. **Check fairness:** can they state the rule, the reason, the chance to respond and the outcome?
4. **Check follow-up:** is there a dated commitment and a recorded outcome?
5. **For a crisis:** separate the confirmed fact, the assessment and the uncertainty, and give one update source.

**When the method runs out.** The method fails when a fact is unknown and cannot be checked before the message is due. Then the message must say that the fact is not yet known, and give the date by which it will be. A message that guesses fails the test of accuracy, however well it reads.

### The silence that feels safe

**Wrong approach:** "The notice is correct and complete in its legal text, so the citizen's failure to act is the citizen's responsibility."

**Reply:** Public communication is judged by whether the reader can act on the message. A correct notice that is not accessible has failed the test, even if the legal text is accurate. The response is to add the accessible route and confirm understanding, not to blame the reader.

**A second wrong approach:** "To avoid panic, say nothing until everything is certain." **Reply:** Silence is a message too. In a risk, the honest course is to say what is confirmed, what is uncertain and when the next update comes. Withholding a safety warning until certainty arrives can cost the protection the warning was for. The reply is early, honest communication with its uncertainty stated.

### UPSC application

Public-dealing items present a situation and ask for the best response. Find the failed question first: is the reader unable to understand, unable to reach the next step, unable to see the fairness of the process, or left without a follow-up? The best answer repairs that question directly. Watch for options that blame the citizen, that accept conflicting instructions indefinitely, that guess a cause, or that stay silent in a risk. Stakeholder items reward the matrix: the affected but less powerful group must be reached.

### Revision points

1. Public communication is judged by whether the affected person can act on it.
2. Good public communication is clear, accessible, accurate, timely, dignified, accountable and open to feedback.
3. Accessibility asks which route is actually open to each reader.
4. Plain language keeps every legal condition in words the reader can use.
5. Procedural fairness: the affected person can state the rule, the reason, the chance to respond and the outcome.
6. The six situations are: procedure not understood; conflicting instructions; stakeholder opposition; rumour; sensitive personal matter; service failure.
7. Each situation has a response architecture. Repair the failed question first.
8. A documented follow-up records the promise, the date and the outcome.
9. A stakeholder matrix checks power, interest, vulnerability, information need, channel and feedback.
10. High power does not erase the need to hear the highly affected.
11. In a risk, separate confirmed fact, current assessment and uncertainty.
12. Transparent correction preserves trust better than a hidden error.
13. When a fact is unknown, say so and give the date of the next update.

## Lesson 8 - Questionnaires and consultation: seven defects, the objective-to-data rule, interview versus questionnaire and the Advanced bias parts

### Visual: a question as an instrument, and the path from decision to data

```text

        |
        v
WHICH FACT WOULD CHANGE THAT DECISION?   ---- no fact changes it ----> do not ask
        |
        v

        |

   leading | loaded | double-barrelled | ambiguous | intrusive | unbalanced choices
   | social-desirability pressure
```

The chain starts at the decision, not at the questions. A survey that begins with questions has no way to tell which questions are needed, so it collects everything and calls the surplus insight.

### Plain intuition

Imagine asking a village whether it wants a new road. If the question is "Do you agree that the road will bring prosperity?", the answer tells you what the question assumed, not what the village thinks. If the question is "Would you use the road to reach the market?", the answer can change a decision. The second question is about a behaviour that would make the road worth building. The first is a leading assertion dressed as a question.

A questionnaire is a measuring instrument. A bent ruler gives confident wrong lengths. A badly worded question gives confident wrong answers, and the confidence is the danger, because the answers look like data.

### Doctrine: defects, the objective-to-data rule, and the choice of method

**Definition.** A survey question is defective when its wording, scope or choice set makes the answer depend on the question rather than on the respondent's view or situation.

**The rule.** The objective-to-data rule says: start with the decision the survey must support, then keep only the data that would change that decision. A datum that is merely interesting, or intrusive, is not a reason to ask.

**The method choice.** An interview and a questionnaire are two instruments with different strengths. The interview allows probing and clarification and carries interviewer-bias risk. The questionnaire allows standardisation and reach and may lack clarification.

### Part A (Core): the seven defects

Seven defects are standard. Each is taught with its reason and its remedy.

**Leading.** The question pushes the respondent towards one answer. "Do you agree that the new scheme is fair?" presents a conclusion as the question. *Remedy:* neutral wording, such as "How fair do you think the scheme is?" with a scale.

**Loaded.** The question contains an accusation or a disputed assumption. "Have you stopped paying the fee?" assumes the respondent paid it at some time, and may accuse them. *Remedy:* separate the premise and ask about the fact directly: "Have you paid the fee in the last year?"

**Double-barrelled.** The question asks two things in one item. "Are the timings and the fares of the bus satisfactory?" can receive one answer for two different judgements. *Remedy:* one issue per question.

**Ambiguous.** A term or a time period is unclear. "Do you often use the bus?" leaves "often" undefined. *Remedy:* define the scope and the period: "How many times in the last week did you use the bus?"

**Intrusive.** The question seeks sensitive information the purpose does not need. *Remedy:* collect only what the purpose requires, and explain why it is needed where the law requires it.

**Unbalanced choices.** The options omit plausible responses. A question with "yes" and "no" but no "not applicable" forces an answer the respondent does not hold. *Remedy:* exhaustive, mutually intelligible options, including "other" or "not applicable" where needed.

**Social-desirability pressure.** The wording encourages the respectable answer rather than the truthful one. "Do you always keep your environment clean?" invites the answer "yes." *Remedy:* privacy, neutral framing and questions about specific, recent behaviour.

*Example, with its limit.* A survey asks, "Would you welcome the new canteen, which will serve healthy food at reasonable prices?" The question is leading (it presents the canteen as welcome and healthy), and it is loaded (the prices are assumed reasonable). A neutral rewrite asks whether the respondent would use a canteen at lunch on weekdays, then asks what price they would expect to pay. The limit: some surveys cannot avoid a stated proposal. The remedy is then to state the proposal neutrally, without adjectives that carry the answer.

### Part B (Core): the objective-to-data rule

The rule has three steps. First, write the decision the survey must support, in one sentence. Second, list the facts that would change that decision if they were known. Third, keep only the questions that collect those facts, in neutral, single, clearly scoped form.

The rule protects against two errors. The first is the survey that collects everything and then looks for a story. The second is the survey that asks a question because it is interesting, even though no answer would alter the decision. Both waste the respondent's time and create data that is intrusive or noise.

*Example, with its limit.* A municipality wants to decide whether to open a crèche at a market. The decision is one sentence. The facts that would change it are the number of children under six in households that use the market, and whether the carers can reach the market on foot. A question about the respondent's favourite food does not change the decision, and a question about exact monthly income is intrusive for this purpose. The limit: if the municipality also has to decide the crèche's fees, household income becomes relevant, and it may then be collected in bands with a stated privacy reason.

**Failure condition.** The rule fails when the objective is vague. "Improve the market" supports no single decision, so every question seems relevant. The first repair is to write the decision more narrowly, before any question is drafted.

### Part C (Core): interview versus questionnaire

An interview gives the interviewer the chance to ask a follow-up, to ask for an example, and to clarify an answer that is unclear. That is its main strength. It carries the risk of interviewer bias: the interviewer's tone, expectations and reactions can change the answers, and two interviewers may record the same answer differently.

A questionnaire gives every respondent the same words in the same order, which standardises the data. It reaches many respondents at low cost. Its weakness is that it cannot clarify. A respondent who misreads a question has no one to ask, and the misreading becomes data.

The choice is a trade-off. Where the purpose needs depth and clarification on a small number of cases, an interview is better. Where the purpose needs comparable answers from a large number of people, a questionnaire is better. Where both are needed, a short questionnaire can be followed by interviews with a sample of respondents.

*Example, with its limit.* A health department wants to understand why a clinic's attendance has fallen. A questionnaire can count the reasons respondents give, but a respondent who says "the timings" may mean the opening hour, the closing hour or the wait. An interview with ten respondents can ask for the example that resolves the ambiguity, and then a questionnaire can count the reasons with that clarity. The limit: ten interviews cannot establish how common each reason is, so they cannot replace the questionnaire.

### Part D (Advanced, A7): behavioural and survey biases

*This part is Advanced. It names the biases that affect both the people being surveyed and the people running the survey. It is taught in full because it explains why a clean questionnaire can still mislead.*

| Bias or problem | Consequence | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation bias | The listener or designer selects evidence that supports the current view | Ask what would disprove the view |
| Authority gradient | Juniors hide risk information from seniors | Explicitly invite dissent, and protect those who report |
| Groupthink | Premature consensus that no one tested | Gather independent views before discussion |
| Framing effect | Different wording of the same fact changes the response | Use neutral, parallel wording and test both |
| Acquiescence bias | Respondents agree by default | Balanced choices and reversed-wording checks |
| Social desirability | Respondents conceal views they think are stigmatised | Privacy, indirect or neutral questions |
| Non-response bias | Those who respond differ from those who do not | Follow-up and a check of representativeness |

These biases do not mean the survey is worthless. They mean that the reading of the results must allow for them. A result that depends on the wording, on who answered, or on the presence of a senior listener has to be read with that dependence in view.

*Example, with its limit.* A consultation on a new fee is held in a meeting chaired by the senior officer who proposed it. The junior staff who object stay silent. The authority gradient has produced a result that looks like consent. The countermeasure is to collect written views in private before the meeting, so that the objections exist on the record. The limit: a private written channel can be ignored by the same authority. The countermeasure must include a recorded response to each objection.

**Failure condition.** The countermeasures fail when they are announced but not applied, for example when a private written channel exists but its contents are ignored. Then the bias remains, and a recorded reply to each objection is the only visible test that the channel had effect.

### Building a checked questionnaire

1. **Write the decision** the survey must support, in one sentence.
2. **List the facts** that would change the decision.
3. **Draft one question per fact**, neutral, single and scoped.
4. **Check each question** for the seven defects.
5. **Check the choice set** for balance, and check the wording for social-desirability pressure.
6. **Choose the instrument:** interview for depth, questionnaire for comparable answers at scale, or both.
7. **Plan the bias check** for the people running the survey and for the respondents.

**Limit of the method.** The method fails when the decision cannot be written in one sentence. Then the survey has no purpose it can serve. The first step is to return to the decision, not to add questions.

### The data that seems useful

**Wrong approach:** "Ask everything you can, and filter the useful answers later. More data is always better."

**Reply:** More data is better only when the data changes the decision. Extra questions add intrusion, fatigue and noise, and a respondent who answers many irrelevant questions gives less care to the ones that matter. The objective-to-data rule keeps the survey proportionate and the answers usable.

**A second wrong approach:** "A question that states the benefit clearly helps people understand the proposal." **Reply:** Stating the benefit inside the question leads the respondent. Describe the proposal neutrally, then ask about the respondent's view or behaviour.

### UPSC application

Questionnaire items usually describe a survey or a consultation and ask which question, head or design is defective, or which item should be kept. Start with the decision. Keep the head that changes the decision, reject the head that does not, and reject any head that is intrusive. Then check the defects. In interview-versus-questionnaire items, identify the need: depth and clarification favour the interview; standard comparison at scale favours the questionnaire. Advanced bias items reward the countermeasure that makes the bias visible, such as private written views or a neutral wording check.

### Revision points

1. A survey question is defective when its wording, scope or choices make the answer depend on the question.
2. The seven defects are leading, loaded, double-barrelled, ambiguous, intrusive, unbalanced choices and social-desirability pressure.
3. Each defect has a remedy: neutral wording, separated premises, one issue, defined scope, minimum collection, balanced choices, and privacy with neutral framing.
4. The objective-to-data rule: write the decision, keep only the data that would change it.
5. A datum that is merely interesting is not a reason to ask.
6. An interview allows probing and clarification, and carries interviewer-bias risk.
7. A questionnaire standardises and reaches many, and cannot clarify.
8. Use an interview for depth, a questionnaire for comparable answers at scale, or both.
9. Behavioural biases include confirmation, authority gradient, groupthink and framing.
10. Survey biases include acquiescence, social desirability and non-response.
11. Countermeasures make the bias visible: disconfirming questions, invited dissent, independent written views and checks of who responded.
12. A result that depends on wording, respondents or a senior listener must be read with that dependence in view.

- **B — Correct:** The number of weekday trips to the employment centre is the fact that shows whether a route would be used. It changes the decision.
- **C — Incorrect:** Car make and model is intrusive for a route decision and has no bearing on it. A fuel-subsidy file is a different purpose.
- **D — Incorrect:** Religious affiliation is sensitive and does not bear on the route decision. A general picture is not the purpose the survey serves.

**MCQ 23**

Which strength belongs to an interview rather than to a questionnaire?

- A. Identical wording for each respondent, which keeps the answers comparable.
- B. Reach to a very large number of respondents at low cost per response.
- C. The ability to probe an unclear answer and ask for a concrete example at once.
- D. Freedom from the risk that the conductor's tone changes the answers.

**MCQ 23 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Identical wording is a questionnaire strength. An interview lets the wording vary with the conversation, which is why it can probe.
- **B — Incorrect:** Low-cost reach is a questionnaire strength. Interviews are slow to conduct across many respondents.
- **C — Correct:** Probing and asking for an example are the interview's central strengths, because the interviewer can clarify an unclear answer as it arises.

The gates run in order, and an option that fails an early gate is rejected without reaching the later ones. The matching routine is short on purpose: it is a discipline for the items where the time goes.

### Plain intuition

A scenario item describes a situation and offers four responses. Each response sounds reasonable in isolation. The skill is to test them in a fixed order, so that a tempting response does not win only because it sounds kind, quick or firm. The engine turns a vague sense of "the right thing" into a sequence of questions that any reader can apply and check.

The final sentence of the visual is the principle. The best response is not the softest. It is the one that is lawful, fair, clear, proportionate and effective for the facts given. A soft response that does not act on the facts fails the engine. A firm response that breaks the law fails it too.

### Doctrine: the seven gates

**Definition.** The seven-gate test is a sequence of seven questions applied to each option, in order. The first gate that an option fails rejects it. The option that passes all seven, or that passes more of them with fewer failures, is the best response.

Each gate has a failure condition. Learn the failure, not only the question.

- **Gate 1, facts.** Failure: the option assumes a fact that the stem does not give, or accepts an allegation as proven. A response that acts on an unverified claim fails here.
- **Gate 2, legality and ethics.** Failure: the option uses deception, discrimination, retaliation or an avoidable harm. On this file's reading, the gate is a precondition rather than a trade-off (an analytical inference from the order of the gates). An option that fails it cannot be rescued by its other merits.
- **Gate 3, proportionality.** Failure: the option is more coercive, more public or more drastic than the situation requires. A response that escalates a minor matter to discipline fails here.
- **Gate 4, communication quality.** Failure: the option does not clarify, listen or confirm. A response that announces a decision without checking understanding fails here.
- **Gate 5, feasibility.** Failure: the actor cannot do what the option requires, within their role or time. An option that requires a power the actor lacks fails here.
- **Gate 6, accountability.** Failure: the option acts without a record or an escalation where one is needed.
- **Gate 7, follow-up.** Failure: the option removes the visible symptom but does not prevent recurrence.

**The principle.** The best option is the most lawful, fair, clear, proportionate and effective response to the facts given. The principle does not reward the softest response or the strongest one. It rewards the response that the facts support.

*Example, with its limit.* A supervisor learns that a team member has been late repeatedly. One option is to dismiss the person at once. It fails Gate 3 (disproportionate), and it may fail Gate 1 if the facts are not yet established. Another is to ignore the lateness. It fails Gate 7 (no recurrence prevention) and perhaps Gate 4 (no clarification). A third is to ask for an explanation, record the dates, agree a change and review it. It passes most gates. The limit: if the lateness was caused by an unrecorded personal emergency, the gates ask the supervisor to check that fact before acting, which may change the best option.

### Part A (Core): the timed matching method

Matching items pair two lists. They are efficient when handled in order, and costly when the candidate debates each pair.

The method has two passes. In Pass 1, spend about thirty seconds writing a one-word purpose beside each member of the first list. The purpose is not an essay. It is the reason the member exists in the situation: instruct, consult, warn, record, or the like. Then spend about fifteen seconds eliminating rows in the second list that contain one impossible pairing. If one row remains, it is the answer. If two rows remain, park the item and move on, because the remaining time is better spent on items that can be finished.

In Pass 2, return to the parked items with the time saved. Decide each one by the same purpose test, and do not reopen the items that were finished.

**Failure condition.** The method fails when the purpose words are wrong, or when the candidate writes a purpose for a member that was not in the list. The check is to re-read the two lists once after writing the purposes, and to confirm that each purpose matches the member's wording rather than a remembered pattern.

### Part B (Core): using the gates on a matching or scenario item

The gates apply to scenario options. A matching item can be solved by the purpose test in Part A. A scenario option is tested by the seven gates in order. In practice the first two gates eliminate most distractors, because they remove the options that assume unverified facts or that use improper means. The remaining options are then compared on proportionality, communication, feasibility, accountability and follow-up.

A useful habit is to write the failing gate beside each rejected option. The note makes the elimination visible, and it shows which gate the candidate tends to skip under time pressure.

### Part C (Advanced, A8): option elimination

*This part is Advanced. It names eight defects that make a scenario option wrong even when it sounds sensible. Each defect is taught with its reason, an example, and the point at which the reject stops being valid, so that it is applied as a test and not as a slogan.*

**1. Secrecy presented as confidentiality.** The option keeps information from everyone, including the body that must act on it. The reason to reject it is that justified confidentiality protects against unnecessary disclosure; it does not hide a matter from a lawful authority. *Example:* a response that withholds a safety complaint from the inspecting authority is rejected for this reason. *Where the reject stops:* confidentiality shared with the authority itself is fine, so the reject applies only when the authority is kept out.

**2. Certainty promised before the facts are known.** The option asserts an outcome that the facts cannot yet support. It fails the facts gate, because the outcome is a prediction. *Example:* a promise that a complaint "will be resolved by Monday" made before any inquiry has started. *Where the reject stops:* a target date for an update is different from a promised outcome, and once the facts are established a firm outcome may be the right statement.

**3. Consensus sought before the affected parties are heard.** A vote or a meeting of other officials is not a hearing of the people the decision touches. *Example:* an officer gathers managers to agree a response to a complaint before the complainant has been contacted. *Where the reject stops:* consulting colleagues for expertise is proper, and once the affected parties have been heard, the consensus that follows is evidence.

**4. Punishing the messenger rather than testing the information.** Penalising the person who raises an issue removes the channel for information and judges the person rather than the report. *Example:* a junior who reports a safety defect is moved to a worse post. *Where the reject stops:* a messenger who acted unlawfully may face process, but that process must be separate from the merit of the report and must be shown to be so.

**5. Public disclosure where a narrower channel suffices.** A wider audience adds harm, such as reputational damage or loss of privacy, without serving the purpose. *Example:* publishing a disciplinary finding to all staff when only the affected officer and the authority need it. *Where the reject stops:* where the public must be warned for safety, wide disclosure is the purpose, and a narrow channel that would not reach the people at risk is not sufficient.

**6. Delaying urgent protection in the name of consultation.** Protection cannot wait for a consultation whose outcome does not change the need for it. *Example:* deferring a water advisory until a public meeting has been held. *Where the reject stops:* consultation may run alongside protection, and a protective step that is itself disproportionate must still be tested against the gates.

**7. Documentation treated as a substitute for communication.** A file note shows that an event was recorded; it does not show that the affected person understood. *Example:* a letter placed on file and never sent, but presented as notification. *Where the reject stops:* a person who has in fact received and understood the message needs no further communication, and a record remains necessary for accountability.

**8. A technically correct but inaccessible remedy.** A remedy the affected person cannot use does not meet the accessibility test, however correct its content. *Example:* a grievance route available only online to a citizen who has no internet access. *Where the reject stops:* an online route is acceptable where an assisted or paper route also exists and is offered.

When two options both seem reasonable, prefer the one that combines fact verification, an appropriate channel, proportional action, feedback and follow-up. The rejects above show which part is missing from the weaker option.

*Example, with its limit.* A scenario offers two responses. One publishes a complaint to the whole staff so that everyone knows the facts. The other informs the investigating authority privately and asks the complainant to continue through the proper process. The first is public disclosure where a narrower channel suffices, reject 5. The second uses a narrower channel and keeps the process proper. The limit: if the complaint involves immediate danger, the authority must act at once, and the narrow channel must be used without delay. The reject is against unnecessary disclosure, not against all disclosure.

### Part D (Advanced, A9): the safety-dispute scenario

*This part is Advanced. It is a transfer exercise that combines the gates, the stop rules and the advanced elimination rules in one situation. It is not required for the Core answer, but it is taught in full.*

A contractor alleges that a junior engineer delayed a safety clearance for personal reasons. The engineer says the inspection record is incomplete. A manager can order an immediate public apology, privately broker a compromise that lets the work resume, or pause the affected work, secure the records, hear both accounts separately and use the authorised safety process.

The best response is to pause the affected activity if the risk is material, preserve the evidence, separate verified facts from allegations, use the authorised process, communicate the interim status to those who need it, and review after a recorded decision.

The reasoning runs through the gates. Gate 1 requires the manager to separate the facts from the allegation: neither the contractor's claim of personal motive nor the engineer's claim of an incomplete record is yet proven. Gate 2 forbids a response that attributes motive without proof. Gate 3 favours the pause over a public apology, because the pause is proportionate to the risk. Gate 6 requires the authorised process and a record. Gate 7 requires the review that prevents recurrence.

The public apology fails Gate 1, because it attributes motive without proof. The private compromise fails Gate 2, because it cannot bargain away mandatory safety. The pause passes the gates because it protects people first and still allows a fair hearing and feedback.

*Example, with its limit.* The pause costs time and may anger the contractor. The gates do not say the cost is zero. They say the protection of people must come before the convenience of the schedule, and the fair hearing must follow. The limit: if the risk is not material, the pause may be disproportionate, and the gate test then points to a narrower step, such as a re-inspection, instead.

**Failure condition.** The method fails when the pause is applied to a risk that is not material, or when the fair hearing is skipped to save time. In the first case a narrower step, such as a re-inspection, is the proportionate answer; in the second the decision loses its basis.

### The softest or firmest option

**Wrong approach:** "The softest option is best, because it keeps everyone calm and avoids conflict."

**Reply:** The best option is not always the softest. A soft option that fails Gate 1 acts on an unverified claim, and one that fails Gate 7 hides the symptom. The engine rewards the response the facts support, and it does not reward calm for its own sake.

**A second wrong approach:** "The firmest option is best, because it shows authority and ends the matter quickly." **Reply:** A firm option that is disproportionate fails Gate 3, and a firm option that is unlawful fails Gate 2. Firmness is a quality of a response, not a test of it.

**A third wrong approach:** "In a timed set, I should finish each item in order and check every option carefully before moving on." **Reply:** That spends the minutes that the harvest items need. The two-pass routine banks the certain marks first and returns to the close items with the time saved.

### UPSC application

Scenario items describe a situation and ask for the best response. Run the gates in order and write the failing gate beside each rejected option. Items that use a matching list reward the two-pass routine: purposes first, eliminations second, a parked item when two rows remain. Watch for distractors that use the Advanced defects in Part C, such as confusing secrecy with confidentiality or using public disclosure where a narrower channel suffices. Where two options both pass the early gates, the better one usually combines verification, channel, proportion, feedback and follow-up.

### Revision points

1. The seven gates run in order: facts, legality and ethics, proportionality, communication quality, feasibility, accountability and follow-up.
2. An option that fails an early gate is rejected at once.
3. Gate 2 is treated as a precondition (analytical inference). An option that uses deception, discrimination, retaliation or avoidable harm cannot be rescued by its other merits.
4. The best option is the most lawful, fair, clear, proportionate and effective response to the facts given.
5. The best option is not automatically the softest or the firmest.
6. In matching, write a one-word purpose beside each list member in about thirty seconds.
7. Eliminate rows with one impossible pairing in about fifteen seconds.
8. When two rows remain, park the item and return to it on the second pass.
9. Write the failing gate beside each rejected option, to see which gate you tend to skip.
10. Advanced elimination: reject options that confuse secrecy with confidentiality, promise certainty too early, or use public disclosure where a narrower channel suffices.
11. Documentation is not a substitute for communication.
12. In a safety dispute, the pause protects people first, and the authorised process and the record allow a fair hearing.

**MCQ 24**

A proposal would let an officer skip the normal procedure, and its supporter says the shortcut is 'faster for everyone'. Which gate should be tested first?

- A. Feasibility, since the shortcut must be possible within the officer's role.
- B. Follow-up, since the shortcut must prevent recurrence of the same problem.

**MCQ 24 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **B — Incorrect:** Follow-up is the last gate. The recurrence question cannot be answered before the facts are known.
- **C — Incorrect:** Proportionality is the third gate. It assumes a coercive effect that the stem does not establish, and it comes after the facts.
- **D — Correct:** The first gate is facts, and the claim is an assumption to verify. The gates run in order, so the fact test comes first.

**MCQ 25**

In a timed matching set, a candidate has written a one-word purpose beside each list member and eliminated one row. Two rows remain. What does the two-pass method direct next?

- A. Park the item and return on the second pass.
- B. Choose one remaining row at random, to avoid spending more time on this item.
- C. Re-read the passage and rewrite the purposes before choosing.
- D. Mark the longer of the two remaining rows, since longer answers are usually more complete.

**MCQ 25 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** Two rows left is the point at which the method parks the item. The candidate moves on, banks the certain marks, and returns with the saved time.
- **B — Incorrect:** A random choice between two rows is a guess with no elimination behind it. The method requires a justified elimination before any guess.
- **C — Incorrect:** Rewriting every purpose spends the time the method is meant to save. The purposes are already written.
- **D — Incorrect:** Length is not a test of correctness. Choosing by length is a heuristic the method rejects, because the purpose test is the criterion.

**MCQ 26**

A response offers to keep a complaint secret from everyone, including the authority that must investigate it. What is the main defect of the response?

- A. It protects the complainant's privacy, which makes it proportionate to the complaint.
- B. It confuses secrecy with justified confidentiality, which yields where the law requires disclosure.
- C. It publishes the complaint widely, so that the complainant's side is known.
- D. Disclosing the complaint's existence to all staff is also a defect, because the audience is wider than the investigation needs.

**MCQ 26 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Protecting privacy is a real value, but the response goes further by excluding the authority that must investigate. Calling it proportionate misreads the defect.
- **B — Correct:** Justified confidentiality protects against unnecessary disclosure. It does not hide a complaint from a lawful investigator, because confidentiality is not absolute where the law requires disclosure.
- **C — Incorrect:** Wide publication is the opposite of secrecy. It is a defect of its own, but the response in the stem hides the complaint rather than publishing it.
- **D — Incorrect:** Disclosing the complaint's existence to all staff is also a defect, a wider audience than needed. The stem describes exclusion of the investigator, which this option does not.

**MCQ 27**

A teacher is accused of shouting at a student; the teacher says the student was disrupting the class. The parent demands immediate suspension. Which first step best fits the seven-gate method?

- A. Suspend the teacher at once, and tell the parent the matter is in hand.
- B. Ask the parent to set the sanction, and confirm that the school will follow it.
- C. Hear each account separately, and defer a sanction until the facts are checked.
- D. Hold a meeting of the teacher and the parent, and agree a sanction on the day.

**MCQ 27 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Suspension before the facts are checked acts on an unverified allegation. It fails the facts gate and the proportionality gate.
- **B — Incorrect:** Letting the parent set the sanction gives the party making the allegation the decision on it. It fails the accountability gate, because the school's process must decide.
- **C — Correct:** It tests the facts first by hearing each account separately, and it defers any sanction until the facts are checked. It passes the facts, communication and proportionality gates.
- **D — Incorrect:** Agreeing a sanction in a joint meeting before the facts are known fixes an outcome without a fair hearing. It fails the facts gate and the communication gate.

**MCQ 28**

A manager must issue an urgent safety instruction to a site team of thirty, some of whom work nights. Which plan meets the purpose, the urgency and the need to confirm understanding?

- A. A notice pinned on the board, a copy in the canteen, and a note in the book.
- B. A group-chat post in the morning, a request for a thumbs-up from each member, and a reminder at lunch.
- C. A telephone call to the most senior team member, a written summary, and a relay by that member.
- D. A direct briefing per shift, a written summary on file, and a question each shift answers.

**MCQ 28 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Pinned notices may never reach the night staff, and nothing in the plan checks understanding. The purpose needs both reach and confirmation.
- **B — Incorrect:** A thumbs-up shows that a message was seen, not that it was understood. A morning post also misses the night shift.
- **C — Incorrect:** Relying on one senior member adds a layer of paraphrase and leaves no record. The relay fails the audience and feedback needs.
- **D — Correct:** Each shift receives a direct briefing, a written summary provides the record, and a question each shift answers tests decoding. The plan covers urgency, reach, record and confirmation together.

**MCQ 29**

A supervisor's note to a team says only that its reports need better communication. Two weeks later the team says it did not know which report was meant, and the same error recurs. Which response combines a feedback property with a barrier diagnosis?

- A. Name the report and the error, treat the unclear reference as a semantic barrier, and set a check before sending.
- B. Tell the team to communicate better, and ask it to take more care in future.
- C. Repeat the same note more loudly at the next meeting and at the following one, so that the team takes it seriously.
- D. Blame the team for not asking questions, since the note was clear enough for the team to act on without help.

**MCQ 29 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** It names the report and the error, which is specific feedback, and it identifies the unclear reference as the barrier. The check it sets gives the remedy a step the team can take.
- **B — Incorrect:** The statement is general and names no report or behaviour. It fails specificity and diagnoses no barrier, so it cannot change the recurrence.
- **C — Incorrect:** A louder repetition addresses physical interference, which the stem does not show. The barrier is the unclear reference, not the volume.
- **D — Incorrect:** Blaming the team does not fix an ambiguous instruction, and the stem shows the note did not name the report. It treats a barrier as a discipline problem.

**MCQ 30**

A candidate writes a purpose for a list member that was already correctly placed, and then runs out of time on the second pass. The candidate can state the purpose test correctly when asked afterwards, made no guess, misread nothing in the stem and used no outside knowledge. Which two error codes describe the two errors?

- A. C (concept gap) and G (guess error).
- B. A (application slip) and T (time error).
- C. R (reading error) and X (calculation error).
- D. P (passage-only violation) and T (time error).

**MCQ 30 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** The stem says the purpose test was known and no guess was made, so neither a concept gap nor a guess describes the errors.
- **B — Correct:** Writing a purpose for a placed member is the right idea applied at the wrong step, which is an application slip. Running out of time on the second pass is a time error.
- **C — Incorrect:** The stem says nothing was misread and no arithmetic is involved in a matching set, so reading and calculation codes do not fit.
- **D — Incorrect:** The stem says no outside knowledge was used, so a passage-only violation does not apply. The time error fits, but the first code does not.

**MCQ 31**

Two colleagues both claim a shared vehicle on the same day. Each has now stated the reason they need it. The dispute must leave a record that a later reviewer can check. What should the facilitator do next?

- A. Rank the colleagues by seniority, and let the higher-ranking claim prevail.
- B. Propose a midpoint between the two positions, and record it as the agreed outcome.
- C. Generate options that meet both stated needs, and record the agreed option with a review date.
- D. Record the two stated needs and the outcome, and close the file without a review date.

**MCQ 31 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Ranking by seniority settles the claim by status and ignores the needs just stated, so no record of reasons would support the result.
- **B — Incorrect:** A midpoint between positions ignores the needs just stated, and it may leave both needs unmet while the record looks settled.
- **C — Correct:** Options that meet both needs can be generated, and the agreed option recorded with a review date gives a checkable record. That combines the mediation step with documented follow-up.
- **D — Incorrect:** Recording the needs and the outcome documents the dispute but gives no point at which the agreement is checked, so the record cannot show whether it worked.

**MCQ 32**

A council wants feedback on a bus fare change from residents, many of whom have no smartphone. Its draft is a single online survey whose first question asks residents to confirm that the change is fair. Which combined choice best meets the channel and bias requirements?

- A. Keep the online survey, add a question on the fare's benefits, and treat the response rate as representative.
- B. Keep the online survey only, and reword the first question to ask what residents think of the fare.
- C. Ask only residents who already use the bus, since they are the people the change affects most.
- D. Offer paper and assisted neutral versions, then compare respondents with those who did not reply.

**MCQ 32 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** A benefits question keeps the leading design, and treating the online response as representative is untested. It fails both the channel and the bias tests.
- **B — Incorrect:** Rewording the first question improves the wording but keeps a channel that excludes residents without smartphones. It fails the accessibility test.
- **C — Incorrect:** Limiting the survey to current bus users removes the people the change affects, so it cannot measure the effect on residents. It fails the representativeness test.
- **D — Correct:** Paper and assisted versions reach residents without smartphones, neutral questions remove the lead, and a comparison of respondents with non-respondents checks non-response bias. It answers both requirements.

**MCQ 33**

A junior officer says, 'You never listen to us.' The manager wants to respond assertively and reduce defensiveness. Which reply best combines a paraphrase with an assertive statement?

- A. You feel I dismiss your input, and I do need specific concerns from you by Friday.
- B. You feel I am not listening, and I will not discuss this until the accusations stop.
- C. I am sure I have listened well, so let us move on to the agenda.
- D. You seem sensitive about this, so please do not take my comments personally.

**MCQ 33 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** It paraphrases the concern and offers it back for confirmation, then states the manager's need in a specific, dated form. That combines paraphrase and assertiveness.
- **B — Incorrect:** Refusing to discuss the matter until the complaint stops is aggressive. It closes the exchange and leaves the concern unaddressed.
- **C — Incorrect:** Denying the concern without paraphrasing it is passive dismissal. It leaves the complaint unaddressed.
- **D — Incorrect:** Labelling the junior as sensitive is a global judgement. It attributes a trait instead of paraphrasing the concern.

# TIMED-SET PRACTICE AND SCORING RUBRIC

This section turns the methods of the nine lessons into a timed routine. The numbers in it are a practice frame. The personal target is a learning goal you set for yourself, not an official cut-off, and no cut-off is stated here as a fact.

## Scoring frame

The CSAT framework records the paper as 80 items in 200 marks and two hours, with equal marks for each item. Each correct answer earns 2.5 marks, each wrong answer loses one-third of that, which is 5/6 of a mark, and a blank carries no penalty. Confirm the current instruction page before any real attempt, because the framework itself says so. For a practice set the score is:

```text
score = 2.5 x (number correct)  -  (5/6) x (number wrong)
blank items score nothing
```

The expected value of an attempt with success probability p is:

```text
EV(p) = p x 2.5  -  (1 - p) x 5/6
EV = 0 when p = (5/6) / (2.5 + 5/6) = 1/4
```

| Situation after your work | Chance of being right (p) | Expected value per item | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| No idea, four live options | about 0.25 | 0 | Break-even; skip it unless you accept the variance. |
| One option eliminated, three remain | 1/3 | +5/18 (about +0.28) | Attempt. |
| Two options eliminated, two remain | 1/2 | +5/6 (about +0.83) | Attempt. |
| Confident in the method | 0.8 to 1.0 | +11/6 to +2.5 | Attempt. |

A blind guess has an expected value of zero. Every elimination you can justify makes the guess positive, so eliminate first and guess second. This is also why a rushed guess under time pressure is a costly habit: it spends a blank's safety on a coin-toss.

## Time budgets by item type

The budget below is the time each item type should take in a practice set. It is set so that the eighty-item paper, which allows about ninety seconds on average, can be completed with a return pass.

| Item type in this session | Budget per item | How to spend it |
|---|---|---|
| Single-step recall-in-scenario (Lessons 1, 2, 3 and 7) | about 60 seconds | Name the purpose, state the test, choose, check once. |
| Barrier diagnosis or interpersonal style (Lessons 4 and 5) | about 60 seconds | Name the diagnostic feature or the style pattern, then match. |
| Matching set (Lessons 2, 4 and 9) | about 45 seconds in Pass 1, plus the Pass 2 return if parked | Thirty seconds for purposes, fifteen seconds for elimination, park if two rows remain. |
| Scenario with four options (Lessons 5, 6, 7 and 9) | about 75 to 90 seconds | Run the gates in order; write the failing gate beside each rejected option. |
| Statement evaluation or numbered heads (Lessons 2 and 8) | about 75 seconds | Judge each statement or head alone before reading the codes. |
| Questionnaire defect or objective-to-data item (Lesson 8) | about 60 seconds | Write the decision in one sentence, then test the heads against it. |

## Three-pass control routine

**Pass 1: harvest.** Take every item that can be finished in about a minute with confidence: single-step recall, a clear barrier feature, a direct style pattern, a one-gate scenario. Bank these first.

**Pass 2: grind.** Return to the items that need the gates or the two-pass matching method. Cap each at about ninety seconds. If two rows remain in a matching set, or two gates seem to pass, park the item and move on.

**Pass 3: close.** Return to the parked items with the time that is left. Eliminate by stated gate or by stated feature. Guess only when at least two options have been eliminated for a reason you can name. Leave a blank where no elimination is possible.

**Rule of thumb.** Never let one stubborn scenario consume the time of three harvest items. The later pass is always available, and the marks are not.

## Marks per minute

The rubric below compares the marks an item type can return with the minutes it takes when the attempt is correct. It shows why the harvest-first routine is worth its discipline.

| Item type | Minutes per item | Marks per minute if correct | Expected marks per minute at p = 0.8 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Recall, about one minute | 1.0 | 2.50 | 1.83 |
| Matching, about 45 seconds | 0.75 | 3.33 | 2.44 |
| Scenario with gates, about 90 seconds | 1.5 | 1.67 | 1.22 |
| Statement evaluation, about 75 seconds | 1.25 | 2.00 | 1.47 |

The expected-marks column uses the expected value at p = 0.8 from the scoring frame, which is 11/6 (about 1.83) marks per item, divided by the minutes each item type takes. The scenario type returns the fewest marks per minute, so it is the item type most likely to consume a paper's time unless it is handled by the gate order and the Pass 2 cap.

## Practice-set routine and personal target

A practice set of ten communication items should be taken in ten minutes, with the answer blocks closed until the set is finished. Score it with the formula above. The personal target below is a learning goal, not an official threshold.

**Personal target (learner-set, not official):** seven or more correct in a ten-item set, with no more than one wrong, and every wrong item logged with its error code. Lower the target if the set is taken in less time than the budget, and raise it only after two sets in a row meet the current target.

**Worked score.** A set of ten items with seven correct, two wrong and one blank scores 2.5 x 7 - (5/6) x 2 = 17.5 - 1.67 = 15.83, out of a possible 25. The two wrong items cost 1.67 marks, and the blank cost nothing. The learner should check whether the two wrong items shared a gate or a lesson, because a shared cause is the most useful thing the set reveals.

## Error codes for post-test analysis

Every wrong item in a practice set receives one code. The codes are the Master Framework's, so the same log serves the whole paper. Track the mix of codes, not only the count, because a single dominant code points to one habit to repair.

| Code | Error type | What it looks like in this topic | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | Concept gap | Confusing formal with firm, or treating a barrier label as the diagnosis | Re-read the lesson's doctrine, then redo two items from it. |
| A | Application slip | Right idea applied to the wrong list member or the wrong gate | Slow the setup; write the purpose or the gate before choosing. |
| X | Calculation error | Arithmetic slip in a score or a time budget | Add a plug-back check to the score. |
| R | Reading error | Missing "not," "only" or "first" in a stem; misreading a list | Read the stem twice; underline the limiting word. |
| P | Passage-only violation | Using outside assumptions about a scenario that the stem does not give | Ask "which line of the stem proves this?" before choosing. |
| T | Time error | Spending a harvest item's time on a grind item | Enforce the per-item ceiling; park and return. |
| G | Guess error | A guess made with no elimination behind it | Guess only after a named elimination. |

# REMEDIATION

Remediation is organised by the lesson that owns each error. When an item is wrong, do not reread the whole session. Find the lesson, find the part named in the entry, and redo the method on a different situation before returning to the practice set. The retry prompts below are not practice items. They are prompts for a fresh situation that you write yourself, using the method.

## If you missed a lesson 1 item

Typical codes: C (confusing transmission with communication) and A (applying the cycle to the wrong link).

Revisit Part C of Lesson 1 and the five-check method. Retry prompt: write a message you have sent this month, state its purpose in one word, and write the feedback question that would have shown whether it was understood.

## If you missed a lesson 2 item

Typical codes: C (mixing formal and informal with oral and written) and A (choosing a channel by habit).

Revisit Part C (channel criteria) and Part E (purpose-to-tool matching). Retry prompt: take one message from your last week, and ask who must act on it and who must see that it arrived. State which channel the test supports, and whether the channel you used matched it.

## If you missed a lesson 3 item

Typical codes: R (reading the response as agreement) and A (answering before the paraphrase).

Revisit Part A (the HEAR chain) and Part C (paraphrase in practice). Retry prompt: write a three-sentence complaint from a friend, then write a paraphrase of its facts and of its concern separately, then say what the speaker would have to confirm.

## If you missed a lesson 4 item

Typical codes: C (a remedy for the wrong barrier) and R (missing the diagnostic feature in the stem).

Revisit Part A (the eight barriers) and Part B (diagnosis before remedy). Retry prompt: describe a communication failure you have seen, name its diagnostic feature in one line, and choose the barrier and the remedy. If two barriers act at once, use the Advanced sequence in Part C.

## If you missed a lesson 5 item

Typical codes: C (confusing assertive with firm and loud) and P (assuming a fact to justify empathy).

Revisit Part B (the three styles) and Part C (the difficult-conversation structure). Retry prompt: rewrite one of your own complaints as an observation that a third person could check, an impact, a question for the other person, a boundary and an agreed next step.

## If you missed a lesson 6 item

Typical codes: C (compromise as always correct) and A (using a mediator where a stop rule applies).

Revisit Part A (position and interest), Part B (compromise limits and escalation) and the stop rules in the visual. Retry prompt: take a dispute you have seen, write each side's position and then its interest, and check whether any constraint is non-negotiable before offering a midpoint.

## If you missed a lesson 7 item

Typical codes: C (treating a correct notice as a complete one) and P (guessing a cause for a service failure).

Revisit Part A (accessibility), Part C (the six situations) and Part D (documented follow-up). Retry prompt: write a public notice you have read, then write the route an affected reader must take, and the dated follow-up that would close the loop.

## If you missed a lesson 8 item

Typical codes: R (missing the defect's feature in the wording) and C (keeping a datum because it is interesting).

Revisit Part A (the seven defects) and Part B (the objective-to-data rule). Retry prompt: write one survey question you have seen, name its defect, then rewrite it in neutral, single, scoped form.

## If you missed a lesson 9 item

Typical codes: T (time) and G (guess), and sometimes C (missing the gate order).

Revisit Part A (the timed matching method), Part B (using the gates) and Part C (option elimination). Retry prompt: take a scenario with four options, write the failing gate beside each rejected option, and time yourself on the process. Repeat on a second scenario and compare the time.

## If you missed a cumulative item

Cumulative items join two methods. Identify which two lessons the item joined, then revisit both. Typical codes are A (applying one method where the item needs two) and C (a concept gap in the second method).

Retry prompt: write a situation in which a channel choice and a feedback step must both be right, then state what goes wrong if only one is.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

These notes are a compressed recall sheet. They do not repeat the teaching; they name the definitions, the sequences, the contrasts, the traps and the PYQ routes for every subtopic, in the order a reader will need them under time pressure. Each heading names a subtopic from the lessons above.

## The communication cycle and the success test

- Communication = the receiver grasps the intended meaning well enough for the purpose. Sending is transmission only.
- Loop: sender (purpose, authority) -> encode -> channel (noise may enter) -> receiver decodes -> feedback returns to sender.
- Eight checks: sender, purpose, message, channel, receiver, feedback, context, noise.
- Receipt, a register entry or a "noted" reply show arrival, not understanding.
- The sender owns the meaning that arrives; the receiver owns their own decoding.
- Before an important message: name the purpose in one word, state what the receiver must understand, choose the channel, name the likeliest noise, decide how feedback will confirm it.
- Mixed purpose means split the message or state which purpose governs.

## Channels, forms and cues

- Seven forms: oral (fast feedback, weak record); written (precise, durable, slow feedback); non-verbal (attention and emotion, never proof); visual (patterns, risk of distortion); digital (reach and searchability, access and privacy); formal (authority and traceability); informal (speed, weak accountability).
- Formal and informal describe relationship and authority. Oral and written describe medium. Do not mix the pairs.
- Verbal cue = content plus wording, pace and tone. Non-verbal cue = gesture, posture, eye contact, distance, timing and silence.
- Cues are culture- and context-sensitive; they cannot override clear words automatically. Align words, tone and context; ask when they conflict.
- Channel criteria: purpose, urgency, complexity, sensitivity, need for a record, receiver accessibility. A two-part message (briefing for urgency, written summary for record) often serves best.
- Firm = closes a decided matter. Open-ended = keeps an undecided matter open. Match the intention, not a habit.
- Medium and purpose (analytical inference): match the form to the purpose and the audience, and ask who must act on the message and who must see that it arrived.

## Listening, questions and feedback

- HEAR -> ATTEND -> CLARIFY -> PARAPHRASE -> RESPOND -> CONFIRM. Move forward only on confirmation; a "not quite" returns the listener to clarify.
- Understanding (accurate grasp) is not agreement (acceptance). A listener may understand exactly and disagree lawfully.
- Clarifying question asks for the missing piece without supplying it.
- Open question = explanation; closed question = a fact or confirmation.
- Leading question pushes; loaded question presupposes an unaccepted claim.
- Paraphrase separates facts from concern and is tied to what was said.
- Feedback: timely, specific, behaviour-focused, actionable, and two-way.

## Barriers and diagnosis

- Eight barriers: physical; semantic; psychological; organisational; cultural or social; technological; information overload; selective perception.
- Remedies: change place or channel; plain language and shared definitions; psychological safety and private clarification; one authoritative instruction and an escalation path; respectful adaptation; alternative channel and accessible format; prioritise and summarise; ask for evidence and invite contrary information.
- Diagnose before remedy. Louder repetition fixes only physical interference. More technical detail deepens a language mismatch. Public blame cannot fix an ambiguous instruction.
- Labels vary across sources (for example cognitive or affective for families that overlap the list). The fact pattern controls.
- Several barriers at once: stabilise safety or service; separate facts from allegations; identify audiences; choose channel and spokesperson; say known, unknown and action; create feedback loops; document and update.

## Interpersonal conduct and the styles

- Eight skills: empathy (understands constraints, does not accept wrongs); respect (dignity; a respectful refusal is still a refusal); assertiveness (fact, need, limit, respectful); emotional regulation (not suppression of needed information); cooperation (toward a legitimate objective); trustworthiness (keeps commitments, corrects own errors, confidentiality not absolute); adaptability (changes language or channel, not substance); persuasion (checkable reasons; manipulation and coercion are warning signs).
- Passive: avoids a legitimate concern; problem stays. Aggressive: threatens or humiliates; dignity and trust lost. Assertive: states issue, evidence, boundary and next step.
- Difficult conversation: observation (checkable) -> impact -> perspective (ask first) -> requirement or boundary -> agreed action and review.
- Avoid: motive without evidence, global labels, public humiliation, false promises, threats beyond authority, debate while the other cannot engage.

## Conflict, negotiation and mediation

- Position = stated demand; interest = why. Ask why to move from position to interest.
- Stop rules first: safety at risk; serious illegality or abuse; evidence to preserve; matter beyond the people in the room.
- PAUSE chain: facts; hear each side and identify interests; separate person from problem; identify lawful and non-negotiable constraints; generate feasible options; agree responsibility, timeline and review; document where accountability needs it.
- Compromise is not automatically correct. Unlawful, discriminatory or unsafe conduct cannot be split.
- Fair process first: hear each side, check the facts, and prefer problem-solving to coercion, unless a stop rule applies. "Talk privately first" does not override mandatory reporting or urgent protection.
- Preparation: interests, options, objective criteria and a lawful BATNA (the best lawful alternative to a negotiated agreement).
- Objective criteria: law, evidence, precedent, cost, published rule.
- Advanced: reservation point (minimum acceptable boundary); zone of possible agreement (overlap of ranges); integrative (creates value across interests); distributive (divides a fixed value). Never bargain away law, safety or equal treatment.
- Mediation: neutral facilitator sets ground rules, lets each account be given, reframes into issues and interests, identifies common and disputed facts, tests options, records agreement and review. Unsuitable where urgent protection, severe power imbalance, coercion or a mandatory legal process applies.

## Public and administrative dealing

- Test: clarity, accessibility, accuracy, timeliness, dignity, accountability, feedback.
- Accessibility asks which route is open to each reader; plain language keeps every legal condition.
- Procedural fairness: the affected person can state the rule, the reason, the chance to respond and the outcome.
- Six situations and their first repair: procedure not understood (plain steps, documents, confirm understanding); conflicting instructions (controlling authority, one corrected instruction, remove obsolete versions); stakeholder opposition (hear interests, disclose constraints, record objections); rumour (verify, credible channel, correct without repeating); sensitive personal matter (privacy, minimum disclosure, secure channel); service failure (acknowledge, known facts, remedy and timeline, update).
- Documented follow-up records: promise, date, outcome.
- Consensus is not proof of correctness or legality; check the rule and the facts before recording agreement. A note the citizen never received does not count as telling them.
- Stakeholder matrix: power, interest, vulnerability, information need, channel, feedback. High power does not erase the need to hear the highly affected.
- Risk and crisis: early communication; separate confirmed fact, current assessment and uncertainty; specific actions; one update source; visible prompt correction; no false reassurance or unsupported alarm.

## Questionnaires and consultation

- Seven defects: leading (neutral wording); loaded (separate the premise); double-barrelled (one issue per item); ambiguous (define scope and period); intrusive (collect only what the purpose needs); unbalanced choices (exhaustive, mutually intelligible); social-desirability pressure (privacy, neutral framing, specific recent behaviour).
- Objective-to-data rule: write the decision in one sentence; keep only the data that would change it; reject the merely interesting and the intrusive.
- Interview: probing and clarification; interviewer-bias risk. Questionnaire: standardisation and reach; cannot clarify. Both: a questionnaire with a sample of follow-up interviews.
- Behavioural and survey biases: confirmation (ask what would disprove it); authority gradient (invite dissent, protect reporters); groupthink (independent views first); framing (neutral parallel wording); acquiescence (balanced and reversed checks); social desirability (privacy, indirect questions); non-response (follow-up, representativeness).

## Scenario decision and the matching routine

- Seven gates in order: facts; legality and ethics (a precondition, an analytical reading); proportionality; communication quality; feasibility; accountability; follow-up.
- An option that fails an early gate is rejected at once. Note the failing gate beside each rejected option.
- Best option: the most lawful, fair, clear, proportionate and effective response to the facts given. Not automatically the softest or the firmest.
- Advanced elimination: reject options that confuse secrecy with confidentiality; promise certainty before facts; seek consensus before hearing the affected; punish the messenger; use public disclosure where a narrower channel suffices; delay urgent protection for consultation; treat documentation as communication; offer an inaccessible remedy.
- Matching: about 30 seconds for a purpose beside each list member; about 15 seconds for elimination; two rows left means park and return on the second pass.
- Safety-dispute transfer: the pause protects people first when the risk is material; secure records; hear both accounts separately; use the authorised process; record and review.

## PYQ routes and traps

- Linked items: 2013 Q79 and 2026 Q72 to Q77 (provisional), each linked in the PYQ section with its approach line. No entry in 2014 to 2025.
- Family before calculation: identify the family from the stem first; it names the lesson method.
- Trap: treating a written channel as always formal, or an oral one as always informal.
- Trap: choosing a remedy that targets the wrong barrier.
- Trap: a matching item solved by medium rather than purpose and audience.
- Trap: a questionnaire head kept because it is interesting, not because it changes the decision.
- Trap: a scenario option that is soft but unverified, or firm but disproportionate.
