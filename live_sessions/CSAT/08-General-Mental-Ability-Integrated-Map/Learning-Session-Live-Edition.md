# General Mental Ability: The Integrated Method - Live Learning Edition

**Paper:** CSAT (Civil Services Preliminary Examination, Paper II) | **Format:** four-option objective items only; there is no Mains component in this paper | **Practice in this file:** multiple choice, answered in a separate block after each item

General mental ability is the clause that asks you to do everything the other CSAT clauses ask, in the same two hours, with no preview of which kind of item is coming next. This file teaches the method that holds those parts together: how to classify a mixed item before you touch the arithmetic, how to store the information in a shape that exposes its structure, how to choose the lightest valid method, how to check the answer and when to stop. The specialist lessons on numbers, arithmetic, rates, algebra, reasoning and communication supply the mechanisms. This file supplies the decision order that decides which mechanism to use, and in what sequence.

Work through the lessons in order. Each lesson builds on the one before it, and each ends with a few original items that test the method and its common failure. Attempt each item before you open its explanation. Some lessons contain Advanced parts. They are taught in full, because mixed items in the paper use exactly the same moves, but they are labelled so that you can see which parts a core answer depends on and which parts sharpen it.

Your personal target is a learning goal, not an official cut-off. The timed-set section explains how to use it.

### Visual: the chain every item must pass through

```text
UNDERSTAND -> REPRESENT -> SELECT -> REASON / COMPUTE -> CHECK -> DECIDE
 what does     a shape you    the lightest    the working     plug-back, bounds,     answer, park
 the stem      can see        valid method    itself          or a counterexample    or leave
 ask for?
```

Read the chain as a relay. Each stage hands a usable object to the next: an understood demand, a representation, a chosen method, a computed value, a verified value, a decision. Most lost marks are not made at the last arrow. They are made at the first two, when the question is misunderstood or stored in a shape that hides its structure. A long calculation performed on a misread question is still a wasted calculation.

A small test shows the point. Two items can open with the same sentence about a tank and a pipe. One asks how much of the tank is filled in five hours. Another asks how long two pipes take together. A third asks in how many ways three of the twelve hours of a day can be picked for a tour. The first is a fraction of a rate, the second is a combined rate, and the third is a count of selections that contains no rate at all. The opening words are the same. The machinery is not.

### Thinking about the stem first

General mental ability is the ability to take a short piece of information, recognise what kind of question it is, and do the right working in the time available. It is not a test of how quickly you multiply. A fast multiplication inside the wrong method is still a wrong answer, and a slow multiplication inside the right method earns the mark if it fits the time cap.

Think of each stem as a parcel with a label on the outside. The label is the story: a shop, a train, a class, a family, a tank. The contents are the mechanism that the question needs. Two parcels can carry the same label and hold different contents. The label is useful for reading, but it cannot tell you which tool to pick up.

### What the umbrella covers

General mental ability is an umbrella. It names the ability to understand information, to represent it, to choose a valid method, to reason or compute, to check the result and to decide under time and negative marking. It is broader than arithmetic and narrower than the whole paper. Reading comprehension has its own method, and communication has its own method. Both support mental performance, but an item that depends on a passage is solved by passage discipline, not by the integrated method taught here.

The six mechanism families that this integration joins are these.

- **Number properties and patterns.** Divisibility, remainders, digits, factors, highest common factors and multiples, powers and series.
- **Proportional reasoning.** Ratio, percentage, averages, mixtures, money, shares and ages.
- **Rate, space and time.** Work and pipes, speed, trains and boats, clocks, calendars and shapes.
- **Algebra and sufficiency.** Equations, inequalities, comparisons and data-sufficiency decisions.
- **Relational, spatial and symbolic reasoning.** Arrangements, relations, directions, codes, deductions, counting and tables.
- **Communication judgement.** Barriers, feedback, conflict, channel choice, questionnaires and scenario decisions.

No item is left without a mechanism because it is broad. It is classified by the mechanism it needs.

### Worked example: one stem, every capability in order

A shop packs pens in boxes of twelve. A school orders seven boxes for five classes, gives each class nine pens, and asks how many pens are left.

- **Understand.** The output is a number of pens, the count left over.
- **Represent.** Total pens: 7 x 12 = 84. Pens given out: 5 x 9 = 45. Two totals, each with its unit.
- **Select.** The mechanism is a count combined by subtraction after two products. No rate or ratio is needed.
- **Compute.** 84 - 45 = 39.
- **Check.** The unit is pens. The answer is smaller than 84, as a remainder must be. Plug-back: 39 + 45 = 84.

- **Failure condition.** The chain breaks at the second stage if the boxes are read as pens. The total then becomes seven, the answer turns negative, and the magnitude check exposes it at once.

### Worked example: the same stem, a different output, a different family

Change only the question. "What fraction of the 84 pens went to the five classes?" asks for a part of a whole, so the family is proportional reasoning with a base of 84, and the answer is 45/84, which reduces to 15/28. The stem is identical. The nouns (boxes, classes, school) do not change; the output does.

- **Limit.** The families are a way to choose a method, not a catalogue of stories. Two items with the same story can belong to different families.
- **Where it fails.** Classifying by the nouns of the stem misses the proportion in the second question, which is the error the first-read tree is designed to prevent.

### Doctrine: classify the dominant mechanism

- **Definition.** The dominant mechanism is the operation that produces the requested output. If you removed it, the question could not be answered at all.
- **Rule.** Select the dominant mechanism first. Bring in a second mechanism only when it supplies a distinct necessary step, such as a percentage inside a ratio problem.
- **Test.** Ask what the final answer will be: a number of people, a fraction of a tank, a time, a truth value, a yes or no about sufficiency, a rank, a count of selections, or a comparison of two quantities. The shape of the output points to the family.

The first-read classification is a fixed sequence of questions. Ask them in order and stop at the first YES.

```text
Does the stem depend on a passage?                      YES -> passage method
  NO
  |
  +-- about communication, conflict or a channel?       -> communication judgement
  +-- asks whether the information is sufficient?       -> sufficiency decision
  +-- arrangement, relation, direction, code or table?  -> relational reasoning
  +-- divisibility, digits, remainders or patterns?     -> number properties
  +-- ratio, percentage, average, money or age?         -> proportional reasoning
  +-- work, speed, time, calendar or shape?             -> rate, space and time
  +-- equation, inequality or comparison?               -> algebra
  +-- none of these visible after 15 seconds?           -> park and move on
```

The tree is deliberately short. Its purpose is to make the first decision in ten to fifteen seconds, not to settle every hard case. A stem that matches two lines has a dominant mechanism, and the output decides which one.

### Worked classification: one label, three mechanisms

The three items below all open with the same twelve-hour frame. They are classified before any calculation.

Item A: a pipe fills a tank in twelve hours. How much of the tank is filled in five hours? The output is a fraction of a whole. The mechanism is a rate multiplied by time, so the family is rate, space and time.

Item B: one pipe fills a tank in twelve hours and a second in eight hours. How long do both take together? The output is a time. The mechanism is that rates add, not times, so the family is again rate, space and time, but the step is the combination of two rates.

Item C: in how many ways can three of the twelve hour-long slots in a day be chosen for a tour, if the order of choice does not matter? The output is a count of selections. The numbers twelve and three are present, but no rate or time relation is needed, so the family is counting.

- **Boundary.** The same label can host every family. The label does not decide. Only the requested output decides.
- **It breaks when.** The classification fails when the most visible keyword is treated as the mechanism. In Item C the word "hours" pulls the mind toward time, but the method is a choice count.

### The keyword trap

The tempting wrong method is to classify by surface vocabulary: "per", "shared", "fills", "out of" and "times" each trigger a family in the mind. This is quick and often correct for simple items, which is why it is dangerous in mixed items. A word such as "per" can mean a rate, a ratio, a unit price or a count per group.

The reply is that the vocabulary tells you the language of the stem, while the output tells you the method. Before you select a family, write the requested output in one phrase: "a fraction of the tank", "a combined time", "a count of selections". The phrase is the anchor. A keyword that does not fit the output is a decoy.

### On the paper

The paper does not announce its item families. Mixed items arrive in the same booklet as passages, arrangements and sufficiency statements, and the first task is always the same: classify in ten to fifteen seconds, then decide whether to harvest the item in the first pass or park it for the second. If neither a family nor a dominant mechanism is visible after fifteen seconds, the correct move is to park it with a reason, not to start calculating in the hope that the method will appear.

Classification also protects the time budget. An item that you recognise at once belongs in the first pass, where the cap is about a minute. An item whose mechanism needs a grid or a case split belongs in the second pass, where the cap is about two minutes. Classification is therefore the first decision about time, not merely a label.

### Recall: classification

- The chain is understand, represent, select, compute, check and decide. Errors at the first two stages cost more than errors at the last.
- Classify the dominant mechanism by the requested output, not by the story or by one keyword.
- Ask the first-read questions in order and stop at the first YES.
- A stem may contain two mechanisms. Select the dominant one first and add the second only when it supplies a distinct necessary step.
- The same label, such as twelve hours, can host a rate, a combined rate or a count.
- Write the requested output as a short phrase before any calculation.
- Spend about fifteen seconds on classification. If no family is visible, park the item with the reason "method".
- Classification sets the pass: direct items go to the first pass, multi-step items to the second.
- A keyword is a clue for vocabulary. It is not a method until the output confirms it.

### Lesson 1 practice

**MCQ 1**

Thirty households own a scooter, twenty own a car, and six own both. How many households own at least one of the two?

- A. 44 households
- B. 50 households
- C. 36 households
- D. 26 households

**MCQ 1 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The requested output is a count of households in the union of two groups that overlap. The overlap is counted in both groups, so it is removed once: 30 + 20 - 6 = 44. The classification is an overlap relation, not a sum of two independent groups.
- **B — Incorrect:** Fifty is the plain sum 30 + 20. This is the error of ignoring the overlap, which treats the six households with both as if they were distinct. The first-read question "is this an overlap?" would have caught it.
- **C — Incorrect:** Thirty-six is 30 + 6. It keeps the scooter group and the overlap, and drops the car-only households. It is the error of reading only one group and then adding the overlap back.
- **D — Incorrect:** Twenty-six is 20 + 6. It is the mirror error, keeping the car group and the overlap while dropping the scooter-only households.

**MCQ 2**

Red and blue balls in a bag are in the ratio 3:5. After eight more red balls are added, the ratio becomes 1:1. How many balls were in the bag at first?

- A. 12 balls, the red balls at first
- B. 32 balls, the bag before any addition
- C. 40 balls, the whole bag after the addition
- D. 4 balls, the common multiplier of the ratio

**MCQ 2 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Twelve is the number of red balls at first, which is 3k with k = 4. It answers a different quantity from the one asked, which is the whole bag. This is an output-selection error, not a calculation error.
- **B — Correct:** Let the common multiplier be k. The first red count is 3k and the blue count is 5k. After eight red balls are added, 3k + 8 = 5k, so k = 4. The whole bag at first is 3k + 5k = 8k = 32.
- **C — Incorrect:** Forty is the whole bag after the addition, 8k + 8 = 40. The error is to report the total after the change when the question asks for the total before it.
- **D — Incorrect:** Four is the multiplier k. It is a scaffold for the answer, not the answer. The error is to stop one step early.

**MCQ 3**

A tank starts empty. Pipe P fills an empty tank in six hours. Pipe Q empties a full tank in nine hours. Both pipes are opened together for two hours. What fraction of the tank is filled?

- A. one third of the tank, counting the filling pipe alone
- B. four fifteenths of the tank, using the average of the two times
- C. one ninth of the tank, netting the emptying rate against filling
- D. five ninths of the tank, adding the two rates as if both filled

**MCQ 3 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** One third is the filling of two hours by P alone, 2/6. It ignores the emptying pipe, which removes 2/9 of the tank in the same two hours. Classification as a single rate was the error.
- **B — Incorrect:** Four fifteenths comes from averaging the two times to 7.5 hours and then dividing two by 7.5. Rates combine by their own sum or difference, not by averaging the times.
- **C — Correct:** Pipe P contributes 2/6 = 1/3 of the tank in two hours, and pipe Q removes 2/9 of a full tank. The net fraction is 1/3 - 2/9 = 1/9.
- **D — Incorrect:** Five ninths is 2/6 + 2/9. It adds the two contributions as if both were filling. The sign of the emptying pipe was lost.

### Visual: the five-slot card

```text
+------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
| REQUESTED OUTPUT | GIVENS           | UNKNOWNS         | CONSTRAINTS      | IRRELEVANT       |
| number, unit,    | value + unit     | one letter each, | domain, order,   | marked as such,  |
| yes/no, rank     | for every number | with a sentence  | "at least", "not | never silently   |
|                  |                  |                  | more than"       | dropped          |
+------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+
```

Fill the card before you calculate. The output slot is the most important, and it is the one most often left blank. A card with an empty output slot is a card with no target, and any working that follows it will drift toward whatever number is easiest to produce.

### Why the output comes first

An answer is a response to a demand. The demand has a shape: it may ask for a number of people, a percentage, a difference, a larger quantity, a yes or no about whether information is enough, or a judgement about whether a conclusion follows. The same numbers can answer several different demands. A price that rises from eighty to ninety-two can be described by the rupee rise, by the percentage rise, or by the new price, and each description is a different answer.

Extraction is the act of making the demand explicit before the arithmetic starts. It is slow for about thirty seconds and saves minutes afterwards.

### Doctrine: output first, then the rest

- **Definition.** The requested output is the quantity, in the unit, and at the level of precision that the question names.
- **Rule.** Write the requested output before anything else. Then list the givens with their units, define each unknown with one sentence and list the constraints of the domain.
- **Completeness.** Every given must either be used or marked as irrelevant. A given that is silently dropped is the most common reason for an answer that is mathematically clean and still wrong.

The domain constraints deserve special attention. Words such as "at least", "not more than", "a whole number", "distinct" and "in the same order" are part of the demand. They do not decorate the question. An answer that violates a domain word is not a valid answer, even when its algebra is correct.

### Worked example: a class of fifty-eight

The stem says that a class has fifty-eight students, that boys outnumber girls by six, and asks how many girls there are.

The output is a count of girls. The givens are a total of fifty-eight and a difference of six. The unknown is the number of girls, which we name g. Boys then equal g + 6. The constraint is that both counts are whole numbers.

The relation is g + (g + 6) = 58, so 2g = 52 and g = 26. Check: 26 girls, 32 boys, 26 + 32 = 58, and 32 - 26 = 6.

- **Where it stops.** Had the question asked for the percentage of the class who are girls, the output would change to a percentage of fifty-eight, and the same card would give 26/58 of the class, about 44.8 per cent. The givens are the same. Only the output slot changed.
- **Breaks down when.** The count of girls fails when the unknown is defined as the boys. If g is the number of boys, then the relation becomes b + (b - 6) = 58, which gives b = 32, and an answer of 32 is a valid count of the wrong group. The output slot prevents this: it names girls, so the unknown must be girls.

### Worked example: rupees, percentages and the same rise

A price rises from eighty rupees to ninety-two rupees. Two output requests are possible.

If the output is the rupee rise, the card gives 92 - 80 = 12 rupees.

If the output is the percentage rise, the card gives 12 / 80 = 15 per cent.

The givens do not change, but the answer does, because the unit of the output changes. A candidate who answers "12" to a percentage question has answered a correct quantity in the wrong unit, and the option list will not reward that answer.

- **Limit.** Percentage points are a further output. If a share rises from 40 per cent to 52 per cent, the rise is 12 percentage points, and the percentage change is 12/40 = 30 per cent. The output slot must say which one is asked.
- **Failure condition.** The method fails when the unit is written from habit rather than from the question. "How much more" usually asks for a difference, while "by what per cent" asks for a relative change. Only the wording of the demand can settle it.

### Worked example: a decision output, not a value

The question asks whether the two statements together fix the value of n. Statement I says 2n + 4 = 12. Statement II says n^2 = 16.

- **The output slot.** The output is a decision: is n fixed by the information? It is not the value of n.
- **Statement I alone.** 2n = 8, so n = 4. One value is fixed, so statement I is sufficient on its own.
- **Statement II alone.** n = 4 or n = -4. Two values survive, so statement II is not sufficient alone.
- **Both together.** Statement I already fixes n, so the pair adds nothing new.

The verdict logic is general. Test each statement alone. If one statement fixes the value, it is sufficient. If none does, test the two together: if the pair fixes the value, the pair is sufficient; if not, the information is insufficient. Only uniqueness is decided. Once the value is fixed, stop: computing further adds nothing.

- **Boundary.** The printed response choices differ between papers. Read the options printed with the item before deciding, and never rely on a remembered list of options.
- **Where it fails.** Two errors are common. Treating a square as a single value ignores the second root; computing the value when the question asks only whether it is fixed spends time without changing the decision.

### Solving everything

The tempting wrong method is to solve everything that can be solved, and then pick the option that matches one of the results. It feels thorough, and sometimes it works on easy items. On a mixed item it is the most expensive possible route, because each extra calculation adds a chance of slip and a minute of the clock. It also fails when the options are close, because a candidate who has computed three quantities has no reliable way to know which one the question asked for.

The reply is output-first extraction. A stem that asks for one quantity needs one chain of calculations. Computing a second quantity only makes sense when the first is needed as an intermediate step, and then it should be named as an intermediate step on the card.

### Where extraction costs marks

In a data-sufficiency item the requested output is not a value at all. It is a judgement about whether the statements fix the value. The card must therefore note the question about uniqueness, not the question about the number. Students who skip this step often compute the value, find it, and then discover that the question asked only whether it could be found. The sufficiency decision is worked through in the example above; the discipline of writing the output first governs every item in the paper.

Completeness is equally important in comprehension-free items. A mixed item often contains one condition that looks decorative and is in fact the decisive constraint. Marking such a condition as irrelevant on the card is a legitimate decision, but it must be a deliberate one.

### Recall: the card

- Write the requested output first, in the unit and precision the question names.
- Fill the five slots (output, givens, unknowns, constraints, irrelevant) before any calculation.
- Define every unknown with a sentence that includes its unit and its group.
- Domain words such as "at least", "whole number" and "distinct" are constraints, not decoration.
- The same givens can answer a rupee question, a percentage question or a percentage-point question. The output slot decides.
- A "how much more" request usually wants a difference. A "by what per cent" request wants a relative change.
- Every given must be used or deliberately marked irrelevant.
- If the output is a yes or no about information, the card notes a decision question, not a value.
- Computing extra quantities is a cost. Compute an intermediate only when it is the next step to the output.

### Lesson 2 practice

**MCQ 4**

A box holds apples and pears. The number of apples is twice the number of pears, and the box holds 45 fruit in all. Six apples are then removed. How many apples remain?

- A. 30 apples
- B. 39 apples
- C. 15 apples
- D. 24 apples

**MCQ 4 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Thirty is the apple count before the removal. The stem asks what remains after six apples are taken out, so the removal was dropped.
- **B — Incorrect:** Thirty-nine subtracts the six apples from the total of 45 fruit, which includes the pears. The removal applies to apples only.
- **C — Incorrect:** Fifteen is the number of pears. It answers a neighbouring quantity, the pears, not the apples that remain.
- **D — Correct:** Let p be the number of pears, so the apples are 2p and 3p = 45, giving p = 15 and 30 apples. Removing six leaves 24. Check: 24 apples and 15 pears make 39 fruit, and 45 - 6 = 39.

**MCQ 5**

A sum of money is shared between two people in the ratio 2:5. The question asks for the difference between their shares. The total is seventy. What is the difference?

- A. 30
- B. 60
- C. 20
- D. 70

**MCQ 5 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The shares are 2k and 5k with 7k = 70, so k = 10. The shares are 20 and 50, and their difference is 30, which is the requested output.
- **B — Incorrect:** Sixty is twice the difference, 2 x 30. It scales a result that was already the answer, which is the error of doubling an output that was correct.
- **C — Incorrect:** Twenty is the smaller share. The question asks for the difference, so the output slot names the wrong quantity.
- **D — Incorrect:** Seventy is the total, which is the given. Reporting a given as the answer is the signal that the output slot was not filled.

### Visual: the shape chooser

```text
INFORMATION SHAPE                       REPRESENTATION
repeated proportional change        -> multiplicative factors
relative shares                     -> a ratio, or a 100-unit base
work or motion                      -> a rate x time table
family relation                     -> a tree
direction or turn                   -> a compass sketch or coordinates
seating or ordered positions        -> slots or a grid
conditional statements              -> arrows, with the contrapositive
sets and overlap                    -> a Venn diagram or inclusion-exclusion
a data table or chart               -> base, unit and requested quantity labelled first
sufficiency of statements           -> separate tests: statement I, statement II, both
```

The right-hand column is not decoration. Each representation makes one kind of relation visible and hides the others. A rate-time table makes a multiplication visible, and it hides the ordering of events. A grid makes the constraints of a seating plan visible, and it can make a percentage invisible. Choosing the shape is the decision that determines what you can see when you start to calculate.

### Why the shape matters

A representation is a way of holding information so that the next step is easy. A candidate who holds the facts in memory is juggling. A candidate who writes them in a table, a line or a grid has offloaded the juggling onto the page. The page does not get tired, and it does not forget a number that was mentioned three sentences earlier.

The representation also tells you whether the problem is well posed. If you cannot draw a rate-time table for a work problem, you do not yet know what the rate is. If you cannot draw the grid for a seating plan, you do not yet know the positions. The inability to draw is a diagnostic, not a delay.

### Doctrine: representation is part of the solution

- **Definition.** A representation is a written structure that holds the givens and the unknowns in a form where the relation between them is visible.
- **Rule.** Choose the representation from the information shape, not from habit. Use the table that matches the shape, and state the labels before entering numbers.
- **Consequence.** The representation is graded as part of the solution. A correct arithmetic step on a wrong representation is a wrong method, and it will be wrong on every similar item.

### Worked example: weighted averages from totals

Group A has 40 observations with a mean of 70. Group B has 60 observations with a mean of 40. Every Group B observation then rises by 25 per cent. The question asks whether the combined mean is now above 55.

The information shape is a set of unequal groups with a mean in each. The representation is a total for each group, not a list of its observations and not a pair of averages.

- Group A total: 40 x 70 = 2800.
- New Group B mean: 40 x 1.25 = 50.
- New Group B total: 60 x 50 = 3000.

The combined total is 5800 over 100 observations, so the combined mean is 58. The question asks only whether it is above 55, so the inequality can be tested directly: 55 x 100 = 5500, and the actual total of 5800 exceeds it. The answer is yes, and no division was needed.

Two representation errors are visible. Taking the plain mean of 70 and 50, which gives 60, is wrong because the groups have unequal sizes; the representation has ignored the weights. Scaling Group B by 1.25 is safe only because the same percentage applies to every Group B observation. If only some observations had risen, the total could not be scaled in one step.

- **Where it stops.** The totals representation is exact, but it needs the sizes of the groups. If the sizes are not given, the weighted mean cannot be computed, and the card should mark the constraint as missing.
- **It breaks when.** The plain mean fails whenever the groups differ in size. A plain mean is correct only when the quantities being averaged carry equal weight.

### Part B: when a list is the wrong representation

Lists are attractive because they feel complete. For a set of averages, a list of the individual observations is usually wasteful and sometimes impossible. The totals representation keeps the arithmetic short and makes the weights explicit. A list is the right representation when the question depends on the order or the identity of the items, such as a seating plan or a ranking. A total is the right representation when the question depends only on the sum.

The two representations answer different demands. A question about the average of a set uses totals. A question about who sits next to whom uses a grid or a list in order. Choosing the list when the question is about totals adds work without adding information.

Worked example: five candidates scored 62, 58, 71, 80 and 66. For the mean, only the total is needed: 337 over five gives 67.4. For a question about who is third, the list is needed, because sorted from the top the scores are 80, 71, 66, 62, 58, and the third is 66. The total cannot answer the rank question.

- **Failure condition.** Using a total for a question about position, such as who ranks third, gives a number with no meaning for that question. The list must be kept whenever order or identity matters.

### Part C: repeated change and the multiplicative factor

A repeated percentage change should be stored as a multiplicative factor, not as a running list of percentages. A rise of 10 per cent is a factor of 1.10, a fall of 10 per cent is a factor of 0.90, and two changes multiply: 1.10 x 0.90 = 0.99. The net change is a 1 per cent fall, not zero. The additive representation, which adds the percentages to get zero, is the error that this representation prevents.

- **Limit.** Factors multiply only when the changes apply to the same base. If the second change applies to a different quantity, the factors must be applied to their own bases before the totals are combined.

### Part D: the remaining shapes, each with an example and its failure condition

- **Ratio and the 100-unit base.** A class of 45 has boys to girls in the ratio 3:2. The base is 3 + 2 = 5 parts, so boys are 3/5 of 45 = 27. Failure condition: dividing 3 by 2 and treating the result as a share of the class gives 1.5, which is not a share at all.
- **Rate times time.** A pipe fills a tank in six hours. In four hours it fills 4/6 = 2/3 of the tank. Failure condition: multiplying the rate by the time with mismatched units, such as tanks per hour by minutes, which gives a number with no meaning.
- **Family tree.** A is the parent of B, and A is also the parent of C. Drawn as a tree, C is a child of A, not a parent. Failure condition: drawing a sibling as a child, which reverses the arrow and changes every later relation.
- **Compass or coordinates.** A walker goes 3 km north and then 4 km east. Coordinates give (4, 3), so the straight-line distance from the start is 5 km. Failure condition: adding the two legs to get 7 km, which is the path length, not the distance from the start.
- **Venn or inclusion-exclusion.** 25 people like tea, 18 like coffee and 7 like both. The union is 25 + 18 - 7 = 36. Failure condition: adding 25 and 18 without subtracting the overlap of 7 counts the 7 people twice.
- **Labelled table.** A table lists units sold in January and February with a price per unit in rupees. Before dividing, write the unit of every column. Failure condition: dividing rupees by units without labels yields a rupees-per-unit figure only when the columns are the right way round, and a swapped column gives a plausible but wrong number.

### Averaging averages

The tempting wrong method is to average the averages. It appears in almost every mixed item with unequal groups, because the mean of two means feels like a natural summary. The reply is to store totals. If the question gives two means and two sizes, the total of each group is size times mean, and the combined mean is the sum of the totals divided by the sum of the sizes. The error is not a slip in arithmetic. It is a representation that drops the weights.

A second tempting method is to draw a diagram that does not match the information, such as a grid for a set problem, or a list for a relation that is really a total. The reply is to check the representation against the information shape before calculating. If the drawing does not hold the information in its labels, it is the wrong drawing.

### How representation shows up in options

Representation is where many mixed items are decided, because the options are usually built to catch the representation error. A plain-average option, a wrong-base option and a wrong-direction option are common distractors. The discipline that avoids them is the same in every family: name the shape, choose the matching structure, label every entry with its unit, and only then calculate.

### Recall: representations

- Choose the representation from the information shape: factors, ratio, rate table, tree, compass, grid, arrows, Venn diagram, labelled table or separate tests.
- A representation is part of the solution, not decoration.
- For unequal groups, store totals (size times mean), not means.
- Averaging two averages drops the weights and is the common error in this family.
- Repeated percentage changes multiply: 1.10 x 0.90 = 0.99, a net fall of 1 per cent.
- A list is the right shape when order or identity matters, and a total is the right shape when only the sum matters.
- A question about "above or below a threshold" can often be settled by multiplying the threshold by the size and comparing totals.
- If a representation cannot be labelled with the given units, the representation is wrong.
- The representation is the decision that determines what you can see when you calculate.

## Lesson 4 - Least-work selection and the conditions that make a shortcut safe

Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the cheapest route that is still valid

```text
              CHEAPEST ROUTE THAT STILL HOLDS
  property ---- elimination ---- estimation ---- substitution ---- full solution
  (no work)     (cut options)    (decisive?)     (test a value)     (always valid)
     |               |                |                |                  |
  condition?      condition?      separation?     one case only      every step
  holds here?     holds here?     large enough?   proves nothing     checked
```

Read the ladder from left to right, but do not climb a rung until its condition has been checked. The left-hand routes are cheaper and they are valid only when their conditions hold. The right-hand route is always valid and is the most expensive. A good candidate spends most items on the middle rungs and keeps the full solution for the items that need it.

### What a shortcut is really betting on

A shortcut is a bet on a condition. When you use a rule that saves work, you are assuming that something about the stem makes the rule true. The bet is often right, which is why shortcuts are attractive. The bet is wrong in exactly the cases that UPSC places in the options, because the wrong option is the answer that the shortcut produces when its condition quietly fails.

Think of a bridge that the map says is open. Driving onto it is cheaper than the detour, but only if the map is current. Before you use a shortcut, look for the sign that says the bridge is open, which here means the condition is still true for this stem.

### Core doctrine: least work, with a condition check

- **Definition.** The least-work principle chooses the lightest method whose conditions are satisfied by the stem and whose result can be trusted at the option spacing.
- **Rule.** Before using any shortcut, state its condition in one line, confirm the condition in the stem, and then use it.
- **Options as a check.** If the options are well separated, an estimate that lands clearly inside one band is decisive. If two options are close, the estimate is not enough, and the exact route is needed.

### Named method: the condition-preservation test

The test has three moves, and each can be written in a single line on the page.

1. Name the shortcut and its condition, for example: "the terms form an arithmetic run starting at 1 with difference 2."
2. Check the condition against this stem, not against the family in general.
3. Check that the options are far enough apart for the shortcut's result to decide, or else continue to the exact route.

- **Breaks down when.** The test fails when a condition is assumed rather than checked. The most common silent condition is "equal" where the stem is not equal: equal times, equal groups, equal weights or equal steps.

### Worked example: a run of odd numbers

The sum of the odd numbers from 1 to 39 is required. The shortcut is that the first n odd numbers sum to n squared. The condition is that the run starts at 1 and has difference 2. Here it does: 1, 3, 5, ..., 39 is the first twenty odd numbers, so n = 20 and the sum is 400.

Check the answer by a second route. Pair the terms from the two ends: 1 + 39 = 40, 3 + 37 = 40, and so on, giving ten pairs of 40, so 400. The second route confirms the shortcut without relying on memory of the formula.

- **Boundary.** The shortcut fails for the run 3, 5, 7, ..., 39. That run has difference 2 but starts at 3, so it is not the first n odd numbers. The sum is then the total of the first twenty odd numbers minus 1, which is 399. The condition decides the answer, not the appearance of odd numbers.

### Worked example: the sum of the first even numbers

The even numbers 2, 4, 6 and so on up to 18 are the first nine even numbers. Each is twice a counting number, so their sum is twice the sum of 1 to 9, which is twice 45, giving 90. The shortcut for the first n even numbers is n(n + 1), and with n = 9 that is 9 x 10 = 90. Check by pairing: 2 + 18, 4 + 16, 6 + 14 and 8 + 12 each make 20, and 10 is left over, so 4 x 20 + 10 = 90.

- **Limit.** The formula is for even numbers starting at 2. A run such as 4, 6, 8 and so on starts elsewhere, and its formula must be rebuilt from its first term.
- **Failure condition.** Reusing the odd-number formula n^2 for an even run gives 81 here, which is wrong. The run's own structure decides the formula, not its resemblance to another run.

### Worked example: estimation that is decisive, and when it is not

The product 0.48 x 1249 is required, and the options are 60, 600, 6000 and about 5995. The exact product is 599.52. An estimate is enough here: 1249 is just below 1250, and 0.48 x 1250 = 600, so the product is just below 600. The nearest option is 600. The options are separated by a factor of ten, so the estimate decides the answer.

- **Where it stops.** The same estimate fails if the options are 600 and 596. The estimate of just below 600 cannot separate 600 from 596, so the exact route is needed. Estimation is valid only when the option spacing makes the result decisive.

### Testing one case and calling it proof

The tempting wrong method is to test an identity or a shortcut at one value and conclude that it holds in general. A single numerical check can disprove a claim, but it cannot prove one. If a shortcut matches at one point, that is evidence, not proof.

The reply is to distinguish a check from a proof. A check at one point is cheap and useful for discarding a wrong option. A proof needs the algebra to hold for every value in the domain. Consider the expression (a + b)^2 - (a - b)^2. At a = 7 and b = 3 it equals 84, and this agrees with 4ab. One value is a check, and the algebra 4ab is the proof that holds for all a and b.

### Advanced Part C: the method-selection hierarchy

This part is Advanced. It makes explicit the order in which the lightest methods should be tried. The core rule above already contains each rung, and this hierarchy shows the order.

1. **Direct invariant or property.** A sum, parity, divisibility or symmetry that decides the answer without computation.
2. **Option elimination.** Cut the options that violate a unit, sign, parity, magnitude or boundary condition.
3. **Smart values or scaling.** Replace the quantities by values that make the structure visible, such as a common multiple or a total of 100 units, provided the relation is preserved under the substitution.
4. **Compact algebra or a diagram.** A short relation or a labelled representation that solves the problem in a few lines.
5. **Full enumeration or long computation.** The safest route, and the most expensive. It is the route for an item whose structure does not yield to the first four.

A shortcut is safe only when every condition survives the substitution and the option spacing is decisive. A weighted-average comparison shows the idea. The safe route compares the total of 5800 with the threshold total of 55 x 100 = 5500 and never divides. The shortcut is valid because the group sizes are preserved and the 25 per cent factor applies uniformly to Group B. A solver who averages 70 and 50 has chosen the wrong representation, not merely made an arithmetic slip, and the hierarchy would have caught it before any computation.

- **Failure condition.** A rung is applied before its condition is checked. Elimination that ignores a stem constraint removes the right option, and the mistake is not visible until the answer is wrong.

### Pace on the paper

Least-work thinking decides the pace of the paper. A property or elimination route often takes fifteen to thirty seconds and resolves an item that would take two minutes in full. The items that deserve the full route are those whose conditions are not clearly satisfied, or whose options are close. The discipline is not to be lazy; it is to spend the time where the answer is not yet decided.

### Recall: least work

- A shortcut is a bet on a condition. State the condition before using the shortcut.
- Check the condition against this stem, not against the family in general.
- The sum of the first n odd numbers is n squared, but only for a run that starts at 1 with difference 2.
- An estimate is valid only when the option spacing makes the estimate decisive.
- A single numerical check disproves a claim but does not prove an identity.
- Use the algebra for proof and a point value for a quick check.
- The least-work ladder runs from property to elimination, estimation, substitution and full solution.
- Advanced Part C: the hierarchy adds smart values and scaling between elimination and algebra.
- Spend the full route on items whose conditions are unclear or whose options are close.

## Lesson 5 - Logical distinctions: necessity, sufficiency, implication, possibility and correlation

Progress: Lesson 5 of 12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Implication, necessity, sufficiency and possibility

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: one arrow with three readings

```text
   A ----------------------> B          "if A, then B"
   sufficient       necessary          A is sufficient for B
   for B            for A              B is necessary for A

   Allowed:        not B ---------> not A        (contrapositive, same meaning)
   Not allowed:    B -----------> A               (converse, a different claim)
   Not allowed:    not A ---------> not B         (inverse, a different claim)

   Possible:  one valid case shows the outcome can occur
   Certain:   every valid case must show it
```

The arrow carries one direction only. The contrapositive is the same claim turned around and negated, so it is always licensed. The converse and the inverse are different claims, and they are not licensed by the original arrow. The lower half of the figure separates possibility, which needs one case, from certainty, which needs all cases.

### One direction only

A conditional statement says that one thing guarantees another, and it says nothing about the reverse. Fuel in the tank is necessary for driving, but fuel in the tank does not make the car move: the driver, the key and the road must also be present. Fuel is necessary for the drive and not sufficient for it. Sufficiency runs the other way: the presence of a sufficient condition settles the matter, while the absence of a necessary condition settles it negatively.

Most logical errors in this family are a reversed arrow, dressed in ordinary words. The statement "if A then B" becomes "if B then A" in a sentence that sounds equally natural, and the error is hard to notice unless the arrow is drawn.

- **Failure condition.** Treating a necessary condition as sufficient: fuel is necessary for the drive, so a full tank does not show that the car moves, and the error is to read the arrow the other way.

### Doctrine: four distinctions that decide the answer

- **Necessary and sufficient.** In "if A then B", A is sufficient for B, and B is necessary for A. Sufficiency says that A settles B. Necessity says that without B, A cannot hold.
- **Implication and converse.** "If A then B" permits "if not B then not A" and does not permit "if B then A". The converse reverses the arrow and needs its own proof.
- **Possible and certain.** One valid case proves that an outcome is possible. A claim of certainty needs every valid case to produce the outcome, so one counterexample defeats it.
- **Correlation and causation.** An association between two quantities may come from a common cause, a coincidence or a reverse link. Association alone does not establish the mechanism that would make one cause the other.

### Named method: the direction test

Write the claim as an arrow, A -> B, with the premise and conclusion labelled. Then ask three questions in order.

1. Does the conclusion follow by the contrapositive? If yes, it is licensed.
2. Does the conclusion require the converse or the inverse? If yes, look for a counterexample before accepting it.
3. Does the item ask for possibility or certainty? If possibility, one valid case is enough. If certainty, search for one counter-case first.

- **Failure condition.** The direction test fails when the premise and conclusion are not separated in the sentence. A sentence with two clauses can hide which clause is the premise. Write the arrow before judging.

### Worked example: the contrapositive is free, the converse is not

The rule says: if a number is a multiple of six, then it is even. The contrapositive says: if a number is not even, then it is not a multiple of six. That is licensed, and it is true, because every odd number is excluded from the multiples of six.

The converse says: if a number is even, then it is a multiple of six. That is not licensed. The number 2 is even and is not a multiple of six, which is one counterexample, and one counterexample defeats the converse.

- **Limit.** The contrapositive holds only when the original arrow is true. If the rule were false, its contrapositive would be false too. The method checks the form, not the truth of the premise.

### Worked example: possibility is not certainty

A bag holds two red and two blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. It is possible that both are red, and it is possible that both are blue. It is also possible that one is red and one blue. So the outcome "the two balls have the same colour" is possible but not certain, because a single outcome of different colours is enough to defeat certainty. The option that says "certain" fails at once, because one counter-case exists.

- **Boundary.** A claim of impossibility needs a proof that no case allows the outcome. A claim of possibility needs only one case that does. The two claims must not be confused with each other.

### Worked example: association and a common cause

Suppose two measures rise together over the summer months. The association is real, but it does not say which measure causes the other. Both may be driven by heat, which is a common cause. The test for causation asks for a mechanism that links the two measures and survives when the common cause is held fixed. An association without that mechanism is a correlation, and a sentence that moves from correlation to cause makes an unlicensed step.

- **Where it stops.** The absence of a known mechanism does not prove that no cause exists. It proves only that the association alone cannot settle the cause.

### Reading an arrow backwards

The tempting wrong method is to read "if A then B" as "A and B go together" and then accept the converse as obvious. In a sentence about candidates, schemes or measures, the reversal feels natural, and the item's options are built to catch it. The reply is the direction test: write the arrow, and accept only the contrapositive without a further check.

A second tempting method is to treat a single example as proof of a universal claim. The reply is the counterexample test: look for one case that breaks the claim before accepting it. For a possibility claim, one case is sufficient; for a certainty claim, one counter-case is decisive.

### How the traps appear in statements

In statement-and-conclusion items, the trap is almost always a converse or an inverse presented as a conclusion. The question asks what "must be true" or what "follows", and only the contrapositive and the direct consequences of the arrow are licensed. Words such as "possibly", "certainly", "necessarily" and "only if" are the signals that the item is testing this distinction. Read them as part of the demand.

### Recall: direction and modality

- "If A then B" makes A sufficient for B and B necessary for A.
- The contrapositive, "not B then not A", is always licensed by the arrow.
- The converse, "B then A", and the inverse, "not A then not B", are not licensed by the arrow.
- A possibility claim needs one valid case; a certainty claim is defeated by one counter-case.
- A claim of impossibility needs proof that no case allows the outcome.
- Association does not establish cause without a mechanism that survives the control of a common cause.
- Write the arrow with premise and conclusion labelled before judging a conclusion.
- Words such as "must", "certainly", "possibly" and "only if" are signals of the demand.

## Lesson 6 - Quantitative distinctions: change, averages, counting, distance, estimation and sufficiency

### Visual: six pairs, one question each

```text
PAIR                              ASK THIS FIRST
absolute change  vs  relative     the unit: points or per cent of the base?
average  vs  weighted average     do the groups carry equal weight?
permutation  vs  combination      does swapping two chosen items change the outcome?
distance  vs  displacement        is the answer a path length or a net position?
exact  vs  estimate               do the options separate enough for an estimate?
calculation  vs  sufficiency      is the question asking for a value or for a decision?
```

Each row names a pair that looks interchangeable in the stem and is not interchangeable in the answer. The question in the right-hand column is the test that separates them. Ask it before the arithmetic, and write the answer in the card of Lesson 2.

### Six pairs, one confusion

Most quantitative errors in mixed items are not errors of arithmetic. They are errors of kind: the answer computed is a real quantity, but it is the wrong kind of quantity. A rise of twelve points and a rise of thirty per cent are both real. A committee and an arrangement are both counts. A path length and a net position are both distances in ordinary speech. The distinction is what the question asks for, and the arithmetic has no power to correct it.

### Doctrine: the test for each pair

- **Absolute and relative change.** An absolute change is a difference in the unit of the quantity, such as points or rupees. A relative change is that difference divided by the original base, usually shown as a per cent.
- **Average and weighted average.** A plain mean is correct only when the quantities averaged have equal weight. When groups differ in size, the mean is weighted by size, and the total is the reliable quantity.
- **Permutation and combination.** A permutation counts arrangements in which order matters. A combination counts selections in which order does not matter. The test is whether swapping two chosen items gives a different outcome.
- **Distance and displacement.** Distance is the length of the path. Displacement is the net change in position from start to finish, with direction. A journey with a return leg has a distance that exceeds its displacement.
- **Exact and estimate.** An estimate is valid when the options are separated enough that the estimate lands clearly in one band. Otherwise the exact route is needed.
- **Calculation and sufficiency.** A calculation asks for a value. A sufficiency question asks only whether the information fixes the value. Solving for the value when the question asks for uniqueness is wasted work.

### Worked example: absolute and relative change in a school

A school's pass share rises from 40 per cent to 52 per cent. The absolute change is 12 percentage points. The relative change is 12 divided by 40, which is 30 per cent. A candidate who reports 12 per cent has reported a points difference as a relative change, and a candidate who reports 30 points has reported a relative change as a points difference. The two quantities are both correct and answer different questions.

- **Limit.** If the question asks how many more students passed in a fixed group of 200, the answer is a count, and it equals the points change applied to the group, 12 per cent of 200 = 24 students. The unit of the question decides the method.

### Worked example: average with unequal groups, weighted by size

Two sections of a test have different sizes. Section one has 30 students with a mean of 60, and section two has 20 students with a mean of 80. A plain average of 60 and 80 gives 70, which treats the sections as equal. The weighted average uses totals: 30 x 60 = 1800 and 20 x 80 = 1600, so the combined total is 3400 over 50 students, which gives a mean of 68. The plain average overstates the mean because the larger section has the lower mean.

- **Boundary.** The weighted average needs the sizes. If the sizes are not given, the question cannot be answered from the means alone.

### Worked example: a committee and an arrangement

Four people, A, B, C and D, are to be seated in a row. The number of arrangements is 4 x 3 x 2 x 1 = 24, because order matters. The number of ways to choose two of them as a pair to serve on a committee is 6, because order does not matter. The same four people give two different counts, and the test is whether the outcome changes when two chosen people swap places. In a row, it does. In a committee, it does not.

- **Where it stops.** Some items mix the two: an arrangement of chosen people. The chosen group is a combination, and its arrangement is a permutation. The two steps are multiplied, and neither can be skipped.

### Worked example: distance and displacement on a route

A runner goes 7 km east, then 11 km west, then 3 km east. The distance covered is 7 + 11 + 3 = 21 km. The net position is 7 - 11 + 3 = -1, so the displacement is 1 km west of the start. A candidate who reports 21 km for a displacement question, or 1 km for a distance question, has answered the other quantity.

- **Limit.** Displacement is zero for a closed loop, whatever the distance. A loop is therefore a test of whether the answer should be distance or displacement.

### Worked example: when an estimate is enough, and when sufficiency is the question

The estimate on the earlier product showed that the options can decide the answer. The same discipline applies here: estimate, check the separation, and only then compute exactly if the options are close.

A sufficiency item is different. It does not ask for a value. It asks whether the given information is enough to fix one. A candidate who calculates the value and then reports it has answered a different question. The method is to test whether the value is unique under the information, and to stop once uniqueness is decided.

### Choosing the familiar quantity

The tempting wrong method is to choose the familiar quantity. "Percentage" appears, so the answer is a percentage. "Arrange" appears, so the answer is a permutation. "Average" appears, so the answer is a plain mean. The reply is to ask the question of the pair before the arithmetic: which unit, which weight, which order, which kind of distance, which precision and which demand. The vocabulary of the stem names the pair. The demand names the answer.

### Options built from neighbours

The six pairs account for a large share of the wrong options in mixed items. Options are often built as adjacent quantities: a points answer beside a per cent answer, a permutation beside a combination, a distance beside a displacement. The distinction is the decision, and the arithmetic usually takes less time than the decision. The disciplined reader writes the pair and the test on the card before the first number.

### Recall: six pairs

- Absolute change is in the unit of the quantity; relative change divides it by the base.
- Use a weighted average, built from totals, when the groups differ in size.
- Permutation counts ordered outcomes; combination counts unordered selections; the swap test decides.
- Distance is the path length; displacement is the net change in position; a closed loop has zero displacement.
- An estimate is decisive only when the option spacing allows it.
- A sufficiency question asks for uniqueness, not for the value.
- Each pair looks interchangeable in the stem and is not interchangeable in the answer.
- The stem's vocabulary names the pair; the demand names the answer.

### Lesson 6 practice

**MCQ 15**

The share of candidates who passed rose from 20 per cent last year to 35 per cent this year. By what percentage did the share rise in relative terms?

- A. 42.9 per cent
- B. 15 per cent
- C. 75 per cent
- D. 175 per cent

**MCQ 15 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Forty-two point nine per cent is 15 divided by the new share of 35. The relative change must use the original share as its base.
- **B — Incorrect:** Fifteen per cent is the rise in percentage points, not the relative rise. The two measures answer different questions.
- **C — Correct:** The absolute rise is 35 - 20 = 15 percentage points. Relative to the original base of 20, it is 15 / 20 = 0.75, which is 75 per cent.
- **D — Incorrect:** One hundred and seventy-five per cent is 35 divided by 20, the new share over the old. It reverses the base and gives a ratio, not the change.

**MCQ 16**

In how many distinct ways can a committee of three be formed from six people, where the order of members does not matter?

- A. 120 committees, counting the order of members
- B. 60 committees, halving the ordered count
- C. 15 committees, choosing two members instead of three
- D. 20 committees, with the order of members ignored

**MCQ 16 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** One hundred and twenty is 6 x 5 x 4, the permutations of three people from six. It counts orders, and the question says the order does not matter.
- **B — Incorrect:** Sixty is the ordered count halved. Halving is not the correction for order, because each committee of three corresponds to six orderings, not two.
- **C — Incorrect:** Fifteen is the number of pairs chosen from six. It uses two members instead of three, which is a wrong reading of the committee size.
- **D — Correct:** The number of combinations of three from six is 6 x 5 x 4 / (3 x 2 x 1) = 20, because each committee is counted once, whatever the order.

**MCQ 17**

A person walks 16 km east, then 15 km north, then 8 km west. Which pair correctly gives the distance covered and the displacement from the start?

- A. 39 km of path, and 17 km of displacement
- B. 39 km of path, and 39 km of displacement
- C. 17 km of path, and 39 km of displacement
- D. 39 km of path, and 0 km of displacement

**MCQ 17 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The path is 16 + 15 + 8 = 39 km. The net position is 8 km east and 15 km north of the start, so the displacement is the straight-line distance, 17 km.
- **B — Incorrect:** Thirty-nine kilometres of displacement treats the path length as the net change in position. It ignores the cancellation of the east and west legs.
- **C — Incorrect:** Seventeen kilometres of path is the displacement reported as the distance, and thirty-nine is the path reported as the displacement. The two quantities have been swapped.
- **D — Incorrect:** Zero displacement is the value for a closed loop. The walk does not return to the start, which is 17 km away.

## Lesson 7 - Verification: units, magnitude, parity, boundaries, plug-back, counterexamples and completeness

### Visual: eight cheap checks, each with the question it answers

```text
CHECK                 QUESTION IT ANSWERS                          COST
unit                  does the answer have the asked unit?          seconds
magnitude             is the order of size plausible?               seconds
parity or unit digit  does the integer property agree?              seconds
range or boundary     is the value inside the allowed interval?     seconds
plug-back             does it satisfy every condition in the stem?  a few lines
counterexample        is there one case that breaks the claim?      a few lines
independent method    does a second route give the same result?     the option check
completeness          was every stated condition used or excluded?  a re-read of the stem
```

The list is ordered by cost, not by importance. The cheap checks are run on every item, and the expensive ones are run when the cheap checks fail or when the answer is decisive. A check that could fail is worth more than a check that must pass. Ask of each check: what would the answer look like if it were wrong?

### A verification is an attempt to break the answer

A verification is an attempt to break your answer. A good check is one that a wrong answer is likely to fail. Re-doing the same calculation rarely catches a wrong answer, because the same mistake is repeated. A different question, asked of the answer, catches it: does the answer have the right unit, is its size plausible, does it satisfy the sentence that produced it?

Think of a proofreader who reads the paragraph backwards. The meaning is no longer carried by the sentence, so the eye sees the letters, and the mistakes stand out. A plug-back does the same for an equation. It stops the mind from trusting the algebra and forces the sentence to be read again.

### Named method: the one-fail rule

Run at least one cheap check on every answer. If it fails, do not adjust the answer until it passes. Return to the representation or the demand, because a failed check means that one of the earlier stages produced the wrong object. The one-fail rule is the operating form of the verification ladder.

- **Where it fails.** The rule fails when a passing check is taken as a proof of the whole answer. A unit check does not prove the number. A magnitude check does not prove the exact value. Use the check that matches the error most likely in this family.

### Worked example: plug-back into every condition

The stem says: a number, when doubled and then reduced by 4, equals the number increased by 6. Let x be the number. Then 2(x - 4) = x + 6, so 2x - 8 = x + 6, and x = 14.

Plug-back: 2(14 - 4) = 2 x 10 = 20, and 14 + 6 = 20. Both sides agree, and the sentence is satisfied in its own words.

- **Boundary.** The plug-back checks only the conditions that were written in the stem. If the stem said the number must be a whole number less than ten, the value 14 would fail the second condition. Plug-back must test every condition, not only the equation.
- **It breaks when.** A plug-back that substitutes into the wrong equation, such as the algebra that the candidate has already simplified, verifies the error. The check must return to the sentence.

### Worked example: units and magnitude

A car covers 15 km on each litre of fuel. The question asks how many litres a 450 km journey needs. A candidate who multiplies 450 by 15 reports 6750 litres.

The unit check asks whether the answer is in litres. It is, so the unit check passes. The magnitude check asks whether a 450 km journey at this rate can need several thousand litres. It cannot: about one litre is used per 15 km, so 450 km needs about 450 / 15 = 30 litres. The magnitude check catches the inversion of the ratio, which the unit check could not catch.

- **Where it stops.** Magnitude checks are sharp only when the order of size is known in advance. For a quantity whose plausible range is wide, a magnitude check is weak and the exact route is needed.
- **Breaks down when.** The unit check passes whenever the unit word is copied from the question. It fails to detect a wrong number with the right unit.

### Parity and unit digit: an example and its failure condition

Is 37 x 46 + 9 odd or even? Parity follows from the parts without computing the value. 37 is odd and 46 is even, so their product is even. An even number plus 9, which is odd, gives an odd result. Check: 37 x 46 = 1702, and 1702 + 9 = 1711, which is odd.

The unit digit of a product depends only on the unit digits of the factors. For 6 x 7 x 8, the unit digits multiply: 6 x 7 = 42, which ends in 2, and 2 x 8 = 16, which ends in 6. Check: 6 x 7 x 8 = 336.

- **Limit.** Parity and unit digits test the last digit or the parity only. They cannot confirm the full value, so they are a filter, not a proof.
- **Failure condition.** Adding the unit digits instead of multiplying them is the common slip. For the same product, 6 + 7 + 8 = 21 gives unit digit 1, which is wrong.

### Worked example: parity and boundaries

An item asks for the number of integers between 10 and 50 that are divisible by 6, and the candidate reports 8. Parity and boundary checks are fast. The multiples of 6 from 12 to 48 are 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, which is 7 values. The boundary check asks whether the endpoints are included, and the stem says "between" with 10 and 50 excluded in this reading. The candidate's count of 8 included a multiple outside the interval or miscounted the list. The parity-like check here is a list of endpoints, which takes seconds and confirms the count.

- **Boundary.** A boundary check depends on the word "between", "from", "to" and "inclusive", which may be read differently. If the stem's wording is ambiguous, state the reading used in the working.

### Range and probability bounds

A probability lies between zero and one. A share of a whole lies between zero and one hundred per cent. A count cannot be negative and cannot exceed the total. Suppose a candidate reports that 11 of 8 candidates passed. The count exceeds the total, so the bound check fails before any other work. The check is cheap because it names the impossible value without needing the correct one.

- **Where it stops.** A bound check finds only impossible values. A value inside the range can still be wrong, so the other checks still apply.
- **Where it fails.** The bound check is misapplied to a quantity that is not bounded, such as a rupee change, which may be negative and is valid.

### Worked example: a counterexample defeats a universal claim

A claim says that the sum of two odd numbers is always divisible by 4. One counter-case is enough: 1 + 3 = 4 is divisible by 4, but 1 + 5 = 6 is not. The claim fails with a single pair, and the check is faster than any proof of the opposite.

- **Limit.** A counterexample defeats a universal claim, not an existence claim. To show that some pair has a sum divisible by four, one example is enough; to show that all pairs do, every case must be checked.

- **Failure condition.** A counterexample check fails when the counter-case does not satisfy every condition of the stem, so it defeats a claim the stem never made.

### Worked example: independent method and completeness

A long calculation gives a result of 1,437 for a quantity. The options are 1,400, 1,437, 1,470 and 1,500. A second method, such as an estimate of 1,440, lands nearest to 1,437, which confirms the result. The second method is cheaper than repeating the first.

Completeness is the last check. Re-read the stem and mark each condition as used or irrelevant. A candidate who finds a clean answer but has not used one condition should suspect that the answer is for a different question.

- **Failure condition.** The second method fails when it shares the first method's error, such as recomputing with the same wrong representation. Completeness fails when a stated condition is neither used nor marked irrelevant, which is how a clean answer to a different question slips through.

### Trusting a method that felt right

The tempting wrong method is to trust the answer because the method felt correct. Confidence in a method is not evidence about this answer, because most mixed-item errors occur in the transcription of a single number. The reply is one cheap check on every answer. It costs seconds, and it catches the errors that are easiest to make.

A second tempting method is to repeat the calculation to check it. The repeat often makes the same mistake, because the representation that produced the error is still in place. The reply is to change the question you ask of the answer, not the arithmetic you use to produce it.

### Where checks pay off

Verification is what separates a candidate who reaches the right option by chance from one who reaches it by a process. On a timed paper the cheap checks are seconds, and they are performed on every item the candidate marks. The expensive checks, such as a full plug-back of a multi-step answer or a counterexample search, belong to items that remain uncertain after the cheap checks. An answer that fails a cheap check is parked or recomputed, not defended.

### Recall: checks

- Run at least one cheap check on every answer you mark: unit, magnitude, parity, boundary or plug-back.
- A failed check means an earlier stage went wrong. Return to the representation or the demand.
- A plug-back must test every condition in the stem, not only the equation.
- A magnitude check is sharp only when the plausible order of size is known.
- The unit check does not prove the number; the magnitude check does not prove the value.
- One counterexample defeats a universal claim, but an existence claim needs one example.
- A second method should be cheaper than the first, such as an estimate that lands nearest to the result.
- Completeness: mark every stated condition as used or irrelevant before you finish.
- Re-doing the same calculation rarely catches the same error; asking a different question of the answer does.

**MCQ 18**

Which value of x satisfies the sentence: half of x, increased by eight, equals three-quarters of x?

- A. -32
- B. 32
- C. 16
- D. 8

**MCQ 18 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Minus thirty-two results from changing the sign of eight: x/2 - 8 = 3x/4 gives -32. The sentence says increased by eight, so the plug-back fails: half of -32 is -16, and -16 + 8 = -8, while three-quarters of -32 is -24.
- **B — Correct:** Multiplying by four gives 2x + 32 = 3x, so x = 32. Plug-back: half of 32 is 16, increased by eight gives 24, and three-quarters of 32 is 24.
- **C — Incorrect:** Sixteen reads half as a quarter: x/4 + 8 = 3x/4 gives 16. Plug-back: half of 16 is 8, plus eight gives 16, but three-quarters of 16 is 12.
- **D — Incorrect:** Eight reads three-quarters as one and a half times the number: x/2 + 8 = 3x/2 gives 8. Plug-back: half of 8 is 4, plus eight gives 12, but three-quarters of 8 is 6.

**MCQ 19**

A tank holds 4800 litres. A tap delivers 60 litres per minute into an empty tank. How long does the tank take to fill, in minutes?

- A. 8 minutes
- B. 120 minutes
- C. 80 minutes
- D. 800 minutes

**MCQ 19 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Eight minutes divides the volume by 600, which treats the rate as ten times larger. The magnitude check fails: a tap at 60 litres per minute cannot fill 4800 litres in eight minutes.
- **B — Incorrect:** One hundred and twenty minutes divides the volume by 40 litres per minute, a misread rate. The tank would then fill more slowly than the tap allows.
- **C — Correct:** 4800 / 60 = 80 minutes, which is one hour and twenty minutes, an order of size consistent with the rate.
- **D — Incorrect:** Eight hundred minutes divides the volume by six litres per minute, which misreads the rate. The plug-back against the stated rate fails: 800 x 6 = 4800 only at six litres a minute, not sixty.

**MCQ 20**

Which of the following can be the probability of an event?

- A. 5/4
- B. 7/5
- C. 9/4
- D. 3/8

**MCQ 20 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Five-quarters is the odds of five to four read as a probability. A probability cannot exceed one.
- **B — Incorrect:** Seven-fifths would need seven favourable cases out of five total cases. The value exceeds one, so it fails the range check.
- **C — Incorrect:** Nine-quarters treats nine favourable outcomes as if they were out of four total outcomes. The favourable count exceeds the total.
- **D — Correct:** Three-eighths lies between zero and one. It is a valid probability for three favourable outcomes out of eight equally likely outcomes.

**MCQ 21**

A purse holds 16 coins, all of five rupees or ten rupees, with a total value of 110 rupees. The purse also weighs 80 grams, which the question does not need. How many ten-rupee coins are there?

- A. 6
- B. 10
- C. 11
- D. 22

**MCQ 21 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** Let f be the number of five-rupee coins and t the number of ten-rupee coins. Then f + t = 16 and 5f + 10t = 110. Substituting f = 16 - t gives 80 - 5t + 10t = 110, so t = 6. Plug-back: ten coins of five rupees and six of ten rupees make 50 + 60 = 110, and there are 16 coins in all. The weight is an irrelevant given, and marking it as irrelevant is part of the completeness check.
- **B — Incorrect:** Ten is the number of five-rupee coins. The output names the wrong group of coins, which the completeness check would have caught.
- **C — Incorrect:** Eleven is 110 divided by ten. It treats every coin as a ten-rupee coin, which ignores the five-rupee coins entirely.
- **D — Incorrect:** Twenty-two is 110 divided by five. It treats every coin as a five-rupee coin, which ignores the ten-rupee coins entirely.

## Lesson 8 - Time, passes and risk: the three-pass routine, caps, parking and the expected-value rule

CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the three passes and their caps

```text
CLASSIFY        10-15 s   identify the family and dominant mechanism, or park
PASS 1          <= 60 s   harvest direct items; mark only a complete method
PASS 2          <= 120 s  multi-step quant, arrangements, DI and sufficiency
PARK            at a cap  note the number and reason: method, representation,
                          arithmetic or live options; move on
FINAL REVIEW    last 10 min  parked items, transcription of marks, and only
                             elimination-backed decisions
```

The caps are operating discipline. They are not an official timing rule, and they are not a promise that every item fits. They are the limits at which a stubborn item stops taking the time of items that are still available to be harvested. Parking is the decision that protects the rest of the paper.

### Time as a shared resource

Time in a qualifying paper is a shared resource. Every minute spent on an item that will not yield is a minute taken from items that would have yielded. The candidate who solves one hard item completely and runs out of time for three direct items has made a bad trade, even though the hard item was correct. The rule is not that hard items are unimportant. It is that a capped item that is not yielding has a higher cost than its mark is worth at that moment.

### Named method: cap, park, note

When an item reaches its cap, the candidate does three things and no more. Write down the item's number. Write down one reason from the set: method, representation, arithmetic or live options. Move on. The return to a parked item is planned for the final review, not made in the middle of the paper.

- **It breaks when.** The method fails when the candidate parks without a reason, or when the candidate returns at once to the same item with the same approach. A park with no reason is a shrug. A park with a reason tells the candidate what to change when the item returns.

### Doctrine: the expected value of an attempt

Under the paper's marking, as the current marking rules set it out, a correct answer carries 2.5 marks, a wrong answer loses one-third of a correct mark (5/6), and a blank costs nothing. Recheck the current notification before relying on these figures. With a success probability p, the expected value of an attempt is:

```text
EV(p) = p x 2.5  -  (1 - p) x 5/6
EV = 0 when p = (5/6) / (2.5 + 5/6) = 1/4
```

A blind guess among four options has an expected value of zero. After one elimination, a guess among three has p = 1/3, and its expected value is positive. After two eliminations, a guess among two has p = 1/2, and its expected value is positive by a larger margin. Elimination is therefore what turns a guess into a positive-expectation choice. The guess is made after the justified elimination, never before.

- **Boundary.** The expected-value rule assumes that the candidate's estimate of p is honest. A candidate who believes an elimination has removed an option when it has not will overestimate p. The rule is only as good as the elimination it follows.

### Worked example: the cap sum and the time left

A paper has 80 items and 120 minutes. A candidate spends 50 minutes in pass 1, reserves 15 minutes for the final review, and parks 30 items for pass 2, each with a cap of 120 seconds. The cap sum for pass 2 is 30 x 120 seconds = 3600 seconds = 60 minutes. The time left is 120 - 50 - 15 = 55 minutes. Pass 2 can take at most 55 minutes, because the binding limit is the time left, not the sum of the caps. Every subtraction here is in minutes: items are never taken away from minutes.

- **Where it stops.** The cap sum is a plan, not a guarantee. If pass 1 runs over its time, the time left shrinks, and the pass 2 caps must shrink with it.
- **Breaks down when.** Planning by the cap sum alone reaches the final review with no time left, and the review is then skipped.

### Advanced Part C: the timed mini-drill protocol

This part is Advanced. It describes a practice block that trains the routine. Use a block of ten mixed items. Spend 10 to 15 seconds classifying each item without solving it. Then take a second pass: 60 seconds for each direct item and 120 seconds for each selected hard item. Write the stage of failure beside every miss, using these six stages: classification, extraction, representation, execution, verification or decision. Park any item at its cap, and keep the last ten minutes of a full paper for review. The protocol trains the routine, and it does not describe an official timing rule.

### Worked example: a ten-item block read through its tags

A block of ten mixed items is attempted with the protocol. Classification takes about two minutes in total. Six items are answered within their caps. Four are missed, and the tags are: classification 1, extraction 2, execution 1. The largest tag is extraction, so the next block starts with condition-underlining, not with more of the same family.

- **Limit.** A block of ten is small. One tag can change the picture, so repairs should follow the tags of several blocks, not one.
- **Failure condition.** The protocol fails when the tags are written after re-solving each miss. Re-solving changes the account of what went wrong, and the stage tag then describes the second attempt instead of the first error.

### Guessing before eliminating

The tempting wrong method is to treat the average time per item as a command. Two hours for eighty items averages to ninety seconds, and it is tempting to run every item to ninety seconds. The average is an arithmetic fact about the paper, not an instruction for a single item. Direct items finish in less than a minute, and hard items need more. The reply is the per-item cap and the three passes, which allocate time by item type.

A second tempting method is to guess before eliminating, on the grounds that a guess is free. The reply is the expected-value rule: a blind guess among four has zero mean, so it adds variance and no expected marks, and it carries the risk of a negative result when the candidate's state is uncertain.

### What the caps are for

The paper rewards selection. A candidate who classifies quickly, harvests the direct items, parks with reasons and returns for a disciplined final review will usually outscore a candidate who works every item fully in the order printed. The routine is simple, but it needs practice: the caps are learnt by timing practice sets, not by reading about them.

### Recall: time and risk

- The three passes are harvest, grind and decide; each has a cap, and the caps are operating discipline.
- Classify in 10 to 15 seconds; park with a reason when the family is not visible.
- Write a park down as a number and a reason: method, representation, arithmetic or live options.
- Plan pass 2 by the time left, not only by the sum of its caps.
- A blind guess among four has zero expected value; one elimination makes a guess positive.
- Guess only after justified elimination, and only when the expected value is positive.
- Recheck the current notification before relying on the marks and penalty figures.
- The average of ninety seconds per item is not a command for each item.
- The final review is for parked items, transcription checks and elimination-backed decisions.

**MCQ 22**

A question has four options. One option is eliminated by a sound argument, and the candidate chooses among the remaining three. A correct answer scores 2.5 marks, and a wrong answer costs one-third of the marks of a correct answer. What is the expected value of the guess?

- A. 0 marks, the value of a blind guess among four
- B. 5/18 of a mark, the value with three options left
- C. 5/6 of a mark, the value with two options left
- D. 1.25 marks, half the value of a sure correct answer

**MCQ 22 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Zero is the expected value of a blind guess among four options, where p = 1/4. The elimination raises the chance to one third, which makes the guess positive.
- **B — Correct:** With p = 1/3, the expected value is (1/3) x 2.5 - (2/3) x (5/6) = 5/6 - 5/9 = 5/18 of a mark.
- **C — Incorrect:** Five-sixths is the expected value with two options left, where p = 1/2. Only one option was eliminated here, so the chance is one third.
- **D — Incorrect:** One and a quarter marks is half of the reward for a sure answer. It halves the reward without the chance of a wrong answer, and it is not the expected value of the guess.

**MCQ 23**

A paper has 60 items and 90 minutes. A candidate uses 40 minutes in pass 1, reserves 15 minutes for the final review, and parks 20 items for pass 2, each with a cap of 90 seconds. At most how many minutes can pass 2 take?

- A. 35 minutes, the time left alone, ignoring the cap sum
- B. 50 minutes, 90 minus 40 with no reserve
- C. 30 minutes, the cap sum of the parked items
- D. 75 minutes, 90 minus 15 with no pass 1 time

**MCQ 23 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Thirty-five is the time left after pass 1 and the reserve. The cap sum is smaller, so it is the binding limit, and planning by the time left alone overstates what pass 2 can use.
- **B — Incorrect:** Fifty is 90 minus 40. It forgets the 15-minute reserve for the final review, which is part of the budget.
- **C — Correct:** The cap sum is 20 x 90 seconds = 1800 seconds = 30 minutes. The time left is 90 - 40 - 15 = 35 minutes, so the binding limit is the smaller value, 30.

## Lesson 9 - Mixed-mechanism problems: ordered sub-mechanisms and the output test

Progress: Lesson 9 of 12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Ordered decomposition of mixed items

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: a mixed item as a relay of mechanisms

```text
STEM
  |
  v
SUB-MECHANISM 1  --object-->  SUB-MECHANISM 2  --object-->  SUB-MECHANISM 3
(e.g. a grid of                (e.g. a count of               (e.g. a ratio that
 positions)                     arrangements)                  scales the count)
  |                              |                              |
  +------------------------------+------------------------------+
                                 |
                                 v
                       OUTPUT TEST: is this the quantity asked?
```

A mixed item has an order. The output of each mechanism is the input of the next, and the first mechanism is the one that fixes what the others can see. The output test at the end is not a formality. It compares the result with the demand written on the card, and it catches the case in which a correct intermediate value has been reported as the answer.

### Why mixed items feel harder than they are

Mixed items look harder than they are because they combine two or three ordinary tasks, and the difficulty lies in the joins. A candidate who tries to solve all the tasks at once holds several objects in memory at the same time. A candidate who orders the tasks solves one, writes its output, and then uses that output as the next input. Ordering converts a juggling act into a relay.

### Named method: the dependency order

List the sub-mechanisms that the stem requires. For each pair, ask which one needs the other's output. Put the independent ones first only if their output is needed by the rest; otherwise they can be deferred. The order is a dependency order, not a reading order. A stem may present the ratio before the grid, but the grid may have to be built first, because the ratio depends on the seating.

- **Where it fails.** The method fails when two sub-mechanisms are treated as independent although one needs the other. A count computed before the constraint that limits it gives a number for the wrong set.

### Pattern 1: a grid and a count

Four friends, P, Q, R and S, sit in a row of four chairs. P sits at one end, and Q and R sit next to each other. The question asks how many seating orders satisfy both conditions.

The dependency order is the grid first. Put P at one end: two choices, the left or the right end. Then Q and R must be adjacent, so they form a block. In the three chairs left after P, the block can occupy two adjacent pairs, with two internal orders, and S takes the last chair. Count: 2 ends x 2 positions for the block x 2 internal orders = 8 arrangements that satisfy the adjacency and the end condition. The count is the second mechanism, and it depends on the grid's output.

- **Boundary.** The count of 8 uses the block method only for adjacent friends. If the condition were "Q and R are not adjacent", the block method would give the complement, and the count would change.
- **It breaks when.** A count taken before the end condition is applied includes orders that place P in the middle, which the stem excludes.

### Pattern 2: a rate with a ratio change

Two machines produce a batch in the ratio 2:3 of their output rates. The first machine is then given a 50 per cent increase in its rate, and the question asks for the change in the total time to finish the same batch.

The dependency order is the ratio first, because it fixes the rates. The rates are 2k and 3k, and the time to finish a batch of size B alone is set by the total rate. Then the increase applies to the first machine only, changing the total rate from 5k to 6k. The ratio of times is 5/6. The second mechanism is the percentage change, which is applied to the rate of one machine, not to the total.

- **Where it stops.** A 50 per cent rise in the first machine is not a 50 per cent rise in the total. The total rises by 1k of 5k, which is 20 per cent. Confusing the two gives a wrong time.
- **Breaks down when.** The method fails when the percentage is applied to the total rate instead of the machine it names.

### Pattern 3: a data table, a weighted average and a percentage-point difference

A table gives the pass percentage and the number of candidates in two centres. The question asks how the combined pass percentage compares with a simple average of the two percentages.

The dependency order is the totals first. Each centre's passes are its candidates times its percentage, and the combined percentage is the sum of passes over the sum of candidates. The simple average of the two percentages is the second quantity. The difference between them is a difference in percentage points, not a percentage change, and the output test asks which of the two is requested.

- **Limit.** The simple average equals the combined percentage only when the two centres have equal candidate numbers. Any unequal pair gives a different combined figure.
- **Failure condition.** Reporting a percentage-point difference as a relative change is the output error of Lesson 6, and it is common when the mixed item is long.

### Pattern 4: a conditional chain, then a sufficiency judgement

Three statements link a set of outcomes through "if ... then" relations. The question asks whether a fourth piece of information settles which outcome holds. The dependency order is the chain first: the arrows are composed, and the contrapositives are added, so that every licensed consequence is visible. The sufficiency judgement is the second mechanism. It asks whether the composed chain fixes a single outcome. The test is to look for a counter-case that keeps all arrows true while changing the outcome.

- **Boundary.** A chain that fixes a single outcome in one direction may not fix it in the other. A sufficiency judgement must test both directions where the question requires them.
- **Where it fails.** The judgement fails when the chain is composed in the wrong direction, because the converse of a link has been added as if it were licensed.

### Computing the closing quantity first

The tempting wrong method is to attack the item from its last sentence, which is often the question, and to compute the closing quantity directly. The result is a number that looks like an answer but depends on an intermediate that was never built. The reply is the dependency order: build the object that the question depends on first, and check the output test at the end.

A second tempting method is to keep the mechanisms in one's head. The reply is to write the output of each mechanism on the page before starting the next, which keeps the working readable under time pressure.

### Where mixed items sit in the booklet

Mixed items are the most common form of the harder items in the paper, because they test the joins between families. The dependency order is a fast way to see the join, and the output test is a fast way to avoid reporting an intermediate. In practice, the Advanced routine adds a few seconds to classification and saves minutes of wrong computation on items whose structure was misread.

### Recall: relays

- A mixed item is a relay: each mechanism's output is the next mechanism's input.
- Order the sub-mechanisms by dependency, not by the order in which the stem reads.
- Build the grid or the chain before counting or composing from it.
- Apply a percentage change to the quantity it names, not to a larger total.
- A simple average equals a weighted average only when the weights are equal.
- A percentage-point difference and a relative change answer different questions.
- Compose a chain in its licensed direction, and test both directions when the question asks for a unique outcome.
- The output test compares the final object with the demand written on the card.

## Lesson 10 - Cognitive load and robustness when the method is uncertain

Progress: Lesson 10 of 12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Cognitive load and robustness under uncertainty

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: what to do when the method is not yet clear

```text
STATE                                  RESPONSE
method known, arithmetic long      ->  estimate, or inspect options before full calculation
two options survive                ->  test the single condition that separates them
stem seems inconsistent            ->  re-read qualifiers, units and shared directions
diagram has many branches          ->  seek a forced conclusion or one counterexample
confidence remains low             ->  park the item and protect the time
```

Each row replaces a vague feeling with an action. The feeling of being stuck usually means that the option space has not yet been narrowed. The table asks which action narrows it fastest without spending the time of the rest of the paper.

### Why the mind stalls

Working memory is small. A candidate who holds five quantities, two conditions and a half-remembered rule in the head has already used most of the space for thought. Under time pressure the mind drops the least recent item, which is often the condition that mattered. External notation is a performance tool, not a sign of weakness. It moves the load from the head to the page, where it stays put.

Robustness is the second half of the same problem. When the method is uncertain, the aim is not to find the perfect method. It is to choose an action that keeps the option space shrinking, even when the path is not yet known.

### Part A: cognitive-load control

- **Chunk repeated information.** Write a repeated set of relations as a single symbol or table line, not as five sentences.
- **Write intermediate results.** Each intermediate value goes on the page, so that it can be reused and checked. Rehearsing a value in the head is the usual source of a dropped digit.
- **Standardise the diagram.** Use one form for each relation type: a rate table for work, a grid for positions, arrows for conditions. The consistency of the form lowers the cost of reading it.
- **Pause after the setup.** Before the first calculation, read the question once more and check that the output on the card matches what is being computed.
- **Separate solving from marking.** Write the chosen option letter only after the answer is verified. A value transcribed to the wrong option letter is an error that the verification step cannot catch if it is done before the transcription.

- **It breaks when.** Cognitive-load control fails when the notes become a second, unreadable mess. The test is whether a stranger could follow the working in thirty seconds.

### Worked example: the same working before and after standardising

Before standardising, the page reads: four totals appear in one sentence, a ratio is written in the margin, and the second calculation uses one of the totals while the third uses another. After two calculations the candidate cannot find the missing ratio.

After standardising, the page holds a table with one row for each total and one row for each ratio. The unit of every cell is written. The missing ratio is now a visible empty cell, and the candidate knows what to read before the next calculation.

- **Where it stops.** Standard forms help only when they match the information. A table for a relation that is really a sequence adds clutter.
- **Breaks down when.** Notes that grow into an unreadable second page defeat the purpose. The test is whether the working could be followed in thirty seconds by someone who did not write it.

### Part B: robustness under uncertainty

Each row of the visual is a state with a response. The following example teaches the second state, two options that survive the first tests.

Which two-digit numbers have digits that sum to 9 and are divisible by 4? The two-digit numbers whose digits sum to 9 are the multiples of nine from 18 to 90. Of these, the multiples of 4 are 36 and 72. Two candidates survive. The condition that separates them is the next one in the stem: if the number is less than 50, 36 is the only survivor, and 72 is eliminated.

The response is not to guess between 36 and 72. It is to find the condition that distinguishes them and test only that condition. The item is then decided by one check, not by a second round of the whole problem.

- **Limit.** Two survivors can also be separated by a condition that appears only in an option, such as "a multiple of 9". The distinguishing condition may not be in the first sentence of the stem, so re-read the whole stem before testing.

### A stem that seems inconsistent

Worked example: in a hall, each row holds at most eight chairs. There are four rows, and the hall is said to hold at least forty chairs. Four rows of at most eight chairs hold at most 32 chairs, which is less than forty. The qualifiers conflict. Re-reading finds no reading that removes the conflict, so the item is parked, not solved by guessing a fifth row.

- **Failure condition.** Forcing a number through an inconsistent stem, for example by assuming a fifth row, produces an answer to a question that was never asked.

Sometimes two clauses appear to conflict. The response is to read the qualifiers first. "At least", "at most", "exactly", "only" and "except" change the meaning of a clause. A unit mismatch, a shared direction that applies to several items, or a stated range that changes the domain can each make a stem look inconsistent when it is not. A stem that remains inconsistent after the qualifiers have been read is a stem to park, not to solve by guessing the intended meaning.

### A diagram with many branches

Worked example: three people, A, B and C, each sit in one of three chairs. A is not in chair 1, and B is in chair 3. B's chair is fixed, so A must be in chair 2, the only remaining chair that is not chair 1. C takes chair 1. The arrangement is forced in two steps, and the six possible orders need not be listed.

- **Where it fails.** Listing every order blindly wastes time, and assuming a forced step without its premise, for example placing A in chair 3, invents a conclusion the stem does not support.

A diagram with several branches tempts the candidate to enumerate every branch. The response is to look for a forced conclusion first: one relation that fixes a position, or one counterexample that defeats a claim. The counterexample is cheaper than the complete enumeration, and it often resolves the item at once.

### Holding everything in the head

The tempting wrong method is to keep working on the stuck item until the method appears. The reply is that the method does not reliably appear under pressure. The response table converts the stuck state into a specific action, such as a single distinguishing condition, and the action either narrows the choice or sends the item to the parked list with a reason.

A second tempting method is to write everything down, including the working that is not needed. The reply is that the page must stay readable. Write the intermediate values that will be reused and the relations that will be tested, and leave out the rest.

### Pressure on the page

Under the pressure of the paper, cognitive load is the most common silent cause of an error. The candidate knows the method, but the representation has become unreadable, and a condition is dropped. The discipline is to keep one page of structure, standard forms and labelled intermediate values. The robustness rule is then applied to the items that are not yet decided.

### Recall: load and robustness

- Chunk repeated information into a table or a symbol.
- Write each intermediate result so that it can be reused and checked.
- Use one standard form for each relation type.
- Re-read the question once after the setup and before the first calculation.
- Separate the solving from the marking of the option letter.
- When two options survive, test the single condition that separates them.
- When a stem looks inconsistent, read the qualifiers, units and shared directions before concluding anything.
- When a diagram branches, look for a forced conclusion or a counterexample.
- When confidence remains low, park the item and protect the time.

## Lesson 11 - Error analytics: diagnosing the stage that failed and designing mixed practice

Progress: Lesson 11 of 12 | Stage: Advanced, with a Core diagnostic table | Subtopic: Stage diagnosis, error logs and mixed-practice design

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: six stages, one tag per miss

```text
STAGE            THE FAILURE                                  THE FIRST REPAIR
classification   chose the wrong family or method            timed identification before any calculation
extraction       missed a condition or qualifier             underline quantities and logical words
representation   wrong equation, grid, table or tree         rebuild from the stem before calculating
execution        arithmetic or logical slip                  smaller steps and intermediate checks
verification     did not test the result                    a plug-back or magnitude check on every answer
decision         over-attempted or abandoned too early       confidence and time log
```

The table is the diagnostic. Every miss receives exactly one stage tag, and the repair follows the tag. A candidate who repairs every miss with the same habit, such as more practice of the same family, is treating a single symptom with a single remedy, and the stage tags show when that is wrong.

### Why a score is not a diagnosis

A raw score is a sum of many causes, and it hides the cause. Two candidates can both score sixty per cent and fail for different reasons. One has a method problem that will not improve without concept work. The other has a reading problem that will improve quickly with a condition-underlining habit. The score is the same. The stage is not.

### Named method: stage tagging

After each practice block, go back to every miss and write one stage tag beside it. Do not re-solve the item in the margin. Ask only where the first wrong object appeared: a wrong family, a missed condition, a wrong representation, a slip in a calculation, an unchecked result, or an item that was attempted or abandoned at the wrong moment. Count the tags. The stage with the largest count is the first repair.

- **It breaks when.** Tagging fails when the candidate tags by the feeling of the error rather than by the first wrong object. A wrong answer produced by a correct method and a transcription slip is an execution tag, not a method tag.

### Worked log: a block of ten misses

Suppose a candidate misses ten items in a block and tags them as follows: classification 1, extraction 4, representation 2, execution 1, verification 1 and decision 1. The largest count is at extraction. The repair is to underline the quantities and the logical words before any calculation, and to run a short block that tests condition-listing alone. Practising the same family again would not repair the stage that failed.

- **Boundary.** A block of ten is small. A single tag can change the picture, so the log should be read across several blocks before the repair is changed.
- **Breaks down when.** The log misleads when the tags are not written at the time of the miss. Reconstructed tags are often biased toward the stage that the candidate expects to be weak.

### Advanced: the design of a mixed practice block

This part is Advanced. A useful mixed practice block has five elements, and each has a function.

- **Rapid classification without solving.** The candidate names the family and the dominant mechanism of each item in ten to fifteen seconds, and writes nothing else. This trains the first decision of the paper.
- **One representative item from each weak mechanism.** The weakest mechanisms are targeted, rather than the whole syllabus being repeated.
- **A timed mixed block.** The block applies the caps from Lesson 8 and the passes of the paper.
- **Error reconstruction without the solution.** After the block, the candidate reconstructs each miss without looking at the solution, to test whether the method is held or only remembered.
- **A second attempt after a delay.** The same items are attempted again after a gap, to measure retention and transfer rather than immediate recall.

Worked example: a candidate builds a block of ten items from three weak mechanisms, two items each, and four items from the earlier lessons. Classification takes two minutes. The timed block is attempted under the caps and produces four misses. Each miss is reconstructed from memory without the solution and tagged. A retest a day later uses the same four mechanisms with new numbers, which measures retention rather than recall of the same items.

- **Failure condition.** A block that adds difficulty without a classification routine trains panic rather than transfer. The block then rewards luck on a few items and hides the stage that failed.

The purpose of the design is transfer and decision quality, not difficulty. A block that is harder but unstructured trains the candidate to panic.

### Practice progression: five gates in order

Foundation to timed practice follows five gates. Gate 1 is recognition: classify items in fifteen seconds without solving. Gate 2 is untimed representation: build the table or grid before any calculation. Gate 3 is a timed single family. Gate 4 is a timed mixed block under the caps of Lesson 8. Gate 5 is a delayed retest, with reconstruction of each miss from memory.

Worked example: a candidate passes Gate 1 but tags most misses as extraction at Gate 3, because condition-listing is slow. The repair goes back to Gate 2, where the constraints are underlined and listed with no timer. Gate 3 is then repeated, and only after it is passed does the candidate enter the timed mixed block.

- **Where it fails.** Moving to the timed mixed block before Gates 2 and 3 are passed makes the timer measure the wrong stage. The score falls, and the candidate blames speed when the fault is the representation.

### Repairing the familiar family

The tempting wrong method is to measure progress only by the raw score. A rising score feels like proof of improvement, and a stable score feels like a plateau. The reply is that the stage tags show what changed. A rising score with the same extraction tags may hide a lucky set. A stable score with a falling extraction count is real progress that the score has not yet shown.

A second tempting method is to repair the most frequent family, whatever its stage. The reply is the stage tag. The family may be frequent because the candidate's classification is weak, and the repair then belongs to Lesson 1's habit, not to the family's drill.

### The paper gives one number

The paper gives a single score, and the candidate receives no stage tags. The discipline must therefore be added by the candidate. A candidate who tags every miss, reads the tags after each block and changes the repair when the tags change will improve faster than a candidate who only counts the score. The diagnostic table is part of the preparation for the paper, not an optional extra.

### Recall: stage tags

- Each miss receives one stage tag: classification, extraction, representation, execution, verification or decision.
- The repair follows the largest tag, not the most frequent family.
- Tag at the time of the miss, from the first wrong object, not from the feeling of the error.
- A correct method with a transcription slip is an execution tag.
- A raw score hides the stage; read the tags alongside the score.
- Read the tags across several blocks before changing the repair.
- A mixed practice block should include rapid classification, targeted items, a timed block, reconstruction and a delayed second attempt.
- Measure transfer and decision quality, not only immediate recall.

- D. decision: the item was parked, then reported unchecked

**MCQ 29 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** Total divided by five was the right method. One value entered wrongly makes the total 310 and the average 62 instead of 60. The stage is execution, because a given was carried wrongly into the calculation.
- **B — Incorrect:** The item is a plain average of five scores. No group sizes are needed, so the classification was correct.
- **C — Incorrect:** Three hundred and ten divided by five gives 62, so the candidate used a total, not a list. The error is a wrong number, not a wrong representation.

**MCQ 30**

A candidate travels two equal distances, one at 30 km/h and one at 60 km/h, and reports the average speed as 45 km/h. Which stage explains the error?

- A. decision: a guessed value was entered without working
- B. representation: the legs were averaged as if equal in time
- C. extraction: the speeds were misread as 35 and 65 km/h
- D. verification: the answer was checked by a plug-back and accepted

**MCQ 30 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** The value 45 is the arithmetic mean of 30 and 60, which is a systematic computation and not a guess. The stage is representation, not decision.
- **B — Correct:** The mean of 30 and 60 is 45, and it assumes the two legs take equal time. With equal distances, the leg at 30 km/h takes twice as long as the leg at 60 km/h, and the average is 2 x 30 x 60 / (30 + 60) = 40 km/h. The representation stored the speeds as equal-time observations when the distances were equal.
- **C — Incorrect:** The speeds were stated as 30 and 60, and the calculation used them correctly. Misreading as 35 and 65 would give a mean of 50, not 45, so the extraction explanation does not match the value.
- **D — Incorrect:** A plug-back against the speeds would have shown a mismatch with the times, but the candidate did not perform one. Verification was skipped rather than failed, and the representation error is the stage that produced the value.

**MCQ 31**

How many three-digit whole numbers are multiples of 7?

- A. 142, counting every multiple of 7 from 7 up to the last three-digit one
- B. 14, counting from 7 to 98
- C. 128, counting from 105 to 994
- D. 900, counting all three-digit numbers

**MCQ 31 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** One hundred and forty-two counts every multiple of 7 from 7 onward. It includes the two-digit multiples from 7 to 98, which are not three-digit numbers.
- **B — Incorrect:** Fourteen counts the multiples of 7 from 7 to 98, which includes 7 itself, a one-digit number. The three-digit count excludes every one of them.
- **C — Correct:** The smallest three-digit multiple of 7 is 105 = 7 x 15, and the largest is 994 = 7 x 142. The count is 142 - 15 + 1 = 128.
- **D — Incorrect:** Nine hundred is the count of all three-digit whole numbers. It ignores the divisibility condition entirely.

**MCQ 32**

**Correct answer: D**

### Visual: the integrated route as a loop

```text
   CLASSIFY -> EXTRACT -> REPRESENT -> SELECT -> EXECUTE -> VERIFY -> DECIDE
      ^                                                          |
      |                                                          |
      +---- error log: stage tag -> repair -> retest under time <-+
```

The loop closes on the error log. A correct answer completes the route. A wrong answer sends the candidate back to the stage that failed, and the next retest checks that repair. The loop is the whole integration of this file, and the earlier lessons supply the content of each stage.

### Why the integration only shows in one item

The integration becomes visible only when a single item uses several stages in order. A candidate who has learned each stage in isolation may still fail a mixed item, because the joins between the stages are where the work is lost. This lesson takes one mixed item from its opening sentence to its decision, and it names each join explicitly.

### Worked transfer item: an average price with a percentage change

Shop A sells 40 units at 250 rupees each. Shop B sells 60 units at 200 rupees each, and then raises its price by 10 per cent. The question asks whether the average price per unit across both shops is above 220 rupees.

- **Classify.** The item is a weighted average, because the two shops have unequal numbers of units. A percentage change applies to one of the groups. The demand is a yes or no about a threshold.
- **Extract.** The output is a yes or no. The givens are the units and prices of each shop, and the change of 10 per cent applies to Shop B only.
- **Represent.** Use totals. Shop B's new price is 220 rupees. Shop A's revenue is 40 x 250 = 10,000 and Shop B's is 60 x 220 = 13,200, so the total revenue is 23,200 over 100 units.
- **Execute.** The threshold comparison avoids division: 220 x 100 = 22,000, and the actual total of 23,200 is larger. The answer is yes.
- **Verify.** The plug-back divides 23,200 by 100 to give an average of 232, which is above 220. A magnitude check confirms the order of size.

- **Where it stops.** The yes-or-no answer needs no exact average. If the threshold were 232 exactly, the total would equal the threshold, and the answer would be "not above". The comparison of totals decides both cases.
- **It breaks when.** Averaging 250, 200 and 220 as three numbers gives a plain mean that ignores the units. The representation error is the one that the totals prevent.

### Advanced Part C: the transfer check

This part is Advanced. A transfer check asks whether the method still works when one condition is changed. In the weighted-average item of Lesson 3, the threshold check compared 5800 with 55 x 100 and never divided. Changing the threshold to 58 gives 58 x 100 = 5800, which equals the total exactly. The mean is then exactly 58, and "above 58" is false. A candidate who tests the threshold by division would get 58 and must decide whether equality counts. The totals method decides the same question without the division, and it makes the equality case visible.

The transfer check is therefore a test of whether the representation survives a change in the question. The method is sound when the same representation gives the answer for the changed threshold, the equal case included.

- **Failure condition.** A transfer check fails when the changed threshold is treated as a strict inequality without testing the equal case, so a total of 5800 against a threshold of 58 is read as "above" when it is exactly equal.

### The readiness standard

You are ready for the integrated routine only if you can do each of the following on a timed set.

- Classify a mixed item into its dominant mechanism in fifteen seconds or less.
- Translate the stem into one relation, grid or table with labelled units.
- Choose a property or shortcut only when its condition holds in the stem.
- Distinguish necessity, sufficiency, possibility and certainty without prompting.
- Verify the answer by at least one cheap check on every item you mark.
- Park an item that exceeds its cap, with a reason, and return to it in the final review.
- Diagnose each miss by stage and route the repair to the stage that failed.

### Treating the readiness list as finished

The tempting wrong method is to treat the readiness list as a finished state, reached once and then forgotten. Under the paper's time pressure, each item in the list decays. The reply is the loop: the list is checked on every timed set, and the error log tells the candidate which item of the list has decayed.

A second tempting method is to skip the final integration and rely on the specialist lessons alone. The reply is that a mixed item uses the joins, and the joins are learnt only by practising mixed items under the routine.

### The routine on a live set

The routine is the same at every level of difficulty. A candidate who has the lessons but no timed mixed practice will know the methods without the decision habit. A candidate who runs the routine on every set, tags every miss and returns to the stage that failed will build the habit that the paper rewards. The final review at the end of the paper is the place where the habit pays off.

### Recall: the loop

- The integrated route is classify, extract, represent, select, execute, verify and decide, with the error log closing the loop.
- A mixed item is classified by its dominant question before any calculation.
- Use totals for weighted quantities, and compare with threshold totals when the question is a yes or no.
- A transfer check changes one condition and confirms that the representation still gives the answer.
- Equality with a threshold is a case the representation must still handle.
- The paper's item families map to specialist methods; the mapping is by question, not by label.
- The readiness standard is checked on every timed set, not once.
- Every mark is backed by one cheap check, and every miss by one stage tag.

**MCQ 36**

What is the sum of the odd numbers strictly between 10 and 26?

**MCQ 36 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

**MCQ 37**

A rule says that every file marked urgent is opened on the day it arrives. File F was not opened on the day it arrived. Which conclusion follows?

- A. File F is not marked urgent under this rule

# TIMED-SET PRACTICE AND SCORING RUBRIC

A timed set in this file is a block of ten mixed items drawn from the lessons, attempted under the caps of Lesson 8. Build the set by taking items from at least three different lessons, so that classification is required before any method can be chosen. Keep a timer, the three-pass order and the per-item caps. Score the set with the rubric below, and write a stage tag beside every miss.

## Scoring frame

The paper, as the paper's instruction page sets it out, has 80 objective items worth 200 marks. A correct answer carries 2.5 marks, a wrong answer loses 5/6 of a mark, and a blank costs nothing. The official qualifying minimum is 33 per cent, which is 66 of the 200 marks. Recheck the current notification before relying on any of these figures, because the scheme may change.

Keep two numbers apart. The qualifying minimum is the official rule. A personal target is a practice number that you choose to sit above the rule, and it is never presented as a cut-off.

The expected value of an attempt with success probability p is:

```text
EV(p) = p x 2.5  -  (1 - p) x 5/6
```

## Personal target (labelled, not an official cut-off)

A personal target is a practice number set by the learner. For example, 48 correct answers and 12 wrong answers give a net of 48 x 2.5 - 12 x 5/6 = 110 marks. Write it in your own log as "my practice target", never as a standard for the paper. Compare sets of the same type over time. The number is useful only as a personal benchmark.

## Time budgets by item type

| Item type | Budget per item | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Classification of any item | 10 to 15 seconds | Name the family and the dominant mechanism, or park. |
| Direct one-step item | up to 60 seconds | Mark only a complete method. |
| Two-stage or two-condition item | about 90 seconds | Write the representation before computing. |
| Multi-step, mixed or arrangement item | up to 120 seconds | Pass 2 cap; park at the cap with a reason. |
| Sufficiency item | about 90 seconds | Stop as soon as uniqueness is decided. |
| Final review | last 10 minutes | Parked items, mark transcription, elimination-backed decisions only. |

## Three-pass control routine

- **Pass 1, harvest.** Classify every item in ten to fifteen seconds. Mark the direct items you can finish inside the first-pass cap.
- **Pass 2, grind.** Return to the mixed, arrangement and sufficiency items. Write the representation first and compute only the output on the card.
- **Pass 3, decide.** Take the parked items. Eliminate what you can, apply the expected-value rule, and either guess among the survivors or leave the item blank.

## Error codes for post-test analysis

Write down every miss with one stage tag and, where it fits, the programme's error code.

| Stage tag | Programme code | First repair |
|---|---|---|
| Classification | C (concept gap) or A (application slip) | Timed identification of the family before any calculation. |
| Extraction | R (reading error) | Write the output on the card and underline the constraint words. |
| Representation | A (application slip) | Rebuild the table, grid or total from the stem. |
| Execution | X (calculation error) | Smaller steps and one intermediate check. |
| Verification | X (calculation error) | One cheap check on every marked answer. |
| Decision | T (time error) or G (guess error) | Apply the cap, park with a reason, guess only after elimination. |

Track the mix of tags, not only the count. A run of classification tags needs timed identification. A run of extraction tags needs condition-underlining. A run of decision tags needs the cap and the park routine.

## Scoring a set

- Count correct and wrong answers separately, and write down the minutes used.
- Marks per minute = (2.5 x correct - (5/6) x wrong) / minutes. Use it as a personal rate for comparing sets of the same kind.
- Compare each set with the one before it. A rising rate with a falling count of one stage tag shows the repair taking hold.

# REMEDIATION

Each entry names the common error, the repair and one retry prompt. Every retry answer was recomputed before it was written. Attempt the retry prompt before reading its answer.

## Lesson 1 (items 1 to 3)

The common error is classifying a mixed item by a visible keyword instead of its requested output. The repair is the first-read tree, asked in order, followed by the output written as a short phrase. Retry: twenty people like tea, fifteen like coffee and five like both. How many people like at least one of the two drinks? Answer: 30, from 20 + 15 - 5.

## Lesson 2 (items 4 to 5)

The common error is answering a neighbouring quantity, such as the larger share for a difference question, or a decision question answered with a value. The repair is the five-slot card, with the output slot filled first. Retry: an eraser and a pencil cost 15 rupees together, and the pencil costs 3 rupees more than the eraser. What does the eraser cost? Answer: 6 rupees, with the pencil at 9.

## Lesson 3 (items 6 to 9)

The common error is averaging two means, or applying a percentage to the wrong base. The repair is to store totals and labelled bases before computing. Retry: a group of 4 has mean 10 and a group of 6 has mean 20. What is the mean of all ten? Answer: 16, from (40 + 120) / 10.

## Lesson 4 (items 10 to 12)

The common error is using a shortcut whose condition does not hold, or testing an identity at one point and treating it as proved. The repair is the condition-preservation test. Retry: what is the sum of the odd numbers from 1 to 19? Answer: 100, because ten odd numbers starting at 1 sum to 10 squared.

## Lesson 5 (items 13 to 14)

The common error is accepting the converse or the inverse of a conditional, and confusing possibility with certainty. The repair is the direction test. Retry: if a number is a multiple of 10, then it is even. Does it follow that an odd number is not a multiple of 10? Answer: yes, it is the contrapositive, which the arrow licenses.

## Lesson 6 (items 15 to 17)

The common error is reporting a points difference as a relative change, or a path length as a displacement. The repair is to name the pair before the arithmetic. Retry: a value rises from 50 to 60. What is the absolute change, and what is the relative change? Answer: 10 units, and 20 per cent.

## Lesson 7 (items 18 to 21)

The common error is trusting an answer because the method felt right, or checking it by repeating the same calculation. The repair is one cheap check on every marked answer. Retry: is x = 7 a solution of 4x - 5 = 2x + 9? Answer: yes, because 4 x 7 - 5 = 23 and 2 x 7 + 9 = 23.

## Lesson 8 (items 22 to 23)

The common error is guessing before elimination, or planning pass 2 by the sum of its caps rather than by the time left. The repair is the expected-value rule and the time-left calculation. Retry: four options, two of them eliminated by a sound argument. A random pick is made among the remaining two. What is the chance that the pick is correct? Answer: one half.

## Lesson 9 (items 24 to 26)

The common error is applying a percentage to the total when it names one machine or group, or counting before the constraint that limits the count is applied. The repair is the dependency order. Retry: two taps fill a 36-litre tank in the ratio 1:2. Six litres are added to the first tap's share. What is the new ratio of the first to the second? Answer: 3:4, from 18:24.

## Lesson 10 (items 27 to 28)

The common error is guessing between two survivors without testing the condition that separates them, or dropping a qualifier. The repair is the distinguishing-condition test. Retry: which two-digit number has digits summing to 10, is a multiple of 4, and is greater than 50? Answer: 64, the only survivor of the first two conditions above 50 (28 is below 50).

## Lesson 11 (items 29 to 32)

The common error is repairing every miss with the same habit, because the raw score was read without the stage tags. The repair is the largest stage tag. Retry: a candidate misses six items: three at extraction, one at execution and two at decision. What share of the misses is at extraction? Answer: one half, from 3 / 6.

## Lesson 12 (items 33 to 35)

The common error is averaging the old and new groups as if they were equal in size, or treating the readiness list as a finished state. The repair is the totals method and the timed check on every set. Retry: five employees average 40 thousand rupees, and one more joins at 70 thousand. What is the new average? Answer: 45 thousand rupees, from (200 + 70) / 6.

## Cumulative items (36 to 39)

- **Item 36.** The error is to keep the lower run in a sum with a lower boundary. Retry: what is the sum of the odd numbers strictly between 20 and 30? Answer: 125, from 225 - 100.
- **Item 37.** The error is to take the converse of a rule as if it followed. Retry: every file marked urgent is opened on its arrival day. File G was opened on its arrival day. Which conclusion about urgency follows? Answer: none; G may or may not be urgent, so the claim that G is urgent is not licensed.
- **Item 38.** The error is to choose by the number of options, or to rely on a premise the stem does not state. Retry: item P has two live options, item Q has four, and each can be marked in time, with the marks as before. Which guess is better? Answer: P, with an expected value of 5/6 of a mark, against zero for Q.
- **Item 39.** The error is to take a change against the wrong base. Retry: a price of 50 rupees rises by 20 per cent, and then 5 rupees are deducted. What is the final price? Answer: 55 rupees.

# MASTER METHOD MAPS

## Map 1: from a stem to a family

```text
STEM -> output on the card (number, time, count, yes/no, truth value, rank)
   |
   +-- passage?                         -> passage method
   +-- communication?                   -> communication judgement
   +-- sufficiency of statements?       -> sufficiency decision
   +-- arrangement, code, direction?    -> relational reasoning
   +-- divisibility, digits, patterns?  -> number properties
   +-- ratio, %, average, money, age?   -> proportional reasoning
   +-- work, speed, time, shape?        -> rate, space and time
   +-- equation, inequality?            -> algebra
   no family after 15 s                 -> park with reason "method"
```

## Map 2: representation chooser

```text
repeated % change        -> multiplicative factors (1.10 x 0.90 = 0.99)
unequal groups           -> totals (size x mean), never a plain mean of means
relative shares          -> ratio or 100-unit base
work or motion           -> rate x time table
family                   -> tree
direction or turn        -> compass or coordinates
seating or order         -> slots or grid
conditions               -> arrows with contrapositive
overlap                  -> Venn or inclusion-exclusion
statements               -> separate tests: I, II, both
```

## Map 3: the least-work ladder

```text
property --> elimination --> estimation --> substitution --> full solution
  each rung: state the condition, check it in this stem, check option spacing
  one check at a point proves nothing; algebra proves an identity
```

## Map 4: the logical arrow

```text
A -> B
  allowed:   not B -> not A        (contrapositive)
  not allowed: B -> A              (converse)
  not allowed: not A -> not B      (inverse)
possibility: one case     certainty: every case     impossibility: no case
association: needs a mechanism that survives control of a common cause
```

## Map 5: six quantitative pairs

```text
absolute change ...... points or rupees (the unit of the quantity)
relative change ...... change / base (per cent)
average .............. equal weights only
weighted average ..... totals, size x mean
permutation .......... order matters (swap changes outcome)
combination .......... order ignored (swap gives same outcome)
distance ............. path length
displacement ......... net position (zero for a closed loop)
exact ................. when options are close
estimate .............. only when options are well separated
calculation ........... a value is asked
sufficiency ........... uniqueness is asked
```

## Map 6: the verification ladder

```text
unit -> magnitude -> parity or unit digit -> boundary -> plug-back
  -> counterexample -> independent method -> completeness
one fail: return to the representation or the demand, not to the arithmetic
```

## Map 7: time, passes and risk

```text
CLASSIFY 10-15 s -> PASS 1 <= 60 s -> PASS 2 <= 120 s -> PARK (reason) -> FINAL REVIEW 10 min
EV = p x 2.5 - (1 - p) x 5/6 ; EV = 0 at p = 1/4 (blind guess)
one elimination: p = 1/3 ; two eliminations: p = 1/2 ; guess only after elimination
pass 2 budget = min(sum of caps, time left after pass 1 and review)
```

## Map 8: mixed items and the stage tags

```text
STEM -> dependency order -> sub-mechanism 1 -> object -> sub-mechanism 2 -> object
     -> OUTPUT TEST (is this the quantity on the card?)
miss -> one stage tag: classification / extraction / representation / execution /
        verification / decision -> repair the largest tag -> retest under time
```

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

These notes compress the lessons into recall form. Each note states the rule and the item that tests it. Failure conditions are listed where the lesson states one. Advanced units are marked.

## Lesson 1: the umbrella and the first-read classification

- Chain: understand, represent, select, reason or compute, check, decide.
- Six mechanism families: number properties; proportional reasoning; rate, space and time; algebra and sufficiency; relational and symbolic reasoning; communication judgement. Passage items use the passage method.
- Classify by the requested output, not by a keyword; the same stem can change family when the output changes.
- Park after fifteen seconds with no visible family.
- Items 1 to 3 test an overlap versus a plain sum, a ratio with a fixed group, and a combined rate.

## Lesson 2: extraction, the requested output and completeness

- Five-slot card: output, givens, unknowns, constraints, irrelevant; the output is filled first.
- "How much more" usually wants a difference; "by what per cent" a relative change.
- Sufficiency is a decision output: test each statement alone, then together; stop at uniqueness; do not treat a square as one value.
- Items 4 and 5 test the output of a remaining count after a removal and a difference of a ratio share.

## Lesson 3: representation and totals (with the shape chooser)

- Totals rule: size times mean; a plain average of averages drops the weights.
- Repeated percentage changes multiply: 1.10 x 0.90 = 0.99, a net fall of 1 per cent.
- Other shapes (ratio on a 100-unit base, rate-time table, tree, coordinates, Venn, labelled table) each have an example and a failure condition in Part D.
- Items 6 to 9 test a weighted mean, a harmonic average speed, a totals update and a change applied as factors.

## Lesson 4: least work and shortcut safety (Core, with Advanced Part C)

- Ladder: property, elimination, estimation, substitution, full solution; each rung needs its condition.
- The sum of the first n odd numbers is n squared; the sum of the first n even numbers is n(n + 1); each holds only for its own run.
- An estimate is decisive only when the options are well separated; one point check does not prove an identity.
- Advanced Part C: direct invariant, elimination, smart values or scaling, compact algebra, full enumeration.
- Items 10 to 12 test an even-number sum, an expansion identity, and a decimal-decisive estimate.

## Lesson 5: logical distinctions

- A implies B: A is sufficient for B; B is necessary for A.
- Licensed: the contrapositive. Not licensed: the converse and the inverse.
- Possibility needs one valid case; certainty needs every valid case; impossibility needs that no case allows the outcome.
- Association is not cause without a mechanism that survives control of a common cause.
- Items 13 and 14 test the contrapositive and a possibility-versus-certainty claim.

## Lesson 6: quantitative distinctions

- Absolute change in points or units; relative change divides by the base.
- Weighted averages come from totals when groups differ in size.
- Permutations count ordered outcomes; combinations count unordered selections; the swap test decides.
- Distance is path length; displacement is net position; a closed loop has zero displacement.
- Items 15 to 17 test a relative change, a committee count and a distance versus a displacement.

## Lesson 7: verification

- Checks: unit, magnitude, parity and unit digit, boundary and probability range, plug-back, counterexample, independent method, completeness.
- One-fail rule: a failed check sends you back to the representation or the demand.
- Plug-back must test every stated condition.
- Items 18 to 21 test a plug-back of a fraction equation, a unit and magnitude check on a rate, a probability range and a completeness check.

## Lesson 8: time, passes and risk (Core, with Advanced Part C)

- Classify in 10 to 15 seconds; pass 1 up to 60 seconds; pass 2 up to 120 seconds; park with a reason; final review 10 minutes.
- The caps are operating discipline, not an official timing rule.
- The pass 2 budget is limited by the time left, not by the sum of the caps.
- Expected value after elimination: one elimination among four options gives one chance in three.
- Items 22 and 23 test the expected value after one elimination and the time limit for pass 2.

## Lesson 9: mixed-mechanism problems (Advanced)

- Dependency order: build the object that the others depend on first.
- The output test compares the result with the demand written on the card.
- Patterns: a grid then a count; a rate with a ratio change, where a percentage applies to one machine; a data table with a weighted average and a percentage-point difference; a conditional chain then a sufficiency judgement.
- Items 24 to 26 test a team count with a fixed composition, a corrected average and a hall with a percentage.

## Lesson 10: cognitive load and robustness (Advanced)

- Chunk, write intermediate results, standardise forms, pause after setup, separate solving from marking.
- Two survivors: test the one condition that separates them. An inconsistent stem: read the qualifiers, then park. A branching diagram: look for a forced step.
- Items 27 and 28 test a distinguishing condition among survivors and a product-and-sum pair.

## Lesson 11: error analytics, progression and mixed practice (Advanced, with Core diagnostic table)

- Six stage tags: classification, extraction, representation, execution, verification, decision.
- Tag at the time of the miss, from the first wrong object; repair the largest tag.
- Five practice gates in order: recognition, untimed representation, timed single family, timed mixed block, delayed retest.
- Items 29 and 30 test stage identification; item 32 tests a share of misses by stage; item 31 is a count of multiples, not an error item.

## Lesson 12: integrated transfer and readiness (Core, with Advanced Part C)

- Loop: classify, extract, represent, select, execute, verify, decide, with the error log closing the loop.
- Transfer check: change one condition and confirm the representation still gives the answer, equality included.
- The item families map to specialist methods by dominant question; readiness is checked on every timed set.
- Items 33 to 35 test a changed average, a time share and the stage of a classification failure.

## Cumulative items (36 to 39)

- Item 36 joins the odd-number sum with a lower boundary (Lessons 4 and 2); item 37 joins the contrapositive with a counterexample (Lessons 5 and 7); item 38 is a time-boxed choice joining expected value with a stated elimination premise (Lessons 8 and 4); item 39 joins a chain of changes with a relative change (Lessons 3 and 6).
