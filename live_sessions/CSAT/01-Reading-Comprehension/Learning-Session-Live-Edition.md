# Reading Comprehension - Learning Session Live Edition

**Paper:** CSAT (Civil Services Preliminary Examination, Paper II) | **Format:** 80 four-option items, 200 marks, two hours | **Scoring:** 2.5 marks for each correct answer; one-third of the item's marks (5/6) is deducted for each wrong answer; a blank carries no penalty | **Qualifying bar:** 33 per cent of Paper II, which is 66 of 200 marks

This session teaches the comprehension skill that makes up a large share of CSAT items: the solved library routes 397 CSAT questions from 2013 to 2026 to Reading Comprehension, and the share varies by year, since in some years another CSAT family routes more items. It does not ask you to know anything outside the passage. Every lesson adds one step to a single discipline: decide what the question is asking, decide what the passage can defend, and spend only the time that the decision is worth.

Practice in this session is multiple-choice. Each lesson ends with two to four original items, answered in a separate block so that you can attempt them before reading any explanation. Passages in those items are written for this session. No past-paper passage, question wording or answer appears in the teaching.

## Lesson 1 — Passage-only contract, stem-first workflow, time-box and skip rule

Progress: 1 / 9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Passage-only contract, stem-first workflow, time-box and skip rule

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Open with the budget, because the budget decides which items you can afford to think about. The paper has eighty items in two hours, which averages ninety seconds an item. A reading item that takes four minutes costs you about two and two-thirds average items of time, and many of those items could be answered in thirty seconds.

```text
PAPER BUDGET (average)
Items           80
Time            120 minutes
Per item        90 seconds

ITEM DECISION FLOW
stem  ->  name the type  ->  read the passage once  ->  predict  ->  test options  ->  choose or park
```

The first skill is refusing outside knowledge. A passage-only item is judged on the record in front of you. An option can be true in the world and still lose, because the passage does not defend it. Keep one question in mind for every option: which line of the passage proves this?

**The rule.** A kept option must be stated in the passage or entailed by it. Entailed means the passage would be false if the option were false. The condition under which this rule bends is a stem that asks for a general principle rather than a reading of the passage. Such stems are rare here, so passage-only is the default.

**Four filters, in order.** Stated or entailed? In scope, neither narrower nor wider? Direction kept, no reversed cause or condition? No fact that the passage never gives?

**Shortcut: stem first, then the passage once.** Read the stem before the passage, name its type, and only then read the passage, reading it once with that type in mind. For a passage with several items, read every stem first and mark the scope word in each (only, most, some, all).

*Fails when* the passage is very short, about three sentences. Then the stems cost more than the passage, so read the passage first.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "The bridge closes on Sundays for repairs. Buses use the ferry instead." Stem: "Which inference is best supported?" Options: (a) Buses cannot use the bridge on Sundays. (b) The ferry runs more often than the bridge. (c) Repairs are scheduled for Sundays only. (d) The bridge is the only route for cars. Answer: (a), because the closure plus the detour gives it directly. (b) is outside the passage. (c) adds "only", which the passage does not say. (d) is a plausible claim the passage never makes.

**Shortcut: time-box and skip.** Give each item about ninety seconds. When the ceiling arrives and two or fewer options survive, make the best elimination and move on. When none is defensible, park the item.

*Fails when* the item is one elimination from certainty. Then finish it, because stopping wastes a sure mark.

*Escalation rule.* Never reread the whole passage for one option. Reread only the sentence that option points at; an option that points at no sentence is already wrong.

**Strongest tempting wrong method and the reply.** The tempting method is to answer from general belief. Take a passage saying a clinic opens late on Thursdays. A candidate may pick "patients prefer Thursday mornings" because it sounds reasonable. The reply is the passage-only test: no line supports a preference, so the option loses however sensible it sounds.

**Near-neighbour pair: stated versus entailed.** A stated claim is written in the text. An entailed claim is not written but must hold if the text holds. Both are acceptable. A true claim about the world that the text neither states nor entails is not.

**Distractor families.** Five families recur, and naming the family is the quickest rejection:

- out-of-passage truth: a real fact the passage never states;
- over-generalisation: "some" or "many" turned into "all", "always" or "every";
- scope creep: an extra topic the passage did not raise;
- distortion: a reversed cause and effect, or a swapped qualifier;
- half-right: one clause supported, another not, which still loses.

**Exam application.** Every later lesson starts from this workflow. The stem decides which test you need, and the budget decides how many items you can finish.

Retrieval, in question form: what does a stated claim need that an entailed claim does not, and which filter would you apply first to an option that names a real fact the passage never mentions?

Once you know which evidence the passage admits, the next question is how much of that evidence a central idea must cover. Lesson 2 answers it.

### Practice

**MCQ 1**

A town council replaced its paper bus timetables with a mobile application last year. Officials report that the application now receives about forty thousand queries a month. Several elderly residents, however, say they still depend on printed boards at bus stops because they do not own smartphones. The council has not yet decided whether to keep printing the boards, and it has promised to publish a comparison of usage in the spring.

Which statement is best supported by the passage?

- A. Some residents who lack smartphones still rely on printed boards at bus stops, which remain in place for now.
- B. The mobile application has made bus travel cheaper for many residents across the city.
- C. Elderly residents may prefer printed boards to the application.
- D. The council has already removed the paper timetables from several stops in the district, so the application now carries all the enquiries.

**MCQ 1 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The passage states that some elderly residents still depend on printed boards because they lack smartphones, so the boards remain in use for now.
- **B — Incorrect:** Cost is never mentioned; the claim is outside the passage.
- **C — Incorrect:** The passage gives lack of smartphones as the reason for reliance, not a preference, so the option reverses the reason.
- **D — Incorrect:** The passage says the boards are still relied on and the council has not decided about them, which contradicts removal from several stops.


**MCQ 2**

A coastal district cleared many mangrove stands for shrimp farms during the last decade. Mangroves reduce the force of storm surges by absorbing wave energy. Local fishers in several districts report that their catches have fallen since the clearance began. The passage does not include any catch figures, and it does not rule out other causes of the decline. It recommends that the district survey the effect before deciding on further permits.

Which option a careful reader would reject as lying outside the passage, even though it is factually plausible?

- A. Mangroves can reduce the force of storm surges by absorbing wave energy.
- B. Shrimp farms in other countries have caused coastal erosion by removing protective vegetation, a mechanism that other coastal regions have reported in several studies.
- C. Fishers in several districts report lower catches since the clearance began, as the passage reports.
- D. The passage suggests surveying the effect of the clearance on catches before the district decides on any further permits.

**MCQ 2 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** The passage states this mechanism directly, so it is inside the passage.
- **B — Correct:** Other countries are never mentioned; the claim is plausible but outside the record, so it must be rejected.
- **C — Incorrect:** The passage reports this fishers' claim, so it is supported.
- **D — Incorrect:** The passage recommends this survey, so the option is stated rather than outside.


**MCQ 3**

A school library extended its weekday hours until seven in the evening. Usage rose by about a third in the following term. Staff believe the late hours mainly help students who work during the day, although they have not surveyed those students. Weekend hours were not changed. The librarian observed that some students who came in the evening used the building for quiet study rather than borrowing books. The library's annual report will be published in the spring.

Which option is half-right, meaning one part is supported and another part is not?

- A. Usage rose because the library added free internet terminals, new study desks and extra evening staff, all funded by a grant from the council.
- B. Staff believe the late hours mainly help students who work during the day, though no survey was carried out.
- C. Usage rose by a third after the change, so the change alone explains the rise.
- D. Weekend hours were extended as well, which may explain the rise.

**MCQ 3 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Free terminals, new desks and a council grant are never mentioned; the claim is outside the passage.
- **B — Incorrect:** The passage attributes this belief to staff and says there was no survey, so the option is fully supported.
- **C — Correct:** The rise is supported, but 'alone' goes beyond the passage, which names other uses and no controls; the option is half-right.
- **D — Incorrect:** The passage says weekend hours were not changed, so the option reverses the text.

## Lesson 2 — Central idea and scope

Progress: 2 / 9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Central idea and scope

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Start with the test, not the definition. Read the passage, close it, and write one sentence that a friend would accept as "what this is about and what the author wants you to take away." Then compare that sentence with the options. The sentence you wrote is your summary, and the summary is the tool.

```text
PASSAGE PARTS                      SUMMARY MUST REACH
claim                        ->    claim
qualification                ->    qualification (if present)
supporting example           ->    not needed
remedy or conclusion         ->    remedy (if the passage gives one)

REJECT:  one sentence only  |  side topic  |  extreme version of the claim
```

**The rule.** A central idea covers the passage's main claim and its principal qualification at the same breadth. Breadth matching cuts both ways: an option that narrows to one example misses the claim, and one that widens to a general principle claims more than the passage reaches.

**Quantifier audit.** Words such as some, most, many, all and only set the size of a claim. The option that matches the passage's own size is usually the most modest one the passage supports. An absolute option is right only when the passage is absolute.

**Shortcut: the six-to-ten-word summary.** Write the summary before reading the options. Choose the option that matches it in breadth, not the option with the most vocabulary.

*Fails when* the passage makes a concession or reversal ("it is true that... but"). A summary that drops the concession matches a distractor, so give the concession its own clause.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "A library's catalogue is useful only if members can search it from home; a catalogue that works only inside the building serves few people." Options: (a) Catalogues are useless. (b) Members should buy computers. (c) A catalogue helps only when members can reach it from where they are. (d) The library has the best catalogue in the city. Answer: (c). It keeps the condition, which the other options drop or reverse.

*Second worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "A town's new cycle lanes cut accidents at two junctions. The council warns that fewer cars were on the roads that year, so the lanes may not be the whole cause." Stem: "Which option is the central idea?" Options: (a) Cycle lanes cut accidents at junctions. (b) Fewer cars on the roads reduced accidents across the whole town. (c) Cycle lanes may have cut junction accidents, but fewer cars could explain part of the drop, so the effect is not yet proven. (d) The council should build more junction crossings. Answer: (c). It keeps the claim and its qualification at the passage's breadth. (a) drops the warning; (b) lifts a rival the passage only raises and widens it to the whole town; (d) is a remedy the passage never states.

**Shortcut: reject the single detail.** Discard an option that restates one supporting detail while other sentences develop a separate claim that the option leaves out.

*Fails when* the one sentence is the passage's whole claim. Then that sentence is the central idea and must not be discarded.

**A tempting error.** A reader picks the most memorable sentence, often placed in the middle of the passage. The reply is the coverage test: check whether the qualification and the remedy are also reflected. If not, the option is a detail.

**A failing case for the summary.** A passage says the cause of a harbour's silting is dredging, but that the silting has slowed since the dredging stopped, so dredging alone cannot explain it. A candidate who writes "dredging causes silting" has matched the first half of the passage and dropped the qualification. The summary must carry both halves.

**Near-neighbour pair: central idea versus detail.** A detail is one supporting sentence. A central idea is the claim that the details jointly support. Options that accurately quote a detail are the commonest trap.

Central-idea items are quick when the summary comes first and slow when every option is read at full length.

Try this drill: write the summary for the harbour passage in six words, then say which of the two halves a one-sentence option would drop.

A central idea states what the passage establishes. What follows from that establishment is an inference, which is Lesson 3.

### Practice

**MCQ 4**

Cities with dense concrete and little tree cover can be several degrees warmer than nearby rural areas at night. Planners have responded with tree planting, cool roofs and green corridors. Early evaluations suggest these measures lower peak temperatures in summer. They cannot, however, reverse the heat released by large industrial zones, which continues after dark. The passage therefore argues that cooling measures should be paired with lower industrial heat output if the gains are to last.

Which option best states the central idea?

- A. Tree planting is probably the most effective cooling measure.
- B. Cities are warmer than rural areas at night, which matters for planners.
- C. Cool roofs cost less to install than green corridors, so cities should fund roofs first.
- D. Cooling measures help, but they should be paired with lower industrial heat output if the gains are to last.

**MCQ 4 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** The passage does not rank tree planting; it lists it as one measure among several.
- **B — Incorrect:** This is one background fact, too narrow for the passage's argument, so it is a detail rather than the central idea.
- **C — Incorrect:** Cost is never compared; this claim is outside the argument.
- **D — Correct:** It covers the measures, their limit and the remedy the passage recommends, at the passage's own breadth.


**MCQ 5**

A district hospital introduced a queue-tracking system that sends patients a text message when their turn approaches. In the first quarter, average waiting times fell from ninety to fifty minutes. Staff noticed, however, that patients without smartphones struggled to use the system, so the hospital added help desks. Officials now plan to test a simpler paper token alongside the application, to see whether waiting times stay low for everyone who attends.

Which option best captures the central idea?

- A. A queue-tracking system can shorten waits, but its benefits depend on access.
- B. Smartphones have become essential for modern hospital administration, scheduling, patient communication and record keeping across the health sector in most districts.
- C. Average waiting times fell from ninety to fifty minutes.
- D. Officials plan to abandon the mobile application and return some services to paper forms in the next year.

**MCQ 5 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** It states the gain and its qualification about access, at the passage's breadth.
- **B — Incorrect:** Smartphones are a side point; the passage's concern is access for those who lack them.
- **C — Incorrect:** It is one accurate detail, the classic central-idea trap.
- **D — Incorrect:** The passage says officials will test a paper token alongside the application, not abandon it.

## Lesson 3 — Inference: forced versus most-supported

Progress: 3 / 9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Inference: forced versus most-supported

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Begin with the arrow drill, then build the argument tree. Inference items are decided by translating the passage's wording into arrows, so do that first.

```text
ARROW DRILL (before reading options)
"A only if B"               ->  A -> B      (if A, then B)
"A is necessary for B"      ->  B -> A      (no B without A)
"A leads to B"              ->  A -> B      (not B -> A)
contrapositive of A -> B    ->  not-B -> not-A   (entailed)
converse of A -> B          ->  B -> A      (not entailed)

ARGUMENT TREE
                 passage's claims
                 /              \
        stated facts        qualifiers (can, may, often, only if)
                 \              /
                  forced consequences  <--  test each option here
                         |
              most-supported: fewest added premises
```

**The rule.** Two stem types need two tests. A "must follow" stem needs entailment: the option is forced by the text. A "best supported" or "most logical" stem needs the fewest added premises among the options the text does not contradict. Both reject options that reverse a relation or make an absolute claim the text does not make.

**Forced versus most-supported, in one case.** A passage says a bus runs late only when the road is flooded. "A bus on a dry road is not late for that reason" is forced. "Buses are always on time" is not. A candidate who reads the rule backwards and writes "a flooded road makes the bus late" has made a converse, which the rule does not give.

**Shortcut: translate the qualifier.** When a passage uses "only if", "unless", "necessary" or "can", write the arrow in the margin before the options. Test each option against the arrow.

*Fails when* the passage contains no conditional wording. Then go straight to the forced test.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "The ferry sails only when wind speed stays below twenty knots." Stem: "Which conclusion is forced?" Options: (a) The ferry sails whenever the wind is calm. (b) Every calm day has a sailing. (c) On a day with wind above twenty knots, the ferry does not sail. (d) The ferry is the only boat on the river. Answer: (c). The arrow is "sails → wind below twenty knots", so its contrapositive is entailed. (a) is the converse, (b) overreaches, and (d) is an extension.

**Shortcut: the smaller bridge.** When two options survive a most-supported stem, prefer the one needing fewer added premises.

*Fails when* both survivors need the same premise. Then look for one word in each survivor that goes beyond the text, such as "only" or "always"; the survivor whose extra word the passage does not license loses.

**The tempting error.** Choosing the strongest-sounding conclusion, usually an absolute, feels like the safest answer. The reply: the passage must license the absolute, and "always", "only" or "every" need textual support. If the text says "often", the absolute is a distortion.

**Near-neighbour pair: inference versus assumption.** An inference is a conclusion drawn out of the passage. An assumption is the premise an argument needs underneath its conclusion. Before choosing an inference, check that the option is a conclusion and not a premise the passage requires.

Inference and assumption together make up most of the routed items (146 of the 241 items routed for 2018 to 2026), and inference is where candidates most often turn "can" into "must".

Quick check: the passage says "A is necessary for B". Which arrow is safe, and which is the converse?

An inference is a conclusion, and a conclusion silently needs premises. Lesson 4 asks which premise an argument cannot do without.

### Practice

**MCQ 6**

Many cities now require new buildings to include rainwater tanks. Where enforcement is strict, households have installed tanks in about two-thirds of new homes. The passage notes that tanks reduce dependence on tanker supplies during dry months. It adds, however, that tanks cannot prevent groundwater decline where extraction is heavy, because each tank stores only the rain that falls on a single roof. Municipal engineers say that the tanks need cleaning twice a year to keep the stored water safe to use.

Which inference is best supported?

- A. Rainwater tanks remove the need for groundwater in most cities, because households no longer depend on municipal tanker supplies.
- B. Tanks may ease dry-season dependence on tankers without solving groundwater decline in heavily extracted areas.
- C. Households mainly install tanks to cut their water bills, so enforcement matters less once a city has adopted the rule in most districts.
- D. Households in dry months prefer tanker water to rainwater, so they would rather pay for deliveries than use their own tanks during the whole dry season.

**MCQ 6 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** The passage limits what tanks can do in heavily extracted areas, so 'most cities' widens the claim beyond the text.
- **B — Correct:** It keeps both halves: the modest dry-season benefit and the groundwater limit where extraction is heavy, with 'may' as the qualifier.
- **C — Incorrect:** The passage gives no reason households install tanks; the water-bill motive is outside the text.
- **D — Incorrect:** The passage implies households turn to tanks to reduce tanker dependence, which reverses this preference.


**MCQ 7**

A flood forecast is necessary for timely evacuation, because officials cannot decide when to move people without one. It is not sufficient, however. A forecast that reaches only officials leaves residents uninformed, and uninformed residents do not move. The passage concludes that forecasting systems should be judged by the warnings that residents actually receive, rather than by the accuracy of the forecast alone. Officials have asked for a second review of the warning system once the next season's data are available.

Which conclusion follows most directly?

- A. Forecasts alone are enough to ensure timely evacuation.
- B. Forecasts are unnecessary in districts where residents already receive council text messages.
- C. A forecast that does not reach residents is unlikely to produce timely evacuation on its own, however accurate it is.
- D. Officials should rarely issue forecasts that residents cannot read in their own language.

**MCQ 7 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** The passage says a forecast is not sufficient, which contradicts the sufficiency claim.
- **B — Incorrect:** The passage never mentions text messages; the claim reverses the necessity relation, since a forecast is necessary.
- **C — Correct:** Because the forecast is not sufficient and residents must receive the warning, a forecast that does not reach them is unlikely to bring timely evacuation on its own.
- **D — Incorrect:** The passage does not discuss language or the flood season; the claim is an extension beyond the text.


**MCQ 8**

The canteen reduced its menu to ten dishes at the start of term. Food waste fell by a fifth in the following month, and the cooks reported fewer unused ingredients at closing time. Students, however, complained that the choices were limited, and a few said they had stopped eating at the canteen. The manager said the smaller menu would be reviewed after the examinations, when more students returned. The manager said the canteen would publish the figures monthly.

Which is the most logical inference from the passage?

- A. Reducing choices reduces food waste in most canteens, whatever the menu, the season or the number of students who eat there.
- B. Students were satisfied with the new menu.
- C. The canteen will probably keep the ten-dish menu in its main branch.
- D. A smaller menu may reduce waste, although it can also limit what students choose to eat.

**MCQ 8 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** 'Most canteens' and 'whatever the menu' claim more than one canteen's figures can support.
- **B — Incorrect:** The passage says students complained, which contradicts satisfaction.
- **C — Incorrect:** The menu is to be reviewed after the examinations, so a permanent decision to keep it is unstated.
- **D — Correct:** It takes the waste result and the student complaint as the passage gives them, with 'may' and 'can' as the qualifiers.


**MCQ 9**

A scholarship for each term is paid only to students whose attendance in that term exceeded seventy-five per cent. Asha's attendance this term has been seventy per cent, and the scholarship office has written to her about her record. The office reviews payments each term, and it publishes the threshold in a circular that every college receives before the term begins. Asha has asked the office to reconsider the decision, and her appeal will be heard next week.

Which conclusion is forced by the passage?

- A. Asha is not eligible for this term's scholarship.
- B. Students below seventy-five per cent may still receive the term's payment in full.
- C. Students whose attendance is above seventy-five per cent usually receive the scholarship in full each term.
- D. The office may have decided each payment by reviewing the student's family income as well as attendance before the term began.

**MCQ 9 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The rule pays only students above seventy-five per cent in the term; Asha is at seventy per cent this term, so the rule denies this term's payment.
- **B — Incorrect:** 'Only' excludes payment below the threshold, so this option reverses the rule.
- **C — Incorrect:** The rule gives a necessary condition, not a guarantee for everyone above the threshold; this is the converse of the stated relation.
- **D — Incorrect:** Family income is never mentioned; the claim is outside the passage.

## Lesson 4 — Assumptions and the negation test

Progress: 4 / 9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Assumptions and the negation test

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This lesson is organised as a debate about one argument. Read the argument, hear the rival method, then run the test.

*The argument.* "The clinic should extend its hours, because more patients cannot visit during the working day."

*Rival method.* A candidate says: "The best assumption is the one that makes the clinic look well run, namely that the clinic is the largest in the district." *Reply.* Being well run is not the bridge between the premise and the recommendation. The bridge is the link that makes the premise support the conclusion.

*Negation test.* Take the candidate "Patients who cannot visit during the day would attend in the evening." Negate it: "Patients who cannot visit during the day would not attend in the evening." The argument now gives no reason to extend the hours. It has collapsed, so the candidate is the assumption. Repeat the test with "the clinic is the largest in the district". Negate it, and the recommendation still stands, because a small clinic could also gain from longer hours. That candidate is not needed.

```text
ARGUMENT STRUCTURE
premise:     more patients cannot visit during the working day
conclusion:  extend the clinic's hours
bridge:      evening visits would reach those patients    <- the assumption

NEGATION CHECK
candidate                         negated, does the argument survive?
evening patients would attend     no   -> it is the assumption
clinic is largest in district     yes  -> not needed
```

**The rule.** For an assumption stem, negate each candidate. If the negated candidate breaks the argument, the original candidate was the assumption. If the argument survives, the candidate is not needed. The test is a test of necessity, and it works only on stems that ask what the argument assumes or depends on.

**A real failing case.** The test also breaks on the conclusion itself. The argument "The school should buy a new bus, because pupils cannot reach the sports field on foot" collapses if the candidate "The school should buy a new bus" is negated. The conclusion is necessary to the argument, but it is not an assumption, because it is the claim being argued for. So a negated option that restates the conclusion will "pass" the test without being the assumption. Exclude options that repeat the recommendation before you negate anything.

**Near-neighbour comparison: negation test versus inference test.** An inference test asks whether the option must be true if the passage is true: it looks forward from the text. A negation test asks whether the argument needs the option: it looks backward from the conclusion. A reason can be entailed by the passage and still not be needed by the argument, and an assumption can be needed without being entailed. Use the inference test for "must follow" stems and the negation test for "assumes" stems.

**Shortcut: negate and test.** On an assumption stem, negate the candidate in one short sentence and ask whether the conclusion still follows. This takes about twenty seconds.

*Fails when* the candidate restates the conclusion, as above.

**Shortcut: the comparison bridge.** When the passage compares two groups or two periods, the usual bridge is that the comparison is fair.

*Fails when* the passage controls for the difference you are worried about. Then the comparison is no longer the hidden bridge.

**Exam application.** Assumption items are common in the routed questions. The answer is usually the link the author never states, and it is usually a premise rather than a reason.

Retrieval, in three forms: name the premise, the conclusion and the bridge in one line each for the clinic argument; say which candidate a negation test would wrongly accept; explain in one sentence why a bridge is not the same as a reason.

Once a bridge is identified, the question is what the author may draw from the stated mechanism. Lesson 5 covers corollaries, explanations and the actions a passage licenses.

**MCQ 10**

## Lesson 5 — Corollary, explanation, practical and best-action items

Progress: 5 / 9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Corollary, explanation, practical and best-action items

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Organise this lesson as a ladder of what the passage lets you say, from the nearest step to the furthest. Climb only as far as the stem asks.

```text
RUNG 1  corollary      the next step of a stated mechanism, nothing added
RUNG 2  explanation    the reason the passage gives for a finding or a limit
RUNG 3  licensed action  the step the passage's verb allows, at its strength
RUNG 4  overreach      two steps away, or a stronger verb than the text uses  <- reject
```

Take a finding: "Libraries that lend e-books see fewer late returns, because automatic expiry removes the need to remember due dates." A corollary is what that mechanism delivers directly: late-return charges are likely to fall in e-book libraries that use automatic expiry. An explanation is the reason the passage gives: the expiry rule. A licensed action follows the verb: a library that wants fewer late returns may adopt automatic expiry, but the passage does not say it must.

**The rule.** A corollary follows from a stated mechanism without a new premise. An explanation names the reason the passage actually gives. A practical or best-action option recommends the step the passage supports at the strength of its verb. The condition under which the rule bends: when the passage licenses an action only under a condition, such as "if funds allow". The correct option must keep that condition.

**Shortcut: one step only.** For a corollary stem, state the mechanism in one line and ask which option is the next step of it, with nothing else.

*Limit:* the option needs a second premise, and such an option is an inference that overreaches.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "Libraries that lend e-books see fewer late returns, because automatic expiry removes the need to remember due dates." Stem: "Which option is a direct corollary?" Options: (a) E-book libraries never have late returns. (b) Late-return charges are likely to fall in e-book libraries that use automatic expiry. (c) Printed books are unpopular with members. (d) Every library should adopt e-books. Answer: (b). (a) is absolute, (c) is unstated, and (d) is a policy the passage never states.

*Second worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "A council found that shoppers who used the park-and-ride bus spent more in the centre. It says the bus service may be extended to the outer wards if funds allow." Stem: "Which option is the best action the passage licenses?" Options: (a) The council must extend the bus service to every ward this year. (b) The council may extend the bus service to the outer wards if funds allow. (c) The council should close the car parks in the centre. (d) Shoppers should spend more in the centre. Answer: (b). It keeps the verb's strength ("may") and its condition ("if funds allow"). (a) turns "may" into "must" and drops the condition; (c) is a stronger step the passage never licenses; (d) gives advice to shoppers that the passage does not offer.

**Shortcut: match the verb.** For a best-action stem, find the verb the passage uses ("should pair", "may test"). The correct option uses a verb of the same strength.

*Fails when* the passage uses a conditional verb. Then the option must carry the condition, or it is an extension.

**A tempting error.** The most decisive action often sounds like a solution, so it feels like the right choice. The reply is the licence test: an action beyond the passage's verb is not licensed, however sensible it sounds.

**Near-neighbour pair: explanation versus cause.** A cause in the passage produces the finding. An explanation for a limit tells why the finding cannot be extended. Read the stem's verb first.

These items are quick once the verb is matched, and slow when candidates debate whether an action is wise. The stem asks what is licensed, so test licence.

Test yourself: for the e-book passage, is "e-book libraries should replace printed books" a corollary, an explanation, a licensed action or an overreach? Justify the choice with the passage's verb.

Explanations and actions show how an argument is meant to work. Lesson 6 asks where that argument could fail.

## Lesson 6 — Critical factor: strengthen and weaken

Progress: 6 / 9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Critical factor: strengthen and weaken

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Organise this lesson around one diagram: the gap. Every critical-factor item asks what happens to the gap between evidence and conclusion when a new fact arrives.

```text
EVIDENCE  ----------- gap -----------> CONCLUSION
                       |
        what else could explain the evidence?
        is the comparison fair?
                       |
        +--------------+--------------+
        |                             |
   STRENGTHEN                     WEAKEN
   rules out the rival            opens the rival
   makes the comparison fair      shows the comparison is unfair
```

**The rule.** State the gap in one sentence before reading the options. Then test each option against it. A strengthening option closes the gap. A weakening option opens a rival explanation or shows that the comparison is unfair. An option that adds a new claim elsewhere does neither, however true it is.

**Shortcut: state the gap first.** Write the gap in one line. A candidate who has named the rival explanation can reject most options at a glance.

*Fails when* the argument has no visible gap. Then the stem is probably asking for an assumption, and Lesson 4 applies.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Argument: "Students who joined the club scored higher; so the club raises scores." Gap: students who choose clubs may already be stronger. The option that strengthens: "Club members were no stronger than others before they joined." The option that weakens: "Those who joined were already the highest scorers before the club began." The first rules out the rival; the second confirms it.

*Second worked case (answer shown).* Argument: "Hospitals that adopted the checklist had fewer infections, so the checklist reduces infections." Gap: the hospitals that adopted it may already have been more careful. Options: (a) Infection rates fell in hospitals that never adopted the checklist, over the same period. (b) Hospitals that adopted the checklist were no safer than other hospitals before they adopted it. (c) The checklist takes two minutes to complete. (d) Most hospitals now use the checklist. Answer for a strengthening option: (b), which rules out the rival. Option (a) weakens the claim: the comparison group changed in the same way, so the change may have happened anyway. Options (c) and (d) do not touch the gap.

**Shortcut: the comparison test.** For a with-and-without claim, ask whether the comparison group changed in the same way as the treated group. A comparison group that changed in the same way weakens the claim, because the change may have happened anyway. A comparison group that stayed unchanged while the treated group improved strengthens it.

*Fails when* the comparison group is not described. An option that merely describes a comparison is not enough, and the gap stays open.

**A real failing case.** A health programme reports that participants lost weight. The gap is that participants may have been trying to lose weight already. An option saying "Participants were trying to lose weight" weakens the claim. But an option saying "Participants were healthier at the start" is not a weakening option, because health at the start is not the rival explanation the gap names. A candidate who chooses by general plausibility picks the second, which misses the gap.

**Strongest tempting wrong method and the reply.** The tempting method is to choose the option most related to the argument's subject. The reply: relatedness is not strength. Only an option that closes or opens the gap counts.

**Near-neighbour pair: strengthen versus assumption.** An assumption is the bridge the argument needs. A strengthening option may confirm that bridge, but it can also rule out a rival the author never considered. Check which of the two the option does.

Critical-factor items reward candidates who state the gap aloud and punish candidates who judge options in isolation.

Gap check: for the club argument, write the gap in one line, then name one fact that would weaken it and one that would strengthen it.

Once the weak point of an argument is visible, the attitude that carries it becomes the next question. Lesson 7 reads tone and viewpoint from wording.

## Lesson 7 — Tone, viewpoint, author intent and literary passages

Progress: 7 / 9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Tone, viewpoint, author intent and literary passages

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Read this lesson as an annotated passage. The markers below are the method, so learn them on the page before the rule.

```text
ANNOTATION LEGEND
[+]  warm or admiring word          ("admirable", "promising")
[-]  cool or critical word          ("undoubtedly" used to doubt, "insist")
[T]  turning word                   ("yet", "however", "although")
[V]  the author's conclusion        usually after [T]

SAMPLE, annotated (a different passage; compare it with your own markings before reading on)
"The park board calls the new bandstand [+]a triumph of design[+]. Visitors have praised
 its lines, [T]but the sound system drowns out the music, and [-]nobody has explained[-]
 why the repair was delayed."
```

**The rule.** Read attitude from the words, not from the topic. The tone option must match the strongest evaluative word and the direction of the final turn. The viewpoint option must match the author's conclusion, which is usually the sentence after the turn.

**Shortcut: find the turn.** Locate the turning word and read the clause after it. Tone and viewpoint usually sit there.

*Fails when* the passage has no turning word. Then read the whole passage once and attend to the strongest adjective.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "The scheme's goals sound impressive. However, the document does not say where the funding will come from." Stem: "The author's attitude is best described as:" Options: (a) enthusiastic. (b) neutral. (c) doubtful about how the scheme will be carried out. (d) hostile. Answer: (c). The turn after "however" points to doubt about delivery, not the goal. (d) overshoots, because the author takes the goals seriously.

**Shortcut: separate the goal from the means.** When the author praises an aim and doubts a method, the tone is sceptical about the method. Choose the option that keeps this separation.

*Fails when* the author rejects both aim and method. Then a general negative option is correct.

**Literary worked case: a narrative passage.** Passage: "The river had kept its promises for a hundred years. It fed the fields, carried the boats and asked nothing in return. This spring it rose over the lower road, and the villagers stood on the bank with their hands in their pockets, saying nothing, as though an old friend had broken a long-held word." Stem: "The narrator's attitude towards the river is best described as:" Options: (a) mockingly dismissive. (b) grieving and betrayed. (c) serenely indifferent. (d) cheerfully expectant. Answer: (b). The imagery of a broken promise and the silent bank carry the attitude. "Serenely indifferent" ignores the wording, "cheerfully" conflicts with the rising water, and "mockingly" finds no mockery in the text. *Limit:* a literary passage may use irony, so check whether the imagery undercuts the surface statement.

**Intent worked case.** Passage: "Most reports on coastal erosion end with a list of recommendations that few people read. This one begins with the three villages that have already lost their school, and it places the recommendations after the evidence rather than before it." Stem: "The author's main purpose in this passage is to:" Options: (a) list every recommendation for coastal defence. (b) persuade readers to take the evidence of loss seriously before reading the recommendations. (c) describe the history of the three villages. (d) criticise named officials. Answer: (b). The order is the purpose: the evidence comes first so that readers weigh it before the advice. (c) names content the author uses, not the purpose; (d) names no one. *Limit:* intent is read from structure and stated aim, not from a guess about character.

**Strongest tempting wrong method and the reply.** The tempting method is to choose the most dramatic tone word on offer. The reply is to check the turn: the final attitude decides, and extreme options usually overshoot.

**Near-neighbour pair: tone versus viewpoint.** Tone is how the author feels about the subject as written. Viewpoint is what the author concludes. A passage can be sceptical in tone and still reach a balanced viewpoint.

**Exam application.** Tone and viewpoint make up a small share of the routed items, but they are reliably answerable once the turn is found.

Retrieval, using the annotation legend: mark one warm word, one cool word and one turn in the coastal-erosion passage, then state the viewpoint in one clause.

A viewpoint is a conclusion. A statement set tests several claims at once, so Lesson 8 tests each one separately before reading the code.

## Lesson 8 — Statement-sets and incorrect-statement stems

Progress: 8 / 9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Statement-sets and incorrect-statement stems

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not applicable (aptitude paper)
CA found: not applicable
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Organise this lesson as a truth table. Each statement gets a row, each row gets a verdict with a reason, and the codes are read only after the table is complete.

```text
STATEMENT                                       VERDICT   REASON (passage line or failing clause)
1. a claim the passage states outright            T         stated
2. a claim that adds "only" to a stated fact        F         the qualifier was added
3. (any further statement)                       ?         decide alone

CODES: read after the table; eliminate every code that contains an F
```

**The rule.** Decide each statement independently against the passage. Mark it true only if the passage states or entails it; mark it false if the passage contradicts it or it outruns the passage. Then eliminate codes. Often two rows settle the question, so the third need not be read.

**Shortcut: settle two, then stop.** Judge statements in the order that removes the most codes. Deciding statement 2 usually removes two codes at once.

*Breaks when* the codes all share the same statement; then decide that statement first.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "Clinic visits fell during the summer. Staff think winter visits may have been higher, but they kept no records for that season." Statements: 1. Clinic visits fell during the summer. 2. Winter visits were higher than summer visits. Codes: (a) 1 only (b) 2 only (c) 1 and 2 (d) neither. Answer: (a). Statement 1 is stated; statement 2 turns "may have been" into a fact.

*Second worked case, incorrect-statement stem (answer shown).* Passage: "The bakery's bread sales rose after it opened a second shop, although the owner notes that the rise may partly reflect the summer holidays. Its delivery vans are electric, and the owner plans a third shop next year if funds allow." Stem: "Which statements are NOT supported by the passage?" Statements: 1. Bread sales rose after the second shop opened. 2. The second shop caused the whole rise in bread sales. 3. The owner will open a third shop next year whatever the funds. Codes: (a) 1 only (b) 2 and 3 only (c) 1 and 3 only (d) 2 only.

| Statement | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Supported | Stated directly |
| 2 | Not supported | "may partly reflect" becomes "whole rise" |
| 3 | Not supported | "if funds allow" is dropped |

Answer: (b). Code (a) keeps statement 1, which is supported. Code (c) keeps statement 1 and also keeps statement 3. Code (d) drops statement 3, which also fails. Only the pair 2 and 3 contains exactly the unsupported statements.

**Shortcut: the modal check.** Check every modal verb. A "can" or "may" in the passage becomes an error in a statement that uses "must" or "always".

*Fails when* the statement states a fact the passage gives directly, with no modal to check.

**A failing case for the one-statement rule.** A statement reads: "Recycling reduces energy use and eliminates waste." The first clause is supported, so a quick reader may mark it true. The second clause goes beyond the passage, which says the process produces less waste, not none. Judged alone, the statement is false, even though half of it is correct. Test every clause; one unsupported clause makes the statement unsupported.

**A tempting error.** Accepting a statement because the others look right feels efficient. The reply: a bundle can contain one false member, so each row needs its own verdict.

**Near-neighbour pair: correct-statement stem versus incorrect-statement stem.** In a "which are correct" stem, find the statements the passage supports. In a "which is not supported" stem, find the one the passage contradicts or outruns. Read the question word before the statements.

Statement-set stems make up a large share of the 2026 routed items (ten of twenty-three), and they reward candidates who keep statements apart.

Row drill: write a third statement about the clinic, then give it a verdict and a reason in the table's third row.

Testing each statement alone can still leave two options. Lesson 9 supplies the discriminator word and the two-passage map for that case.

## Lesson 9 — Close options, two-survivor rule, two-passage comparison and word-in-context

Progress: 9 / 9 | Stage: Advanced (taught in full) | Subtopic: Close options, two-survivor rule, two-passage comparison and word-in-context

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Organise this lesson as two columns: the left column tests a single passage, the right column tests two passages. Learn both columns, then the two tools that decide close options.

```text
SINGLE PASSAGE                        TWO PASSAGES
two survivors                         sort each option:
  find the word that goes beyond        P1 only | P2 only | both | neither
  the text (only, all, because)
  mismatch? that option loses         P2-only option survives only if P1 never claims it
  no mismatch? take the smaller bridge
```

**The rule for close options.** When two options survive, find the one added word that the text does not license, and eliminate the option that carries it. If neither carries such a word, take the option that needs fewer added premises. The rule for two passages is to sort each option by source before choosing.

**Near-neighbour comparison: discriminator versus scope match.** A scope match asks whether the option has the passage's breadth; it works on central-idea stems. A discriminator asks whether one word goes beyond the text; it works on close options where breadth is already the same. A candidate who uses scope match on two survivors of identical breadth gets nowhere. Use the discriminator there.

**Shortcut: the two-survivor word test.** Underline the single word in each survivor that goes beyond the text. Eliminate the option whose extra word the text does not support.

*Fails when* both survivors carry the same unlicensed word. Then the word test is silent, and the scope match takes over, as in the failing case below.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "The archive digitised its maps, which makes them easier to search." Options: (a) Digitisation makes the maps easier to find. (b) Digitisation is the only way to make the maps easier to find. Answer: (a). The word "only" in (b) goes beyond the text.

**Two-passage worked case.** Passage 1: "Libraries that stay open late attract students who work during the day." Passage 2: "Late opening raised evening visits in two libraries. In a third library that did not change its hours, evening visits also rose, so the rise may come from another cause." Stem: "Which claim is supported by Passage 2 alone?" Options: (a) Late opening attracts day-workers. (b) Evening visits rose in a library that did not change its hours. (c) Late opening is the cause of all evening visits. (d) Students prefer late hours. Answer: (b). Passage 1 says nothing about the third library. (a) is Passage 1's claim, (c) is contradicted by the third library, and (d) appears in neither passage. *Limit:* an option that sounds like a conclusion from both passages is not supported by either unless a passage states it.

**Shortcut: the context sentence.** For a word-in-context stem, read the sentence around the word and replace the word with a plain word that fits its direction: contrast, result or attitude.

*Fails when* the surrounding sentence is itself the claim being tested. Then read the meaning in the passage's terms, not your own.

*Worked case (answer shown).* Passage: "The coach's remarks were so blunt that several players left the room without speaking." Stem: "In the passage, 'blunt' most nearly means:" Options: (a) gentle and tactful. (b) direct to the point of harshness. (c) hidden under humour. (d) carefully rehearsed. Answer: (b). The silence fits harsh directness; (a) contradicts the effect; (c) and (d) have no support in the sentence.

**A failing case for the discriminator.** Sometimes both survivors carry an unlicensed "only". The word test is then silent, and the scope match decides: keep the option whose subject and breadth match the passage's own subject, and reject the one that widens the claim to a whole category the passage never mentions.

**Strongest tempting wrong method and the reply.** The tempting method is to choose the more sophisticated survivor. The reply: sophistication is not support. Only the word the passage licenses decides a close pair.

**Near-neighbour pair: one passage versus two.** In a two-passage item, an option stated by one passage alone is not a claim of both. Keep the sources separate, and mark the option "both" only when both passages state it.

**Exam application.** Close options cost the most time when candidates reread the passage. The discriminator word and the source map decide the item without rereading.

Retrieval, as a sorting task: for the library pair, sort the four options into P1, P2, both or neither, then say which single word in option (c) would be the discriminator if it were a survivor.

The nine lessons end here. The final sections link every routed question to the lesson that teaches its type, so each past question can be re-tested with the method it needs.

## Approach by lesson

- **Lesson 1, passage-only and stated detail items.** Find the sentence that answers the stem. Reject true-in-the-world options that the sentence does not contain.
- **Lesson 2, central idea and scope.** Write the one-line summary before reading options. Reject single-sentence options and options that drop a qualification.

- **Lesson 4, assumption items.** Mark the premise, the conclusion and the bridge. Negate each candidate. The candidate whose negation breaks the argument is the answer.
- **Lesson 5, corollary, explanation and practical items.** Take the one-step consequence, the reason the passage gives, or the action at the strength of the passage's verb.
- **Lesson 6, critical-factor items.** State the gap before reading options. A strengthening option closes it; a weakening option opens a rival explanation.
- **Lesson 7, tone and viewpoint items.** Read the turning word and the strongest evaluative word. The viewpoint is the author's conclusion.
- **Lesson 8, statement items.** Judge each statement alone. Settle two, then eliminate codes. Check every modal verb.
- **Lesson 9, word-in-context and close-option items.** Use the discriminator word for two survivors, map two-passage options to their source, and read the sentence around a word for its meaning.

# CUMULATIVE MCQS

These six items combine lessons. **MCQ 27**

Our bus service is faster than the metro for short trips, so commuters travelling under five kilometres should switch from the metro to buses. The timetable has been adjusted to match the metro's peak frequency, and buses now run every six minutes during the morning rush. The council expects the switch to reduce crowding on metro platforms during the busiest hours. The council's transport committee will review the figures at the end of the term.

The recommendation assumes that:

- A. Commuters enjoy riding buses more than the metro, the council's surveys suggest.
- B. The metro is more expensive than buses for most trips, including those across the whole city centre.
- C. Buses keep their speed advantage when more commuters ride them.
- D. The metro should be closed for maintenance during the busiest hours of the weekday, when most riders travel and the buses are full.

**MCQ 27 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Enjoyment is neither stated nor entailed by the passage, and the switch depends on journey time, so the argument does not need it.
- **B — Incorrect:** Fares are never compared; this claim adds a cost premise that the passage does not state or entail.
- **C — Correct:** Negate it: if buses lost their speed as crowds grew, the switch would stop saving time and the argument collapses. This is the bridge from the pilot to the new demand.
- **D — Incorrect:** Closing the metro is not proposed; the passage asks only for a switch on short trips, so the option is a two-step extension that the arrow test rejects.


**MCQ 28**

The minister described the housing scheme as a turning point for the state. Independent auditors, however, found that only a third of the promised homes had been completed. They recommended a revised timetable before any new targets are announced. Their report did not dispute the scheme's aims, but it questioned whether the current pace would reach them. The audit covered every district and was completed within the period set by the state, and its findings were presented to the legislature the following week.

Consider the following statements about the auditors' attitude and findings:

1. The auditors question whether the current pace will reach the scheme's aims.
2. The auditors dispute the scheme's aims.
3. The auditors recommend a revised timetable before any new targets are announced.

Which of the statements are correct?

- A. Statement 2 only.
- B. Statements 2 and 3 only, because both describe the auditors' view of the pace.
- C. Statements 1, 2 and 3 together, because the auditors may question the whole scheme.
- D. Statements 1 and 3 only.

**MCQ 28 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** This code keeps statement 2, which reverses the report's stance that it did not dispute the aims, and it drops statement 1, which is supported.
- **B — Incorrect:** This code includes statement 2, which reverses the finding, and it leaves out statement 1, which the passage's doubt about the pace supports.
- **C — Incorrect:** This code keeps statement 2, which reverses the auditors' stance; a 'may' inside the option does not rescue a false statement.
- **D — Correct:** The passage's turn, 'did not dispute the aims, but questioned the pace', supports statement 1; statement 3 is the recommendation; statement 2 is false, so the correct set is 1 and 3.

**MCQ 29**

Most farmers who adopted the new seed reported higher yields in the first season. The gains were largest in districts where rainfall arrived on schedule. In districts where the monsoon failed in the second season, the advantage shrank, and some farmers recorded no gain at all. The researchers caution that the seed's effect cannot be separated from the weather in these records. The farmers' association welcomed the findings and asked for a longer trial in drier districts next year.

Which option is the best inference from the passage, at the breadth the evidence supports?

- A. The seed's benefit appeared to depend on rainfall arriving on schedule.
- B. The seed could raise yields in most regions the researchers studied, whatever the weather.
- C. Yields depend on the seed rather than on the weather in the season, as the researchers' records show clearly.
- D. Farmers in districts without rain recorded lower yields in the second season, so the seed's effect is clearly visible there in the records.

**MCQ 29 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: A**

- **A — Correct:** The largest gains and the failed-monsoon shrinkage point to rainfall as a condition; 'appeared to' keeps the researchers' caution, and the claim stays within the scope of the records.
- **B — Incorrect:** 'Whatever the weather' is too broad for the records and contradicts the shrinkage in a failed monsoon; it fails the breadth test and the inference test.
- **C — Incorrect:** The researchers say the seed's effect cannot be separated from the weather, so this claim reverses their caution.
- **D — Incorrect:** The passage says some farmers recorded no gain, not lower yields, and it does not describe districts without rain; the option distorts both facts.


**MCQ 30**

A transport company argues that cutting bus fares will raise ridership, because cheaper trips attract new passengers. Last year's fare cut, however, coincided with a fall in ridership on two of the company's busiest routes. The company responded that the fall reflects a road closure, although the closure data it supplied covers only one of the two routes. The company's annual report was published the following spring and discussed the fares in general terms. An independent analyst later asked for the route-level data, which the company has not yet released.

Which option best identifies the weakness the passage exposes in the company's argument?

- A. The company has not estimated lost revenue.
- B. The evidence the company cites points the other way on two routes, and its explanation covers only one of them.
- C. Cheaper trips are generally less safe than dearer trips, according to road data.
- D. The company should cut fares on every route at once, because the fall on two busy routes shows that cheaper trips are the answer.

**MCQ 30 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Revenue is not discussed in the passage; the option brings in an outside consideration that the forced-inference test rejects.
- **B — Correct:** It names both parts of the weakness the passage shows: the evidence runs against the claim on two routes, and the explanation covers only one of them.
- **C — Incorrect:** Safety is never mentioned, and road data are not in the passage; the claim is outside the text.
- **D — Incorrect:** A recommendation is not a weakness in the evidence; the passage does not entail that fares should be cut everywhere, so the forced-inference test fails.


**MCQ 31**

A clinic reports that patients who receive reminder calls attend follow-up visits more often than patients who do not. The same clinic extended its opening hours in the year the calls began. The report presents the calls as the cause of the increase. It does not show how many patients received calls compared with the number who attended before the calls started. The clinic's staff say that most patients who missed visits had moved to a different area of the city.

Which option is a valid conclusion from the passage?

- A. Reminder calls alone caused the rise in follow-up attendance over the year.
- B. Longer opening hours had no effect on attendance at all in the clinic's first year, so the calls alone explain the rise.
- C. The passage cannot separate reminder calls from longer opening hours.
- D. Patients may prefer telephone reminders to visiting the clinic in person for routine follow-up care in the district.

**MCQ 31 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Verb strength: the report offers a pattern alongside a second change in the same year, so 'caused' outruns the evidence; the second change is the open gap.
- **B — Incorrect:** The passage does not show that the hours had no effect; the claim is absolute, unsupported, and makes the calls the only cause.
- **C — Correct:** Both changes happened together and the report gives no separating evidence, so the stated limit is the valid conclusion. The licensed verb is 'cannot separate', not 'caused'.
- **D — Incorrect:** Patient preference is never mentioned; the claim is outside the passage and cannot be concluded from it.


**MCQ 32**

Tourist arrivals rose sharply in the year the regional airport opened. Hotel prices also rose that year, and local operators argued that both changes came from the airport. The passage gives no figures on hotel capacity, so it cannot say how far the price rise reflects new demand rather than fewer available rooms. Local newspapers reported both figures in the same week, and the regional tourism office sent observers to the hotels to collect the room rates that operators charged during the season.

Which option is best supported by the passage?

- A. Tourism probably rose because of the airport.
- B. Hotel prices fell after the airport opened in the same year.
- C. The airport was probably the main cause of higher hotel prices, as operators argued at the time.
- D. Arrivals and hotel prices rose together, and the passage cannot confirm how far the airport explains the price rise.

**MCQ 32 — Answer and explanation**

**Correct answer: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** The passage does not establish the airport as the cause of arrivals; 'probably' cannot make an unestablished cause supported.
- **B — Incorrect:** The passage says prices rose, so this reverses the text.
- **C — Incorrect:** The passage says the capacity figures are missing, so it cannot confirm the airport as the main cause; 'main' and the operators' view are not the passage's finding.
- **D — Correct:** It keeps both facts and the stated limit on what can be concluded, and it claims nothing beyond the passage.


# TIMED-SET PRACTICE AND SCORING RUBRIC

This section turns the lessons into a timed routine. The budgets are this session's working plan. They come from the paper's stated structure of eighty items in two hours, which averages ninety seconds an item, and they are not official cut-offs.

## Time budgets by item type

| Item type | Budget per item | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| Word-in-context | 30 seconds | Context decides it quickly |
| Central idea | 60 seconds | Summary test first |
| Tone and viewpoint | 60 seconds | Turning word first |
| Corollary, explanation, practical | 60 seconds | One-step test |
| Inference | 75 seconds | Arrow translation |
| Assumption | 75 seconds | Negate and test |
| Critical factor | 90 seconds | State the gap first |
| Statement-set | 105 seconds | Settle two, then stop |
| Two-passage comparison | 120 seconds | Sort sources, then discriminate |

## Three-pass control routine

1. **Pass 1, harvest.** Answer every item you can finish within its budget with confidence. Mark the rest.
2. **Pass 2, grind.** Return to marked items. Apply the lesson's test, eliminate, and discriminate on the one word that breaks a survivor.
3. **Pass 3, decide.** For items still open, eliminate first. Guess only after at least one elimination. Leave an item blank when no option survives.

## Marks-per-minute rubric

Each correct answer earns 2.5 marks. Each wrong answer costs 5/6 of a mark. A blank costs nothing. Three wrong answers cancel exactly one correct answer.

- **Full accuracy at the average pace:** 2.5 marks per 90 seconds, about 1.67 marks per minute.
- **Worked example:** ten items in a timed set, six correct, two wrong, two blank: 6 × 2.5 − 2 × 5/6 = 13.33 marks.
- **Marks per minute** = marks earned ÷ minutes used.
- **Learner's personal target (not official):** 1.2 marks per minute in timed passage sets, chosen as a practice standard.
- **Official bar, from the syllabus wording:** Paper II is qualifying at 33 per cent, which is 66 of 200 marks. Check the current notification before relying on that figure.

## Core gate

Take six items from three passages, one read per passage, within 12 minutes. Six correct or five correct clears the core gate. At four or fewer, mark each miss P, S, D, A or T, redo only the failed passage untimed, and take a fresh six-item set on another day. Advanced work does not compensate for an uncleared core gate. Six correct in 12 minutes is 15 marks, about 1.25 marks per minute; five correct is 12.5 marks, about 1.04 per minute, which is below the 1.2 personal target, so treat five as a pass on accuracy but a prompt to tighten the time budget.

## Guess rule

A blind guess among four options has an expected value of zero: one-quarter of the time it earns 2.5, and three-quarters of the time it costs 5/6. Once one option is eliminated, the success chance rises to one-third and the expected value becomes positive, at about +5/18 marks per attempt. Guess only after elimination.

## Error-code scheme for post-test analysis

| Code | Meaning | Typical sign | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | Concept gap | Did not know the test | Return to the lesson's rule |
| A | Application slip | Right test, wrong step | Write the arrow or the gap before options |
| R | Reading error | Misread a qualifier or a stem | Underline only, not, some, can |
| P | Passage violation | Chose a true, unstated option | Force the "which line proves this?" check |
| T | Time error | Over-invested or rushed | Enforce the budget; park |
| G | Guess error | Guessed before elimination | Apply the guess rule |
| S | Scope or strength error | Option widened or hardened the claim (only, all, must) | Match the quantifier and the verb strength |
| D | Direction error | Reversed cause, condition or relation | Write the arrow before reading options |

Track the mix of codes, not just the count. A pile of P errors needs passage discipline; a pile of T errors needs the three-pass routine.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

- **Passage-only.** The passage is the only evidence. A true fact outside it loses.
- **Stated and entailed.** Both are acceptable. Entailed means the passage would be false if the option were false.
- **Central idea.** A summary covering the claim and its qualification at matching breadth.
- **Quantifiers.** Some, most, many, all, only and always set the size of a claim. Match the passage's size.
- **Forced inference.** Must follow from the text; reject converse and absolute extensions.
- **Most-supported inference.** The option that needs the fewest added premises.
- **Arrows.** "A only if B" gives A → B. The contrapositive is not-B → not-A. The converse is not entailed.
- **Assumption.** The bridge the argument needs; test it by negation.
- **Corollary.** A one-step consequence of a stated mechanism.
- **Explanation.** The reason the passage gives for a finding or for its limit.
- **Practical action.** The step the passage licenses at the strength of its verb.
- **Critical factor.** The gap between evidence and conclusion; strengthen closes it, weaken opens a rival.
- **Tone.** Attitude read from evaluative and turning words, never from the topic.
- **Viewpoint.** The author's conclusion, usually after a fair hearing of the other side.
- **Statement set.** Judge each statement alone; eliminate codes; watch modal verbs.
- **Two survivors.** Find the one word that goes beyond the text.
- **Two passages.** Sort each option to passage 1, passage 2, both or neither.
- **Word-in-context.** Read the surrounding direction, not your own preferred meaning.
- **Time.** About ninety seconds on average; park rather than panic; finish near-certain items.
- **Guessing.** Only after elimination; a blind guess has zero expected value.
