# Philosophy of Religion Topic 07 — Religion without God

**UPSC Philosophy Optional · Paper II · Philosophy of Religion**

> **Evidence key:** ✅ Fact or standard doctrine supported by the audited sources. ⚠️ Analytical inference, comparison, evaluation or answer-writing judgement. ❓ Contested or textually uncertain claim.

## Learner roadmap

The topic is not settled by asking whether God exists. It is settled by asking what must remain before a practice can still count as religion. The roadmap therefore begins with criteria, tests religious functions one by one, studies the strongest actual counterexamples, examines the limiting case that destroys religion rather than merely removing God, and only then hears the theist's strongest denial.

| Lesson | Learning dependency | Main outcome |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | What makes a practice religious? | Distinguish necessary conditions, sufficient clusters, substantive, functional, multidimensional and family-resemblance definitions |
| 2 | What does God do inside religion? | Separate deity-dependent goods from creator-independent sacred, ritual, ethical, communal and liberative functions |
| 3 | What does “without God” mean? | Distinguish non-theism, atheism, anti-theism, agnosticism and indifference; answer the 2023 demand |
| 4 | Can a full religion omit a creator? | Reconstruct Buddhism as the paradigmatic non-theistic religion and evaluate its devotional forms |

### Visual first — the narrow gate, the wide gate and the workable middle

| Definition | What it treats as central | Immediate advantage | Main failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Substantive | God, gods, sacred beings or a transcendent object | Protects religion from becoming any intense commitment | A God-centred version excludes Buddhism by stipulation |
| Functional | Meaning, orientation, cohesion, transformation or liberation | Includes non-theistic traditions | Can include nationalism, ideology or sport |
| Multidimensional | Doctrinal, mythic, ethical, ritual, experiential, social and material dimensions | Describes actual traditions without one essence | A list alone does not specify the minimum threshold |
| Family resemblance | Overlapping features, with no one feature shared by all | Explains diversity without forcing a single essence | Needs a disciplined judgement about borderline cases |

```text
TOO NARROW                         TOO BROAD
"God is necessary"                "Any ultimate concern is religion"
       \                               /
        \                             /
         v                           v
          WORKABLE SUFFICIENT CLUSTER
          socially embodied discipline
          + sacred or ultimate order
          + transformative/soteriological goal
          + some doctrine, ritual, ethics,
            experience and community
```

*The middle position admits actual non-theistic religions while excluding a political rally or football crowd.*

### 1. Start with the ordinary puzzle

Suppose a tradition has monasteries, vows, sacred texts, rituals, moral discipline, a diagnosis of suffering and a path to liberation, but no creator. Calling it “not a religion” merely because it lacks God would make the definition decide the case before the evidence is heard. Yet calling every life-shaping commitment “religion” would make the category useless.

The examination problem is therefore classificatory:

1. Is a personal creator a **necessary condition**—something every religion must have?
2. Is creator-belief a **sufficient condition**—enough by itself to make a practice religious?
3. If no single feature is necessary, what **cluster** is sufficient?

✅ A necessary condition must be present in every case. Buddhism, Jainism and classical Mīmāṃsā are counterexamples to creator-belief as necessary.

⚠️ Creator-belief is not obviously sufficient either. A bare philosophical assertion that a first cause exists need not generate worship, community, transformation or a religious way of life.

### 2. Ninian Smart and the multidimensional correction

✅ Ninian Smart’s comparative approach identifies doctrinal, mythic, ethical, ritual, experiential, social and material dimensions. Belief in a creator is not an indispensable eighth dimension. The point is not that all seven must be equally strong; it is that actual religions are complexes of practices and forms of life.

**Example and limit:** Buddhism displays doctrine, narrative, ethics, meditation, Saṅgha and material culture. This shows that creator-belief is unnecessary. It does not show that any system with several social practices is religious, because the practices are oriented to an ultimate diagnosis and liberation.

### 3. The working sufficient cluster

Use this in an answer:

> Religion is a socially embodied and disciplined orientation to an ultimate or sacred order, expressed through some combination of doctrine, ritual, ethical formation, experience and community, and directed toward transformation or liberation.

This is a **sufficient cluster**, not a claim that every listed item is separately necessary. Two elements do most of the discriminating work:

- a disciplined relation to an ultimate or sacred order;
- a transformative or soteriological goal.

That pair admits Buddhism, Jainism, Mīmāṃsā and Sāṃkhya-Yoga. It excludes ordinary nationalism because political belonging does not, merely by being intense, diagnose bondage and offer liberation from it. It excludes Cārvāka because Cārvāka removes the sacred order and soteriology as well as God.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A God-centred definition reflects what theistic believers mean by religion and avoids diluting the category into ethics or culture.

**Reply:** It is legitimate as a definition of **theistic religion**, but circular as a cross-cultural definition of religion. It cannot disqualify established traditions solely because they falsify its preferred essence.

**Residual:** Family resemblance requires judgement. There is no algorithm that settles every borderline form of civil religion, secular spirituality or ethical humanism.

### UPSC application

- **2018 Q5(c):** State the definition in the first three lines, then justify the possibility through an actual case.
- **2020 Q6(c):** “Prerequisite” tests necessity; one defensible counterexample can defeat a universal necessity claim.
- **Trap:** “Religion is whatever gives meaning” is too broad.
- **Answer use:** Definition → counterexample → theist’s objection → repaired cluster → qualified verdict.

### Revision notes

1. The topic concerns the necessity of God for religion, not the existence of God.
2. A necessary condition must occur in every religion.
3. A sufficient condition is enough for classification even if not universal.
4. God-centred substantive definitions are vulnerable to question-begging.
5. Functional definitions are vulnerable to over-breadth.
6. Smart analyses religion through multiple dimensions.
7. Family resemblance rejects one essence shared by every case.
8. The working cluster joins sacred order to disciplined transformation.
9. Doctrine, ritual, ethics, experience and community support the cluster.
10. Buddhism is the strongest actual counterexample to creator-necessity.
11. Cārvāka tests whether the cluster excludes too much secular philosophy.
12. A strong answer states and consistently applies its definition.

B. A functional definition centred on transformation and liberation

- **B — Incorrect:** Transformation and liberation can organise a path without a creator, so this account opens rather than closes the possibility.
- **C — Incorrect:** A dimensional account permits different traditions to realise the religious pattern through different combinations of features.

### Visual first — test the functions one by one

| Religious function | Does it require a personal deity? | Non-theistic form |
|---|---|---|
| Petitionary prayer | Normally yes; petition presupposes a responsive addressee | Meditation, aspiration and recitation are not petition in the same sense |
| Propositional revelation from God | Yes by definition; revelation has a divine speaker | Mīmāṃsā claims authorless authority, not divine revelation |
| Grace and providence | Yes in the personal-agency sense | Self-effort, causal law and communal support do different work |
| Worship or veneration | No | Reverence for Dharma, scripture or liberated exemplars |
| Ritual efficacy | No | *Apūrva*, vows, meditation and karmic discipline |
| Moral formation | No | Compassion, non-injury, karma, vows and insight |
| Liberation | No as a generic function | *Nirvāṇa*, Jain release and *kaivalya* |

```text
RELIGIOUS LIFE WITHOUT CREATOR-BELIEF

SACRED ORDER ---> gives orientation and standards
RITUAL --------> embodies and rehearses commitment
ETHICS --------> forms character and disciplines desire
COMMUNITY -----> transmits practice and sustains identity
EXPERIENCE ----> makes ultimacy existentially felt
LIBERATION ----> gives the path a final direction

Missing personal goods: petition, covenant, providence, grace-as-gift
Surviving religious structure: path, discipline, reverence, community, release
```

*Removing a creator changes the form of religious life; it does not automatically remove religious life.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition: a job description

Instead of asking vaguely whether religion “needs God”, ask which jobs God performs. Some jobs logically require a person. A request needs someone who can hear it. Forgiveness as an act of grace needs someone who grants it. Other jobs need only a norm, practice, object of reverence or path.

✅ This is the **separability thesis**: authority, efficacy, moral motivation, community and liberation are distinct functions. A tradition can secure them through different mechanisms.

### 2. Sacred and ultimate are wider than God

The **sacred** is what a community treats as set apart, authoritative or of ultimate worth. **Ultimacy** is what orders other commitments and answers the deepest practical question. Neither concept logically entails a personal maker.

- Buddhist Dharma is authoritative teaching and order, but not a creator.
- Jain *siddhas* are perfected liberated beings, but not governors.
- Mīmāṃsā’s Veda is sacred and authorless.
- Sāṃkhya’s *kaivalya* is liberation through discrimination, not divine rescue.

**Example and limit:** A constitution may be treated with civic reverence. That analogy shows how reverence can be non-personal. It does not make constitutionalism a religion, because the complete sacred-soteriological cluster is absent.

### 3. Ritual, ethics and community

Ritual gives bodily and social form to commitment. Ethics disciplines action. Community preserves memory, corrects practice and sustains the learner. None requires a creator, though each can be interpreted theistically.

In Buddhism, the Saṅgha transmits doctrine and discipline. In Jainism, vows such as *ahiṃsā* reorder life. In Mīmāṃsā, ritual action is central. Their authority structures differ, but each shows that religion can be practice-centred rather than creator-centred.

### 4. Liberation and transcendence

**Transcendence** need not mean a supernatural person outside the world. It can mean overcoming ordinary ego, ignorance, bondage or suffering. Non-theistic soteriology retains a “beyond” of ordinary existence without turning that beyond into a creator.

⚠️ Do not say that Dharma, *nirvāṇa* or *apūrva* “replaces God” one for one. That invites the hidden-God objection. Say that they organise different functions without possessing divine personhood.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A path without grace, providence or a responsive “Thou” is philosophy or therapy, not religion.

**Reply:** The objection identifies a genuine loss in personal devotional structure, but assumes that the relational form of theism exhausts religion. Actual non-theistic traditions retain sacred order, ritual, community and liberation.

**Residual:** Non-theistic religion can be complete under a multidimensional definition while still lacking goods unique to personal theism. “Possible” does not mean “identical in texture”.

### UPSC application

- **2021 Q5(d):** Show how belief, practice and religious life differ; belief in God is one religious belief, not the whole form of life.
- **2019 Q6(c):** The theist’s strongest surviving point is devotional—petition, grace and covenant—not a bare definition.
- **Trap:** Do not claim that worship and petition are synonyms.
- **Answer use:** Divide functions into person-dependent and person-independent columns.

### Revision notes

1. Religious functions are separable.
2. Petition normally requires a responsive addressee.
3. Divine revelation requires a divine speaker by definition.
4. Grace and providence require personal agency in their theistic sense.
5. Veneration can be directed toward teaching or exemplars.
6. Ritual efficacy need not be administered by a deity.
7. Moral motivation can arise from compassion, vows, karma and insight.
8. Liberation needs a diagnosis and path, not necessarily a saviour.
9. Sacred and ultimate are wider categories than God.
10. Community transmits and disciplines a non-theistic path.
11. Non-theistic religion loses some relational goods without losing all religious structure.
12. Functional similarity does not establish ontological identity.

|---|---|---|---|
| Non-theism | Path/doctrine | A creator is not required or central | Yes; this is the core topic |

```text

ATHEISM:    "No God exists."
AGNOSTICISM:"I do not know" / "It cannot be known."
ANTI-THEISM:"Belief is harmful as well as false."
INDIFFERENCE:"This question does not organise my life."
```

*The terms answer different questions; using them interchangeably destroys precision.*

### 1. Non-theism is not atheism

✅ Non-theism describes the role of creator-belief inside a tradition. It can coexist with devas, bodhisattvas, liberated exemplars, ritual and devotion. Atheism makes the stronger existence-denial.

Theravāda Buddhism allows impermanent divine beings within *saṃsāra* while denying that they created the world or can provide final liberation. Jainism reveres liberated beings without treating them as makers. Both cases show why “non-theistic” is more accurate than “atheistic”.

### 2. Agnosticism

✅ T. H. Huxley coined “agnostic” in 1869. The basic position suspends judgement about God because adequate knowledge is unavailable.

- **Weak agnosticism:** God is not presently known.
- **Strong agnosticism:** God is in principle unknowable.

The 2023 question has two deliverables:

1. define and distinguish agnosticism;
2. explain how agnostics conceptualise the relation between religion and God.

An agnostic may treat God as an unresolved metaphysical hypothesis while valuing ethical discipline, ritual participation, contemplative experience or community. This supports **graded religiosity**: participation and orientation need not wait for metaphysical certainty.

### 3. Is an agnostic religious?

It depends on what is retained.

- Mere suspension plus no practice is not religion.
- Suspension plus inherited participation may be culturally religious.
- Suspension plus a disciplined orientation to ultimacy may satisfy the working cluster.

**Example and limit:** A person may practise Buddhist meditation and ethics while withholding all creator claims. The example proves compatibility between suspension and practice; it does not show that agnosticism by itself constitutes a religion.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Religion involves commitment, whereas agnosticism suspends commitment.

**Reply:** It suspends one proposition—God exists—not every practical commitment. A person can commit to a path, community and discipline while remaining epistemically modest.

**Residual:** The agnostic form may be thinner where worship depends on trust in a personal addressee. Suspension preserves some religious functions more easily than others.

### UPSC application

- **2023 Q5(e):** Use two body headings: “What agnosticism is” and “How it relates religion and God”.
- **Trap:** Do not define agnosticism as hesitant atheism.
- **Answer use:** Position → weak/strong distinction → retained functions → objection about commitment → qualified verdict.

### Revision notes

1. Non-theism concerns what a religious path requires.
2. Atheism denies divine existence.
3. Anti-theism adds the judgement that belief is harmful.
4. Agnosticism concerns knowledge, not directly existence.
5. Weak agnosticism means not known.
6. Strong agnosticism means unknowable.
7. Indifference is absence of concern, not a theory.
8. Non-theism can retain deities and devotion.
9. Agnosticism can coexist with ethical and contemplative practice.
10. Agnosticism alone is not a religion.
11. Graded religiosity separates metaphysical assent from practical participation.
12. Petitionary practice is harder to retain under suspension than meditation or ethics.

*Buddhism does not merely omit a creator; it supplies a complete diagnosis-and-path structure.*

### 1. Why the creator hypothesis is idle

✅ Early Buddhism does not place a creator in its account of suffering or liberation. *Pratītyasamutpāda*—dependent origination—explains conditioned arising. Karma explains morally structured consequences. The path removes ignorance and craving. A creator adds no necessary step to this therapeutic sequence.

This is an argument from **explanatory and soteriological redundancy**, not a deductive proof that no God exists.

1. The religious problem is *dukkha*.
2. *Dukkha* arises through conditions.
3. Removing the relevant conditions ends *dukkha*.
4. The path specifies that removal.
5. Creator-belief neither diagnoses nor cures the problem.
6. Therefore creator-belief is not necessary to Buddhist religious life.

### 2. The undeclared questions and the poisoned arrow

✅ The *avyākata* are questions left undeclared because they do not conduce to liberation. The poisoned-arrow illustration makes the practical point: demanding speculative answers before treating suffering delays the cure.

**Limit:** Neither silence nor therapeutic priority proves atheism. Use the parable to show why creator-speculation is religiously idle, not why every divine being is unreal.

### 3. Gods and devotion

✅ Buddhist cosmology may include devas and Brahmā figures, but they are impermanent beings within *saṃsāra*. They are neither creators nor final saviours.

Later Mahāyāna devotion to Buddhas and bodhisattvas deepens worship, aspiration and grace-like practices. Yet performing some religious functions does not turn these figures into an omnipotent creator. Non-theistic does not mean devotion-free.

### 4. Evaluation

**Why Buddhism is decisive:** It is not a philosophical possibility invented to refute a definition. It is a historically established religion displaying doctrine, ritual, ethics, experience, community and liberation.

**Main qualification:** If religion is defined as covenant with a personal creator, Buddhism is excluded—but that is the definition under challenge. If religion is defined through a sacred-soteriological cluster, Buddhism is paradigmatic.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Dharma, karma and *nirvāṇa* simply occupy the place of God, so Buddhism is covert theism.

**Reply:** Functional contribution does not imply ontological identity. None is a personal world-maker who wills, commands, creates and providentially governs.

**Residual:** Devotional Mahāyāna complicates any picture of Buddhism as purely impersonal. The correct conclusion is non-creator religion, not religion stripped of all devotion.

### UPSC application

- **2024 Q7(a), 20 marks:** “State” requires the full Buddhist structure before “evaluate”.
- **2018/2020/2021:** Buddhism is the strongest counterexample to necessity.
- **Trap:** Do not write “Buddha denied all gods.”
- **Answer use:** Definition → non-creator diagnosis → religious dimensions → devotional qualification → objection/reply → verdict.

### Revision notes

1. Buddhism is non-theistic rather than simply atheistic.
2. Dependent origination explains conditioned arising.
3. Karma supplies moral-causal order without a divine distributor.
4. Four Noble Truths form diagnosis, cause, cessation and path.
5. The Eightfold Path integrates wisdom, ethics and meditation.

*The three systems remove creatorhood for different reasons; they must not be blended into one generic “Indian atheism”.*

### 1. Jainism: no creator, but a rich sacred order

✅ Jainism affirms innumerable *jīvas*, karmic matter, bondage, vows and liberation. The perfected *siddha* is an achieved divinity: liberated, passionless and worthy of veneration, but not a maker, governor or dispenser of grace.

Two anti-creator pressures are especially useful:

1. **Motive problem:** A perfect being lacks an unfulfilled need that creation would satisfy. If God creates from desire, perfection is compromised; if without purpose, creation becomes arbitrary.
2. **Embodiment/action problem:** Acting on matter seems to require instruments or embodiment. An embodied creator is limited; a bodiless creator’s causal action needs explanation.

Jain worship is therefore veneration and emulation rather than petition to an intervening Lord.

### 2. Sāṃkhya: order without intelligent design

✅ Classical Sāṃkhya explains the world through the evolution of *prakṛti*, constituted by the three *guṇas*, in the presence of plural conscious *puruṣas*. Liberation occurs through *viveka-khyāti*, discriminative insight that consciousness is distinct from nature.

The milk-for-the-calf analogy illustrates apparently purposive activity without conscious planning. **Limit:** The analogy makes non-conscious purposiveness conceivable; it does not by itself prove that cosmic order has no intelligence behind it.

### 3. Yoga’s Īśvara

✅ Patañjali’s Īśvara is a special *puruṣa*, untouched by affliction, action and karmic result. Devotion to Īśvara can aid concentration. Yet importing the full creator of classical theism into this role would erase the distinction under examination.

⚠️ Yoga is a qualified counterexample: it retains a Lord-like object of devotion while not making world-creation the indispensable basis of liberation.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Unconscious matter or karmic law cannot coordinate purposive order and just results; intelligence is required.

**Reply:** The objection models nature on artifact production. Non-theistic systems treat regularity as law-like and liberation as dependent on impersonal causal relations rather than divine planning.

**Residual:** The intelligibility of purposive-looking order remains a live metaphysical dispute. These systems show that creator-belief is not required by their religious practice, even if their cosmologies remain contestable.

- **Answer use:** Compare the function retained—exemplar, impersonal evolution, devotional aid—rather than merely listing schools.

### Visual first — split the jobs of a theistic God

```text
THEISTIC MODEL

MIMAMSA SEPARATION
Veda is authorless --------> authority without a speaker
```

| Concept | Plain meaning | Philosophical work |
|---|---|---|
| *Apauruṣeya* | Not authored by any person | Avoids dependence on a fallible human or divine speaker |
| *Codanā* | Vedic injunction | Makes duty known where perception and inference cannot |
| *Svataḥ-prāmāṇya* | Cognition is valid in itself unless defeated | Gives default authority without external certification |
| *Apūrva* | Newly generated unseen potency | Connects a perishing ritual act to a later result |
| *Arthāpatti* | Postulation | Licenses *apūrva* as explanation of an otherwise puzzling delayed fruit |

*Mīmāṃsā is powerful because it removes divine authorship, command and reward-distribution separately.*

### 1. Authorless is not revealed

✅ The Veda is *apauruṣeya*: authorless and eternal. Its authority therefore does not depend on proving that a human or divine speaker is reliable. Calling it “revelation” reinstates the revealer that the doctrine removes.

Jaimini’s *Mīmāṃsā-sūtra* 1.1.2, *codanā-lakṣaṇo ’rtho dharmaḥ*, defines dharma as that beneficial object characterised by Vedic injunction. Dharma cannot be perceived, because it concerns unseen duty and result; it is known through injunction.

### 2. Intrinsic validity

✅ *Svataḥ-prāmāṇya* means cognition presents itself as valid unless later defeated. Mīmāṃsā does not first require a divine guarantor to certify every cognition or Vedic statement.

**Limit:** Default trust must still explain error. The standard response is that falsity is detected through later defeating cognition; error is parasitic upon an otherwise trusting epistemic practice.

### 3. The argument for *apūrva*

1. A Vedic injunction promises a result such as heaven from a correctly performed sacrifice.
2. The ritual act is momentary and ceases.
3. The promised result occurs much later.
4. A wholly vanished act cannot directly produce a remote result.
5. Therefore the act generates a persisting, unseen link.
6. That postulated link is *apūrva*.

✅ *Apūrva* is not perceived. It is admitted through *arthāpatti*, postulation of what must be assumed to explain an otherwise inexplicable relation.

### 4. Kumārila and Prabhākara

- **Kumārila Bhaṭṭa:** *Apūrva* is treated as a potency in the act or agent connecting deed and fruit.
- **Prabhākara Miśra:** Emphasises *niyoga* or *kārya*, the “ought-to-be-done” disclosed by injunction.

⚠️ The contrast has an ethical payoff: Kumārila’s account stresses a mechanism of desert; Prabhākara’s is closer to duty grounded in the injunction itself.

### 5. Nyāya’s superintendent objection

**Objection:** An unconscious potency cannot select the correct fruit for the correct agent at the correct time. An intelligent *karmādhyakṣa* is needed.

**Reply:** The relation can be law-like rather than deliberative. Physical laws produce determinate outcomes without choosing them. Requiring an agent assumes the very thesis under dispute.

**Residual:** *Apūrva* may look like an explanatory placeholder. Mīmāṃsā must show why an unseen potency is less costly than an intelligent distributor. Its best defence is theoretical economy: it postulates only the causal bridge required by its ritual commitments.

### UPSC application

- **2020 Q6(c):** Mīmāṃsā is the strongest case that scriptural authority, duty and efficacy can be secured without a creator.
- **Trap:** Never call the Veda God’s revelation.
- **Trap:** Never call *apūrva* an observed force.
- **Answer use:** Three separations—authority, obligation, fruit—then Nyāya’s allocation objection and the law-like reply.

### Revision notes

1. Classical Mīmāṃsā is a ritual religion without a necessary creator.
2. *Apauruṣeya* means authorless.
3. Authorless scripture is not divine revelation.
4. *Codanā* means injunction.
5. Dharma is known through Vedic injunction.

*Cārvāka is not the clearest religion without God; it is the contrast that shows what such religions still retain.*

### 1. The epistemic foundation

✅ Cārvāka gives perception (*pratyakṣa*) privileged or exclusive status as a means of knowledge. The 2025 question is therefore epistemological, not a request for hedonist slogans.

Inference (*anumāna*) requires *vyāpti*, invariant concomitance between the reason (*hetu*) and what is inferred (*sādhya*). The attack asks how this universal relation is known.

1. Perception reaches particular cases, not every past, future and distant case.
2. Inference cannot establish its own *vyāpti* without circularity or regress.
3. Testimony lacks independent authority for the Cārvāka.
4. Repeated observation cannot exclude every hidden condition (*upādhi*).
5. Therefore deviation (*vyabhicāra*) remains possible.
6. Inference may guide expectation, but it cannot certify supersensible entities.

### 2. Applications to religious belief

- **God:** Never perceived; inference from world-effect to maker inherits the *vyāpti* problem.
- **Soul:** Consciousness is a property emerging from the organised body (*dehātmavāda*).
- **Analogy:** Intoxicating power arises from combined fermenting ingredients although absent from each separately.
- **Karma and rebirth:** Imperceptible postulates lacking evidential warrant.
- **Veda:** Testimonial authority rejected.
- **Order:** *Svabhāva-vāda* explains things through their own nature, not design.

**Example and limit:** The fermentation analogy makes emergence intelligible. It does not provide a modern scientific theory of consciousness.

### 3. The strongest criticisms

**Self-refutation:** “No inference is valid” is itself a universal claim not perceived.

**Pragmatic inconsistency:** Daily action, language and argument rely on inference.

**Nyāya reply:** *Vyāpti* is supported by repeated observation, absence of counterinstances and *tarka*, hypothetical reasoning.

**Parity objection:** Perception also errs, so certainty cannot uniquely favour it.

### 4. The best Cārvāka replies

⚠️ **Dialectical reading:** The attack can be a *prasaṅga*, exposing problems in the opponent’s method without asserting a new universal proposition.

❓ **Reported Purandara strand:** Ordinary empirical inference (*laukika*) may be accepted while inference to what is beyond possible perception (*atīndriya*) is rejected. The evidence is fragmentary and transmitted by opponents, so present it as a reported nuance.

This moderate thesis is stronger: inference is legitimate where its regularities remain perceptually testable, not where no check is possible.

### 5. Why Cārvāka does not count as religion

Cārvāka lacks the sacred-soteriological cluster. It has no accepted supersensible order, no liberation from bondage, no sacred ritual efficacy and no community organised around transcendent transformation. It is a philosophical critique of religion.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** If ethical humanism can be religious under a broad functional definition, Cārvāka’s this-worldly orientation could also qualify.

**Reply:** Mere this-worldly value is insufficient. The working cluster requires disciplined relation to an ultimate or sacred order and a transformative path. Cārvāka’s importance lies in refusing precisely those elements.

**Residual:** A maximally broad “religion as worldview” definition might include it, but then the category ceases to distinguish religion from philosophy.

*The four positions differ over truth-conditions, assertion and continued practice.*

### 1. Dewey: religion and “the religious”

✅ In *A Common Faith* (1934), John Dewey distinguishes institutional “religion” from “the religious”, a quality of experience that unifies the self around inclusive ideal ends. “God” can symbolise the active relation between the ideal and the actual rather than name a supernatural person.

This preserves orientation and transformation while revising traditional metaphysics.

### 2. Dworkin: religious atheism

✅ Ronald Dworkin’s *Religion without God* (2013) argues that one may reject a personal God yet affirm:

1. objective intrinsic value in human life;
2. the sublimity and intrinsic beauty of the universe.

The religious element lies in value realism and reverence, not creator-belief.

**Limit:** Objective value and cosmic sublimity may support a religious attitude, but do not automatically supply ritual, institution or a soteriology.

### 3. Religious fictionalism

✅ A fictionalist treats religious claims as having truth-conditions but declines to assert them, while participating for narrative, ethical or communal value. Don Cupitt’s non-realism and Robin Le Poidevin’s explicit fictionalist discussion are important modern landmarks.

Distinguish:

- **Fictionalism:** truth-apt claims, not asserted; practice retained.
- **Non-cognitivism:** utterances express attitudes or commitments rather than state facts.
- **Error theory:** claims are false and should be abandoned.
- **Advaita:** lower-level worship is valid within *vyāvahārika* reality and later sublated; it is not make-believe.
- **Buddhist two truths:** conventional practice is not merely fictional participation.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Worship without belief is play-acting. Its sincerity and motivational power depend on taking the object as real.

**Reply:** Humans can be sincerely transformed by narratives and rituals without literal assertion. Participation can express values and sustain community without deception if its stance is transparent.

**Residual:** Fictionalism remains revisionary because many believers understand themselves as making real claims. It may preserve selected functions while failing to preserve religion as believers recognise it.

### UPSC application

- Use naturalism and fictionalism after the Indian cases, never instead of them.
- **Trap:** Naturalism asserts immanent ultimacy; fictionalism withholds assertion.
- **Trap:** Sublation is not fictionalism.
- **Answer use:** Present them as boundary tests showing how far religious functions detach from supernatural belief.

### Revision notes

1. Religious naturalism locates ultimacy within nature.
2. Dewey distinguishes religion from the religious quality of experience.
3. Dewey’s “God” can name the ideal–actual relation rather than a being.
4. Dworkin defends religious atheism.
5. Dworkin joins objective value to cosmic sublimity.
6. Naturalism asserts its immanent ontology.
7. Fictionalism retains practice without asserting religious claims.
8. Fictionalism differs from non-cognitivism.
9. Fictionalism differs from error theory.
10. Cupitt is associated with non-realism.
11. Le Poidevin develops explicit fictionalism.
12. Advaitic sublation is not pretence.
13. The sincerity objection remains powerful.

*These thinkers criticise or reconstruct theistic religion; they do not all provide examples of religion without God.*

### 1. Projection and consolation

✅ Feuerbach’s thesis is commonly summarised as “theology is anthropology”: humanity projects its idealised essence outward and then worships it.

✅ Marx’s “opium of the people” occurs in a passage that also calls religion the sigh of the oppressed creature and heart of a heartless world. Religion consoles real suffering while potentially obscuring its material causes.

✅ Freud treats religion as illusion and wish-fulfilment, shaped by dependence and father-projection.

These are genealogical or explanatory critiques. Even if an origin is correctly identified, a further argument is needed to show that the belief is false; otherwise the genetic fallacy threatens.

### 2. Comte and the Religion of Humanity

Comte deliberately retains calendars, rituals, solidarity and reverence while replacing supernatural theology with Humanity. It tests the outer boundary of functional religion.

**Limit:** If no sacred order or liberation beyond social cohesion remains, the label may be honorific rather than classificatory.

### 3. Nietzsche: “God is dead”

✅ In *The Gay Science* §125, the madman announces the death of God. The phrase is a cultural diagnosis: the Christian-metaphysical framework has become unbelievable under the pressures of modern truthfulness and critique.

The argument:

1. Inherited European values depended upon a theological-metaphysical horizon.
2. The culture’s own intellectual honesty corrodes that horizon.
3. Values continue temporarily after their ground has weakened.
4. The result is the danger of nihilism.
5. Passive nihilism resigns; active revaluation attempts self-overcoming and new valuation.

Nietzsche’s genealogy of slave morality and *ressentiment* criticises life-denying valuation. His route is not a proof of atheism and not a ready-made non-theistic religion.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Once God is removed, revaluation becomes arbitrary power or elitist self-assertion.

**Reply:** Nietzsche appeals to disciplined self-overcoming, honesty and life-enhancement rather than mere whim.

**Residual:** He lacks an uncontroversial universal standard for distinguishing creative value from domination. This makes Nietzsche a powerful diagnosis of post-theistic crisis but a less secure foundation for shared religion.

- **Trap:** “God is dead” is not a biological or deductive claim.
- **Answer use:** Critique → genetic-fallacy caution → what religious functions survive → residual problem of normativity.

| Salvation | *Nirvāṇa*, Jain release, *kaivalya* | Personal grace is absent |
| Ultimacy | Dharma, sacred order, nature and objective value | Impersonal ultimacy may feel existentially thin |

*The best conclusion is neither “God is irrelevant” nor “God is indispensable”, but a function-by-function verdict.*

### 1. The category objection

**Theist:** Religion is essentially an I–Thou relation with a transcendent personal reality. “Religion without God” is like a triangle without three sides.

**Response:** That definition captures theism but excludes actual non-theistic traditions by stipulation. A cross-cultural category should explain rather than erase the cases.

**Residual:** Definitions are partly shaped by traditions. The debate cannot be settled by dictionary alone.

### 2. The worship objection

**Theist:** Worship is owed only to a supreme personal being. Reverence for a teaching or exemplar is admiration, not religion.

**Response:** Jain veneration, Buddhist refuge and ritual devotion show that acknowledgement of supreme worth can be religious without petition or creatorhood.

**Residual:** Petition, covenant and reciprocal love do require personhood. Non-theistic worship is structurally different.

### 3. The obligation objection

**Theist:** Without a divine commander and judge, moral norms lack binding force and karmic allocation lacks intelligence.

**Response:** Buddhist compassion, Jain vows, Mīmāṃsā injunction and secular moral realism offer alternative grounds. Law-like karmic order does not logically require deliberation.

**Residual:** Each alternative owes its own account of objectivity. Removing divine command shifts rather than eliminates metaethical work.

### 4. The grace and salvation objection

**Theist:** Human effort cannot overcome radical bondage; salvation must be given.

**Response:** That claim belongs to grace-centred theologies. Buddhism diagnoses conditioned suffering and prescribes causal removal; Jainism removes karmic accretion; Sāṃkhya corrects misidentification.

**Residual:** Non-theistic liberation lacks the interpersonal reconciliation central to many theistic traditions.

### 5. The ultimacy objection

**Theist:** An impersonal order cannot love, answer, judge or ground hope. It collapses into philosophy or aesthetics.

**Response:** Religion may orient life through truth, sacred order, disciplined transformation and community without a cosmic person. Buddhism demonstrates existential depth without creator-belief.

**Residual:** Modern naturalism and fictionalism remain more vulnerable than classical non-theistic religions because their soteriology and communal continuity may be thinner.

### 6. Final graded verdict

> A personal creator is sufficient for many religions but not necessary for religion as a cross-cultural category. God is necessary for specifically personal religious goods—petition, covenant, providence and grace-as-gift—but not for sacred order, ritual, ethical formation, community or liberation. Religion without God is therefore actual, not merely conceivable; religion without any sacred order or transformative discipline is not.

This verdict avoids three errors:

- reducing religion to theism;
- broadening religion into every intense commitment;
- pretending that non-theistic and theistic religious lives are identical.

### UPSC application

- **2019 Q6(c):** Spend substantial space constructing the theist’s case before replying.
- **2018/2020/2021:** Use the graded verdict, not a flat yes/no.
- **2022 and 2026:** State explicitly that the audited ledgers contain zero primary-owned questions in both years; absence does not reduce priority.
- **Trap:** Do not answer “How would a religious person deny…” solely from the non-theist’s side.

**Model answer**

A religion without a creator is not defined merely by the absence of God. It retains a socially embodied relation to a sacred or ultimate order, disciplined practice and a transformative or soteriological goal. A secular ethical philosophy may prescribe virtues and reasons for action, yet lack ritual, sacred orientation, community and liberation from a diagnosed existential predicament.

Buddhism illustrates the difference. Dependent origination and karma organise its diagnosis; the Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path direct practice; the Saṅgha embodies the path; *nirvāṇa* is its liberative goal. None requires a creator. Cārvāka, by contrast, rejects God together with soul, karma, rebirth and liberation. It is therefore a critique of religion rather than a religion without God.

The distinction is not absolute at every boundary: religious naturalism may combine ethics, awe and community. Still, sacred orientation plus disciplined transformation provides a better discriminator than either creator-belief or ethics alone.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** “The functions attributed to God are separable.” Examine this claim with reference to Mīmāṃsā and Buddhism. Answer in about 250 words.

**Model answer**

The separability claim holds that scriptural authority, moral obligation, ritual efficacy, providence and liberation are distinct religious functions; they need not all be discharged by one divine person.

Mīmāṃsā makes the thesis explicit. The Veda is *apauruṣeya*, so authority requires no speaker. Dharma is known through *codanā*, Vedic injunction, rather than divine command. Ritual action generates *apūrva*, an unseen potency postulated to connect a perishing act with its delayed fruit. Thus authorship, command and reward-distribution are relocated within scripture and ritual. Nyāya objects that an unconscious potency cannot allocate the correct fruit. Mīmāṃsā replies that law-like determination need not deliberate, though *apūrva* remains metaphysically costly.

Buddhism separates the functions differently. Dependent origination and karma explain conditioned suffering; the Four Noble Truths diagnose it; the Eightfold Path supplies ethical and meditative transformation; the Saṅgha sustains practice; *nirvāṇa* is liberation. A creator neither explains an omitted gap nor grants the goal.

The thesis is not unlimited. Petition requires a hearer, revelation from God a speaker, and grace a giver. Therefore the functions are substantially separable, but specifically interpersonal religious goods remain deity-dependent. God is not necessary for religion as such, though indispensable within some forms of theistic life.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** Is “religion without God” a coherent category, or does it reduce religion to ethics, therapy and culture? Critically discuss with Indian and modern examples. Answer in about 350 words.

**Model answer**

The category is coherent only if it avoids two extremes. A substantive definition that builds a personal creator into religion excludes Buddhism by stipulation. An unrestricted functional definition admits nationalism and lifestyle movements. A defensible middle defines religion as socially embodied discipline oriented to a sacred or ultimate order and directed toward transformation or liberation.

Buddhism is the strongest counterexample to creator-necessity. Dependent origination and karma organise its causal order; the Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path supply diagnosis and cure; ethics, meditation, Saṅgha and *nirvāṇa* complete a religious form of life. Devas remain impermanent and non-creative, while Mahāyāna devotion shows that non-theism need not be devotion-free.

Jainism similarly retains souls, karmic bondage, vows and liberated *siddhas* without a maker. Mīmāṃsā separates functions still more sharply: authorless Veda supplies authority, injunction discloses duty and *apūrva* links ritual to fruit. Sāṃkhya grounds bondage and *kaivalya* in the relation between *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*.

Cārvāka marks the limit. By rejecting the epistemic warrant for God, soul, karma, rebirth and liberation, it removes not only the creator but the sacred-soteriological structure. It is therefore not a religion without God.

Modern religious naturalism locates reverence and objective value within nature; fictionalism retains practice without literal assertion. These positions extend the category but face stronger objections of thinness and insincerity than the classical Indian cases.

The theist rightly notes that petition, covenant, providence and grace require a personal reality. Yet this proves only that some religious goods are theistic. Religion without God is actual and coherent where sacred order, disciplined community and liberation remain; religion reduced to ethics or sentiment alone is not.
