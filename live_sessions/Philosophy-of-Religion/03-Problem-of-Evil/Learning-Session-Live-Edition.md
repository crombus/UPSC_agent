## Lesson 1 — Which Problem of Evil Is Being Asked?

Progress: 1/17 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Four burdens, defence, theodicy, explanation, and justification

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — four questions hidden inside one phrase

```text
EVIL EXISTS
   │
   ├─ LOGICAL: Can God and any evil coexist without contradiction?
   ├─ EVIDENTIAL: Does this amount and pattern of evil make God unlikely?
   ├─ EXISTENTIAL: Can trust, meaning, or prayer survive devastating suffering?
   └─ MORAL-PROTEST: Is it decent to justify another person's agony as a means?

Different question → different burden → different adequate reply
```

*The visual prevents the central error of treating one successful reply as a complete solution.*

### 1. Begin with the human question

A person may ask, “Why did this happen?” That sentence can request a physical cause, a moral explanation, a divine reason, or help in continuing to live. Philosophy becomes confused when it answers one request while pretending to answer all four.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** The classical intellectual problem is sharpest where God is described as omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, creator, and sustainer. Such a being would know every suffering, possess immense preventive power, oppose evil, and bear some responsibility for the framework in which creatures act.

### 2. Four burdens

| Burden | What must be shown | Typical thinker or response |
|---|---|---|
| Logical | The relevant propositions are inconsistent, or possibly compatible | Mackie; Plantinga |
| Evidential | The observed evidence is more or less expected given God | Rowe; Draper; skeptical theism |
| Existential | A sufferer can retain meaning, relation, or protest | Job; Dostoevsky; Camus; religious lament |
| Moral-pastoral | Explanation does not instrumentalise or blame the sufferer | Anti-theodicy; Adams; compassion-first practice |

### 3. Defence is not theodicy

- A **defence** offers a logically possible story sufficient to block contradiction. It need not claim that the story is God's actual reason.
- A **theodicy** claims to state God's actual or probable morally sufficient reason for permitting evil.
- A causal **explanation** says how an evil occurred.
- A moral **justification** says why allowing it was permissible.

**Example with limit:** “A virus caused the illness” explains mechanism but does not justify why a good creator permits viruses. “Freedom possibly requires risk” may defend compatibility but does not show why this particular suffering occurred. The example establishes burden control; it does not decide theism.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Dividing the problem into types lets the theist retreat whenever one argument becomes difficult.

**Reply:** The distinctions do not weaken evidence. They prevent an invalid inference. A possible-world reply may defeat contradiction while Rowe's probability argument and a sufferer's protest remain fully alive.

⚠️ **Residual:** A total theology must eventually address all four burdens, but no single paragraph should pretend to do so.

### UPSC application

Open every answer by naming the version: logical, evidential, existential, horrendous, animal, distributional, or religion-forming. Then state the relevant God-concept. This immediately fixes the scope of the conclusion.

### Revision notes

1. The topic is a family of problems, not one argument.
2. Logical possibility is weaker than actual probability.
3. Explanation is not justification.
4. Defence claims possibility; theodicy claims an actual or probable reason.
5. The existential problem concerns lived trust and meaning.
6. Moral protest asks whether explanation itself can become ethically offensive.
7. Classical creator-theism faces the maximum burden.
8. A successful local reply leaves other burdens open.
9. Pastoral restraint is not an abandonment of philosophical rigour.
10. Name the version in the first two lines of a UPSC answer.

### Local practice

B. It proves that every actual evil produces a greater good.

- **A — Correct:** A defence has the limited modal task of blocking an alleged contradiction.
- **B — Incorrect:** Proving an actual universal purpose would be a far stronger theodical claim.
- **C — Incorrect:** A physical mechanism is an explanation, not necessarily a defence of divine goodness.
- **D — Incorrect:** Logical compatibility does not cancel existential or moral protest.

- **A — Incorrect:** Spelling has no bearing on the philosophical burden.
- **B — Correct:** A possibility defence does not establish that this world's evils are probable under perfect providence.

## Lesson 2 — What Counts as Evil?

Progress: 2/17 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Moral, natural, metaphysical, structural, first-order, and second-order categories

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — Hick's ostensive account of pain, suffering, and wickedness; canonical classifications; complete learner package hard-case sessions.
CA search: "official global disaster conflict suffering ethics September 2026 problem of evil"
CA found: No single current event is used as a philosophical premise; public disasters and conflicts remain examples requiring factual caution and non-instrumental treatment.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — classify before explaining

```text
wrongdoing by agents ───────────────→ MORAL EVIL
non-voluntary natural processes ───→ NATURAL EVIL
institutions and accumulated acts ─→ STRUCTURAL EVIL
finitude or limitation ────────────→ METAPHYSICAL EVIL
direct pain or loss ───────────────→ FIRST-ORDER EVIL
virtue responding to vulnerability → SECOND-ORDER GOOD
```

| Category | Source | Example | Why the distinction matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moral evil | Responsible agency | Cruelty, betrayal, oppression | Free-will replies may apply |
| Natural evil | Non-voluntary natural process | Disease, earthquake, predation | Free will is insufficient |
| Structural evil | Institutions and accumulated choices | Entrenched exploitation | Responsibility is distributed |
| Metaphysical evil | Finitude or limitation | A creature is not omniscient | Limitation is not automatically wickedness |
| First-order evil | Direct harm or loss | Pain, death, deprivation | Requires explanation in its own right |
| Second-order response | Reaction to vulnerability | Courage, compassion | A resulting good does not automatically justify the harm |

*Classification stops a response designed for one source of evil from being misapplied to another.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

If a person deliberately injures another, asking about freedom is relevant. If an infant suffers from disease or an animal is caught in a natural fire, that answer does not reach the source. If a social system distributes avoidable misery without one controlling wrongdoer, an individual-choice account is again incomplete.

### 2. Moral and natural evil

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** **Moral evil** arises through culpable agency. **Natural evil** includes pain and destruction not directly caused by a responsible human choice. Natural processes can be worsened by moral failure, but the categories should not be collapsed. A flood may be natural in origin while negligent planning magnifies its human cost.

### 3. Metaphysical evil

Leibnizian language sometimes calls creaturely limitation “metaphysical evil.” Finitude means a creature lacks some perfections. But not every lack is a harm: a human being's inability to be everywhere is not equivalent to disease or injustice. The term is useful only when carefully distinguished from moral wickedness and experienced suffering.

### 4. First-order evil and second-order good

Courage presupposes danger; compassion presupposes need. This shows that certain virtues require vulnerability. It does **not** show that any degree of danger or every victim's suffering is necessary.

**Example with limit:** A difficult examination can develop discipline. Torture cannot be justified by saying that someone else might learn compassion. The analogy establishes proportionality and personal respect; it does not prove that all ordinary hardship is divinely arranged.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** “Evil” is too evaluative and culturally variable for one neutral classification.

**Reply:** Hick's ostensive method begins from widely recognisable pain, mental suffering, and moral wickedness rather than defining evil by one theology. Disagreement remains at the edges, but severe involuntary suffering and culpable cruelty provide shared cases.

⚠️ **Residual:** The deepest debate concerns not whether suffering exists but whether it is gratuitous, deserved, necessary, defeasible, or finally redeemable.

### UPSC application

When the question says “both moral and natural evil,” divide the body accordingly. Do not let success on moral evil silently stand in for success on disease, disaster, animal pain, or evolutionary history.

### Revision notes

1. Moral evil concerns culpable agency.
2. Natural evil is not directly produced by responsible human choice.
3. Structural evil emerges through institutions and accumulated agency.
4. Metaphysical limitation is not automatically moral evil.
5. First-order evil is direct harm.
6. Second-order goods arise in response to vulnerability.
7. A good caused after suffering is not automatically a justification for permitting it.
8. Mixed cases require causal layering.
9. Animal and infant suffering defeat simplistic punishment accounts.
10. Classification determines which theodicy is even relevant.

## Lesson 3 — Mackie's Logical Problem and Its Hidden Bridges

Progress: 3/17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Inconsistent set, additional premises, adequate and fallacious solutions

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — Mackie's “Evil and Omnipotence”; canonical logical-problem dossier; Oxford Handbook defence/theodicy distinction.
CA search: "J L Mackie logical problem of evil recent scholarly discussion 2026"
CA found: No recent event alters the 1955 argument; primary text and current canonical scholarship control.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — where contradiction actually enters

```text
1. God is omnipotent
2. God is perfectly good
3. Evil exists
              │
              │ not formally contradictory alone
              ▼
4. Perfect goodness eliminates every evil it can
5. Omnipotence can eliminate every evil
              │
              ▼
If 1–5 are unrestricted, 3 should be false
```

*The “bridging premises” convert three compatible-looking claims into an alleged inconsistent set.*

### 1. Mackie's challenge

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** In “Evil and Omnipotence” (1955), J. L. Mackie argues that traditional theism affirms propositions that become inconsistent once familiar assumptions about goodness and power are added. Omniscience is not one of the familiar three propositions, but it blocks the reply that God simply failed to notice an evil.

The dilemma often attributed to Epicurus is safer described as transmitted by Lactantius and traditionally associated with Epicurus; no surviving Epicurean text contains the popular wording.

### 2. Why the bridges are contestable

A good being may permit a lesser evil where preventing it would remove an equal or greater good, violate agency, or produce an equal or worse evil. An omnipotent being cannot actualise a contradiction, such as a choice that is both causally determined by God and libertarianly free in the same respect.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Once these qualifications are admitted, the strict contradiction is no longer automatic. But the theist now owes an account of morally sufficient reasons and feasible alternatives.

### 3. Mackie's “solutions”

Mackie attacks replies that appear to preserve all original claims while covertly weakening one:

- “Good cannot exist without evil” can limit divine power if taken as a necessary relation.
- “Evil is necessary as a means to good” requires necessity and proportionality.
- “The universe is better with some evil” risks treating sufferers as ingredients in an aesthetic whole.
- Free will must explain why God could not create free agents who always choose well.

### 4. Example with limit

A surgeon may cause pain to prevent a greater harm. The case shows that goodness need not eliminate every pain immediately. It does not show that every evil resembles necessary surgery: consent, evidence, proportionality, competence, and benefit-to-the-patient matter.

### 5. Strongest objection, reply, residual

**Objection to Mackie:** Plantinga needs only one coherent possible reason to show that God and moral evil are compatible.

**Reply:** That defeats the universal logical claim only if libertarian freedom and feasible-world limits are coherent.

⚠️ **Residual:** Even after compatibility is established, the observed scale and distribution may remain improbable and morally disturbing.

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQ — 2020 Q6(b), 15 marks:** “What is the logical problem of evil? Discuss.”

**Approach only:** State the triad → add the two bridging premises → explain why the bare triad is not formally inconsistent → present free-will compatibility → distinguish logical defeat from evidential survival → conclude with the shifted burden.

### Revision notes

1. Mackie's strict charge needs additional moral and modal premises.
2. Omniscience closes the ignorance escape.
3. Goodness may permit harm only for a morally sufficient reason.
4. Omnipotence excludes contradictions, not genuine powers.
5. A defence needs possibility, not an actual divine biography.
6. “Necessary for good” requires a demonstrated connection.
7. World-level beauty cannot ignore goodness to persons.
8. Plantinga shifts the debate rather than ending the topic.
9. Logical success can coexist with evidential failure.
10. The best conclusion states what remains unresolved.

## Lesson 4 — Omnipotence Paradox and the Limits of Power

Progress: 4/17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Stone paradox, logically possible action, freedom, and power-revision

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — canonical omnipotence section, 2021 PYQ route, Mackie, Aquinas-style logical-possibility restriction, process contrast.
CA search: "omnipotence paradox philosophy of religion recent scholarly debate 2026"
CA found: None in the last six months that changes the standard paradox or its classical replies.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — the stone dilemma

```text
Can God create a stone God cannot lift?
       ├─ YES → there is something God cannot lift
       └─ NO  → there is something God cannot create

Classical reply:
“a stone an omnipotent being cannot lift”
is not a possible object but a contradictory description.
```

*The visual distinguishes an inability to perform a task from the absence of any coherent task to perform.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Power answers “What can an agent bring about?” A contradiction does not describe an outcome. “Make a square circle” does not name a difficult object. On the standard account, inability to actualise contradictions is not weakness.

### 2. Omnipotence and evil

The more difficult question is not whether God can do contradictions but whether God could create a different feasible world: freer, safer, less cruel, or more evenly protected. The critic therefore moves from the stone paradox to **responsibility for available alternatives**.

### 3. Two models of divine power

| Model | Claim | Evil-related cost |
|---|---|---|
| Classical power | God can do every logically possible act | Must explain non-prevention |
| Voluntary self-limitation | God can coerce but refrains for freedom or relationship | “Why not intervene here?” remains |
| Process power | Coercive control is metaphysically unavailable | Preserves innocence by revising sovereignty |

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Restricting power to the logically possible is harmless, but freedom with less catastrophic consequences appears logically possible.

**Reply:** A stable arena of agency may require regular consequences, and some creaturely counterfactuals may not be controllable without determining choice.

⚠️ **Residual:** Neither point establishes that the actual upper limit of harm is necessary.

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQ — 2021 Q5(e), 10 marks:** “Discuss the paradox of omnipotence of God in the context of the existence of evil.”

**Approach only:** State the stone dilemma → distinguish contradiction from task → connect omnipotence to prevention of evil → compare classical restriction and process revision → conclude that logical coherence does not settle divine responsibility.

### Revision notes

1. A contradiction is not an object of power.
2. The stone paradox tests concept-coherence.
3. Evil tests the use or structure of power.
4. Classical theism retains unilateral possible intervention.
5. Self-limitation is voluntary.
6. Process non-coercion is metaphysically necessary.
7. Restricting contradictions does not answer feasible-world comparison.
8. Freedom need not imply unlimited harmful capacity.
9. The key residual is preventable excess.
10. A 10-marker should present both horns and the qualified resolution.

## Lesson 5 — Plantinga's Free-Will Defence and Layered Responsibility

Progress: 5/17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Significant freedom, feasible worlds, transworld depravity, and creator responsibility

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — Plantinga, *God, Freedom, and Evil*; canonical free-will dossier; Oxford Handbook possible-world treatment; package responsibility modules.
CA search: "Plantinga free will defense recent philosophy of religion scholarship 2026"
CA found: No official current-affairs trigger; the established modal argument remains the controlling source.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — three levels of authorship

```text
CREATOR
  ├─ creates agent and action-framework
  ├─ permits a range of consequences
  └─ may or may not intervene
             ↓
FREE AGENT chooses
             ↓
MORAL EVIL and consequences

Direct authorship ≠ framework responsibility ≠ permission responsibility
```

*The visual allows human culpability and divine responsibility to be assessed together rather than as mutually exclusive.*

### 1. Significant freedom

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** A person is significantly free where an action is morally important and the person can perform or refrain from it without God's determining the choice. A world with such agency may contain goods impossible in a world of programmed behaviour: responsibility, love, fidelity, repentance, and moral authorship.

### 2. The modal argument

1. Significant freedom is a great good.
2. Causally guaranteed libertarian choice is contradictory.
3. It is possible that every world feasible for God with significantly free creatures contains some wrong choice.
4. If so, God cannot strongly actualise freedom plus guaranteed universal right action.
5. Therefore an omnipotent, good God and moral evil are logically compatible.

**Transworld depravity** is a possibility concerning creaturely essences across feasible worlds. It does not mean every actual person is always wicked, and Plantinga does not prove that transworld depravity actually obtains.

### 3. Why “Why not free but always good?” is not trivial

A logically describable world in which free creatures happen always to choose well may not be **feasible** for God if the truth of their undetermined choices is not under divine causal control. Critics then ask what grounds such counterfactual truths and why God did not choose different agents or narrower powers.

### 4. Natural evil and heaven

Plantinga's speculative non-human-agent extension may suffice for a bare possibility argument, but it is not a persuasive account of disease, tectonics, or evolution. The heaven objection adds: if confirmed free goodness is possible in heaven, why not create it immediately? Soul-making replies that formed character may require history; the critic asks why horrors are required for that history.

### 5. Divine responsibility

The free agent can be the direct author while God remains responsible for creating and sustaining the framework. Foreknowledge does not cause the choice, but knowingly selecting the whole system remains morally relevant.

**Example with limit:** A parent is not the author of an adult child's crime. Yet a guardian who knowingly provides an avoidable instrument of catastrophic harm may bear framework responsibility. The analogy establishes layered responsibility; divine creatorhood differs from human guardianship and therefore cannot be reduced to it.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply, residual

**Objection:** Meaningful freedom does not require the power to inflict unlimited harm.

**Reply:** Reliable consequences and serious agency require more than harmless choice.

## Lesson 6 — Augustine: Privation, Defective Will, Sin, and Punishment

Progress: 6/17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: *Privatio boni*, Fall, inherited disorder, and the natural-evil limit

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — Augustine through canonical dossier; Hick's Augustinian taxonomy and criticism; exact 2023 and 2026 PYQ controls.
CA search: "Augustine privation evil natural evil recent scholarly discussion 2026"
CA found: No recent official event changes the doctrine; 2026 UPSC Q6(a) makes the moral/natural split the current exam anchor.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — evil depends on a damaged good

```text
GOOD SUBJECT        DUE FUNCTION          PRIVATION
eye                 sight                 blindness
will                ordered love          disordered choice
community           justice and trust     corruption

evil is parasitic on good
      ≠
evil is imaginary
```

*The visual shows Augustine's metaphysical claim: evil is a real deprivation in something that is itself good.*

### 1. The problem Augustine addresses

If God creates everything and evil is a thing, God appears to create evil. Augustine rejects the Manichaean picture of good and evil as rival substances. Being, insofar as it is being, is good; evil is corruption, disorder, or absence of a good that ought to be present.

### 2. Defective will and moral evil

The will is a good capacity. It becomes morally evil when it turns from the highest good toward a lesser good in a disordered way. God creates the power of willing; the creature supplies the defect in use. This makes the free agent the source of culpable moral evil without positing an evil substance.

### 3. “All evil is either sin or punishment for sin”

Within the Augustinian scheme:

- **sin** is culpable defective willing;
- suffering and disorder can be treated as the **punishment or consequence of sin**;
- the Fall explains a disordered human and natural condition.

An inherited damaged condition is easier to defend than inherited personal guilt. Yet even the condition-view must explain why unequal effects fall on infants, animals, and later persons.

### 4. Natural evil limits

Privation describes disease as malfunction and decay as loss of integrity. That is metaphysically illuminating. It does not by itself justify God's permission. Pre-human predation, evolutionary suffering, and natural disaster strain a historical claim that nature became harmful as punishment for a human Fall.

Hick and Schleiermacher press a further question: how can a flawless creature in a flawless environment spontaneously choose evil? If the defect has no cause, the fall seems unintelligible; if it has a cause, original perfection is qualified.

### 5. Strongest reply and residual

**Reply:** Read the Fall structurally rather than as a scientific chronology: finite freedom can disorder a good creation, and privation remains valid even if natural processes predate humans.

⚠️ **Residual:** This preserves the metaphysics of privation by weakening the full punishment account. It still leaves divine permission and unequal natural suffering unresolved.

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQ — 2023 Q8(a), 20 marks:** “All evil is either sin or punishment for sin.” — St. Augustine. Critically discuss.

**Approach only:** Reconstruct privation and ordered being → explain defective will, Fall, sin/punishment → separate moral from natural evil → present flawless-fall, innocence, animal, and evolutionary objections → compare Hick → issue a split verdict.

**Verified PYQ — 2026 Q6(a), 20 marks:** “In what sense does St. Augustine's identification of all evil as either sin or as the punishment of sin succeed or fail in providing a coherent account of both moral and natural evil? Evaluate.”

**Approach only:** Use the printed moral/natural division as the entire body structure → show success for moral authorship and anti-dualism → show derivative and strained treatment of natural evil → add Hick's criticism and privation-versus-theodicy distinction → conclude that metaphysical coherence exceeds explanatory completeness.

### Revision notes

1. *Privatio boni* means deprivation of due good.
2. A privation can be experientially real.
3. Evil has no independent substance.
4. Moral evil is defective willing.
5. Sin and punishment form Augustine's larger explanatory pair.
6. Inherited condition is more defensible than inherited personal guilt.
7. Privation explains dependence, not permission.
8. Natural and animal evil strain punishment theory.
9. Evolution weakens a historical human-Fall account of all natural evil.
10. The 2026 answer must split moral success from natural failure.
11. A revised Augustine may retain privation while abandoning a total punitive story.

## Lesson 7 — Irenaeus and Hick: A World for Soul-Making

Progress: 7/17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Image and likeness, epistemic distance, law-governed risk, and proportionality

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — Hick, *Philosophy of Religion*, pp. 44–49; *Evil and the God of Love* taxonomy through canonical and package sources.
CA search: "Hick soul making theodicy recent scholarly critique 2026"
CA found: No official current-affairs item changes the doctrine; contemporary suffering sharpens proportionality but is not used as proof.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — from created capacity to achieved character

```text
IMAGE: rational and moral capacity
                 ↓
EPISTEMIC DISTANCE: God is not coercively obvious
                 ↓
STABLE WORLD: actions have reliable consequences
                 ↓
REAL DIFFICULTY: courage, compassion, trust become possible
                 ↓
LIKENESS: freely developed moral and spiritual maturity
```

*The visual shows why the environment cannot be a frictionless “hedonistic paradise” if achieved character is the intended good.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

There is a difference between being programmed to behave well and becoming good through responsible response. A world that changed its laws whenever pain threatened would be safe, but action would lose seriousness and prediction would collapse.

### 2. Irenaean and Hickian structure

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Irenaeus supplies the distinction between humanity created in the divine **image** and growing toward divine **likeness**. John Hick modernises this developmental type through:

1. immature but free persons;
2. **epistemic distance**, allowing non-coerced relation;
3. a stable, law-governed environment;
4. genuine danger and resistance;
5. moral and spiritual development;
6. eventual fulfilment so no finite life is finally wasted.

The phrase “vale of Soul-making” belongs to John Keats's 1819 letter; Hick adopts it. It should not be attributed to Irenaeus or presented as Hick's coinage.

### 3. Why natural evil enters

Unlike a purely free-will defence, soul-making can assign a developmental role to natural difficulty. Reliable fire warms and burns; gravity enables movement and injury; biological vulnerability permits care and loss. Stable law allows knowledge and responsibility.

### 4. Proportionality test

Soul-making requires:

- a subject capable of learning;
- difficulty within some range of response;
- opportunity for recovery;
- a good connected to the sufferer's life;
- fair access to development.

Horrendous trauma may destroy agency. Infant death and animal suffering may lack the relevant learner. Unequal suffering makes the developmental environment appear unfair.

### 5. Strongest objection, reply, residual

**Objection:** The theory uses some persons as instruments for virtues developed by others.

**Reply:** Hick's eschatological universalism extends development and fulfilment beyond earthly life, aiming to prevent any life from being finally wasted.

⚠️ **Residual:** Future compensation is not automatically a justification for prior permission. Adams's person-centred “defeat” sets a stronger test.

### UPSC application

Use soul-making after free will when the question includes natural evil. State epistemic distance, law-governed order, image-to-likeness development, and eschatological completion. Then test amount, distribution, animals, and horrendous evil.

### Revision notes

1. Irenaeus supplies image-to-likeness development.
2. Keats supplies the phrase “vale of Soul-making.”
3. Hick supplies the modern theodicy.
4. Epistemic distance protects non-coerced development.
5. Stable law makes action intelligible and consequential.
6. Ready-made innocence differs from achieved virtue.
7. Soul-making reaches natural evil better than free will alone.
8. Development requires proportional challenge, not any suffering.
9. Animal and infant cases are major limits.
10. Universal fulfilment is Hick's completion condition.
11. Compensation and justification must remain distinct.

## Lesson 8 — Rowe, Draper, and the Evidential Problem

Progress: 8/17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Apparently gratuitous evil, comparative probability, scale, distribution, and intensity

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — Rowe's fawn and canonical evidential dossier; Oxford Handbook argument-from-evil treatment; complete package probability modules.
CA search: "evidential problem of evil Rowe Draper recent scholarship 2026"
CA found: No doctrine-changing official event; contemporary cases must be used only as illustrations, not unverified premises.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — compatibility does not equal probability

```text
Plantinga: Is God + evil POSSIBLE? ── perhaps yes
                         ↓
Rowe: Is this suffering GRATUITOUS? ── perhaps
                         ↓
Draper: Is this pattern more EXPECTED
        on perfect theism or indifferent nature?
```

*The visual shows why the evidential problem begins after the strict logical problem has been softened.*

### 1. Rowe's argument

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Rowe asks whether there are instances of intense suffering that an omnipotent, omniscient being could prevent without losing a greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil. A perfectly good being would prevent such suffering. Therefore the existence of apparently gratuitous suffering counts probabilistically against such a God.

The suffering fawn after a natural fire is an evidential illustration. It is not a deductive proof and not a moral-evil case.

### 2. Four evidential dimensions

| Dimension | Critical question |
|---|---|
| Scale | Why so much suffering? |
| Distribution | Why infants, animals, and unequally burdened lives? |
| Intensity | Why harms that crush rather than form agency? |
| Duration | Why suffering after any apparent lesson or causal role is exhausted? |

Divine hiddenness can intensify the evidence. Hick values epistemic distance because overwhelming divine presence could coerce response. The critic distinguishes coercive display from recognisable care: a parent can remain non-coercive while being present to a terrified child. If a loving God could offer guidance or consolation without cancelling freedom, severe silence becomes an additional interpersonal datum. This is a bounded cross-link, not a substitute for the separate topic of religious experience.

### 3. Draper's comparison

Draper's strategy compares how expected pain and pleasure are on rival hypotheses. A biological pattern shaped by survival rather than welfare may be less surprising on indifferent nature than on perfect providence.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Bayesian language clarifies comparative expectation, but an UPSC answer should not invent numerical priors. Theism may include auxiliary commitments and independent evidence; those must be weighed, not presumed.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Human beings cannot survey every good, consequence, and possible world, so “no reason seen” is weak evidence of “no reason.”

**Reply:** Absence of evidence matters where evidence would reasonably be expected. The debate is about access, not omniscience: should a morally sufficient reason for extreme suffering be at least partly intelligible?

⚠️ **Residual:** If divine reasons are completely opaque, positive claims that providence is good also lose support.

### UPSC application

Contrast Mackie and Rowe explicitly: contradiction versus probability; any evil versus apparently gratuitous evil; possible defence versus plausible explanation. Add one hard case and one epistemic reply.

### Revision notes

1. Evidential evil can grant logical compatibility.
2. Gratuitous evil lacks a justifying greater-good relation.
3. Rowe's fawn is natural and evidential.
4. Scale differs from distribution.
5. Intensity differs from mere occurrence.
6. Draper compares expected evidence under rival hypotheses.
7. Do not invent Bayesian numbers.
8. Auxiliary theodicies need independent motivation.
9. Absence can be evidence only under an access condition.
10. Complete opacity threatens positive providential claims too.

### Visual first — when absence of sight becomes evidence


*The visual states the disciplined skeptical-theist move without reducing it to “God works mysteriously.”*

### 1. Wykstra's access condition

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** CORNEA — the Condition of ReasoNable Epistemic Access — says that one may infer “it appears there is no X” only if it is reasonable to believe that X would likely be discernible from one's cognitive position if present.

Bergmann adds that our known samples of possible goods, evils, and entailment relations may not represent the total moral field. Alston's novice-versus-grandmaster analogy makes the same point about limited competence.

### 2. What the view does not claim

Skeptical theism:

- is a defence against an inference, not a positive theodicy;
- targets evidential rather than logical evil;
- does not show that a hidden reason exists;
- cannot license human passivity.

### 3. Moral-scepticism objection

If unknown goods may justify any apparently gratuitous evil, perhaps agents should not prevent suffering because they may frustrate God's plan. The reply is that human obligation follows accessible reasons: we know pain, danger, and duties of rescue; we do not know a hidden permission that authorises neglect.

### 4. Strongest objection, reply, residual

**Objection:** Restricting scepticism to negative evidence is selective. If we cannot read suffering against providence, why read beauty, answered prayer, or moral order for providence?

**Reply:** Different evidence may offer different access, but the asymmetry must be argued case by case.

⚠️ **Residual:** The broader the scepticism, the less content remains in saying God is recognisably good and trustworthy.

### UPSC application

## Lesson 10 — Existential Evil, Protest, and Horrendous Evils

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — canonical horrendous-evil dossier; complete package on Job, protest, post-Holocaust caution, and Adams; Oxford Handbook horrors discussion.
CA search: "official remembrance atrocity survivor ethics theodicy 2026"
CA found: No single event is appropriated as teaching material; the lesson uses established philosophical and literary sources with pastoral restraint.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — four very different responses to suffering

```text
EXPLAIN it ── causal account
JUSTIFY it ── morally sufficient permission
PROTEST it ── refuse a harmony purchased with victims
DEFEAT it ─── make the sufferer's own life a great good despite it
```

*The visual separates intellectual solution from moral stance and person-centred redemption.*

### 1. Existential challenge

Job dramatises lament and the failure of easy explanations. Dostoevsky's Ivan refuses a final harmony purchased with innocent suffering. Camus emphasises revolt and solidarity without metaphysical vindication. These positions do not refute a modal defence by themselves; they ask whether accepting its price is morally and humanly possible.

### 2. Anti-theodicy

Anti-theodicy warns that a comprehensive explanation can turn victims into instruments. The proper first response to preventable suffering is protection, relief, testimony, and resistance. Refusal to justify is not refusal to think; it is a claim about the ethics and limits of explanation.

Post-Holocaust philosophy and theology sharpen this caution. There is no single “post-Holocaust theodicy”: some thinkers revise divine power, some centre testimony and memory, and others reject comprehensive justification. The safe philosophical lesson is that atrocity must not be described as a neat ingredient in providential progress. Lament and resistance can be religious responses without becoming causal explanations.

### 3. Adams's horrendous evils

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** For Marilyn McCord Adams, a horrendous evil is one whose participation gives the person prima facie reason to doubt that their life can be a great good to them on the whole.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balancing off | Independent goods outweigh an evil in an aggregate |
| Defeat | The evil is overcome within the person's life and relation to the good |
| Global value | The world-total is judged good |
| Goodness to persons | Each participant's life must be good to that person |

Adams uses religion-specific resources such as divine identification with suffering and beatific intimacy. The aim is not to claim that horror was instrumentally required but that it need not finally ruin the participant's life.

### 4. Strongest objection, reply, residual

**Objection:** Eschatological fulfilment sounds like compensation after an unjust permission.

**Reply:** Defeat is stronger than replacement; the evil must be integrated and overcome within the person's good rather than buried under unrelated reward.

⚠️ **Residual:** The account remains dependent on contested religious commitments and may secure redemption without fully justifying initial permission.

### UPSC application

Use the existential layer to deepen, not replace, logical analysis. In a 20-marker, the distinction between global balancing and person-level defeat can lift the answer above a list of theodicies.

### Revision notes

1. Existential evil asks how one lives and trusts amid suffering.
2. Protest can reject the moral price of a total harmony.
3. Anti-theodicy resists instrumentalising victims.
4. Adams defines horrendous evil technically.
5. The standard is whether one's life can be a great good to oneself.
6. Balancing is not defeat.
7. Global value is not enough.
8. Eschatology may redeem without automatically justifying permission.
9. Religion-specific answers are legitimate when a particular theism is under attack.
10. Action and solidarity precede speculative explanation.

## Lesson 13 — Process Theodicy: Power as Persuasion

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — classical permission versus process inability

```text
CLASSICAL THEISM                         PROCESS THEISM
God can unilaterally prevent             Power is intrinsically distributed
        ↓                                           ↓
God refrains or permits                  God offers the best INITIAL AIM
        ↓                                           ↓
Why not intervene here?                  Finite occasions partly self-determine
                                                    ↓
                                         God receives and shares the result
```

*The visual shows that process theodicy revises the premise of unilateral omnipotence rather than merely adding a reason for non-intervention.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Imagine influence without total control. A wise teacher can offer the best possibility but cannot make another's genuinely self-determining response. Process thought turns this analogy into metaphysics: actuality itself includes some creative power.

### 2. Whitehead's framework

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Reality consists in becoming “actual occasions,” each partly conditioned by the past and partly self-creative. Creativity is not God's manufactured property. God is dipolar:

- the **primordial nature** orders possibilities and supplies an **initial aim**;
- the **consequent nature** receives, preserves, and is affected by what the world becomes.

God is the “fellow-sufferer who understands,” not an impassible spectator.

### 3. Hartshorne, Griffin, and Cobb

- **Hartshorne** argues that classical omnipotence is incoherent: if one being has all power, nothing else can be genuinely actual or powerful.
- **Griffin** develops the full process theodicy. Divine persuasion is metaphysically necessary, not voluntary self-restraint.
- **Cobb** systematises the theological development.

These roles are related but not interchangeable.

### 4. The argument

1. To be actual is to possess some self-determining power.
2. Power is therefore distributed.
3. No being can unilaterally determine every becoming.
4. God offers the best available possibility to each occasion.
5. Evil arises through finite deviation, conflict, inherited conditions, and limited causal powers.
6. God is not culpable for failing to perform an impossible coercion and shares every suffering.

### 5. Strongest objection, reply, residual

**Objection:** A God unable to prevent horrors may be unworthy of worship and unable to guarantee victory.

**Reply:** Process thinkers argue that coercive omnipotence is conceptually mistaken; supreme persuasive wisdom, universal participation, and perfect receptivity are more coherent and religiously valuable.

⚠️ **Residual:** Hick notes that a God who urges a risky creative advance may still bear responsibility for initiating or sustaining it. Divine solidarity does not itself guarantee rescue.

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQ — 2025 Q6(b), 15 marks:** “Explain the main tenets of the Process Theodicy as an explanation of the problem of evil.”

**Approach only:** State revision of omnipotence → explain actual occasions and distributed creativity → distinguish primordial/consequent natures and initial aim → name Whitehead, Hartshorne, Griffin, Cobb separately → explain evil and divine suffering → assess worship, assurance, and responsibility.

### Revision notes

1. Process thought revises classical omnipotence.
2. Actual occasions possess self-determining creativity.
3. The primordial nature orders possibilities.
4. The initial aim is God's persuasive lure.
5. The consequent nature receives the world's becoming.
6. Whitehead is the metaphysical source.
7. Hartshorne attacks monopoly power.
8. Griffin makes non-coercion metaphysically necessary.
9. Process differs from voluntary self-limitation.
10. Divine solidarity is central.
11. Guaranteed victory and worship-worthiness remain disputed.
12. The theory buys innocence at the price of sovereignty.

Book context: Queried — canonical owner-module for the 2019 PYQ; Durkheim, Otto, and Eliade distinctions; complete package religion-forming session.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — how a negative predicament can generate religion

```text
PREDICAMENT
sin / impurity / dukkha / avidyā / bondage
        ↓
BOUNDARY
sacred–profane / pure–impure / saved–lost
        ↓
PRACTICES AND INSTITUTIONS
ritual / prohibition / initiation / community / penance
        ↓
SOTERIOLOGY
purification / forgiveness / knowledge / liberation / cessation
```

*Evil and the profane can found religion structurally by defining what is wrong, what is set apart, and how transformation occurs.*

### 1. The crucial distinction

**Profane does not mean evil.** For Durkheim, it is the ordinary or non-consecrated, opposed to the sacred. Evil, ritual impurity, and the demonic are further categories.

### 2. Three thinkers

- **Durkheim:** Religion classifies things into sacred and profane and unites adherents through beliefs and practices relative to sacred things in a moral community.
- **Otto:** The holy is encountered as the **numinous**, *mysterium tremendum et fascinans* — aweful mystery that repels and attracts.
- **Eliade:** A **hierophany** manifests the sacred, creating sacred space around an *axis mundi* and recoverable sacred time against profane homogeneity.

Durkheim's social classification, Otto's experiential category, and Eliade's phenomenology must not be treated as synonyms.

### 3. Indian parity

Buddhism's First Noble Truth makes *dukkha* the clearest case of a predicament founding a religion. Jain *āsrava* and *bandha* name influx and bondage; *saṃvara* and *nirjarā* name the exit. Advaita begins from *avidyā*. Ritual traditions distinguish *śuddhi/aśuddhi* and *pāpa/puṇya*.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Indian traditions often begin from ignorance, bondage, or suffering rather than moral evil. Their soteriology therefore emphasises diagnosis, discipline, and insight more than sin and forgiveness.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Sacred/profane is an imposed binary; traditions often grade reality, and religion also grows from wonder and gratitude.

**Reply:** Treat the binary as an ideal type, not a universal law. The negative predicament is one structural foundation, not the sole origin of religion.

### UPSC application

**Approach only:** Define evil and profane separately → Durkheim's sacred/profane and community → predicament-boundary-institution-soteriology sequence → Otto/Eliade qualification → Indian *dukkha*, bondage, and impurity parallels → conclude that these found religion, not necessarily theism.

1. Profane means ordinary, not wicked.
2. Durkheim's distinction is social and institutional.
3. Otto's numinous is experiential.
4. Eliade explains manifestation, sacred space, and sacred time.
5. Evil supplies a predicament.
6. Profane supplies a boundary against the sacred.
7. Boundaries generate ritual and community.
8. Predicaments generate soteriology.

### 1. Classical theism

Perfect knowledge generates awareness, perfect goodness opposition, power capacity, and creatorhood framework responsibility. Together they create the sharpest expectation that apparently preventable evil should not occur.

### 2. Revision and dissolution

Finite and process theisms reduce culpability by limiting control. Advaita transforms the ultimate reality-status of duality. Non-creator systems remove divine permission from the argument. Yet every move incurs a cost: assurance, worship, empirical adequacy, moral distribution, or causal explanation.

**Open theism** holds that future free acts are genuinely open and known by God as possibilities rather than already settled facts. It reduces the burden created by exhaustive definite foreknowledge, but critics regard this as a loss of perfection. **Molinism** instead retains comprehensive providence through divine “middle knowledge” of what free creatures would do in possible circumstances. It seeks control without determination, but inherits the grounding problem for counterfactuals and the question why a better feasible world was not selected.

### 3. The anti-ad-hoc test

A revision is philosophically legitimate only if it has independent metaphysical or religious motivation. Inventing a weak God solely to escape evil has little explanatory value. Process thought at least offers a wider account of actuality and power; Advaita a wider non-dual metaphysics; Buddhism a wider dependent-origination analysis.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Concept-dependence trivialises the argument because believers can redefine God indefinitely.

**Reply:** Revision is not cost-free. A conception survives only by openly stating what it preserves and abandons, and by remaining coherent with worship and practice.

⚠️ **Residual:** Evil may not refute religion as such, but it can strongly disconfirm a particular providential profile.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** Distinguish between a defence and a theodicy. Why is the distinction indispensable in the problem of evil? Answer in about 150 words.

### Model answer

A **defence** offers a logically possible condition under which God and evil can coexist. Its task is negative: to defeat the claim of contradiction. Plantinga's free-will defence, for example, argues that it is possible that every world feasible for God containing significantly free creatures includes some wrongdoing. It does not claim that transworld depravity is actual or that freedom explains every suffering.

A **theodicy**, by contrast, claims to identify God's actual or probable morally sufficient reason. Hick says that a law-governed and epistemically distant world serves soul-making; process theodicy says that coercive prevention is metaphysically unavailable.

The distinction is indispensable because logical possibility, empirical probability, causal explanation, and moral justification impose different burdens. A defence may answer Mackie's deductive challenge while leaving Rowe's evidential question, animal suffering, horrendous evil, and existential protest untouched. Conversely, a theodicy must be judged for truth, scope, proportionality, and goodness to persons. Thus the correct verdict is burden-specific: compatibility can be established without claiming moral transparency.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** “Freedom explains the source of moral evil but not the permissible magnitude of its consequences.” Examine. Answer in about 250 words.

### Model answer

The free-will defence distinguishes God's creation of agency from the creature's authorship of a wrong choice. Significant libertarian freedom is valuable because love, fidelity, responsibility, and repentance cannot be causally programmed. Plantinga adds that a logically describable sinless free world may not be feasible for God if undetermined creaturely counterfactuals are not under divine control. This is sufficient to weaken a strict contradiction between God and moral evil.

The quoted claim, however, identifies the defence's deeper residual. Freedom to choose wrongly need not imply an unlimited capacity to inflict catastrophic harm. Human institutions restrict weapons, opportunities, and consequences while retaining meaningful agency. Classical theism must therefore explain not only why God does not determine the choice but why the action-framework permits extreme, unequal, and sometimes irreversible consequences. Foreknowledge does not cause the act, yet an omniscient creator's selection and sustenance of the framework remain morally assessable.

Stable-law reasoning supplies a partial reply: predictable consequences make agency serious and cooperation possible; constant intervention would obscure causal order. But regularity explains a risk-bearing world, not every intensity or failure of selective rescue. Structural evil and inherited inequality also exceed an atomistic focus on isolated choices.

Process theodicy avoids the intervention question by denying unilateral coercive power, but pays with weakened sovereignty. Karma expands responsibility across lives, but risks unverifiable desert claims. Hence freedom plausibly explains creaturely authorship and some necessary risk; it does not by itself justify the actual range, distribution, or natural forms of evil.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** Can any adequate response to evil preserve divine goodness without either limiting divine power or weakening human moral knowledge? Discuss with reference to classical and non-classical responses. Answer in about 350 words.

### Model answer

The problem arises most sharply for an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good creator. A response preserving all attributes must explain why a good being permits evil that it knows and can prevent. Classical defences and non-classical revisions distribute the cost differently.

Plantinga preserves power and goodness by invoking significant freedom. If causally guaranteed libertarian goodness is contradictory, and every feasible free world possibly contains wrongdoing, moral evil is compatible with God. Yet this is a defence, not an actual explanation. Natural, animal, structural, and horrendous evils remain; meaningful freedom also does not obviously require catastrophic consequence.

Augustine preserves creator goodness by treating evil as *privatio boni* and locating culpability in defective will. The metaphysics successfully denies an evil substance, but punishment and Fall explanations strain before innocent and pre-human suffering. Hick's soul-making connects natural risk to achieved virtue through epistemic distance and stable law. It preserves purposive goodness, yet disproportionate suffering and goodness-to-persons require eschatological completion. Leibniz's best-world reasoning adds compossibility, but global optimisation may sacrifice individuals.

Skeptical theism protects goodness by denying that human failure to see a reason warrants belief that none exists. Wykstra's CORNEA is a legitimate access check. However, extensive scepticism threatens ordinary moral judgement and positive confidence in providence. It must therefore preserve local duties to prevent suffering and cannot serve as a hidden-reason theodicy.

Process thought takes the other route. Whitehead's distributed creativity and Griffin's metaphysically necessary persuasion mean God cannot coerce every event. Divine goodness and solidarity are retained, but classical omnipotence and guaranteed victory are surrendered.

Indian comparisons confirm the trade-off. Non-creator Buddhism and Jainism remove divine responsibility while retaining causal, identity, and liberation questions; Advaita changes ultimate reality-status but must respect empirical suffering; karma addresses distribution at the cost of verification and victim-blaming risk.

Therefore no major response is costless. Classical replies can preserve power only through freedom, greater goods, eschatology, or epistemic restraint; each leaves evidential and moral residuals. Process thought preserves recognisable goodness more directly by revising power. An adequate position should admit this graded result, retain human moral knowledge for action, and refuse to turn philosophical possibility into permission for indifference.

A student answers animal suffering entirely through Plantinga. What is the repair?

**MCQ 61: A**

A student writes, “God's ways are mysterious, so Rowe fails.” What must be added?

- **A — Correct:** Disciplined epistemic restraint needs a criterion and an account of its consequences.
- **B — Incorrect:** Skeptical theism accepts the evidence of suffering.
- **C — Incorrect:** It does not disclose a divine purpose.
- **D — Incorrect:** Accessible moral obligations remain operative.

**MCQ 62: B**

A student concludes, “The problem disappears in Buddhism, so Buddhism has solved evil.” What is the correction?

A. Buddhism accepts a classical creator after all.
B. The creator contradiction dissolves, but *dukkha*, causal continuity, responsibility, and liberation remain.

- **A — Incorrect:** The non-creator structure is precisely why the classical dilemma does not arise.
- **B — Correct:** Reframing removes one defendant without removing the existential and causal predicament.
- **C — Incorrect:** Buddhism begins from the pervasive fact of unsatisfactoriness.
- **D — Incorrect:** Buddhist no-self and Jain *jīva* are fundamentally different.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Core formulation

1. The problem is strongest against an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good creator and sustainer.
2. Mackie's familiar set needs bridging principles about goodness and power.
3. Omniscience blocks ignorance; creatorhood adds framework responsibility.
4. Logical evil asks compatibility; evidential evil asks probability.
5. Existential evil asks whether trust and meaning survive.
6. Moral protest asks whether explanation instrumentalises victims.
7. A defence claims possibility; a theodicy claims an actual or probable reason.
8. Explanation and justification are separate.

## B. Evil-types

1. Moral evil comes from culpable agency.
2. Natural evil includes disease, disaster, predation, and non-voluntary suffering.
3. Structural evil emerges from institutions and accumulated choices.
4. Metaphysical evil means finitude or limitation and must not be equated with wickedness.
5. First-order evils are direct harms.
6. Second-order goods arise in response but do not automatically justify harms.
7. Scale, distribution, intensity, and duration are distinct evidential dimensions.

## C. Mackie and Plantinga

1. The popular Epicurean dilemma is transmitted through Lactantius; quote with attribution caution.
2. Mackie's 1955 essay makes the logical charge explicit.
3. Goodness need not remove a lesser evil where a morally sufficient reason exists.
4. Omnipotence concerns logically possible action.
5. Plantinga values significant libertarian freedom.
6. A describable world may not be feasible for God.
7. Transworld depravity is a possibility, not an established actual fact.
8. The defence is strongest for moral evil.
9. Human authorship and divine framework responsibility can coexist.
10. Freedom does not establish the permissible magnitude of harm.

## D. Augustine and Hick

1. Augustine: being as being is good; evil is *privatio boni*.
2. Privation is real lack, not illusion.
3. Defective will explains moral fault.
4. Sin/punishment and Fall extend the account to suffering.
5. Innocent, animal, and pre-human suffering strain the punitive extension.
6. Privation can survive even if a historical natural Fall is rejected.
7. Irenaeus supplies image-to-likeness development.
8. Keats supplies “vale of Soul-making”; Hick adopts it.
9. Hick adds epistemic distance, stable law, freedom, and eventual fulfilment.
10. Soul-making explains proportionate adversity, not automatically horrors.

## E. Evidential and advanced problems

1. Rowe's fawn exemplifies apparently gratuitous natural suffering.
2. Draper compares expected evidence under rival hypotheses.
3. Do not assign invented probabilities.
4. CORNEA asks whether a hidden reason would likely be detectable.
5. Skeptical theism is a defence against inference, not a theodicy.
6. Moral scepticism and positive-evidence parity are its main costs.
7. Adams's horrendous evil threatens whether one's life can be good to oneself.
8. Balancing off differs from defeat.
9. Animal suffering defeats human-only free-will and punishment stories.
10. Deep time pressures a human-Fall origin of all natural evil.

## F. Leibniz and process

1. Leibniz selects the best complete compossible world.
2. Best whole does not mean best local event.
3. Stable law enables knowledge, action, and responsibility.
4. Regularity does not justify every excess.
5. Eschatology can complete, restore, or defeat.
6. Compensation does not automatically justify permission.
7. Whitehead's actual occasions possess creativity.
8. Primordial nature orders possibilities and initial aims.
9. Consequent nature receives the world's becoming.
10. Hartshorne rejects monopoly omnipotence.
11. Griffin makes persuasion metaphysically necessary.
12. Process preserves goodness and solidarity by revising sovereignty.

## G. Indian and non-theistic comparison

1. Karma extends moral causation across lives.
2. Beginninglessness removes a first event, not the need for moral evaluation.
3. Nyāya can make Īśvara karmic administrator.
4. Mīmāṃsā does not require a creator.
5. Advaita preserves empirical suffering while denying ultimate duality.
6. Buddhism begins from *dukkha* and dependent origination.
7. Jainism explains karmic bondage and emphasises *ahiṃsā*.
8. Sāṃkhya diagnoses misidentification without creator-theodicy.
9. Removing God dissolves one contradiction, not suffering.
10. No observer may infer a victim's specific karmic guilt.
11. Compassion, aid, and reform remain present duties.

## H. Evil, profane, and religion

1. Profane means ordinary/non-consecrated, not evil.
2. Durkheim treats sacred/profane as a social classification.
3. Otto treats the holy as numinous.
4. Eliade explains hierophany, sacred space, and sacred time.
5. Evil and bondage supply a predicament.
6. Sacred/profane and pure/impure supply boundaries.
7. Boundaries generate practices and institutions.
8. Predicaments generate soteriology.
9. Buddhism's *dukkha* is the clearest non-theistic illustration.
10. These categories found religion structurally, not necessarily theism.
