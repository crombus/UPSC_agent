## Lesson 1 — What Must a Proof of God Actually Establish?

### Visual: the three-gate test

```text
PREMISES  --valid inference?-->  IMMEDIATE CONCLUSION  --adequate?-->  GOD OF RELIGION
   |                                   |                               |
true or defensible?             cause / designer /              one, personal,
                                necessary ground                 good, omniscient,
                                                                  worship-worthy?
```

The word *proof* hides three different tests: validity, soundness and the adequacy of the conclusion.

### 1. Proof does not always mean demonstration

An ontological argument aims at deduction from the concept of God. Cosmological arguments begin with
change, causation or contingency. Design arguments usually work by analogy or inference to the best
explanation. Kant’s moral argument offers a practical postulate rather than theoretical knowledge.
Nyāya presents inference; Madhva gives scripture a role that natural theology cannot replace.

Therefore begin every answer by identifying the claimed force:

| Force | Question to ask | Typical family |
|---|---|---|
| Deductive | Would denying the conclusion contradict the premises? | Ontological; some cosmological reconstructions |
| Inductive or abductive | Does the evidence make theism more reasonable than rivals? | Design; cumulative case |
| Practical | Is belief required by moral rationality rather than theoretical explanation? | Kant |
| Testimonial | Is the conclusion disclosed through an accepted source of knowledge? | Madhva’s scriptural strand |

### 2. *A priori* and *a posteriori*

- An ***a priori*** argument does not infer God from an observed feature of the world. Anselm reasons
  from the concept of the greatest conceivable being.
- An ***a posteriori*** argument begins from the world: change, efficient causation, contingency,
  purposive order, moral experience or the composite cosmos.

The distinction concerns the starting point, not automatic superiority. An *a priori* argument promises
necessity but risks defining existence into a concept. An *a posteriori* argument is empirically anchored
but may reach only a cause, arranger or probability.

### 3. Explanatory termination

The cosmological question is not simply “Who caused everything?” A defender distinguishes derivative
reality from an underived terminus. A critic must therefore challenge the rule of dependence, permit an
infinite regress, or accept a brute fact. Asking “Who caused God?” matters only if the argument’s premises
apply to every being without distinction. If they apply only to beings that begin, change or depend, the
question must target the claim that God is exempt.

### 4. The standing conclusion-gap

An unmoved actualiser is not yet a loving person. A first efficient cause is not yet morally perfect. A
designer is not necessarily unique. A karmic governor is not automatically the material source of the
world. A good answer therefore ends with a graded verdict:

> The argument may support a limited explanatory conclusion while leaving the richer attributes of the
> God of worship to separate reasoning.

### UPSC use

For the 2022 comparison of *a priori* and *a posteriori* arguments, rank them by an announced criterion:
logical necessity, empirical accessibility, explanatory range or theological adequacy. Never say merely
that one is “better.”

### Traps

1. Calling every argument deductive.
2. Treating validity and soundness as synonyms.
3. Assuming “first cause” already means the full God of classical theism.
4. Rejecting a proof because it is not certainty, when it claims only probability.

### Revision notes

- Proof-force: deductive, abductive, practical or testimonial.
- Validity concerns inferential form; soundness adds true premises.
- Theological adequacy asks what kind of God has actually been reached.
- *A priori* starts from concepts; *a posteriori* starts from the world.
- Explanatory termination must be defended, not merely named.
- Every conclusion needs a “how far does this reach?” sentence.

### Retrieval check

Without notes, explain in three sentences why “the argument is valid” does not settle whether God exists.

## Lesson 2 — Ontological Reasoning: Can a Concept Entail Existence?

              v

      |                |

### 1. Anselm’s reductio

Anselm defines God as “that than which nothing greater can be conceived.” Even a denier understands the
expression, so God exists at least in the understanding. Suppose God existed only in thought. A being
otherwise identical but existing in reality would be greater. The supposed greatest conceivable being
would therefore not be greatest. The supposition is contradictory; God exists in reality.

The argument is *a priori*: no observed event or object functions as evidence.

### 2. Necessary existence

The stronger claim is not merely that actual existence improves a concept. God is conceived as a being
whose non-existence is impossible. Descartes similarly treats existence as inseparable from supreme
perfection. The dispute then shifts from ordinary existence to whether necessary existence can be built
into a coherent concept and instantiated by analysis alone.

### 3. Gaunilo

Gaunilo’s island parody asks whether the same pattern could establish a supremely excellent island. If
conceiving unsurpassable excellence entailed reality, absurd entities could be defined into existence.
An Anselmian reply says islands admit indefinite improvement and are contingent by nature, whereas God is
claimed as necessarily unsurpassable. The parody still forces the defender to explain why the God-concept
is unique rather than privileged by definition.

### 4. Kant

Kant’s central objection is that existence is not a real predicate. One hundred actual units do not
contain an extra descriptive property absent from one hundred possible units; actuality posits that the
concept is instantiated. Adding “exists” does not enlarge the concept, so conceptual analysis cannot
cross into reality merely by listing another perfection.

### 5. Modal repair

A modal version reasons:

1. It is possible that a maximally great being exists.
2. Maximal greatness includes necessary existence.
3. If such a being exists in any possible world, it exists in every possible world.
4. Therefore it exists in the actual world.

This avoids treating ordinary existence as a first-order predicate, but it relocates the burden to premise
1 and to the modal system connecting possible necessity with necessity. A critic may claim that the
possibility of maximal greatness is exactly what has not been established.

### 6. Indian comparison

There is no exact classical Indian equivalent. Nyāya starts from effects, order, language and karma;
Madhva relies decisively on scripture. Do not force either into Anselm’s conceptual method.

### UPSC use

For 2018, answer the word **entail**: distinguish conceptual containment from real instantiation. For
2020, “logical” requires separate verdicts on validity and soundness. For 2024’s 10+5 split, devote the
larger share to exposition and the smaller to Gaunilo and Kant.

### Revision notes

- Anselm uses reductio, not observation.
- Gaunilo challenges the transferable form.
- Kant denies that existence enriches a concept.
- Necessary existence is not ordinary excellence.
- Modal repair changes the contested premise; it does not end the debate.
- No exact Indian analogue should be invented.

### Retrieval check

State Kant’s objection without saying merely “existence is not a predicate”; explain what follows from it.

## Lesson 3 — Cosmological Families and Aquinas’s First Three Ways

Progress: 3/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: change, efficient causation, contingency, PSR and temporal beginning

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: four questions, not one

```text
CHANGE now --------> source of actualisation        Aquinas Way 1
CAUSES now --------> non-derivative causal source   Aquinas Way 2
CONTINGENCY --------> underived necessary being     Aquinas Way 3 / Leibniz
TEMPORAL BEGINNING -> cause of beginning             kalām
```

### 1. Why “first” need not mean earliest

Aquinas is concerned with dependence, not with proving that the universe had a first temporal instant.
An accidentally ordered series can extend historically: a parent generates a child who later acts
independently. An essentially ordered series involves derivative power here and now: a stick moves a
stone only while moved by a hand. The argument claims that derivative actualisers or causes require a
non-derivative source.

### 2. Way 1: change

Change is the actualisation of a potential. What is merely potential in a respect cannot actualise itself
in that same respect; something actual must account for the transition. A series whose members possess
only derivative actualising power cannot explain that power merely by adding more derivative members.
Therefore Aquinas posits an unmoved actualiser.

**Pressure:** Why must every change depend on a distinct concurrent actualiser, and why may the series not
be infinite? The Thomist replies that self-change can involve different respects, but derivative power
still requires a source. The disputed point is whether natural causal powers really are derivative in
this strict sense.

### 3. Way 2: efficient causation

Nothing can be its own efficient cause in the same respect, because it would have to be prior to itself.
In an essentially ordered causal series, intermediate causes exercise borrowed causal power. Remove the
first explanatory cause and the intermediate causes cannot operate. Hence a first efficient cause.

**Pressure:** Humean criticism permits an infinite or brute causal order. The answer must show why the
series is essentially ordered; otherwise “an infinite regress is impossible” is only an assertion.

### 4. Way 3: possibility and necessity

Aquinas observes generation and corruption: things can exist and cease to exist. He argues that if
everything could fail to be, then at some time nothing would have existed; from absolute nothing, nothing
could arise; yet things exist. Hence not everything is merely contingent. Any necessary being that gets
its necessity from another points beyond itself to a being necessary through itself.

The standard objection exposes a quantifier shift:

```text
For every contingent thing, there is some time when it does not exist
                         does not entail
There is one time when no contingent thing exists
```

Overlapping contingent beings provide a countermodel. A sympathetic reconstruction drops the universal
empty-time step and asks why contingent reality exists at all. That reconstruction is philosophically
stronger but should not be misreported as Aquinas’s exact intermediate reasoning.

### 5. Leibniz and sufficient reason

Leibniz asks why there is something rather than nothing and why this contingent world exists. Even an
eternal series of contingent states does not contain its sufficient reason if every member depends on
another contingent member. The proposed terminus is a necessary reason outside the contingent totality.

The critic may reject a strong principle of sufficient reason, permit a brute totality, or ask why God is
an acceptable terminus while the world is not.

### 6. Temporal-beginning argument

The *kalām* form says that whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began; therefore it has a
cause. Its special burdens are the beginning premise and the transition from a cause of temporal origin
to a personal deity. It must not be substituted for Aquinas.

### UPSC use

The 2026 contingency question is specifically Way 3. Give its stages, the quantifier-shift objection, a
dependency-based reply and the gap between necessary being and religious God. Do not narrate all Five Ways.

### Revision notes

- Aquinas’s first means explanatory, not necessarily temporal, priority.
- Way 1 concerns potentiality and actuality.
- Way 2 concerns derivative efficient causation.
- Way 3 contains a vulnerable all-things-absent step.
- Leibniz asks for a sufficient reason of contingent totality.
- *Kalām* makes temporal beginning explicit.

### Retrieval check

Draw the quantifier-shift objection to Way 3 and then give one charitable reconstruction.

## Lesson 4 — Aquinas’s Fourth and Fifth Ways, and the Five Ways as a Whole

### Visual: the Five Ways do not repeat one argument

| Way | Starting datum | Immediate terminus |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | change | unmoved actualiser |
| 2 | ordered efficient causes | first efficient cause |
| 3 | contingent and derived necessary beings | underived necessary being |
| 4 | degrees of goodness, truth and nobility | maximal source of perfection |
| 5 | regular goal-directed action of non-rational nature | intelligent director |

### 1. Way 4: degrees and participation

Aquinas observes that things are called more or less good, true or noble. He connects degrees to a
maximum and then treats the maximum as the cause of the perfection found in lesser things.

A crude version fails: temperatures can vary without a hottest possible temperature, and even an actual
maximum need not cause the lower instances. The more charitable Thomist reading invokes participation:
limited instances possess being or goodness derivatively and therefore depend on a plenitude that is not
limited in the same way.

That reply reveals the real burden. The proof works only if these perfections are participated realities
requiring a source, not merely comparative predicates. Way 4 therefore cannot be defended by repeating
“degrees require a maximum.”

### 2. Way 5: natural finality

Non-rational natural things regularly act toward determinate outcomes. Since they do not consciously know
their ends, Aquinas infers direction by intelligence, illustrated through an arrow directed by an archer.

This is not Paley’s watch argument. Way 5 concerns immanent end-directedness in natural processes; Paley
compares organismic arrangement with manufactured artifacts. Natural laws, self-organisation and
evolution challenge the need for an external director. A Thomist responds that describing stable laws or
selection still presupposes natural powers directed toward characteristic outcomes. The remaining issue
is whether such directedness requires a separate intelligence.

### 3. Evaluating the Five Ways together

Use four columns:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Internal form | Does each conclusion follow from its stated premises? |
| Premise burden | Must dependent series terminate? Are perfections participated? Is finality irreducible? |
| Immediate reach | What does each Way establish before later theology is added? |
| Comparative interest | Which Way has the best explanatory reach relative to its controversial assumptions? |

For the 2022 question, choose one Way by a declared criterion. Way 3 may be most interesting because it
asks why contingent reality exists, but its printed empty-time step is weak. Way 5 may be chosen because it
raises a deep issue about natural directedness, while conceding that biological adaptation does not by
itself prove a designer.

### 4. Collective strength and collective limit

The Ways may be read as convergent routes: change, causal power, contingency, perfection and finality all
point beyond derivative reality. Yet aggregation cannot silently repair false premises. Nor does the set
immediately establish divine unity, perfect goodness or providential concern. Those attributes require
additional argument.

### UPSC use

A “critical exposition” must give the starting point and terminus of every Way, then evaluate at least
two disputed moves. A list of five labels is not exposition. A personal choice needs a criterion and a
cost statement.

### Revision notes

- Way 4 needs participation metaphysics, not a simple highest-member claim.
- Way 5 concerns natural finality, not the watchmaker analogy.
- Each Way has a different datum and immediate conclusion.
- “This all call God” is the start of identification, not its completion.
- A reasoned preference names a criterion before choosing.
- Collective convergence does not excuse weak premises.

### Retrieval check

In one minute, explain why a scale can have degrees without a maximum and how a Thomist tries to reply.

## Lesson 5 — Design, Purpose and Hume’s Critique

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the design inference under pressure

```text
artifacts: ordered parts + purpose -> intelligent maker
                         |
                    analogy extended
                         v
nature: adaptation + coordinated parts -> cosmic designer?
                         |
      weak analogy / finite effect / many designers / evil / evolution
```

### 1. Paley’s watch

Finding a watch, one infers a maker because its parts are adjusted toward a function. Paley argues that
organisms display even more intricate coordination; therefore purposive intelligence best explains them.
The conclusion depends on a relevant similarity between artifacts and natural organisms.

### 2. Hume’s criticism

Hume’s critique attacks both the inference and the theological conclusion:

1. **Weak analogy:** the universe is unique and not sufficiently like a manufactured machine.
2. **Proportionality:** a finite, mixed world warrants at most a finite and imperfect cause.
3. **Plurality:** complex productions may have many makers; one designer does not follow.
4. **Alternative analogies:** the world may resemble an organism generated by internal principles more
   than a machine assembled from outside.
5. **Disorder and suffering:** defects count against a perfectly good and competent designer.

Hume does not need to prove that there is no designer. He shows that the observed world underdetermines
Paley’s strong theological conclusion.

### 3. Darwin

Natural selection explains the appearance of design in biological adaptation through variation,
inheritance and differential survival without foresight. It directly weakens Paley’s organismic evidence.
It does not by itself explain why there are laws, why the universe exists or whether fundamental constants
require explanation. Do not use Darwin as a universal refutation of every teleological argument.

### 4. Probabilistic retreat

A contemporary defender may reduce the claim: order is not a demonstration but one item of evidence in a
cumulative case. This avoids overclaiming, but it requires comparison with rival explanations and cannot
derive an infinite, unique and morally perfect deity from design evidence alone.

### 5. Nyāya comparison

Nyāya’s potter–pot analogy and *kāryāt* reasoning also infer intelligence from an ordered effect. The
Indian debate adds eternal atoms and karmic allocation. Similar inferential form does not imply identical
cosmology.

### UPSC use

The 2025 question asks for design **and Hume**. Give exposition and criticism balanced space. Conclude:
the argument retains suggestive or abductive force only after surrendering demonstrative certainty and
limiting its theological reach.

### Revision notes

- Paley reasons from functional arrangement to a maker.
- Hume attacks analogy, proportionality, uniqueness and moral perfection.
- Darwin is strongest against biological adaptation as direct design evidence.
- Probabilistic design requires explicit rival comparison.
- Aquinas’s finality and Paley’s artifact analogy must remain distinct.
- Nyāya supplies a structural parallel, not an identical doctrine.

### Visual: never merge these routes

| Route | Starting point | Claimed divine role | Main pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conscience/law | felt authority of duty | commander or judge | social and psychological explanations |
| Objective value | stance-independent moral truth | ground of value and obligation | moral realism may be non-theistic |
| Kant | highest good: virtue proportioned to happiness | practical postulate securing rational hope | projection and autonomy objections |

### 1. Lawgiver route

Binding moral obligation may be interpreted as the command of a personal authority. Yet conscience can
be explained through socialisation, rational reciprocity or evolved moral capacities. Even objective duty
does not immediately entail a commander.

### 2. Value-ground route

If moral values are objective, a defender may argue that they require a transcendent ground. The critic
can accept moral realism while denying that truths need a divine person. The Euthyphro problem adds:
are acts good because God commands them, or does God command them because they are good? The first horn
threatens arbitrariness; the second suggests an independent moral standard.

### 3. Kant’s highest good

Kant rejects speculative proofs of God. Moral law obligates autonomously; God does not create its authority.
Practical reason nevertheless directs us toward the highest good, in which virtue and happiness are
appropriately united. Finite moral agents cannot guarantee this harmony. God and immortality are therefore
postulated as conditions of rational moral hope.

The sequence is:

```text
autonomous duty -> pursuit of highest good -> inability to secure full harmony
                 -> practical postulation of God and immortality
```

### 4. Objections and reply

- **Projection:** the argument turns a wish for cosmic fairness into reality.
- **Autonomy:** adding divine reward may corrupt duty performed for duty’s sake.
- **Denial of completion:** morality can remain meaningful even if virtue and happiness never converge.

Kant’s reply is that the postulate does not generate obligation or promise selfish reward. It concerns the
coherence of sustained rational commitment to the highest good. The residue is that practical necessity
does not amount to theoretical proof.

### 5. Indian comparison

Nyāya’s *karmādhyakṣa* assigns karmic fruits, but this is an explanatory administrator of moral order.
Mīmāṃsā answers with self-operating *apūrva*. Neither should be collapsed into Kant’s argument from the
highest good.

### UPSC use

For 2021, lead with the fact that “moral argument” names several routes. Give Kant the largest share,
because his postulate is frequently misdescribed as a proof from conscience.

### Revision notes

- Moral lawgiver, moral ground and Kantian postulate are distinct.
- Kant preserves autonomy before introducing God.
- The highest good combines virtue and proportionate happiness.
- A postulate is practical, not speculative knowledge.
- Euthyphro pressures divine-command grounding.
- Nyāya’s karmic governor is a comparison, not Kant in Indian form.

### Retrieval check

## Lesson 7 — Nyāya and Udayana: A Cumulative Inferential Case

Progress: 7/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Īśvara, eight grounds, karma and objections

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not used — canonical Markdown and the approved Nyāya benchmark control terminology.
CA search: not relevant.
CA found: none used.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: Nyāya’s division of causal labour

```text
eternal atoms = material constituents
adṛṣṭa       = karmic disposition
Īśvara       = omniscient efficient arranger and allocator
                      |
                      v
ordered world + fitting distribution of fruits
```

### 1. The divine profile relevant to proof

Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika does not require God to create atoms from nothing. Atoms, selves, space and time are
eternal. Īśvara is the intelligent efficient cause who combines atoms, orders cosmic processes and
administers karmic results. This efficient/material distinction is indispensable in comparison with
Aquinas.

### 2. Udayana’s eight grounds

The eight terms are safest when each is given with one precise clause:

| Ground | Core reasoning |
|---|---|
| ***kāryāt*** | the composite world is an effect requiring an intelligent maker |
| ***āyojanāt*** | inert atoms cannot initiate their own ordered conjunction |
| ***dhṛtyādeḥ*** | support, maintenance and dissolution indicate governance |
| ***padāt*** | original linguistic and practical instruction requires a competent teacher |
| ***pratyayataḥ*** | Vedic cognition’s validity calls for a trustworthy source |
| ***śruteḥ*** | scripture itself testifies to Īśvara |
| ***vākyāt*** | sentences are ordinarily produced by persons, suggesting an author |
| ***saṃkhyāviśeṣāt*** | the determinate numerical cognition initiating dyadic combination requires a knower |

Translations and groupings of the later terms vary. State the Sanskrit and the specific inferential job;
do not turn all eight into one vague “order proves God” claim.

### 3. Karma and *adṛṣṭa*

Unconscious merit and demerit do not appear capable of selecting which embodied self receives which fruit
at which time. Nyāya therefore adds an omniscient *karmādhyakṣa*. The argument requires omniscience because
the administrator must know all selves, acts and deserts.

### 4. Main objections

- **Cārvāka:** inference cannot legitimately reach a supersensible God.
- **Mīmāṃsā:** the Veda is eternal and authorless; *apūrva* links ritual action to fruit without a deity.
- **Buddhism:** the world-as-effect to conscious-maker relation is observed in artifacts, not in the cosmos;
  dependent origination makes a creator redundant.
- **Sāṃkhya:** *prakṛti* transforms through its own constitution.
- **Advaita:** inferred Īśvara belongs to empirical reality and is not the non-dual absolute.

### 5. Nyāya replies and residual cost

Nyāya argues that inference routinely reaches unperceived entities when *vyāpti* is secure. It also claims
that unconscious atoms and karma cannot coordinate purposively. The residual problem is whether artifact
cases establish the universal relation needed for the cosmos. If the inference is reduced to probability,
its conclusion becomes correspondingly modest.

### 6. Cumulative reading

Udayana’s grounds work better as mutually supporting strands than as eight independent demonstrations.
World-effect, atom coordination, governance, language, scripture and karma address different explanatory
features. Yet strands that share the same disputed artifact analogy are not fully independent evidence.

### UPSC use

For 2023 and 2025, present at least the major grounds with premises, then group objections by epistemology,
cosmology, scripture and moral order. For the 2018 Aquinas comparison, compare causal material, the role of
karma and immediate conclusions rather than merely pairing names.

### Revision notes

- Īśvara is efficient arranger, not material creator of atoms.
- *Kāryāt*, *āyojanāt* and *dhṛtyādeḥ* form the main cosmological-design cluster.
- Scripture-related grounds must remain distinct.
- *Saṃkhyāviśeṣāt* concerns determinate cognition involved in dyadic formation.
- *Adṛṣṭa* motivates an omniscient allocator.
- Udayana is cumulative but inherits analogy and inference objections.

### Visual: correct the stem before answering it

```text
"Jain proofs for God"
          |
          +--> no proof of a creator
          |
          +--> arguments against a creator
          |
          +--> divinity as achieved perfection of jīva
```

### 1. The uncreated order

Jainism treats the cosmos and its basic substances as beginningless. Karma operates through material
bondage to souls. A creator, sustainer and judge is therefore not part of its explanatory economy.

### 2. Six arguments against a creator

1. **Motive:** a perfect being lacks unmet need; action for a purpose suggests deficiency.
2. **Regress:** if everything needs a maker, the maker needs one; if something may be unmade, the world may.
3. **Material and instrument:** an immaterial creator before the world lacks material, location and tools.
4. **Suffering:** a compassionate omnipotent creator is difficult to reconcile with pervasive inequality.
5. **Redundancy:** substances, karma and natural order already perform the explanatory work.
6. ***Svabhāva*:** things act according to their natures without continuous divine supervision.

### 3. Theistic replies

A classical theist can deny that creation satisfies a need: goodness may be freely diffusive rather than
deficiency-driven. Nyāya can argue that unconscious karma cannot allocate precise fruits. The Jain reply
is that karmic maturation is law-like, just as causal processes need no supervisor at each operation.

The fair verdict is targeted: the motive argument is strong against an anthropomorphic needy maker, less
decisive against a timeless and non-needy source.

### 4. Perfected souls

- An **arihanta/kevalin** has attained omniscience while embodied.
- A **Tīrthaṅkara** is an arihanta who re-establishes and teaches the fourfold community.
- A **siddha** is fully liberated and disembodied.

These beings do not create, judge or answer petitions. Worship honours realised qualities and holds up a
path of perfection. Every Tīrthaṅkara becomes a siddha, but not every siddha was a Tīrthaṅkara.

### UPSC use

For 2019, line 1 should correct the question: Jainism propounds no creator proof. Then give the negative
case and the positive redefinition of divinity. Merely writing “Jainism is atheistic” is incomplete.

### Revision notes

- Jain cosmology is uncreated and beginningless.
- Perfection/motive and karmic redundancy are central objections.
- The theistic non-needy-creation reply narrows the motive argument.
- Divinity is achieved, not a cosmic office.
- Tīrthaṅkara and siddha must not be equated.
- Non-theism does not entail absence of worship or religious discipline.

### Retrieval check

Answer in five lines: why is the 2019 wording a trap, and how should its two halves be handled?

## Lesson 9 — Buddhism Against an Unchanging Creator

Progress: 9/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: diagnosis, causal dilemma, dependent origination and moral pressure

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not used — the permitted topic file and PYQ ledger control the argument.
CA search: not relevant.
CA found: none used.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: three independent levels

```text
GENEALOGY: why creator-belief arises
METAPHYSICS: can a permanent cause produce temporal effects?
MORALITY: what follows from a creator's responsibility for suffering?
```

### 1. Diagnosis is not yet refutation

Some Buddhist materials explain creator-belief as error or set cosmological speculation aside as
soteriologically unhelpful. Such accounts can weaken authority or relevance, but explaining why someone
believes a proposition does not itself show the proposition false. A strong answer says this explicitly.

### 2. The permanence–production dilemma

Later Buddhist argument attacks an unchanging creator:

1. A real cause must be causally efficacious.
2. A permanent cause produces either simultaneously or successively.
3. If it produces all effects simultaneously, the whole temporal world should appear at once.
4. If it produces successively, some new condition must distinguish one production from another.
5. Acquiring or depending on that condition compromises immutability or sole sufficiency.

This *krama–akrama* dilemma is stronger than merely saying “the Buddha was silent.”

### 3. Auxiliary conditions

If divine production waits upon matter, time or karmic readiness, those conditions help explain the
effect. God is no longer the sole sufficient cause. If no condition is needed, the delay and sequence of
effects remain unexplained.

Nyāya replies that God’s will is eternal while karmic conditions determine when effects occur. The
Buddhist rejoinder is that a will whose efficacy is timed by something else does not perform the complete
explanatory role originally claimed.

### 4. Inference and dependent origination

The relation “effect implies conscious maker” is secured for artifacts, not for mountains or worlds.
Thus the required *vyāpti* is disputed. Conditioned co-arising explains events through interdependent
causes without a first creator. A creator becomes explanatorily redundant.

### 5. Moral pressure

If all states ultimately express divine will, responsibility for suffering and meaningful karmic effort
become difficult to preserve. This argument is strongest against an omnipotent and benevolent creator,
not against every possible transcendent principle.

Buddhism can accept devas as impermanent beings within saṃsāra while rejecting a creator. Non-theism is
not the denial of every elevated religious being.

### UPSC use

For 2018, organise the answer into diagnosis, metaphysical refutation and moral criticism. Give the
permanence–production dilemma the largest space, then include Nyāya’s timed-effects reply and a residue.

### Revision notes

- Genealogical explanation is not logical refutation.
- A permanent cause faces simultaneous/successive production pressure.
- Auxiliary conditions threaten sole sufficiency.
- Artifact *vyāpti* may not cover the cosmos.
- Dependent origination supplies a non-creator causal model.
- Buddhism rejects a creator, not all devas.

## Lesson 10 — Three Other Indian Refusals: Cārvāka, Mīmāṃsā and Sāṃkhya

Progress: 10/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: epistemic, scriptural-ritual and cosmological alternatives

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not used — canonical Markdown and the approved Mīmāṃsā benchmark control the distinctions.
CA search: not relevant.
CA found: none used.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: same conclusion, different routes

| School | Primary challenge | What replaces divine explanation? |
|---|---|---|
| Cārvāka | no secure cognition of supersensible God | perceptible world and restricted inference |
| Mīmāṃsā | scripture and ritual efficacy need no author | authorless Veda and *apūrva* |
| Sāṃkhya | unconscious nature can transform without a maker | *prakṛti* and its guṇas |

### 1. Cārvāka: the epistemic challenge

Cārvāka pressures inference to an unobserved God. Everyday inference may work where relations are
repeatedly observed, but no one observes worlds being made by deities. The Nyāya response is that valid
inference can reach unperceived causes if the invariable relation is otherwise established. The dispute is
therefore about the scope and security of inference, not simply about whether God has been seen.

### 2. Mīmāṃsā: authorless authority

Udayana’s Veda-author grounds are rejected because the Veda is beginningless and authorless. Its authority
does not depend on a trustworthy divine speaker. Words and their relations are treated as eternal, while
ritual injunction reveals *dharma*.

### 3. *Apūrva*

Ritual action generates an unseen potency connecting performance with a later result. This allows moral
and ritual efficacy without an omniscient allocator. Nyāya objects that an unconscious potency cannot
select exact persons, fruits and timings. Mīmāṃsā replies that law-like efficacy need not be a conscious
decision any more than fire’s burning requires a supervisor.

### 4. Sāṃkhya: transformation without creation

The world evolves from *prakṛti* through the interaction of its three guṇas. No intelligent material maker
is required. Nyāya replies that unconscious nature cannot explain purposive coordination for the
experience and liberation of selves. Sāṃkhya can answer that teleology need not be conscious intention;
natural structure can serve the ends of *puruṣa* without a creator.

### 5. Why the distinctions matter

These objections cannot be used interchangeably:

- Cārvāka attacks the means of knowing God.
- Mīmāṃsā removes God’s authorial and karmic functions.
- Sāṃkhya offers an alternative account of cosmic evolution.

None of this automatically proves that religious life is impossible. Mīmāṃsā is ritual orthodoxy without
a creator; Sāṃkhya can be spiritually rigorous; Buddhism and Jainism sustain full religious paths.

### UPSC use

Use these schools as precise objections in Nyāya or comparative answers. One line naming each school is
not enough; attach the exact divine role it rejects.

### Revision notes

- Cārvāka questions supersensible inference.
- Mīmāṃsā rejects a divine Vedic author.
- *Apūrva* replaces conscious karmic administration.
- Sāṃkhya’s *prakṛti* supplies non-theistic cosmic evolution.
- Nyāya replies through inference, intelligence and allocation.
- No-creator and no-religion are different claims.

## Lesson 11 — Madhva: Dependence, Realism and Scriptural Priority

Progress: 11/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: *svatantra/paratantra*, three pramāṇas and graded strands

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not used — the canonical Madhva module and 2026 supplement control this lesson.
CA search: not relevant.
CA found: none used.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the division of epistemic labour

```text
PERCEPTION -> plurality and difference are real
INFERENCE  -> dependence can point toward an independent ground
SCRIPTURE  -> identifies that ground as Viṣṇu with determinate attributes
```

### 1. Ontology before argument

Madhva distinguishes the one independent reality, ***svatantra***, from all dependent reality,
***paratantra***. Viṣṇu alone is independent; souls and matter are real but dependent. The fivefold
difference classifies real distinctions among God, souls and matter. It is not five proofs of God.

Beginninglessness does not equal independence. Souls and matter may lack a first temporal origin while
remaining dependent in existence, power and transformation.

### 2. Three means of knowledge

Madhva accepts perception, inference and verbal testimony. Perception and inference cannot be dismissed,
because scripture itself is heard, read and interpreted through ordinary cognitive capacities. If these
capacities were wholly invalid, scriptural knowledge would be inaccessible. Their reliability supports
real plurality against Advaitic reduction.

### 3. Four strands, graded

| Strand | What it contributes | Proper weight |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture | identifies the independent ground as Viṣṇu and discloses attributes | decisive for theology |
| Dependence | finite realities do not contain the ground of their existence and operation | central metaphysical reconstruction |
| Order | suggests intelligence | limited; yields an architect more readily than an ontological ground |
| Perfection | clarifies the supreme divine concept | not an Anselmian existence-proof |

The settled position is modest about natural theology: reason secures realism and can articulate
dependence, while scripture supplies the supersensible identity of God.

### 4. Main objections and replies

- **Circularity:** scripture proves God while God guarantees scripture.
  **Reply:** scriptural testimony is treated as an independent means of knowledge, not validated by a
  prior proof of divine authorship. **Residue:** non-Vedic critics reject that authority.
- **Underdetermination:** dependence may yield an independent ground, not Viṣṇu.
  **Reply:** precisely; scripture supplies identity. This is Madhva’s division of labour.
- **Rival eternals:** beginningless souls and matter seem to limit God.
  **Reply:** they remain ontologically dependent despite temporal beginninglessness.
- **Design weakness:** order proves at most an arranger.
  **Reply:** this limitation is accepted rather than hidden.

### 5. Historiographic caution

Later Dvaita thinkers, especially Jayatīrtha and Vyāsatīrtha, systematise much of the formal dialectic.
Do not attribute every polished syllogism of the tradition directly to Madhva.

### UPSC use

For 2026, “explain and examine” requires roughly three-fifths exposition and two-fifths evaluation. Start
with dependence and the three *pramāṇas*, grade the four strands, then assess circularity,
underdetermination and the design limitation.

### Revision notes

- *Svatantra* is Viṣṇu alone; all else is *paratantra*.
- *Pañca-bheda* classifies differences, not proofs.
- Perception and inference secure realism.
- Scripture identifies the independent ground.
- Design is deliberately limited.
- Later Dvaita dialectic should not all be assigned to Madhva.

### Retrieval check

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: comparison by function

| Explanatory function | Western route | Indian pro-theistic route | Indian alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| effect to intelligence | Paley; Aquinas Way 5 | Nyāya *kāryāt/āyojanāt* | Buddhist challenge to artifact *vyāpti* |
| derivative causation | Aquinas Ways 1–2 | Nyāya efficient arranger | infinite/brute order; *prakṛti* |
| contingency/dependence | Aquinas Way 3; Leibniz | Madhva *paratantra* dependence | brute fact; beginningless non-creator order |
| moral distribution | Kantian hope differs in form | Nyāya *karmādhyakṣa* | Mīmāṃsā *apūrva*; Jain karma |
| revelation and language | separate revealed theology | Udayana’s Vedic grounds; Madhva’s decisive scripture | Mīmāṃsā authorless Veda |

### 1. Aquinas and Nyāya

Both reason from a dependent or ordered world toward intelligence. They differ in causal background:
Aquinas ultimately connects God with the being of creatures, whereas Nyāya retains eternal atoms and
treats God as efficient arranger. Nyāya also gives karmic administration and Vedic authority a role absent
from the Five Ways.

### 2. Similar objections, different targets

Hume’s analogy criticism and the Buddhist challenge to artifact *vyāpti* both question extension from
human products to the cosmos. Mīmāṃsā attacks scripture-author and karmic-governor grounds rather than
Aquinas’s potentiality. Jain motive criticism targets creator action, while Kant’s predicate objection
targets conceptual existence. Good comparison preserves the target.

### 3. Cumulative-case logic

A cumulative case says several individually limited strands jointly increase rational support:

```text
contingency + order + moral experience + religious experience
                     |
       independent evidential contribution?
                     v
             net support for theism
```

A strand may be **C-inductive** if it raises the probability of theism without making it more likely than
not. Several such strands may jointly become stronger. The crucial conditions are:

1. each strand has positive evidential force;
2. the strands are not merely restatements of one contested premise;
3. prior plausibility is not ignored;
4. counterevidence, especially evil, is included in the net balance;
5. rival hypotheses are compared.

### 4. Leaky buckets and rope

The “leaky buckets” objection says several failed proofs do not become a successful proof by accumulation.
The defender replies that weak evidence is not zero evidence: strands of a rope may jointly support weight.
The reply works only if each strand contributes something and the contributions are sufficiently
independent.

### 5. Underdetermination remains

Even a successful cumulative case may favour:

- one perfect personal God;
- a finite designer;
- several designers;
- an impersonal necessary ground;
- an axiological principle.

Simplicity may be invoked for one unlimited agent, but simplicity itself is contestable. Cumulative force
therefore raises probability at most; it does not automatically identify the God of a particular religion.

### UPSC use

For comparative answers, announce axes before comparing: starting datum, causal model, divine role,
objection and conclusion. For the 2022 preference question, a defensible verdict may prefer *a posteriori*
arguments for public accessibility while admitting their weaker theological reach.

### Revision notes

- Compare functions and conclusions, not names alone.
- Aquinas creates no atomistic Nyāya equivalent.
- Hume and Buddhist critics converge on extrapolation but arise in different systems.
- Cumulative strength requires positive and non-duplicative evidence.
- Counterevidence must be included in the net.
- Probability does not identify a unique worship-worthy deity.

### Retrieval check

Give two similarities and three differences between Aquinas and Nyāya without using the phrase “both prove God.”

# OPTIONAL ADVANCED DEPTH — NOT REQUIRED FOR A CORE ANSWER

## Visual: Advanced material must remain subordinate

```text
Core exposition -> one Advanced refinement -> return to directive -> verdict
```

## A1. Modal possibility and symmetry

The modal ontological argument gains force in a system where possibly necessary existence entails
necessary existence. Its vulnerability lies in the possibility premise. A critic may argue that an
incoherent package of maximal attributes is not genuinely possible. A reverse-modal parody can attempt
to posit possible necessary non-existence, but it succeeds only if that possibility is coherent. The
lesson is methodological: modal logic transfers rather than eliminates metaphysical burden.

**Deployment:** one paragraph after Kant, only in a 15- or 20-marker.

**Stop rule:** do not turn an Anselm answer into a technical survey of modal systems.

## A2. Weak and strong principles of sufficient reason

A weak explanatory principle asks reasons for many ordinary contingent facts. A strong version demands
a sufficient reason for every contingent fact or for the contingent totality. Rejecting the strong
version does not abolish local explanation; accepting local explanation does not automatically commit one
to an explanation of the whole.

**Deployment:** use to sharpen Leibniz or the brute-fact objection.

**Stop rule:** return to the printed cosmological argument after one distinction.

## A3. Qualitative Bayesian comparison

Evidence supports theism when it is more expected under theism than under a rival. A cumulative case must
ask:

- What is the prior plausibility of each hypothesis?
- How expected is the evidence on each?
- Are evidence-items independent?
- What counterevidence lowers the balance?

No equations are needed in an UPSC answer. The value lies in making “probable” comparative rather than
rhetorical.

**Deployment:** a short refinement in cumulative or design evaluation.

**Stop rule:** never present undefined probability language as numerical precision.

## Advanced traps

1. Treating possible necessity as self-evident.
2. Assuming every acceptance of explanation entails the strongest PSR.
3. Counting several versions of the same design intuition as independent evidence.
4. Using Bayesian vocabulary without naming rival hypotheses.

## Advanced retrieval checks

1. Where does the burden move in a modal ontological proof?
2. How can one reject a strong PSR while preserving ordinary explanation?
3. Why does evidential dependence weaken cumulative confirmation?

## Advanced revision notes

- Modal repair relocates burden to coherent possibility.
- Explanatory principles come in strengths.
- Bayesian support is comparative and prior-sensitive.
- Evidence must be net, not one-directional.
- One Advanced move is enough.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** Why does the conclusion “there is a necessary being” fall short of classical theism?

### Model answer

A necessary being is introduced to terminate an explanatory chain of contingent or derived reality. The
conclusion initially establishes only underived existence. Classical theism, however, attributes unity,
intelligence, freedom, perfect goodness, omniscience and providential relation to the world.

The gap is not verbal but inferential. Necessity alone does not show personality; an impersonal ground may
also be proposed. Nor does being underived entail moral perfection. A defender may add arguments from
order, moral experience or simplicity, but each introduces new premises. Indian comparison confirms the
point: Madhva uses scripture to identify the independent ground as Viṣṇu, while Nyāya adds intelligence
through effect and coordination arguments.

Thus a cosmological proof may rationally motivate an underived terminus without independently proving the
complete God of worship. Its success should be graded by immediate conclusion, not inflated by later
theology.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** Compare the Nyāya inference from the world as effect with Paley’s design argument.

### Model answer

Both arguments move from observed order to intelligence. Paley begins with artifacts: a watch’s parts are
adjusted to a function and therefore imply a watchmaker. Organisms display still greater adjustment, so
he infers design. Nyāya’s *kāryāt* treats the composite world as an effect requiring a maker, while
*āyojanāt* argues that inert atoms need intelligent coordination.

The similarities are formal: both extend reasoning from familiar products to nature and both face a
Humean challenge about the world–artifact analogy. Their metaphysical settings differ. Paley commonly
presses biological organisation; Nyāya retains eternal atoms and makes Īśvara their efficient arranger.
Nyāya also adds karmic administration, language and Vedic authority, none of which belongs to Paley’s
watch.

The objection is that observed artifacts establish only that some effects have makers, not that every
composite world does. Nyāya replies that inference may reach unperceived causes and that unconscious
atoms and *adṛṣṭa* cannot coordinate themselves. Yet if the analogy is probabilistic, the conclusion is at
most an intelligent arranger, not automatically one infinite and perfectly good God.

Therefore the two arguments converge as design-like inferences but diverge in causal ontology and
religious function. Both are suggestive when read cumulatively, not conclusive as isolated demonstrations.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** “The Indian debate about God is not a simple conflict between belief and unbelief, but a
competition among explanatory systems.” Discuss.

### Model answer

The proposition is justified because Indian schools dispute not only whether God exists but which
explanatory tasks require intelligence. Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika assigns Īśvara the roles of efficient arranger of
eternal atoms, governor of cosmic order and omniscient allocator of karmic fruits. Udayana supports these
roles through a cluster including *kāryāt*, *āyojanāt*, *dhṛtyādeḥ*, linguistic transmission, Vedic
authority and determinate numerical cognition.

Rival schools do not merely say “no.” Cārvāka attacks the epistemic route: an inference from artifacts
cannot securely reach a supersensible cosmic maker. Mīmāṃsā preserves Vedic ritual while rejecting a
divine author; the authorless Veda reveals *dharma*, and *apūrva* connects action with delayed fruit.
Sāṃkhya offers *prakṛti* and the guṇas as an account of world-evolution without intelligent creation.
Jainism invokes beginningless substances, karmic law and intrinsic natures; it also argues that a perfect
being lacks a motive to create. Buddhism supplies dependent origination and presses the
*krama–akrama* dilemma: an unchanging cause cannot coherently generate a temporally ordered world either
all at once or successively.

Madhva complicates the binary further. Dvaita affirms Viṣṇu as the sole independent reality, but does not
make a Nyāya-style design proof decisive. Perception and inference defend realism and dependence, while
scripture identifies the independent ground. Thus even pro-theistic systems disagree over whether reason
alone reaches God.

Nyāya replies that unconscious mechanisms cannot select exact outcomes, and that valid inference need not
be confined to perception. Rivals answer that law-like causation does not require a supervisor. The
dispute therefore concerns explanatory sufficiency, the legitimacy of supersensible inference and the
relation between consciousness and order.

Indian philosophy presents a many-sided contest among maker, allocator, authorless authority, natural
evolution, karmic law, conditioned origination and revealed dependence. “Theism versus atheism” is too
coarse; the philosophically useful unit is the explanatory job and the rival proposed to perform it.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Register 1 — Evaluation grammar

- Validity: conclusion follows if premises are granted.
- Soundness: validity plus defensible premises.
- Adequacy: immediate conclusion reaches the attributes required by the stem.
- *A priori*: concept-based; *a posteriori*: world-based.
- Demonstrative, abductive, practical and testimonial force must not be merged.
- Explanatory termination requires a rule distinguishing derivative from underived reality.

## Register 2 — Ontological argument

- Anselm: greatest conceivable being; merely mental existence would permit a greater.
- Descartes: supreme perfection cannot lack necessary existence.
- Gaunilo: parody tests illicit concept-to-reality movement.
- Kant: existence posits instantiation; it does not enlarge a concept.
- Modal repair: possible maximal greatness plus necessary existence.
- Residue: coherent possibility bears the burden.
- Indian warning: no exact Nyāya or Madhva analogue.

## Register 3 — Cosmological family

- Way 1: potentiality actualised by actuality → unmoved actualiser.
- Way 2: derivative efficient causation → first explanatory cause.
- Way 3: contingent beings → underived necessary being.
- Third Way criticism: distributed possible absence does not entail universal simultaneous absence.
- Way 4: degrees → maximal source; participation is needed to avoid crude maximum reasoning.
- Way 5: non-conscious finality → intelligence; distinct from Paley.
- Leibniz: sufficient reason of contingent totality.
- *Kalām*: temporal beginning → cause; beginning and personhood each need defence.

## Register 4 — Design

- Paley’s watch: coordinated means and ends imply maker.
- Hume: weak analogy, finite effect, plural designers, alternative natural analogies, evil/disorder.
- Darwin: non-intentional explanation of biological adaptation.
- Probabilistic retreat preserves limited force, not demonstration.
- The conclusion may be an arranger, not one infinite morally perfect God.

## Register 5 — Moral argument

- Lawgiver route: duty points to commander; psychological and social alternatives.
- Value-ground route: objective morality points to transcendent ground; non-theistic realism remains possible.
- Euthyphro: arbitrary command or independent goodness.
- Kant: autonomous duty first; highest good second; God and immortality as practical postulates.
- Practical necessity is not theoretical knowledge.

## Register 6 — Nyāya–Udayana

- Īśvara: omniscient efficient arranger of eternal atoms and allocator of karma.
- *Kāryāt*: world as effect.
- *Āyojanāt*: atom conjunction.
- *Dhṛtyādeḥ*: support and governance.
- *Padāt*: original teaching.
- *Pratyayataḥ*: Vedic cognitive validity.
- *Śruteḥ*: scriptural testimony.
- *Vākyāt*: sentences imply personal authorship.
- *Saṃkhyāviśeṣāt*: numerical cognition for dyadic formation.
- *Adṛṣṭa*: unconscious merit requires intelligent allocation.
- Main counters: Cārvāka inference, Mīmāṃsā authorless Veda/*apūrva*, Buddhist artifact *vyāpti*, Sāṃkhya *prakṛti*.

## Register 7 — Jain and Buddhist critiques

**Jain**

- uncreated beginningless cosmos;
- motive/perfection, regress, material/instrument, suffering, redundancy and *svabhāva* objections;
- non-needy creation is the strongest theistic reply;
- arihanta, Tīrthaṅkara and siddha are not creators.

**Buddhist**

- diagnosis of belief differs from refutation;
- permanent cause must produce simultaneously or successively;
- simultaneous production collapses sequence;
- successive production implies change or auxiliary dependence;
- dependent origination and disputed artifact *vyāpti* remove explanatory need;
- devas may exist without a creator.

## Register 8 — Other Indian alternatives

- Cārvāka: supersensible inference lacks established universal relation.
- Mīmāṃsā: Veda is authorless; *apūrva* links act and fruit.
- Sāṃkhya: *prakṛti* evolves through guṇas without intelligent creation.
- Rejecting a creator is not identical with rejecting religious practice.

## Register 9 — Madhva

- Viṣṇu alone is *svatantra*; souls and matter are *paratantra*.
- *Pañca-bheda* gives five differences, not five proofs.
- Perception and inference defend realism.
- Dependence points toward an independent ground.
- Scripture identifies the ground and divine attributes.
- Design yields an architect more readily than comprehensive dependence.
- Beginninglessness does not imply independence.
- Jayatīrtha and Vyāsatīrtha systematise later dialectic.

## Register 10 — Comparison and cumulative judgement

- Aquinas and Nyāya converge in world-based reasoning but differ over matter, karma and divine role.
- Hume and Buddhist critics both challenge artifact extension.
- Evidence accumulates only if each strand has force and is not duplicative.
- Evil and rival explanations belong in the net assessment.
- Cumulative probability still underdetermines the type of ultimate reality.
- Final formula: state what follows, on which premise, and what remains unproved.

## Register 11 — Last-page answer checklist

1. Have I classified the argument?
2. Have I numbered its premises?
3. Have I identified the exact disputed assumption?
4. Have I attached criticism to a premise, inference or conclusion?
5. Have I given the strongest reply?
6. Have I preserved Indian school differences?
7. Have I obeyed the directive and mark split?
8. Have I limited the conclusion to its actual reach?
