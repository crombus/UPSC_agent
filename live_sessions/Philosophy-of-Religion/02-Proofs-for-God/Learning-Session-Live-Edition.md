### Visual first — three gates between an argument and the God of religion

```text
ARGUMENT
   │
   ├─ Gate 1: VALIDITY
   │  If the premises are true, does the conclusion follow?
   │
   ├─ Gate 2: SOUNDNESS
   │  Are the premises true or adequately warranted?
   │
   └─ Gate 3: DIVINE ADEQUACY
      Does the conclusion establish one necessary, intelligent,
      personal, perfectly good and worship-worthy God?

Possible result:
valid argument → doubtful premise → modest conclusion
```

*The visual shows why “the proof fails” is too vague. A critic may attack the inference, a premise, or the theological identity of the conclusion.*

### 1. Begin with the ordinary puzzle

Suppose someone says, “There must be an explanation for the universe.” Even if that sentence is reasonable, three further questions arise. Must the explanation be a being? Must that being be intelligent? Must that intelligent being be the God worshipped by a religious tradition? The syllabus word **proof** therefore cannot be treated as a guarantee of mathematical certainty.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Classical arguments perform different jobs:

| Kind of reasoning | What it claims | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Deductive demonstration | Denial of the conclusion conflicts with accepted premises | Anselm’s ontological argument |
| Explanatory or abductive inference | God is required or is the best explanation | Cosmological, design, Nyāya |
| Practical postulation | Moral commitment rationally requires hope in God | Kant |
| Cumulative confirmation | Several limited arguments jointly raise probability | Swinburne; structurally, Udayana |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Judging all four by one standard creates false victories and false defeats. Kant’s moral postulate is weak if assessed as a deductive proof, but that is not the status Kant claims for it.

### 2. A priori and a posteriori

Plainly stated:

- **A priori** reasoning begins from a concept or necessary relation rather than an observed feature of the world.
- **A posteriori** reasoning begins from motion, causation, contingency, order, obligation, testimony, or another experienced fact.

| Axis | A priori | A posteriori |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Concept of God | World or experience |
| Main example | Ontological argument | Cosmological, design, moral, Nyāya |
| Promised strength | Necessary existence | Evidential contact |
| Main danger | Defining existence into a concept | Inferring too much from a finite effect |
| Typical verdict | Strong modality, contested starting point | Better dialectical start, weaker conclusion |

**Example with a limit:** A geometrical proof can derive properties from a defined triangle, but it cannot show that a triangle is drawn on the table. This helps explain Kant’s complaint against ontological reasoning. The analogy stops there because the ontological defender argues that God is unlike ordinary objects: necessary existence allegedly belongs to maximal greatness itself.

### 3. Explanatory termination

Many arguments ask where explanation may legitimately stop. The theist says that contingent or derivative reality cannot be ultimate. The critic may reply with:

1. an infinite regress;
2. a brute contingent totality;
3. a non-personal necessary principle;
4. a natural process sufficient for the observed order.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** “The universe is a brute fact” does not disprove cosmological reasoning. It rejects or limits the principle that every contingent fact needs a sufficient reason. Conversely, an unexplained scientific question is not positive evidence for God.

### 4. The standing burden of proof

Every argument must defend three separate moves:

```text
starting datum
    ↓
bridge principle
    ↓
explanatory terminus
    ↓
identification with God
```

For example, the design argument moves from order to intelligence. It must still show why the intelligence is one rather than many, perfect rather than finite, and good rather than morally indifferent.

### UPSC application

**Syllabus use:** This distinction controls every “critically examine” question in the topic.

**Verified PYQ linkage:** 2022 Q8(a), 20 marks: “Discuss the main points of distinction between a priori and a posteriori arguments for the existence of God. Which one according to you should be preferred over the other? Give reasons and justifications for your answer.”

**Demand:** Compare source, method, strength, vulnerability, and conclusion; then prefer one by a named criterion.

**Approach only:** Define both → compare on four or five axes → use ontological versus Aquinas/Paley/Nyāya → prefer a posteriori for evidential contact while conceding its weaker modal conclusion → state that neither alone reaches the God of religion.

### Revision notes

1. “Proof” may mean demonstration, explanatory inference, practical postulate, or cumulative confirmation.
2. Validity asks whether the conclusion follows.
3. Soundness asks whether the premises are true or warranted.
4. Divine adequacy asks whether the conclusion reaches the God of religion.
5. A priori arguments begin from concepts; a posteriori arguments begin from experience.
6. Ontological reasoning is the main a priori family.
7. Cosmological, design, moral, and Nyāya arguments are world- or experience-based.
8. A finite cause, first cause, or designer is not automatically one perfect personal God.
9. A brute-fact reply rejects explanatory demand; it does not refute logic by itself.
10. An explanatory gap is not positive evidence for God.
11. The 2022 preference must use a criterion, not a bare personal choice.

### Local practice

**MCQ 1: A**

A. An argument may reach a first cause or designer without establishing the one, infinite, personal and perfectly good God of developed religion.
B. An argument may contain a grammatical error even when its conclusion is true.
C. An empirical premise may be difficult to measure numerically.
D. A religious person may refuse to debate a non-believer.

- **A — Correct:** It names the gap between the modest philosophical terminus and the richer religious conception of God.
- **B — Incorrect:** Grammatical quality is not the philosophical test of whether the conclusion possesses divine attributes.
- **C — Incorrect:** Empirical measurability concerns evidence, not the identity of the being reached.
- **D — Incorrect:** Willingness to debate has no bearing on an argument’s theological conclusion.

**MCQ 2: B**

Why is a posteriori reasoning often preferred in debate even though its conclusion is weaker?

A. It guarantees a necessary being by definition.
B. It begins from features of experience that an opponent may already grant.
C. It is immune to scientific and philosophical revision.
D. It always yields the God of a specific religion.

- **A — Incorrect:** Necessary existence is the special promise of ontological reasoning, not the normal result of world-based inference.
- **B — Correct:** Motion, contingency, order, and moral obligation provide shared starting points, though their interpretation remains contested.
- **C — Incorrect:** Darwinian explanation and cosmological alternatives show that empirical arguments remain revisable.
- **D — Incorrect:** A posteriori arguments characteristically underdetermine divine personality, perfection, and religious identity.

**Remediation cue:** If you confuse validity with adequacy, rewrite any proof in four lines: datum → bridge → terminus → divine identification. Name where the critic intervenes.

## Lesson 2 — Ontological Reasoning: Can the Concept of God Entail Existence?

Progress: 2/14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Anselm, Descartes, Gaunilo, Kant, and modal necessity

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — Anselm’s reductio and the place where critics intervene

```text
God = that than which nothing greater can be conceived
                    │
Assume God exists only in the understanding
                    │
The same being existing in reality would be greater
                    │
A greater-than-the-greatest is conceivable: contradiction
                    │
Therefore God exists in reality

Critics:
Gaunilo → does this form prove perfect islands?
Kant    → is existence a greatness-making predicate?
Modal reply → is maximal greatness genuinely possible?
```

*The argument is a reductio: it assumes mental existence alone and claims that the assumption contradicts the definition.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Anselm’s thought is not “I can imagine something, therefore it exists.” It is narrower: the greatest conceivable being cannot lack a mode of existence that would make it greater. The question is whether **existence** can do that conceptual work.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Anselm defines God as “that than which nothing greater can be conceived.” “Greater” means more perfect, not physically larger.

### 2. *Proslogion* 2 and *Proslogion* 3

**First form: existence in reality**

1. The denier understands the God-concept.
2. God exists at least in the understanding.
3. Existing in reality is greater than existing only in understanding.
4. If God existed only mentally, a greater being could be conceived.
5. That contradicts the definition.
6. Therefore God exists in reality.

**Second form: necessary existence**

1. A being that cannot be thought not to exist is greater than one that can fail to exist.
2. The greatest conceivable being cannot be conceivably non-existent.
3. Therefore God exists necessarily.

The second form matters because it introduces **aseity**, existence from oneself rather than dependence on another.

### 3. Descartes’ form

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** In *Meditations* V, Descartes treats existence as inseparable from a supremely perfect being, as the sum of a triangle’s angles is inseparable from its nature. His form is an **essence-containment** argument, whereas Anselm’s first form is a comparative reductio.

**Limit:** Descartes’ causal argument from the idea of infinity in *Meditations* III is a different proof and must not be merged with the ontological argument.

### 4. Gaunilo’s parody and Anselm’s reply

Gaunilo proposes a supremely excellent island. If the argument’s form is sound, the island must exist, because a real island would be greater than a merely imagined one.

Anselm replies that islands are contingent objects with no intrinsic maximum. Adding another tree or beach can always alter the ranking. God alone is conceived as non-dependent and necessarily existent.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** This reply protects the second form better than the first, but transfers the burden to the coherence of **maximal greatness**. The theist must show that the divine perfections are jointly possible.

### 5. Kant’s objection

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Kant argues that “existence is not a real predicate.” A predicate ordinarily adds content: “a hundred silver coins” and “a hundred heavy silver coins” differ conceptually. “A hundred real coins” and “a hundred possible coins” do not differ in the concept of the coins; reality changes what is instantiated, not what the concept contains.

Hence:

```text
conceptual analysis can tell us:
IF God exists, God exists necessarily.

conceptual analysis cannot by itself tell us:
THERE IS an instance of that concept.
```

Post-Fregean analysis reinforces the point by treating existence as quantification or instantiation rather than an ordinary first-order property.

### 6. Modal repair

A modern modal form argues:

1. It is possible that a maximally great being exists.
2. Maximal greatness includes necessary existence and maximal excellence in every possible world.
3. If such a being exists in any possible world, it exists in every possible world.
4. Therefore it exists in the actual world.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The burden moves to premise 1. If maximal greatness is impossible or incoherent, the argument never begins. Modal logic clarifies the dispute but does not neutrally settle it.

### 7. No exact classical Indian analogue

Nyāya existence-claims require inferential relations grounded through *vyāpti*; Advaita relies on *śruti*, reasoning, and realization. Neither offers a classical transition from a definition alone to existence.

### Strongest objection, best reply, residual

| Stage | Content |
|---|---|
| Strongest objection | Kant: existence does not enrich a concept |
| Best reply | Necessary existence is a modal status, not an ordinary perfection |
| Residual | The possibility and coherence of maximal greatness remain unproved |

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQs:**

- 2018 Q6(c), 15 marks: “Does the concept of God entail the existence of God? Examine from the perspective of ontological argument.”
- 2020 Q6(a), 20 marks: “Is the ontological proof for the existence of God by St. Anselm logical? Discuss.”
- 2024 Q7(c), 15 marks (10+5): “Present an exposition of ontological proof for the existence of God along with its criticism.”

### Revision notes

1. Anselm’s definition is “that than which nothing greater can be conceived.”
2. *Proslogion* 2 moves from mental existence to real existence.
3. *Proslogion* 3 moves to necessary existence and aseity.
4. Descartes uses essence-containment, not Anselm’s exact reductio.
5. Gaunilo tests whether the form proves too much.
6. Anselm replies that an island is contingent and lacks a coherent maximum.
7. Kant denies that existence is a real predicate.
8. Existence posits an object; it does not add descriptive content to the concept.
9. Fregean analysis treats existence as instantiation.
10. Modal versions make necessary existence central.
11. Their possibility premise carries the decisive burden.
12. No exact classical Indian ontological argument exists.
13. The safest verdict is conditional entailment without actual instantiation.

### Visual first — three cosmological questions that must not be merged

```text
PRESENT DEPENDENCE             CONTINGENT TOTALITY          TEMPORAL BEGINNING
Aquinas Ways 1–2              Aquinas Way 3; Leibniz      kalām
What actualises change now?    Why is there anything?      What caused the beginning?
        │                              │                           │
unmoved actualiser /           necessary ground /          cause of the universe’s
first efficient cause          sufficient reason           beginning

“First” = explanatory          Beginning need not be       Beginning is an explicit
priority, not first moment     assumed                     premise
```

*The visual prevents the most common mistake: turning every cosmological argument into “the universe began, therefore God.”*

### 1. The Five Ways as five distinct routes

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:**

| Way | Starting point | Terminus |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Change: potency becoming act | Unmoved actualiser |
| 2 | Ordered efficient causes | First efficient cause |
| 3 | Contingent beings | Necessary being |
| 4 | Degrees of goodness, truth, nobility | Maximum/source of perfection |
| 5 | Regular directedness of non-rational nature | Directing intelligence |

Ways 1–3 form the central cosmological family. Way 4 depends on participation metaphysics. Way 5 is teleological and receives fuller treatment in Lesson 4.

### 2. Essentially and accidentally ordered series

```text
Accidentally ordered:
grandparent → parent → child
Earlier cause may cease; later cause retains its own power.

Essentially ordered:
hand → stick → stone
The intermediate cause has borrowed power now.
Remove the hand; the present action stops.
```

Aquinas’ strongest interpretation concerns an essentially ordered series. An infinite number of borrowers does not supply original causal power.

**Example with a limit:** A power strip receives electricity from another strip. Adding more strips never creates electricity. The example illustrates derivative power, but unlike God, the electrical source is still a physical dependent object.

### 3. Ways 1 and 2

**Way 1**

1. Change occurs.
2. Change is the actualization of a potential.
3. What is merely potential in a respect cannot actualize itself in that same respect.
4. Derivative actualizers require a non-derivative source.
5. Therefore an unmoved actualizer exists.

**Way 2**

1. Efficient causal order exists.
2. Nothing can be its own efficient cause, because it would be prior to itself.
3. A wholly derivative causal series cannot explain current causal efficacy.
4. Therefore a first efficient cause exists.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** A critic can accept causal dependence yet deny that every real causal series is essentially ordered. The Thomist must argue for the structure, not merely name it.

### 4. Way 3: contingency

The Third Way distinguishes beings that can fail to exist from a being whose necessity is not received from another.

1. Many beings are generated and perish.
2. Such beings are contingent.
3. If everything were contingent, Aquinas argues, there could have been a time when nothing existed.
4. Nothing comes from nothing.
5. Therefore not everything is contingent.
6. A chain of beings necessary through another cannot explain necessity finally.
7. A being necessary through itself must exist.

**Core criticism:** Step 3 may shift illicitly from “each thing has some time at which it does not exist” to “there is one time at which nothing exists.”

**Reply:** A contemporary dependency version need not use that temporal step. It asks whether the whole contingent order has the reason for its existence within itself.

### 5. Way 4 and Way 5 in outline

Way 4 reasons from degrees to a maximum and source of perfections. The criticism is immediate: comparative degrees do not always require an existing maximum. Its defence requires a metaphysics in which participated perfections depend on a primary source.

Way 5 reasons from regular goal-directed activity in non-conscious nature to intelligence. It is not Paley’s watch analogy; its model is the arrow directed by an archer.

### 6. Leibniz and the principle of sufficient reason

Leibniz asks: **Why is there something rather than nothing?**

1. Every contingent fact has a sufficient reason.
2. The total contingent series does not explain itself merely because every member has another contingent member behind it.
3. Its sufficient reason must lie outside the contingent series.
4. Therefore a necessary sufficient reason exists.

The critic may reject the unrestricted principle of sufficient reason and allow a brute totality.

### 7. The kalām

1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore the universe has a cause.

The beginning premise is explicit. Further reasoning is needed to identify the cause as timeless, immaterial, powerful, personal, or good.

### Strongest objections, replies, residuals

| Objection | Reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| Infinite regress may be possible | An infinite derivative series still lacks non-derivative power | Must show the dependence series is essentially ordered |
| Fallacy of composition | The argument concerns dependence of the totality, not transfer of a property | Critics may still permit a brute contingent totality |
| Kant: “necessary being” is not yet God | Further metaphysical arguments derive attributes | Those derivations are distinct and contestable |
| Why must the cause be personal or good? | Other arguments may supplement the conclusion | Single-proof adequacy remains limited |

**Verified PYQs:**

- 2022 Q6(a), 20 marks: “Present a critical exposition of different arguments offered by St. Thomas Aquinas to prove the existence of God also known as ‘Five Ways’. Which one of them do you find philosophically most interesting? Give reasons in support of your answer.”
- 2026 Q5(b), 10 marks: “Discuss Aquinas' argument from the contingency of the world to prove the existence of God.”

**Approach only:** For 2022, state all five distinctly, explain essentially ordered dependence, criticize each proportionately, and defend one Way by an explicit criterion. For 2026, write only the Third Way: possibility/necessity → temporal or modal pressure → necessary *per se* being → quantifier-shift and composition objections → qualified verdict.

### Revision notes

1. Aquinas offers five distinct Ways.
2. Ways 1–3 concern motion, efficient cause, and contingency.
3. Way 4 concerns degrees and participation.
4. Way 5 concerns directedness of non-conscious nature.
5. “First” normally means explanatory, not temporal, priority.
6. An essentially ordered series contains derivative causal powers.
7. The hand–stick–stone example illustrates present dependence.
8. Aquinas does not require a first moment of the universe.
9. The Third Way distinguishes contingent from necessary being.
10. Its temporal step faces a quantifier-shift objection.
11. Leibniz uses the principle of sufficient reason.
12. The kalām alone explicitly premises a temporal beginning.
13. Kant argues that the move from necessary being to God needs further support.

### Visual first — three arguments often mislabeled as one

| Form | Evidence | Inference | Direct rival |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aquinas, Way 5 | Regular directedness of non-rational nature | Directed activity implies intelligence | Natural-law accounts |
| Paley | Organismic complexity resembles purposive artifacts | Watch-like adjustment implies a designer | Humean analogy critique; Darwin |
| Fine-tuning | Life-permitting physical parameters | Design may make the data less surprising | Multiverse, necessity, selection effect |

*The table shows why Darwin directly attacks Paley’s biological evidence but does not by itself answer every question about law or cosmic parameters.*

### 1. Aquinas is not Paley

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Aquinas argues that entities lacking cognition regularly act toward ends. As an arrow reaches a target through an archer, non-rational directedness requires intelligence.

Paley argues by artifact analogy. A watch found on a heath contains coordinated parts that serve timekeeping; we infer a watchmaker. Organisms display comparable means–end adjustment; therefore an intelligent designer is inferred.

### 2. Paley’s case and its limits

Paley adds that the inference survives:

1. never having observed a watch being made;
2. imperfections in its operation;
3. ignorance of some parts’ functions.

His case is cumulative: eye, wing, fin, planetary pattern, and seasonal order.

**Example with a limit:** A camera lens and an eye both focus light, so the analogy makes purposive arrangement vivid. It cannot establish that their causal histories are alike; convergent function does not prove identical origin.

### 3. Hume’s five-front critique

Hume’s *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* predates Paley’s *Natural Theology*. The objections are carried principally by Philo.

| Front | Criticism | Target |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | A stable universe must display mutual adjustment; an Epicurean process may yield order | Evidential force |
| 2 | The universe is not sufficiently like a machine; organic analogies are equally available | Analogy |
| 3 | A finite effect warrants only a proportionate finite cause | Divine adequacy |
| 4 | Many, imperfect, inexperienced, or limited designers fit the evidence | Underdetermination |
| 5 | Waste, suffering, and disorder resist inference to a perfectly good designer | Counter-evidence |

Hume does not demonstrate that no designer exists. He limits what the evidence licenses.

### 4. Darwin and biological adaptation

Natural selection explains adaptation without foresight:

```text
heritable variation
      ↓
differential survival and reproduction
      ↓
accumulated fit between organism and environment
```

This removes the need to infer a designer from biological adjustment alone. It does not explain why there are laws of nature or why fundamental conditions permit complex life.

### 5. Fine-tuning as a bounded refinement

Contemporary versions often say:

1. Life-permitting parameters occupy a narrow range.
2. Such data may be more expected on a design hypothesis than on a single unguided chance hypothesis.
3. Therefore the data raise the probability of design.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** This is probabilistic, not deductive. Probability assignments, multiverse hypotheses, observation-selection effects, deeper laws, and the identity of a designer remain disputed. Fine-tuning should refine a UPSC conclusion, not replace Aquinas, Paley, and Hume.

### 6. Nyāya parallel

The pot–potter and world–maker reasoning resembles Paley. Nyāya additionally says inert atoms require intentional combination. Hume’s analogy and underdetermination objections therefore travel across traditions.

### Strongest objection, reply, residual

- **Objection:** The world is too unlike a human artifact for the inference to be reliable, and natural processes explain adaptation.
- **Reply:** Treat design as abductive and move from organismic complexity to general law or fine-tuning.
- **Residual:** The conclusion still fails to select one infinite, perfect, morally good God over rival designers or impersonal explanations.

### Visual first — three moral routes and one boundary

```text
MORAL EXPERIENCE
   ├─ conscience → personal judge or lawgiver
   ├─ objective value/duty → transcendent ground
   └─ Kant’s highest good → God and immortality as postulates

RELIGIOUS EXPERIENCE
   experience of the divine
        ↓
   may support a cumulative case
        ✕
   does not by itself become a public deductive proof
```

*The diagram separates three moral arguments and keeps religious experience within its proper boundary.*

### 1. Conscience and lawgiver

Newman-style reasoning says that guilt, responsibility, and the felt authority of conscience point beyond society to a personal moral judge.

**Objection:** Conscience may be explained through psychology, social formation, empathy, or practical reason. The inference assumes what it must prove: that moral authority cannot be naturalistically grounded.

### 2. Objective moral value and duty

Another route argues:

1. Objective moral obligations exist.
2. Objective obligations require an adequate personal or transcendent ground.
3. God is that ground.

The critic may accept moral realism without theism. The Euthyphro dilemma asks whether acts are good because God wills them or God wills them because they are good. The first horn risks arbitrariness; the second seems to place goodness beyond divine command.

**Reply:** Modified divine-command theories ground value in God’s necessarily loving nature and obligation in divine commands.

**Residual:** Calling the divine nature “loving” appears already to use a moral standard.

### 3. Kant’s argument from the highest good

Kant denies that theoretical reason proves God. Practical reason proceeds differently.

```text
moral law obligates
      ↓
highest good must be promoted
(virtue + happiness proportioned to virtue)
      ↓
“ought implies can”
      ↓
complete virtue requires endless moral progress → immortality
nature must be capable of moral proportion      → God
freedom is presupposed by obligation
```

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** The three postulates are freedom, immortality, and God. They are not theoretical knowledge.

God does not create obligation in Kant’s argument. Autonomous reason obligates; God is postulated as a condition for the possible harmony of virtue and happiness.

### 4. Objections to Kant

1. **Projection:** Cosmic moral completion may express a wish for fairness.
2. **Autonomy tension:** If morality is self-legislated by reason, perhaps God is dispensable.
3. **Highest-good dispute:** A critic may deny that moral life requires a final proportion of happiness to virtue.
4. **Adequacy:** A moral governor is not yet the creator or object of worship.

Kant’s reply to projection is that reward-seeking would destroy moral worth; the postulate arises from practical reason’s structure, not selfish calculation.

### 5. Nyāya parallel

Nyāya infers an actual *karmaphaladātā*, distributor of karmic fruit. Kant postulates a condition of rational moral hope. Thus Nyāya claims knowledge where Kant claims practical commitment.

### 6. Religious experience: why it belongs only at the edge here

Religious experience may be used in a cumulative case:

1. A subject reports an apparent awareness of a transcendent reality.
2. Ordinary epistemic practice often grants prima facie trust to experience unless defeated.
3. Testimony from many subjects may add support.

But the experience is interpretation-sensitive, traditions describe different objects, natural explanations remain possible, and first-person certainty does not automatically become public proof.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The argument from religious experience can supplement natural theology but should not swallow this topic. Its phenomenology, object, and veridicality belong to the separate religious-experience syllabus clause.

3. Objective-value arguments seek a transcendent ground.
4. Secular moral realism is a major rival.
5. Euthyphro targets divine-command grounding.
6. Modified divine command appeals to God’s necessarily good nature.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — Nyāya’s cosmic division of labour

```text
ETERNAL MATERIAL AND CONDITIONS
atoms · selves · space · time · ether · manas · karmic adṛṣṭa
                           │
                           │ ordered and governed by
                           ▼
              ĪŚVARA — omniscient special self
              ├─ intelligent efficient cause
              ├─ combines atoms
              ├─ sustains and dissolves the cosmos
              ├─ knows karmic deposits
              └─ distributes fruits impartially

God = nimitta-kāraṇa, not upādāna-kāraṇa
```

*Nyāya’s God does not create the ultimate material from nothing. God supplies intelligent ordering, governance, and moral allocation.*

### 1. Why Nyāya thinks the world needs intelligence

Begin with a pot. Clay, wheel, stick, and motion do not become a pot without a potter who knows the material and intended form. Nyāya extends this pattern to the ordered composite world.

The inference is not merely “everything has a cause.” It is:

```text
composite and purposively arranged effect
                 ↓
maker with knowledge, desire, and volition
```

**Limit of the example:** A pot is known to be made by humans, while no one observes universes being produced. The analogy therefore requires an independently defended *vyāpti*, an invariable relation between the relevant kind of effect and intelligent agency.

### 2. The nature of Īśvara

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** In classical Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika, God is:

- eternal and one;
- omniscient;
- a special self never bound by karma;
- the intelligent efficient cause of cosmic order;
- preserver and dissolver, not a withdrawn deistic maker;
- *karmaphaladātā*, distributor of karmic results.

Atoms, selves, space, time, ether, and mind are not produced from God’s substance. This sharply distinguishes Nyāya from Vedāntic systems that make Brahman both material and efficient cause.

### 3. Agency, freedom, and karma

Human agents act and incur merit or demerit. God is the directive cause who coordinates circumstances and results according to karmic desert. Nyāya therefore preserves relative agency while placing it within divine moral governance.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** This creates both strength and vulnerability. It explains why outcomes are morally specific, but also raises the question whether God is genuinely supreme if co-eternal karma constrains distribution.

### 4. Why omniscience enters

Nyāya atoms are imperceptibly minute. The agent who initiates their appropriate combinations must know every material condition and every self’s karmic deposit. The inferred maker is therefore not merely intelligent but omniscient.

The move is disputable: perhaps lawful order does not require a mind that separately cognizes each element. Still, the derivation must be stated before it is criticized.

### 5. Theism rather than deism

| Deistic picture | Nyāya picture |
|---|---|
| Maker starts a system and withdraws | God continuously orders, sustains, dissolves, and allocates |
| Moral order may run independently | Moral results are intelligently administered |
| Intervention is exceptional | Governance is constitutive of cosmic order |

### 6. The basic objections

1. **Artifact analogy:** The world may not be relevantly like a pot.
2. **Law sufficiency:** Atoms and karmic law may coordinate without an extra mind.
3. **Divine limitation:** Co-eternal matter and karma seem to restrict God.
4. **Moral problem:** Why is a world of suffering the chosen mode of karmic administration?

Nyāya replies that inert atoms cannot initiate purposive conjunction and unconscious merit cannot identify the appropriate recipient and result.

### UPSC application

**Cross-linked verified demand:** 2018 Q8(a) and the 2023/2025 Nyāya questions require the nature of the inferred cause before the arguments can be evaluated.

**Approach only:** Open with “efficient, not material, cause” → explain eternal atoms and selves → show why ordered combination and karmic allocation allegedly require omniscience → distinguish theism from deism → then proceed to Udayana’s individual grounds.

### Revision notes

1. Nyāya’s God is an eternal omniscient special self.
2. God is the efficient cause, not the material cause.
3. Eternal atoms supply material causation.
4. Selves and karmic deposits are also beginningless.
5. God initiates, sustains, and dissolves cosmic order.
6. God is *karmaphaladātā*, distributor of karmic fruits.
7. Human agency remains relative rather than absolute.
8. The pot–potter example carries the basic inference.
9. Its limit is the uncertain analogy between artifacts and the cosmos.
10. Omniscience is derived from knowledge of atoms and karmic histories.
11. Nyāya is theistic rather than deistic.
12. Co-eternal matter and karma create a divine-redundancy pressure.

### Visual first — Udayana’s eightfold rope

```text
kāryāt ───────── world as effect
āyojanāt ─────── atomic combination
dhṛtyādeḥ ────── support, maintenance, dissolution
padāt ────────── linguistic transmission
pratyayataḥ ──── Vedic credibility
śruteḥ ───────── scriptural testimony
vākyāt ───────── sentences imply a speaker
saṃkhyāviśeṣāt ─ dyadic number requires primordial cognition
          \          |          /
           \         |         /
             cumulative case
                    ↓
                  Īśvara
```

*No single strand carries the whole weight. The strategy is cumulative, though the strands must still possess independent evidential force.*

### 1. *Kāryāt* — from effect

The world’s composite entities are produced effects:

1. What has parts and intermediate magnitude is an effect.
2. Mountains, bodies, and other composites have these marks.
3. An effect of organized parts requires an intelligent maker.
4. Therefore the world requires an intelligent maker.

The main pressure falls on premise 3. Artifact cases establish makers for pots and houses; the generalization to natural wholes remains contested.

### 2. *Āyojanāt* — from conjunction

Inert atoms cannot initiate the first purposive combinations by themselves. A conscious will must connect them in the configurations from which bodies and worlds arise.

**Objection:** Stable laws or intrinsic dispositions may explain conjunction without intention.

**Reply:** Laws describe regularity but do not supply agency, selection, or a reason why one ordered cosmos emerges.

**Residual:** The reply risks treating every unexplained regularity as evidence for will.

### 3. *Dhṛtyādeḥ* — from support and governance

The sustained order and periodic dissolution of the world require a governing intelligence. The “and so on” includes maintenance and destruction.

The critic argues that self-regulating causal systems need no governor. Nyāya answers that purposive moral-cosmic coordination differs from mere repetition.

### 4. *Padāt* — from linguistic transmission

Language, arts, and practical conventions require original instruction by a competent teacher. A first teacher at the beginning of a cosmic cycle must possess extraordinary knowledge.

**Limit:** Language may emerge socially without a single first legislator. The argument is strongest within a cyclic cosmology that requires transmission to restart.

### 5. *Pratyayataḥ*, *śruteḥ*, and *vākyāt*

- ***Pratyayataḥ*:** The Veda’s reliability points to a trustworthy, knowledgeable source.
- ***Śruteḥ*:** Vedic testimony itself speaks of Īśvara.
- ***Vākyāt*:** Meaningful sentences are normally personal productions; Vedic sentences therefore imply an author.

Mīmāṃsā rejects the shared premise: the Veda is *apauruṣeya*, authorless, and its validity is intrinsic rather than borrowed.

### 6. *Saṃkhyāviśeṣāt* — from determinate number

At the beginning of creation, dyads arise through conjunction of atoms. Numerical determination is connected with cognition; no embodied finite self is available at the initial stage. Udayana therefore posits divine cognition.

❓ **Contested formulation:** Translations and summaries vary. In an exam, give the Sanskrit term, the dyad/number-cognition point, and acknowledge that its interpretation is technically disputed.

### 7. The argument from *adṛṣṭa*

```text
action → merit/demerit stored in a self
                   │
                   ├─ unconscious
                   ├─ temporally delayed
                   └─ person-specific
                            ↓
           intelligent allocation required
                            ↓
                   God as karmādhyakṣa
```

Mīmāṃsā replies with self-fructifying *apūrva*; Jainism treats karmic matter as law-like; Buddhism uses conditioned causation. The disagreement concerns whether unconscious norm-governed processes can be intrinsically directed.

### 8. Circularity and the two orders

The objection says: God authenticates the Veda, while the Veda proves God.

Nyāya distinguishes:

| Order | Direction |
|---|---|
| Existence | God → Veda |
| Human knowledge | Veda → knowledge of God |

Reciprocity across different explanatory orders is not automatically vicious.

### 9. Cumulative strength and shared weakness

Udayana’s proof-cluster is more resilient than one analogy because it adds effect, order, language, testimony, and moral allocation. Yet some strands are correlated: several depend on the idea that order or normativity cannot be impersonal. If that bridge fails, many strands weaken together.

Progress: 8/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Creator critique, perfected soul, and the 2019 trap

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — two senses of “God”

```text
CREATOR GOD                         PERFECTED SOUL
creates/sustains/judges             destroys karmic obscuration
unique cosmic office               many liberated siddhas
answers prayer                     non-interventionist exemplar
            ✕ rejected             ✓ affirmed

Jain verdict:
no world-maker, but achieved godhood
```

*The 2019 PYQ is trap-shaped: a correct answer must deny Jain creator-proofs while explaining the positive doctrine of perfected divinity.*

### 1. The uncreated Jain cosmos

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** The cosmos is beginningless and operates through six substances: *jīva*, *pudgala*, *dharma*, *adharma*, *ākāśa*, and *kāla*. Substances persist while modes arise and perish. Hence Jainism does not need a creator to explain why anything exists.

### 2. Six anti-creator arguments

1. **Motive:** A perfect being lacks nothing; action from need would compromise perfection.
2. **Regress/parity:** If all things need makers, God needs one; if God may be uncaused, the world may be uncaused.
3. **Material and instrument:** Creation requires material, space, means, and action; before creation these are unavailable unless co-eternal.
4. **Moral:** A compassionate omnipotent creator is difficult to reconcile with suffering and inequality.
5. **Redundancy:** Karma and the six substances already explain bondage, change, and release.
6. ***Svabhāva*:** Things operate according to their own natures; order does not require an external superintendent.

### 3. Strong theistic replies

- Creation need not satisfy a deficiency; classical theism may describe it as a free diffusion of goodness.
- “Everything needs a cause” is not the refined premise; cosmological arguments target contingent or dependent beings.
- Law-like karma may describe regularity without explaining its intelligible specificity.

**Residual:** These replies save a sophisticated creator more effectively than an anthropomorphic artisan. Jain criticism remains powerful against a needy, changing, interventionist maker.

### 4. What Jainism affirms

| State | Meaning | Cosmic role |
|---|---|---|
| Arihanta/Kevalin | Embodied omniscient being after destruction of obscuring karmas | Teacher; not creator |
| Tīrthaṅkara | A special arihanta who re-establishes the fourfold community | Ford-maker and preacher |
| Siddha | Disembodied liberated soul at *siddhaśilā* | No creation, judgement, or response |

Every Tīrthaṅkara becomes a Siddha, but not every Siddha was a Tīrthaṅkara. Worship is commemorative and aspirational rather than petitionary.

### 5. Divinity as achieved perfection

The liberated soul manifests *ananta-catuṣṭaya*: infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, and energy. Godhood is not a unique creator’s office; it is the fully unobscured condition of a soul.

**Example with a limit:** Cleaning a covered mirror illustrates manifestation rather than production of knowledge. The example stops because Jain karmic matter is a technical metaphysical doctrine, not literal dust.

### 6. Nyāya objection and Jain reply

Nyāya: unconscious karmic matter cannot allocate precise fruits.

Jain reply: karmic influx, bondage, maturation, and release are intrinsic law-like relations between soul and matter, just as poison has effects without a supervising poisoner.

Residual: The analogy shows non-conscious efficacy, but desert-sensitive allocation remains the debated point.

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQ:** 2019 Q5(e), 10 marks: “State and evaluate the proofs for the existence of God as propounded in Jainism.”

**Approach only:** Correct the premise respectfully in line one → Jainism gives no creator-proof → state the uncreated cosmos and anti-creator arguments → explain arihanta/Tīrthaṅkara/siddha and achieved divinity → evaluate internal presuppositions and the classical-theist reply → conclude “proof-structure against creator, doctrine of perfected godhood.”

### Revision notes

1. Jainism rejects a creator, sustainer, and judge.
2. The universe and six substances are beginningless.
3. Substance persists while modes originate and decay.
4. A perfect being’s motive to create is questioned.
5. The regress argument turns the creator premise against itself.
6. Suffering pressures divine compassion and omnipotence.
7. Karma and substances make a creator explanatorily redundant.
8. *Svabhāva* supplies intrinsic regularity.
9. Arihanta is an embodied omniscient victor.
10. Tīrthaṅkara is a teaching and community-founding role.
11. Siddha is the disembodied liberated soul.
12. Every Tīrthaṅkara becomes Siddha; not every Siddha was Tīrthaṅkara.
13. Jain worship is exemplary rather than petitionary.
14. *Ananta-catuṣṭaya* names four infinitudes of perfected soul.
15. The 2019 question must not be answered by inventing Jain creator-proofs.

## Lesson 9 — Buddhism Against a Permanent Creator

Progress: 9/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Canonical diagnosis, causal dilemma, dependent origination, and moral critique

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — canonical Buddhist anti-creator dossier; exact learner package; Paper I Buddhist sources; cited Nikāya and Śāntarakṣita/Dharmakīrti argument summaries.

CA found: No relevant current event. Classical canonical and causal-metaphysical arguments control.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — the three strata of the Buddhist case

```text
CANONICAL-CRITICAL
Brahmā belief can arise through error; cosmogony may be soteriologically idle
                         ↓
CAUSAL-METAPHYSICAL
permanent cause must produce all at once or successively
                         ↓
MORAL
suffering and divine determination pressure goodness and moral effort

Alternative explanation throughout:
pratītyasamutpāda — conditioned co-arising
```

*The strongest Buddhist case is not merely “the Buddha was silent.” It is the argument that permanent, self-sufficient agency cannot explain temporal production.*

### 1. Canonical diagnosis

The *Brahmajāla Sutta* presents a genealogy of creator belief: a being reborn first in a Brahmā realm mistakenly interprets later arrivals as products of his wish. The *Aggañña Sutta* gives a cyclic account rather than a once-for-all creation.

The *avyākata* questions and poisoned-arrow parable set aside speculative questions that do not end suffering.

**Limit:** Explaining how a belief may arise does not show that its proposition is false. Genealogy is diagnosis, not refutation.

### 2. The permanence–production dilemma

The causal-metaphysical argument can be reconstructed:

1. What is real must possess causal efficacy, *arthakriyā-kāritva*.
2. A permanent God is wholly unchanged.
3. Such a cause must produce either all effects simultaneously or effects successively.
4. If simultaneously, the entire temporal world should appear at once.
5. If successively, some new condition must differentiate one moment from another.
6. Acquiring or depending on that condition compromises immutability or self-sufficiency.
7. Therefore a permanent creator cannot adequately explain a temporally ordered world.

This is the *krama-akrama-virodha*, conflict between successive and non-successive production.

### 3. Auxiliary-condition objection

If God needs matter, time, karma, or other auxiliaries, God is not the sole sufficient cause. If no auxiliaries are required, the delay and order of effects are unexplained.

Nyāya replies that God’s will is eternal while karmic readiness times the effects.

Buddhist rejoinder: If another condition determines efficacy, the original claim of sole sufficiency has been surrendered.

### 4. Evidential objection to Nyāya inference

The *vyāpti* “every effect has a conscious maker” is established only for artifacts. Natural formations and momentary causal series do not justify universal extension from pot to cosmos.

Nyāya answers that dissimilarity affects strength, not the legitimate form of inference from complex organization.

### 5. Dependent origination

*Pratītyasamutpāda* explains arising and cessation through conditions:

```text
when this is, that arises
when this ceases, that ceases
```

It avoids both an unconditioned world-maker and a permanent substance behind events.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Dependent origination shows that a coherent causal account can be non-theistic. Whether it explains why there is any conditioned order at all is a further question.

### 6. Moral pressure

If an omnipotent benevolent being designed the world, severe suffering is counter-evidence. If every event is divine will, karmic responsibility and present effort appear threatened. The *Devadaha Sutta* is relevant to the broader criticism of views that neutralize present effort.

Buddhism does not deny all devas. Devas remain impermanent beings within saṃsāra; the rejected thesis is a creator outside or sovereign over conditioned arising.

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQ:** 2018 Q8(b), 15 marks: “Critically evaluate the Buddhistic arguments against the existence of God as the creator of the world.”

**Approach only:** Define the target as creator, not every divine being → organize canonical, causal, and moral strata → make *krama-akrama* the philosophical centre → present eternal-will/adṛṣṭa reply and Buddhist rejoinder → conclude strongest against an immutable sole creator, weaker where merely genealogical.

### Revision notes

1. Buddhism rejects creatorhood, not every deva.
2. The *Brahmajāla* account diagnoses a possible origin of creator belief.
3. The *Aggañña* gives a cyclic alternative.
4. *Avyākata* questions are set aside for soteriological reasons.
5. Genealogical explanation is not logical refutation.
6. *Arthakriyā-kāritva* links reality with causal efficacy.
7. *Krama-akrama-virodha* tests permanent causation.
8. Simultaneous production collapses temporal sequence.
9. Successive production appears to require change or auxiliaries.
10. Auxiliary dependence pressures divine sufficiency.
11. The artifact *vyāpti* may not extend to the cosmos.
12. *Pratītyasamutpāda* offers a non-creator causal framework.
13. Suffering challenges benevolent omnipotent design.
14. Divine determinism may weaken karma and effort.
15. The case is strongest metaphysically and weakest when merely genealogical.

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — canonical objection sections; exact learner package; Paper I Mīmāṃsā, Cārvāka, Sāṃkhya, and Advaita files; Chatterjee–Datta and C. D. Sharma book evidence.

CA found: No doctrine-specific current event. The four schools must be kept distinct through their own epistemic and metaphysical commitments.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — one rejection, four reasons

| School | What does the explanatory work? | Main challenge to theism |
|---|---|---|
| Mīmāṃsā | Authorless Veda, injunction, *apūrva* | God is unnecessary for authority and karmic fruit |
| Cārvāka | Perceptible elements and ordinary causation | Supersensible inference overreaches |
| Sāṃkhya | *Prakṛti* transforming for *puruṣa* | Unconscious nature can generate order |
| Advaita | Empirical Īśvara within *māyā*; Brahman as absolute | Nyāya’s finite arranger is not ultimate |

*The schools do not form one generic “Indian atheism.” They reject different premises and sometimes retain a religious absolute.*

### 1. Mīmāṃsā: authority without an author

The Veda is *apauruṣeya*: not human-authored and not in need of divine authorship. Authorlessness protects authority from an author’s ignorance, deceit, or limited intention.

The validity of cognition is normally intrinsic, *svataḥ-prāmāṇya*. Nyāya’s proposal is considered cumbersome:

```text
prove God exists
      ↓
prove God is omniscient
      ↓
prove God intends truth
      ↓
only then validate the Veda
```

Mīmāṃsā begins instead with the Veda’s beginningless normative structure.

### 2. *Apūrva* and moral fruit

Correct ritual produces an unseen potency, *apūrva*, which persists and matures into its result. This rejects Nyāya’s assertion that an unconscious principle needs a divine allocator.

**Nyāya reply:** A potency can connect act and result but cannot intelligently proportion person, timing, and consequence.

**Mīmāṃsā rejoinder:** Fruit-directedness belongs to the ritual-causal structure itself; adding a divine supervisor creates no new prediction.

### 3. Cārvāka: the epistemic challenge

Cārvāka treats perception as the secure means of knowledge and rejects inference to an unobserved divine being where the required universal relation is not established.

The world can be explained through material elements and their combinations.

**Nyāya reply:** Cārvāka’s general denial of inference itself uses inferential generalization; disciplined inference cannot be discarded wholesale.

**Residual:** Even if ordinary inference is accepted, inference to a supersensible cosmic maker requires a far stronger *vyāpti* than smoke–fire reasoning.

### 4. Sāṃkhya: unconscious nature as sufficient

Sāṃkhya explains manifestation through the disequilibrium and transformation of *prakṛti* in the presence of many *puruṣas*. No creator is required.

Nyāya objects that unconscious nature cannot coordinate purposively for experience and liberation.

Sāṃkhya uses analogies such as milk nourishing a calf without conscious deliberation: natural processes can serve ends without a planning agent.

**Limit:** The analogy displays unconscious purposiveness but does not by itself explain why *prakṛti* has precisely that ordered capacity.

### 5. Advaita: empirical God and absolute Brahman

Advaita does not simply deny Īśvara. It places creator-Īśvara at the empirical level, conditioned by *māyā*, while nirguṇa Brahman is the non-dual absolute.

Thus Nyāya may infer a cosmic arranger but has not reached ultimate reality. Its atomism also faces Śaṅkara’s challenge:

- if atoms are intrinsically active, creation would be continuous;
- if intrinsically inactive, creation could not begin;
- if both, contradiction threatens;
- if activity comes externally, the explanatory regress returns.

### 6. A useful three-part classification

```text
ECONOMY: the system already explains the datum
  apūrva · prakṛti · material combination

COHERENCE: the proposed God conflicts with the system’s metaphysics
  finite arranger · atomism · causal change

EPISTEMIC RESTRICTION: the proof lacks a valid pramāṇa
  Cārvāka attack on supersensible inference
```

### UPSC application

These positions are required in the evaluation of the 2018, 2023, and 2025 Nyāya PYQs. They should appear as distinct objections, not as a single sentence saying “Indian schools reject God.”

**Approach only:** Mīmāṃsā first for Veda and karma → Cārvāka for inference → Sāṃkhya for natural order → Advaita for levels of reality and atomism → give Nyāya’s strongest reply to each → conclude that the dispute tracks rival theories of knowledge and causation.

### Revision notes

1. Mīmāṃsā treats the Veda as *apauruṣeya*.
2. Intrinsic validity is *svataḥ-prāmāṇya*.
3. Authorlessness avoids dependence on divine omniscience and truthfulness.
4. *Apūrva* links ritual action with future fruit.
5. Nyāya challenges unconscious allocation.
6. Cārvāka restricts secure knowledge primarily to perception.
7. Its objection attacks supersensible inference rather than God directly.
8. Nyāya replies that blanket denial of inference is self-undermining.
9. Sāṃkhya uses *prakṛti* as the sufficient material-dynamic principle.
10. Milk nourishing a calf illustrates non-conscious purposiveness.
11. Nyāya asks why unconscious nature is so coordinated.
12. Advaita accepts empirical Īśvara but denies that the arranger is absolute.
13. Nirguṇa Brahman is not established through Nyāya’s artifact inference.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — Madhva’s division of epistemic labour

```text
PERCEPTION
confirms real plurality and difference
       │
       ▼
INFERENCE
organises dependence and defends realism
       │
       ▼
SCRIPTURE
identifies the independent ground as Viṣṇu
and discloses divine attributes

Reason reaches structure.
Scripture supplies theological identity.
```

*Madhva is not a Nyāya-style natural theologian. His system gives reason a real but limited task.*

### 1. Ontology before proof

Reality divides into:

- ***svatantra-tattva***: independent reality, Brahman/Viṣṇu alone;
- ***paratantra-tattva***: dependent reality, including souls and insentient matter.

Dependent beings are real, not illusory. Their dependence constitutes permanent difference from God. This realist position is called *tattvavāda*.

The fivefold difference, *pañca-bheda*, distinguishes:

1. God and soul;
2. God and matter;
3. soul and matter;
4. one soul and another;
5. one material entity and another.

It is a classification of real differences, not five proofs of God.

### 2. Three means of knowledge

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Madhva accepts *pratyakṣa* (perception), *anumāna* (inference), and *śabda/āgama* (testimony, especially scripture).

His realist argument is transcendental:

1. Scripture is heard or read through perception.
2. Its meaning is interpreted through cognitive and inferential activity.
3. If perception and inference were fundamentally unreliable, scripture could not be known.
4. Therefore the ordinary plurality they disclose cannot be dismissed as wholly unreal.
5. Scripture must be read consistently with valid experience.

The famous *atat tvam asi* reading expresses this anti-identity hermeneutic, though it is idiosyncratic and should be presented cautiously.

### 3. Four strands, graded

| Strand | Reasoning | What it establishes | Madhva’s weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scriptural | Vedānta testimony discloses Viṣṇu and divine perfections | Identity and attributes of God | Decisive |
| Metaphysical dependence | Finite real beings lack independence; dependent reality presupposes an independent ground | An independent ontological ground | Structurally central reconstruction |
| Order/design | Ordered cosmos suggests intelligence | Architect or governor | Deliberately limited |
| Perfection | God is the highest perfection conceivable | Concept and attributes | Not an Anselmian existence-proof |

Britannica’s summary is explicit: in Madhva’s system God’s existence cannot be proved and is learned from scripture. This is not a denial of reason; it is a limit on reason’s theological reach.

### 4. Dependence argument

```text
souls and matter are real
        ↓
their existence and powers are not self-grounded
        ↓
they are paratantra — dependent
        ↓
dependent being points to svatantra reality
        ↓
scripture identifies that independent ground as Viṣṇu
```

This is ontological dependence, not necessarily temporal production from nothing. Beginningless souls and matter can still depend continuously on God.

### 5. Madhva’s reservation about design

B. N. K. Sharma reports that Madhva was not greatly attracted to the teleological argument because order yields at most an architect. It does not establish the being on whom everything depends for its existence and activity.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** This anticipates Hume’s adequacy objection. Madhva recognizes that a designer may be too modest a conclusion.

### 6. Objections and replies

| Objection | Dvaita reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture and God are circular | Vedic testimony is an independent, intrinsically valid *pramāṇa* | Non-Vedic schools reject that authority |
| Dependence does not identify Viṣṇu | Correct; scripture supplies identity | Natural theology remains intentionally incomplete |
| Beginningless souls rival God | Beginninglessness is compatible with total ontological dependence | The asymmetry must be defended, not stipulated |
| Design reaches only a demiurge | Madhva agrees and gives it little weight | Reason alone remains unable to close the gap |
| Later formal arguments are attributed to Madhva | Jayatīrtha and Vyāsatīrtha systematize the dialectic | Historiographic care is mandatory |

### 7. Comparison with Nyāya and Aquinas

| Axis | Nyāya | Aquinas | Madhva |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary route | Inference from effect/order | Reason from actuality, cause, contingency | Scripture on a realist epistemic base |
| Matter | Eternal atoms | Created being | Beginningless yet dependent matter |
| Design | Central | Fifth Way | Explicitly insufficient |
| What reason reaches | Omniscient arranger | First/necessary source | Independent ground at most |
| Identity of God | Inferred cumulatively | Developed through further metaphysics | Revealed as Viṣṇu |

### UPSC application

**Verified PYQ:** 2026 Q8(b), 15 marks: “Explain and examine Madhvacharya's arguments for the existence of God.”

## Lesson 12 — Cumulative Cases and Epistemic Limits

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — rope, chain, and net

```text
CHAIN: one failed link breaks the argument
premise → premise → premise → conclusion

ROPE: several partly independent strands add support
cosmos ╲
order   ╲
morality ─── probable theism
experience╱

NET: evidence also pulls the other way
supporting strands − evil − hiddenness − diversity = overall balance
```

*A cumulative case is neither a deductive chain nor a one-way pile. It must include counter-evidence and dependence among strands.*

### 1. C-inductive and P-inductive

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** In Swinburne’s terminology:

- a **C-inductive** argument raises the probability of a conclusion;
- a **P-inductive** argument makes the conclusion more probable than not.

Several C-inductive arguments may, under conditions, jointly create a P-inductive case.

### 2. Conditions for legitimate accumulation

1. Each strand must have some evidential force.
2. The strands must not merely repeat one hidden assumption.
3. Priors and comparison hypotheses must be stated or at least acknowledged.
4. Counter-evidence must be included.
5. The conclusion must remain proportionate to the evidence.

### 3. Udayana and the Western cumulative case

Udayana’s eight grounds function like converging strands: effect, arrangement, governance, language, testimony, and moral order. Swinburne adds cosmological order, consciousness, providence, moral awareness, and religious experience.

The traditions differ in technical framework but share the strategy of not allowing one failed route to decide the whole case.

### 4. Flew’s “leaky buckets”

If every bucket has no capacity to retain water, stacking them does not create a sound vessel. Likewise, zero-evidence arguments do not become evidence through repetition.

**Reply:** A C-inductive argument is not zero-valued; genuinely independent weak evidence can combine, as in a legal case.

**Residual:** If the arguments share one disputed bridge—such as “order requires mind”—their apparent independence is overstated.

### 5. Simplicity and rival hypotheses

Swinburne argues that a single unlimited personal explanation may be simpler than many finite causes. Critics answer that:

- “infinite” may be less simple than claimed;
- a finite designer, committee, axiarchic principle, multiverse, or brute law may fit parts of the evidence;
- simplicity alone cannot supply personality, goodness, or worship-worthiness.

### 6. The net-evidence requirement

Evil, divine hiddenness, and religious diversity are not optional afterthoughts. A cumulative case must compare how likely the total evidence is on theism and its rivals.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The cumulative method improves honesty by abandoning demonstrative overclaim. It cannot escape underdetermination merely by adding more contested data.

### UPSC application

There is no separately owned historical PYQ in the 2018–2026 bank asking only about cumulative theism, but the logic strengthens conclusions to the design, Nyāya, Aquinas-comparison, and a priori/a posteriori questions.

**Approach only:** State that individually non-demonstrative arguments may jointly confirm → define C-/P-induction → test independence → include Flew and reply → subtract evil/hiddenness/diversity → conclude probability without theological completeness.

### Revision notes

1. A cumulative case is a rope rather than a deductive chain.
2. C-inductive evidence raises probability.
3. P-inductive evidence makes a conclusion more probable than not.
4. Weak evidence can combine only if it has non-zero force.
5. Independence matters; correlated arguments cannot simply be added.
6. Udayana structurally anticipates cumulative natural theology.
7. Swinburne uses probabilistic confirmation.
8. Mitchell’s legal-evidence analogy explains convergence.
9. Flew’s leaky buckets challenge zero-value accumulation.
10. The rope reply works only for genuine evidential strands.
11. Simplicity is a contested criterion.
12. Rival hypotheses preserve underdetermination.
13. Evil, hiddenness, and diversity are counter-evidence.
14. The final output is probability, not demonstration.
15. A probable ultimate cause is still not automatically the God of worship.

## Lesson 13 — Comparative Synthesis: What Each Tradition Is Really Arguing About

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first — comparative parity bench

| Function | Western route | Indian pro-theistic route | Indian critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effect to cause | Aquinas Way 2 | Nyāya *kāryāt* | Buddhist attack on *vyāpti* |
| Dependence to ground | Aquinas Way 3; Leibniz | Madhva *paratantra* → *svatantra* | Mīmāṃsā allows impersonal order |
| Order to mind | Way 5; Paley | *Āyojanāt*, *dhṛtyādeḥ* | Jain *svabhāva*; Sāṃkhya *prakṛti* |
| Moral proportion | Kantian postulate | *Karmaphaladātā* | *Apūrva* or karma without God |
| Concept to existence | Anselm, Descartes | No exact classical analogue | Indian *pramāṇa* theories resist it |
| Scripture | Revealed theology beyond natural proof | Udayana’s testimony grounds; Madhva decisive scripture | Mīmāṃsā authorless Veda |

*Comparison works only when the common question and the metaphysical difference are both stated.*

### 1. Similar form does not mean identical conclusion

Aquinas and Nyāya both infer beyond observed effects. Yet Aquinas seeks a source of actuality and being, while Nyāya seeks an omniscient arranger of eternal atoms and karmic conditions.

Madhva is closer to contingency language because all finite beings are dependent, but he refuses to let inference alone identify the ground as Viṣṇu.

### 2. Four levels of disagreement

```text
LEVEL 1 — DATA
Is there genuine order, contingency, obligation, or experience?

LEVEL 2 — BRIDGE
Does order require intelligence? Does dependence require a terminus?

LEVEL 3 — TERMINUS
Designer, necessary being, allocator, independent ground, or something else?

LEVEL 4 — THEOLOGICAL IDENTITY
Why one, personal, omniscient, omnipotent, good, and worship-worthy?
```

Critics may agree at one level and resist the next. Hume may grant order but deny a perfect designer. Mīmāṃsā may grant moral regularity but deny an allocator.

### 3. Master objection–reply–residual map

| Objection | Best reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| Existence is not a predicate | Recast necessary existence modally | Possibility premise |
| Infinite regress or brute fact | Essentially ordered dependence still needs non-derivative power | Structure of the series disputed |
| Weak artifact analogy | Use abductive law/fine-tuning reasoning | Designer identity underdetermined |
| Natural processes suffice | Ask why laws and dispositions exist | Risk of moving the gap rather than explaining |
| Karma can self-operate | Determinate desert seems intelligence-sensitive | Impersonal normativity remains possible |
| Permanent God cannot produce succession | Eternal act with timed conditions | Conditions compromise sole sufficiency |
| Scripture is circular | Treat testimony as independent *pramāṇa* | Outsiders reject its authority |
| Suffering counts against design | Free will, law, soul-making, or limited design | Net evidence remains contested |

### 4. Epistemic humility

The debate shows several different kinds of rational stopping point:

- necessary being;
- brute fact;
- self-ordering nature;
- impersonal karmic or ritual law;
- dependent reality grounded in a scripturally known God;
- probability rather than certainty.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The strongest conclusion is not “all proofs fail” or “God is proved.” It is that each argument identifies a genuine explanatory pressure while carrying a theory-laden bridge and a limited terminus.

### 5. What would change a verdict?

A mature answer can state its falsifiability conditions:

- The ontological verdict would change if maximal greatness were independently shown coherent and uniquely possible.
- The cosmological verdict would change if unrestricted sufficient reason or necessary grounding were established.
- The design verdict would change if probabilities and rival hypotheses were well specified.
- The Nyāya verdict would change if impersonal karmic allocation were shown impossible.
- The Madhva verdict depends openly on accepting scriptural testimony as a valid independent source.

### UPSC application

This synthesis supports every “compare,” “critically examine,” “which should be preferred,” and “which Way is most interesting” directive.

**Approach only:** Announce the comparison axis → reconstruct both sides → identify the metaphysical commitment producing the divergence → attach the strongest objection and reply → end with a criterion-named verdict.

### Revision notes

1. Aquinas and Nyāya share a posteriori form but not identical metaphysics.
2. Aquinas seeks a source of being; Nyāya seeks an arranger.
3. Madhva uses dependence but reserves theological identity for scripture.
4. Indian philosophy has no exact classical ontological analogue.
5. Kantian moral postulation parallels karmic administration only partially.
6. Mīmāṃsā offers authority and moral fruit without a divine author.
7. Buddhism targets permanent productive agency.
8. Jainism attacks motive, suffering, and redundancy.
9. Sāṃkhya defends unconscious purposive nature.
10. Cārvāka contests the epistemic route.
11. Data, bridge, terminus, and identity are four separate dispute levels.
12. Replies often preserve a weaker conclusion while sacrificing demonstration.
13. Residual pressure must remain visible after every reply.
14. A mature verdict states what evidence or premise would change it.
15. Cross-tradition comparison must explain why schools differ.

→ graded conclusion

taxonomy and full argument

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker 1

**Question:** Distinguish explanatory priority from temporal priority in cosmological arguments. (10 marks, about 150 words)

### Model answer

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Temporal priority asks what happened first in a sequence. Explanatory priority asks what possesses non-derivative causal or ontological power, even if the sequence has no first moment.

Aquinas’ first two Ways are strongest when read through an essentially ordered series. A stick moves a stone only while a hand moves the stick; the intermediate member has borrowed power. “First cause” therefore means a sustaining or grounding source here and now, not the earliest event in cosmic history. Aquinas can consequently allow that philosophy does not demonstrate a temporal beginning.

The kalām argument is different. It explicitly claims that whatever begins has a cause and that the universe began; its conclusion concerns a cause of that beginning.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** The distinction blocks a common regress objection from being applied indiscriminately. A temporal infinite may be compatible with Aquinas while an infinite series of wholly derivative powers remains disputed. The residual question is whether the actual world really contains the essentially ordered dependence Aquinas requires.

## Original 10-marker 2

**Question:** Why is Kant’s moral argument a postulate rather than a proof? (10 marks, about 150 words)

### Model answer

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Kant limits theoretical knowledge to possible experience and rejects speculative proofs of God. Practical reason nevertheless commands pursuit of the highest good, the union of virtue and happiness proportioned to virtue. Because “ought implies can,” practical reason must regard that highest good as possible.

Complete moral perfection requires endless progress, giving the postulate of immortality. Nature does not by itself align happiness with virtue, giving the postulate of God. Freedom is presupposed by moral responsibility.

A postulate is therefore a proposition practically required by commitment to the moral law but theoretically undemonstrable. It is an object of rational faith, not empirical knowledge or deductive certainty.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Calling it a proof mistakes its status and also makes Kant heteronomous. God does not create obligation; autonomous moral reason obligates, while God makes final moral proportion rationally thinkable. The argument may still be declined by one who rejects the highest good’s cosmic completion.

## Original 15-marker 1

**Question:** Does the modal ontological argument escape Kant’s objection? Discuss. (15 marks, about 250 words)

### Model answer

Kant argues that existence is not a real predicate: it does not add descriptive content to a concept but asserts that the concept is instantiated. This attacks Anselm’s first-form comparison and Descartes’ claim that existence is a perfection.

Modal defenders move to necessary existence. The argument says that if a maximally great being is possible, it exists in some possible world; maximal greatness includes necessary existence; what exists necessarily in one possible world exists in every possible world; therefore it exists actually.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** The modal argument avoids treating ordinary existence as one more perfection. Necessary existence is presented as a modal status.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** It therefore escapes the narrow wording of Kant’s objection more successfully than Descartes does. Yet the entire force now rests on the possibility premise. If maximal greatness contains incompatible attributes, or if its possibility is no more warranted than its impossibility, modal machinery cannot settle the dispute. A parallel premise that a maximally great being is impossible blocks the inference at its first step.

Gaunilo’s island does not reproduce necessary existence easily because islands have no intrinsic maximum. Still, the theist must independently show that divine maximality does have a coherent maximum.

The modal version is consequently a clarification rather than a demonstration. It changes the question from “Is existence a perfection?” to “Is maximal necessary perfection genuinely possible?” Kant’s broader warning remains: analysis of a disputed concept cannot by itself guarantee an instance.

## Original 15-marker 2

**Question:** Compare Kant’s postulate of God with Nyāya’s inference to God as *karmaphaladātā*. (15 marks, about 250 words)

### Model answer

Both arguments respond to a moral disproportion: virtue and happiness do not reliably coincide within ordinary experience.

For Kant, the moral law commands promotion of the highest good. Since finite agents cannot secure complete virtue or harmonize nature with desert, practical reason postulates freedom, immortality, and God. God is not known theoretically and does not create moral obligation; the postulate preserves rational moral hope.

Nyāya begins from karma. Past acts leave merit and demerit, *adṛṣṭa*, but this deposit is unconscious and cannot allot the exact result to the exact self at the exact time. Individual selves do not know or control their full karmic histories. Nyāya therefore infers an omniscient *karmaphaladātā*.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** Kantian postulation and Nyāya inference assign different epistemic statuses to a similar moral function.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Kant secures less but risks less. The sceptic can reject the need for cosmic completion, but Kant does not claim demonstrative knowledge. Nyāya secures more—an actual moral administrator—but must defeat Mīmāṃsā’s *apūrva* and Jain law-like karma, both of which deny that unconscious causation requires a supervisor.

Thus the comparison turns on epistemic ambition. Kant offers practical rationality; Nyāya offers theoretical *anumāna*. Neither route alone establishes the complete creator and object of worship.

## Original 20-marker 1

**Question:** “Hume did not disprove design; he restricted what design evidence can establish.” Critically examine. (20 marks, about 350 words)

### Model answer

The claim is accurate if Hume’s objections are divided between evidential force and divine adequacy.

Paley argues that a watch’s coordinated parts imply a watchmaker and that organismic adjustment similarly implies a designer. Aquinas’ Fifth Way must be kept distinct: it argues from the regular directedness of non-conscious nature, not from artifact resemblance.

Hume first attacks evidential support. A stable universe must contain mutually adjusted parts; apparent adaptation may therefore be a condition of persistence rather than evidence of intention. His Epicurean proposal imagines particles passing through combinations until a stable arrangement appears. He then argues that the universe is not sufficiently like a machine; it may resemble an organism, and the origin of organisms is itself disputed.

Hume next attacks adequacy. A finite effect warrants at most a proportionate finite cause. The scales illustration permits inference only to enough unseen weight, not infinite weight. The world may have many designers, an inexperienced designer, or a limited one. Suffering and disorder further resist inference to perfect goodness.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** These arguments do not entail that no designer exists. They show that the observed evidence underdetermines both design and the designer’s attributes.

Darwin later strengthens the first group by explaining biological adaptation through selection without foresight. Contemporary fine-tuning shifts attention to pre-biological constants and uses probabilistic rather than artifact reasoning.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Fine-tuning may avoid Darwin and partially answer the weak-analogy objection, but it remains exposed to multiverse, necessity, selection-effect, and designer-identity problems. It also leaves Hume’s moral and plurality pressures largely intact.

The best verdict is therefore graded. Hume destroys the claim that design demonstrates one infinite, perfectly good God. He does not prove the absence of intelligence. Design survives, if at all, as one abductive strand whose conclusion must be proportioned to finite and ambiguous evidence.

## Original 20-marker 2

**Question:** Assess Udayana’s proof-cluster as an Indian cumulative case for God. (20 marks, about 350 words)

### Model answer

Udayana’s natural theology is best understood as a cumulative cluster rather than one syllogism. It seeks Īśvara as an omniscient intelligent efficient cause within a realist cosmos of eternal atoms and selves.

*Kāryāt* argues that composite entities are effects requiring a maker. *Āyojanāt* claims inert atoms need conscious conjunction. *Dhṛtyādeḥ* moves from support, maintenance, and dissolution to governance. *Padāt* links original linguistic instruction with a first teacher. *Pratyayataḥ* appeals to Vedic credibility, *śruteḥ* to testimony, and *vākyāt* to the personal production of meaningful sentences. *Saṃkhyāviśeṣāt* connects primordial dyads and determinate number with an initial cognition. The argument from *adṛṣṭa* adds moral order: unconscious merit and demerit require an omniscient distributor.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** The Sanskrit grounds are glossed differently in some summaries, especially the later members; careful answers state each label with a bounded clause.

The cumulative strategy has a real advantage. Failure of the pot analogy need not immediately defeat the testimony or moral strands. It also develops attributes stepwise: intelligence from order, omniscience from atomic and karmic knowledge, and moral governance from allocation.

The objections are equally cumulative. Mīmāṃsā’s authorless Veda challenges the testimony family, while *apūrva* challenges allocation. Buddhism disputes the artifact *vyāpti* and permanent agency. Jainism treats karmic law as sufficient. Cārvāka contests supersensible inference. Advaita grants at most empirical Īśvara.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Several strands are less independent than they appear. Order, language, and karma repeatedly use the shared premise that norm-governed structure requires consciousness. If impersonal organization is possible, several routes weaken simultaneously. Moreover, the conclusion is an arranger of co-eternal materials, not an unrestricted creator.

Udayana therefore supplies a philosophically serious Indian cumulative case, not a demonstration. Its lasting value lies in making the debate about agency, normativity, and explanation explicit.

## Original 20-marker 3

**Question:** Compare Aquinas, Nyāya, and Madhva on the rational route to God. (20 marks, about 350 words)

### Model answer

Aquinas, Nyāya, and Madhva all connect finite reality with an ultimate source, but they differ on what reason can establish and what sort of dependence is at stake.

Aquinas’ Five Ways begin from motion, efficient causation, contingency, degrees, and purposive order. Ways 1–3 are strongest as arguments from present, essentially ordered dependence rather than temporal beginning. Their terminus is developed toward a source of actuality and being itself.

Nyāya begins from a realist atomistic cosmos. The world as composite effect, the conjunction of inert atoms, cosmic maintenance, linguistic and Vedic authority, and the distribution of *adṛṣṭa* are used to infer an omniscient Īśvara. Yet God is the efficient cause only; atoms and selves remain eternal. The inferred being is therefore an arranger and moral governor.

Madhva divides reality into the independent, *svatantra*, and the dependent, *paratantra*. Perception and inference secure the reality of plurality and help articulate dependence. Scripture, however, decisively identifies the independent ground as Viṣṇu and discloses divine perfections. Madhva deliberately limits design because order yields only an architect.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** The three positions assign different epistemic roles to reason and testimony.

Their critics expose corresponding weaknesses. Hume and Kant challenge regress termination and the move from necessary being to God. Mīmāṃsā, Buddhism, Jainism, and Cārvāka challenge Nyāya’s agency assumptions. Madhva faces circularity and underdetermination: dependence alone does not identify Viṣṇu, and scriptural authority is rejected by outsiders.

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** Aquinas makes the strongest claim for reason, Nyāya the most elaborate inferential administration of order, and Madhva the clearest division between metaphysical structure and revealed identity. The comparison shows that “proof of God” can mean demonstration, cumulative inference, or rational preparation for testimony. None should be evaluated by silently imposing another’s intended endpoint.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Register 1 — Core distinctions

1. A priori arguments use conceptual resources; a posteriori arguments begin from the world or experience.
2. Demonstration, explanatory inference, practical postulate, and cumulative confirmation are different proof-types.
3. Validity, soundness, and divine adequacy must be tested separately.
4. A proof may establish a cause without establishing one personal, perfect God.
5. Explanatory termination may be necessary being, brute fact, infinite regress, or non-personal law.
6. Scientific ignorance is not positive theological evidence.
7. Every proof has a datum, bridge, terminus, and theological-identification stage.
8. The 2022 preference question requires a named criterion and stated cost.

## Register 2 — Ontological argument

1. Anselm: God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived.
2. *Proslogion* 2 moves from understanding to reality through a greatness comparison.
3. *Proslogion* 3 argues that inconceivable non-existence is greater and yields necessary existence.
4. Aseity means non-dependent existence.
5. Descartes treats existence as contained in supreme perfection.
6. Gaunilo’s island is a parody testing whether the form proves too much.
7. Anselm replies that islands are contingent and have no intrinsic maximum.
8. Kant: existence is not a real predicate.
9. Instantiation does not add descriptive content to a concept.
10. Modal versions focus on necessary existence.
11. Their decisive burden is the possibility and coherence of maximal greatness.
12. No exact classical Indian ontological analogue exists.
13. Best verdict: conditional entailment without demonstrated instantiation.

## Register 3 — Cosmological family

1. Way 1: change to unmoved actualizer.
2. Way 2: efficient causal dependence to first cause.
3. Way 3: contingency to necessary being.
4. Way 4: degrees to maximum/source.
5. Way 5: natural directedness to intelligence.
6. Essentially ordered series contain derivative present power.
7. Accidentally ordered series concern succession and may lack a first member.
8. “First” in Aquinas is primarily explanatory.
9. Aquinas does not philosophically require a temporal beginning.
10. The Third Way’s temporal formulation faces a quantifier shift.
11. Leibniz uses the principle of sufficient reason.
12. The brute-fact reply rejects the PSR’s reach.
13. The kalām explicitly argues from a beginning.
14. Kant says necessary being is not yet the *ens realissimum*.
15. Divine attributes require additional argument.

## Register 4 — Design

1. Way 5 is the archer–arrow model of directedness.
2. Paley is the watch–watchmaker artifact analogy.
3. Paley’s case includes eye, wing, fin, and wider natural coordination.
4. Imperfection and unknown parts do not, for Paley, cancel design.
5. Hume says stable worlds necessarily exhibit adjustment.
6. The universe may be unlike a machine.
7. A finite effect warrants a proportionate cause.
8. Many or imperfect designers fit the evidence.
9. Evil and disorder count against perfect goodness.
10. Hume restricts inference; he does not prove no designer.
11. Darwin supplies a non-intentional explanation of biological adaptation.
12. Fine-tuning is probabilistic and pre-biological.
13. Multiverse, necessity, and selection effects remain rivals.
14. Nyāya’s potter analogy faces the same basic analogy pressure.

## Register 5 — Moral and experiential arguments

1. Conscience may be interpreted as awareness of a moral judge.
2. Naturalistic psychology challenges that inference.
3. Objective moral-value arguments seek a transcendent ground.
4. Euthyphro pressures divine-command theories.
5. Modified divine command appeals to a necessarily good nature.
6. Kant denies theoretical proof.
7. The highest good joins virtue and proportionate happiness.
8. “Ought implies can” supports practical postulation.
9. Freedom, immortality, and God are the three postulates.
10. God does not create obligation in Kant’s system.
11. Projection, autonomy, and adequacy are standard objections.
12. Nyāya infers an actual allocator where Kant postulates rational hope.
13. Religious experience may add cumulative support.
14. Private certainty does not automatically become public proof.

## Register 6 — Nyāya–Udayana

1. Īśvara is eternal, omniscient, and free from karma.
2. God is efficient cause only; atoms are material causes.
3. God continuously sustains and dissolves, so the view is not deism.
4. God is *karmaphaladātā*.
5. *Kāryāt*: composite effect to maker.
6. *Āyojanāt*: inert conjunction to arranger.
7. *Dhṛtyādeḥ*: support, maintenance, dissolution to governor.
8. *Padāt*: language and instruction to first teacher.
9. *Pratyayataḥ*: credibility to trustworthy source.
10. *Śruteḥ*: testimony to God.
11. *Vākyāt*: meaningful sentences to personal author.
12. *Saṃkhyāviśeṣāt*: primordial number to cognition; gloss cautiously.
13. *Adṛṣṭa* is unconscious merit/demerit requiring, for Nyāya, allocation.
14. The Veda–God circle is answered through existence/knowledge orders.
15. The cluster is cumulative but several strands share agency assumptions.

## Register 7 — Jain and Buddhist critiques

1. Jainism affirms an uncreated beginningless cosmos.
2. Motive, regress, material, moral, redundancy, and *svabhāva* arguments oppose a creator.
3. Arihanta/Kevalin is embodied and omniscient.
4. Tīrthaṅkara is a ford-maker and teacher.
5. Siddha is disembodied and non-interventionist.
6. Jain divinity is achieved, plural, and non-creative.
7. *Ananta-catuṣṭaya* names infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, and energy.
8. Buddhism permits devas but denies a creator.
9. Canonical genealogy diagnoses belief without refuting it.
10. *Krama-akrama-virodha* challenges permanent productive agency.
11. Auxiliary conditions pressure sole sufficiency.
12. Artifact *vyāpti* may not extend to the cosmos.
13. *Pratītyasamutpāda* gives a conditioned non-creator explanation.
14. Suffering and determinism create moral pressure.
15. The Buddhist case is strongest at the causal-metaphysical level.

## Register 8 — Other Indian critiques and Madhva

1. Mīmāṃsā’s Veda is *apauruṣeya* and intrinsically valid.
2. *Apūrva* allows ritual fruit without a divine distributor.
3. Cārvāka challenges supersensible inference.
4. Sāṃkhya explains order through unconscious *prakṛti*.
5. Advaita grades creator-Īśvara below nirguṇa Brahman.
6. Śaṅkara’s atom dilemma pressures Nyāya cosmology.
7. Madhva: Viṣṇu alone is *svatantra*.
8. Souls and matter are real *paratantra* entities.
9. *Pañca-bheda* is ontology, not five proofs.
10. Perception and inference support realism.
11. Scripture identifies the independent ground as Viṣṇu.
12. Dependence is ontological, not necessarily temporal.
13. Design reaches only an architect and is underweighted.
14. Perfection discussion is not an ontological proof.
15. Jayatīrtha and Vyāsatīrtha systematize later Dvaita argument.

## Register 9 — Cumulative verdict

1. C-inductive arguments raise probability.
2. P-inductive arguments make a conclusion more probable than not.
3. Strands must possess non-zero evidential force.
4. Independence must be tested rather than assumed.
5. Udayana and Swinburne share a cumulative strategy.
6. Flew’s leaky buckets attack zero-value accumulation.
7. The rope reply works for genuine converging evidence.
8. Simplicity is relevant but contested.
9. Rival designers and impersonal grounds preserve underdetermination.
10. Evil, hiddenness, and diversity must be included in net evidence.
11. Cumulation changes probability, not logical type.
12. The proofs function as diagnostics of explanatory pressure.
13. They do not collectively erase the gap to the God of worship.

## Register 10 — Answer protocol

1. Decode the directive before writing doctrine.
2. Define the proof and distinguish its nearest neighbour.
3. Reconstruct premises visibly.
4. Name the load-bearing presupposition.
5. Use the critic’s strongest version.
6. Give the best reply before judging.
7. State the residual pressure.
8. Distinguish validity, soundness, and adequacy.
9. Use Indian comparisons as arguments, not decoration.
10. Preserve exact PYQ wording and marks.
11. Keep PYQ entries approach-only in the live edition.
12. A 10-marker needs one controlled objection.
13. A 15-marker needs full reconstruction and a graded verdict.
14. A 20-marker needs comparison and more than one objection–reply cycle.
15. End by naming the criterion of judgement.
