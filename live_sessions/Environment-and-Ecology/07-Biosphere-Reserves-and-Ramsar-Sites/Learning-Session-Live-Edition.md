# Biosphere Reserves and Ramsar Sites — Live Session Edition

Yes—but the *map*, the *international label* and the *Indian legal instrument* answer different questions. We will build each separately and then test what happens when they overlap.

### Visual — trace a monsoon pulse

```text
Monsoon runoff → wetland basin / floodplain → slower downstream peak
                       │                    ↘ recharge where geology permits
                       ├→ sediments + nutrients retained (capacity is finite)
                       ├→ shallow/deep habitats → fish + waterbirds
                       └→ water, reeds and fish → local use → management choices
Reclamation/fill → less storage + habitat → greater exposure to flood / lost livelihood
```

*Caption: Water level, connections and catchment pressures jointly determine wetland function; the arrows are mechanisms, not promises for every site.*

Imagine filling an urban lake for buildings. The land gained is visible, but the space that held floodwater is gone; stormwater must find another path. A lake, marsh, lagoon or floodplain is not interchangeable with a tank of water: seasonal flooding, sediment, vegetation and movement of organisms help determine its **ecological character**—the combination of ecosystem components, processes and benefits that defines the wetland at a given time.

1. **Identify the system before naming a designation.** Inland and coastal wetlands can be natural or human-made. Some are permanent; others are seasonal. A wetland may be important for birds, fisheries, floodwater storage and groundwater interactions. Recharge depends on local substrate and hydrological connection; not every wetland recharges aquifers equally.
2. **Follow the pressure, not merely the surface area.** Upstream diversion changes inflow; untreated wastewater changes water quality; bunding or filling changes flooding; invasive growth can change habitat. An unchanged outline on a map does not prove unchanged ecological character. Conversely, not every seasonal change signals degradation: compare an observed condition with the site's normal seasonal regime.
3. **Distinguish public benefit from legal status.** Flood moderation is a causal ecological argument, not proof that this particular site has Ramsar status or a notified management plan. The Economic Survey's printed p. 385 describes protected wetlands as buffers for flood moderation and water security under climate stress; applying that general mechanism to a named wetland requires site-specific hydrology.

**Objection:** If many wetlands are altered by people, is any human use necessarily destructive? **Reply:** No. Timing, scale and water-quality controls matter. Fishery use can remain compatible with an intact flood pulse; permanently filling the basin destroys the storage function. The management question is which uses maintain the character of *this* wetland.

**UPSC application:** For 2021 GS-I Q6, identify storage, water quality, habitats, heat and access implications of reclaiming urban water bodies, then distinguish a general urban water body from a Ramsar site. A GS-III answer can pair adaptation benefits with the limitation that "designated" does not demonstrate functioning.

**Mini recap:** Wetland ≠ perpetual open water; seasonal change ≠ automatic damage; filling a floodplain removes a hydrological function; ecological benefit ≠ international listing.

**Revision notes**

1. A wetland may be seasonal or permanent, inland or coastal, natural or human-made.
2. Catchment, inflow, inundation and outflow shape ecological character.
3. Floodplain storage can reduce a downstream peak by slowing and holding runoff.
4. Recharge depends on geology and connection; it is not equal at every wetland.
5. Sediment and nutrient retention have finite capacity.
6. Water quality can change even when mapped surface area appears stable.
7. Shallow and deep habitats support different fish, plants and waterbirds.
8. Fisheries, reeds and water access connect ecological function with livelihoods.
9. Normal seasonal drawdown is not automatically degradation.
10. Reclamation can remove storage, habitat and public access while increasing urban exposure.

Reclamation removes that space or blocks its channels, so a comparable storm can generate a higher local flood peak. ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual — a functional landscape, not three equally strict rings

| From centre outward | Conservation job | Typical compatible use | Governing caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Long-term ecosystem and biodiversity conservation | Monitoring, low-impact research subject to applicable law | Strict protection relies on an actual protected-area/forest-law basis where one applies. |
| **Buffer** | Support and shield the core | Education, research, ecologically compatible tourism or use | Compatibility requires specified limits and enforcement, not a blanket permission. |
| **Transition** | Demonstrate sustainable development with residents | Settlements, farming and other sustainable livelihoods | It is a negotiated management landscape, not a second core. |

*Caption: MAB uses complementary functions across space; the actual Indian legal restrictions must be checked zone by zone.*

```text
Core conservation → buffer investigation of pressures → transition-area
livelihood agreements → feedback to conservation decisions
```

Think of a landscape with a sensitive forest centre and villages outside. A single rule excluding people everywhere would miss the livelihoods; treating the forest as ordinary production land would miss irreplaceable habitat. The **Man and the Biosphere (MAB) Programme** uses a **biosphere reserve** to connect three functions: conservation, sustainable development and logistical support (research, monitoring and education).

1. **Core protects the ecological reference area.** "Strict" describes the management aim. Many Indian cores overlap national parks or wildlife sanctuaries, whose specific legal force comes from domestic law. A research permit cannot be inferred from the UNESCO designation alone.
2. **Buffer makes protection workable.** It can host monitoring that reveals pressure on the core and education that explains rules; some ecologically compatible tourism can finance stewardship. If tourism fragments habitat or displaces users, calling it buffer activity does not make it compatible. Ask who sets limits, who monitors effects and who benefits.
3. **Transition tests coexistence.** Sustainable agriculture or pastoralism may continue, but livelihood activity must be planned against water, grazing and biodiversity limits. UNESCO does not turn an entire settled transition area into a national park. Nor does a transition label make every development sustainable.
4. **Names can overlap without being identical.** Nilgiri is an Indian biosphere-reserve example; a national park within a biosphere landscape can retain its own statutory boundary and rules. A biosphere landscape may contain wetland habitat, but wetland presence alone does not confer Ramsar status.

**Compare two different landscapes.** The Nilgiri reserve was India's first biosphere reserve, declared in **1986**, and entered UNESCO's World Network in **2000**; the Gulf of Mannar is India's first **marine** biosphere reserve. The marine label describes the ecosystem setting, not a fourth MAB zone. Agasthyamala spans forests in **Kerala and Tamil Nadu**. Its constituent protected areas have separately notified legal identities, but a sanctuary's boundary is not the entire biosphere landscape; compare the site's state, ecosystem and scale before equating a park, reserve and protected-area cluster.

**Objection:** Isn't the buffer a loophole through which development damages the core? **Reply:** Potentially. A meaningful buffer monitors effects and excludes incompatible uses under applicable domestic rules; its value is in preventing pressure displacement, not in declaring any peripheral activity harmless. Transition participation can similarly improve compliance, but requires capacity and consent rather than a ceremonial label.

**UPSC application:** In 2019 Prelims GS-I Q27, compare the geographic and statutory scope of each proposed protected-area grouping. For Mains, diagram core → buffer → transition, then explain *why* the zones perform different functions.

**Mini recap:** Core protects; buffer mediates and studies; transition sustains people and ecosystems; same boundary on a map can carry different legal rules.

**Revision notes**

1. MAB means Man and the Biosphere Programme.
2. Biosphere reserves combine conservation, sustainable development and logistical support.
3. Core, buffer and transition are functional zones, not three equally strict rings.
4. Core conservation often relies on an existing Indian protected-area or forest-law basis.
5. Buffer activity must remain ecologically compatible and monitored.
6. Transition areas accommodate settlements and sustainable livelihoods; they are not automatic exclusion zones.
7. Nilgiri was India's first biosphere reserve in 1986 and entered the WNBR in 2000.
8. Gulf of Mannar was India's first marine biosphere reserve.
9. Agasthyamala spans Kerala and Tamil Nadu and contains separately notified protected areas.
10. Overlapping protected areas and biosphere zones retain distinct boundaries and legal effects.

**Question:** A village is inside the transition area of a biosphere reserve. Does UNESCO recognition alone make the village illegal or permit unlimited quarrying? Explain both sides of the mistake.

<details><summary>Worked answer — open after your attempt</summary>

**Model answer:** Neither. The transition area is intended for residents and sustainable livelihoods, not automatic removal. But quarrying still has to satisfy applicable Indian law, local planning and ecological compatibility; being in transition is no unconditional exemption.

**Misconception to avoid:** Projecting the core's strict protection onto the whole landscape—or treating the transition area as unregulated—both erase the zonation logic.

</details>

**Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words.** Discuss how zonation in an Indian biosphere reserve can reconcile conservation and livelihoods without assuming uniform legal protection across its zones.

<details><summary>Scored model and marking notes</summary>

**Model (about 147 words):** UNESCO's MAB model separates a strictly conserved core, an ecologically compatible buffer and a transition area in which people pursue sustainable livelihoods. In a landscape such as Nilgiri, the arrangement can keep sensitive habitat under existing protected-area restrictions while research in the buffer measures pressures and residents in the transition area help decide viable resource use. The three zones work as a system: a prohibition in one location can shift extraction elsewhere unless monitoring and livelihood alternatives are credible. A national park's enforceable restrictions arise from Indian law; MAB recognition does not impose those restrictions on every village. Conversely, a transition label cannot waive applicable forest, wildlife or pollution controls. Site-specific zoning, local participation, shared benefits and monitored ecological outcomes are needed to turn the map into governance. Thus zoning offers a way to ask where conservation and development can coexist, rather than proving that they already do.

### Visual — follow the decision, then the law

```text
India identifies / manages a biosphere landscape
       ↓ national proposal with ecological case, zonation and participation
UNESCO MAB review → possible World Network of Biosphere Reserves inclusion
       ↓ international recognition, exchange and periodic scrutiny
ON THE GROUND: national park / sanctuary / forest and other Indian rules
             + local management and budgets → actual restrictions and outcomes
```

*Caption: A domestic reserve and a World Network member are two different statuses; neither substitutes for examining the legal category of each parcel.*

Suppose a landscape was called a biosphere reserve domestically yesterday and appears on UNESCO's World Network tomorrow. Residents do not wake up in a wholly new national park. The changed status is **international recognition**, not automatic conversion of its entire landscape into a single Indian statutory protected area.

1. **Two-stage identification.** India's Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) coordinates national biosphere-reserve work and international proposals. A country identifies and nominates a reserve that demonstrates conservation, sustainable development, representative ecosystems and functional zonation. UNESCO's MAB intergovernmental process considers inclusion in the **World Network of Biosphere Reserves (WNBR)**. A domestic declaration does not guarantee admission; international recognition depends on separate review.
2. **Inspect the two counts.** India has **18 domestically notified Biosphere Reserves**. After Cold Desert in Himachal Pradesh joined in September 2025, **13** Indian reserves are in UNESCO's World Network. The UNESCO number is a subset of the domestic total; neither may be substituted for the other.
3. **Ask what has legal teeth.** A core overlapping a National Park or Wildlife Sanctuary can be protected by the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 and relevant notifications; other forest and environmental instruments may also apply depending on parcel. The biosphere label alone is not a new uniformly applicable statutory category. Buffer and transition management can be influenced by other binding domestic restrictions: do not claim *only* core zones ever have enforceable law.

**Objection:** Why pursue UNESCO designation if it cannot prosecute an encroacher? **Reply:** Shared monitoring norms, research cooperation, visibility and periodic assessment can change incentives and reveal weaknesses. But recognition is an input to, not proof of, local enforcement. A strong Mains answer traces *recognition → responsible authority → applicable law → measured outcomes*.

**UPSC application:** An objective question might swap "Indian biosphere reserve" with "UNESCO network biosphere reserve." Ask which institution grants each status before checking a numerical assertion. For the 2019 Agasthyamala demand, compare each proposed group by geography and statutory identity.

**Mini recap:** Domestic declaration ≠ WNBR inscription ≠ Indian National Park notification. UNESCO recognition adds scrutiny and exchange, not blanket statutory prohibition.

**Revision notes**

1. Countries identify and nominate biosphere reserves for UNESCO consideration.
2. MoEFCC coordinates India's biosphere-reserve programme and international proposals.
3. WNBR inclusion is a separate international decision, not automatic after domestic notification.
4. India has **18 notified Biosphere Reserves**.
5. **13** Indian reserves are in UNESCO's World Network after Cold Desert joined in September 2025.
6. A UNESCO inscription does not convert the entire landscape into a national park.
7. Park, sanctuary, forest and other Indian laws continue to govern their notified parcels.
8. Buffer and transition zones may also be subject to binding domestic restrictions.
9. Recognition supports research, exchange, monitoring and visibility.
10. Domestic count, WNBR count and legal protected-area count answer different questions.

### Visual — four distinct steps

```text
Candidate wetland
   ↓ meet at least one Ramsar international-importance criterion
National nomination + Ramsar Information Sheet (RIS) / site boundary
   ↓ Convention listing as a Wetland of International Importance
Wise use of wetlands / maintain ecological character
   ↓ Indian authorities + management plan + monitoring → actual results
```

*Caption: A site qualifies for listing by evidence; continuing wise use requires management after the announcement.*

The **Convention on Wetlands**, adopted at Ramsar, Iran, in **1971**, is an intergovernmental treaty, not a UNESCO biosphere programme. Its Secretariat is hosted at the headquarters of IUCN in Gland, Switzerland; this does not make Ramsar an IUCN species-status assessment. **World Wetlands Day, 2 February**, recalls the treaty's adoption. It supports wetland conservation and **wise use**—maintaining ecological character through ecosystem approaches in the context of sustainable development. Wise use does not mean either unrestricted exploitation or "no person may fish."

1. **Understand the admission test.** A country selects a site and supplies a **Ramsar Information Sheet (RIS)** describing its boundary, ecology and the criteria met. The nine criteria cover representative/rare wetland types and biodiversity importance, including threatened species, fish and waterbirds. **Any one qualifying criterion can suffice**; nine is not a checklist that every site must pass.
2. **Don't turn bird thresholds into every site's entrance exam.** Criterion 5 concerns regular support of **20,000 or more waterbirds**; Criterion 6 concerns **1% of a population of a waterbird species/subspecies**. Others concern different ecological values. A fish-important or representative wetland need not meet a bird-number threshold to be listed.
3. **Interpret the RIS correctly.** The information sheet records the evidence and ecological baseline used to describe the site; it is not an Indian permit, a final proof of ongoing recovery or a substitute for site monitoring. Changing water regimes or outdated population data call for updates and site-level inquiry.
4. **Locate the current example precisely.** **Jai Prakash Narayan Bird Sanctuary (Surha Tal), Ballia, Uttar Pradesh**, became India's 100th Ramsar site on 5 June 2026. **Glaw Lake, Arunachal Pradesh**, became the 101st on 3 August 2026. These are wetland designations; neither event itself creates a sanctuary or proves wise-use performance.

**What different wetlands contribute.** In **Jammu and Kashmir**, the Ramsar description of **Hokera Wetland** places its reedbeds in the **Jhelum basin** near Srinagar: marsh vegetation supports feeding and breeding waterbirds, and the wetland supports fish nurseries. By contrast, **Sasthamkotta Lake in Kerala** is a spring-fed freshwater lake used for **drinking water in Kollam district**. The distinction between reedbed habitat and drinking-water supply shows why "wise use" must respond to each site's ecological character rather than assume all listed wetlands have identical functions. These official site descriptions refer to RIS information published in **2005** and **2002**, respectively; they do not certify water quality or bird abundance today.

**Objection:** If Ramsar is an international treaty, doesn't naming a site automatically prohibit fishing and construction throughout its catchment? **Reply:** The treaty commits parties to conservation and wise-use planning; the enforceable Indian restrictions depend on applicable domestic rules, notification and any other site-specific statutory protection. The site's RIS boundary is not automatically identical to a wider river basin or biosphere reserve.

**UPSC application:** Official 2018 GS-III Q7 asks for a wetland definition, Ramsar wise use and **two Indian Ramsar-site examples**. Prepare the three components without treating this paragraph as a solved PYQ. **Attempt the official 2019 Prelims GS-I Q40 before Lesson 6:** its legal-history references concern **2010**, not a rule first framed in 2017. This notice identifies the year for reading the printed paper, not the validity of any statement or a response choice.

**Mini recap:** Ramsar = wetlands treaty; criterion ≠ management plan; RIS = site information, not permission; wise use ≠ strict universal no-use; dated milestone ≠ live count.

**Revision notes**

1. The Convention on Wetlands was adopted at Ramsar, Iran, in 1971.
2. World Wetlands Day is observed on 2 February.
3. A country nominates a mapped wetland and supplies a Ramsar Information Sheet.
4. Nine alternative criteria cover wetland type, threatened taxa, communities, fish and waterbirds.
5. Meeting one qualifying criterion can be sufficient.
6. Criterion 5 concerns regular support for at least 20,000 waterbirds.
7. Criterion 6 concerns regular support for 1% of a waterbird species or subspecies population.
8. RIS records site information and qualifying evidence; it is not an Indian permit or recovery certificate.
9. Wise use maintains ecological character through ecosystem approaches within sustainable development.
10. Surha Tal was the 100th site on 5 June 2026; Glaw Lake raised the total to 101 on 3 August 2026.

### Visual — do not confuse description, warning and enforcement

| Instrument | What it records or enables | What it cannot do by itself |
|---|---|---|
| **RIS** | Site location, criteria and ecological description; updates inform management. | Guarantee that present ecological character matches older data. |
| **Montreux Record** | Ramsar-listed site where human-caused ecological character has changed, is changing or is likely to change. | Delist a site, punish a violator or automatically repair damage. |
| **Ramsar Advisory Mission (RAM)** | Consent-based expert technical advice requested by a Contracting Party for a threatened or difficult Ramsar-site problem. | Operate as an inspection raid, impose an Indian penalty or automatically add/remove a site from Montreux. |
| **Indian site management** | Baselines, water-flow decisions, pollution control and monitoring under applicable institutions/law. | Make an international warning unnecessary if losses are ignored. |

*Caption: RIS describes; Montreux flags concern; authorities must investigate and act.*

```text
RIS baseline → repeated observation → human-caused change or risk?
                                     ↓ if identified
                            Montreux alert
                                     ↓ Contracting Party may request technical help
                         Ramsar Advisory Mission → recommendations
                                     ↓
                     domestic action + repeat monitoring → status review
```

An information sheet is like a medical history; a watch-list is like an alert for complications. Neither performs a treatment. The **Montreux Record** is maintained as part of the Ramsar List for sites where ecological character *has changed, is changing, or is likely to change* owing to technological development, pollution or other human interference. The point is to prompt attention and assistance, not to erase international status.

1. **Use an Indian contrast without claiming a trend today.** Ramsar's historical record lists **Keoladeo National Park, Rajasthan**, and **Loktak Lake, Manipur** as Indian Montreux entries; **Chilika Lake, Odisha**, previously listed, was later removed following restoration. A removal records a specific management assessment, not proof that a lake will remain free of every future threat.
2. **Separate ecological change from ordinary seasonality.** A declining waterbird count in one dry-season observation cannot alone show persistent human-caused change; compare multi-season information about hydrology, water quality and habitat with the RIS and relevant monitoring. Conversely, management should not dismiss an untreated-effluent trend as normal monsoon variation.
3. **Close the accountability loop.** Recognition → site evidence → warning if warranted → diagnosis and management → repeated monitoring. The record signals a problem; national and local agencies must decide what intervention is feasible and lawful. Ramsar can facilitate advisory expertise, but it does not replace Indian enforcement.

4. **Use a Ramsar Advisory Mission for technical help, not punishment.** A RAM is undertaken at the formal request and with the consent of the Contracting Party. The Secretariat and the Party agree the terms of reference and assemble appropriate expertise; the mission can examine the site, consult stakeholders and recommend preventive or remedial action. Its report is advisory. Implementation remains with the country and its competent authorities.

5. **Relate RAM to Montreux without merging them.** Montreux is the warning register; RAM is a technical-assistance procedure. A site need not be on the Montreux Record before a Party can request a RAM, although RAMs are often used for Montreux sites. For a listed site, the mission report can identify actions or conditions relevant to eventual removal from the Record. The mission itself neither removes the entry nor certifies recovery.

**Why the baseline must match the place.** The Ramsar profile of **Renuka Wetland in Himachal Pradesh** describes a spring-fed lake and subterranean karst, linked by a small Himalayan stream towards the **Giri River**; its sanctuary and reserve-forest designations are additional domestic layers. The Ramsar profile of **Rudrasagar Lake in Tripura** describes a lowland sedimentation reservoir, with three perennial inflows and an outlet towards the **Gomti River**; freshwater fisheries and upstream silt load matter to management. A lake receiving sediment and a karst-linked spring system require different monitoring variables. Their available RIS descriptions are dated **2005**, not new ecological-condition reports, and neither is identified here as a Montreux entry.

**Objection:** If a site appears on a degradation list, why keep it on the prestigious Ramsar List? **Reply:** The warning exists *within* the listing regime so authorities can target restoration. Treating it as automatic delisting would hide the continuing duty to maintain ecological character.

**UPSC application:** If asked what Montreux lists, don't confuse it with UNESCO World Heritage, a list of all threatened wetlands or a list of delisted sites. If asked about RAM, identify consent, technical advice and recommendations—not enforcement. For Mains distinguish **baseline**, **reported change**, **technical advice**, **intervention** and **measured outcome**.

**Mini recap:** RIS is evidence at a date; Montreux flags human-caused change or risk; Keoladeo and Loktak are entries, Chilika a historical removal; monitor after restoration.

**Revision notes**

1. RIS is a dated site description and baseline, not proof of present condition.
2. Montreux is a record within the Ramsar List, not a separate designation or delisting.
3. Its trigger is ecological character changed, changing or likely to change through human interference.
4. Keoladeo and Loktak are Indian Montreux entries.

*Caption: The treaty, Indian regulation and funding mechanisms do different jobs; a site's other statutory status must be checked as well.*

Imagine a listed wetland receiving untreated sewage. Writing "Ramsar" on the gate does not stop the discharge. A competent authority needs a mapped boundary, applicable legal restrictions, a treatment or interception plan, resources and measurement after implementation.

1. **Separate the 2010 text from present law.** The earlier **Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2010**, framed under the Environment (Protection) Act, defined the wetland to include a **zone of direct influence**—the **drainage area or catchment region as determined by the authority**—and provided for a **Central Wetlands Regulatory Authority**. A catchment is the land draining towards a wetland; activities there can change downstream inflows even outside open water. These are historical provisions of the 2010 instrument, not quotations from the later 2017 definition. The 2010 framework's treatment of wetlands of international importance did not turn the Ramsar Convention itself into an Indian prohibition covering every water body.
2. **Locate the current enforceable rules.** The **2017 Rules expressly superseded the 2010 Rules**. They define wetland and separately define a **zone of influence** as the part of its catchment where development induces adverse change; do not claim that the 2010 definition's exact catchment wording survives unchanged in the 2017 definition. Under the present rules, State/UT Wetland Authorities coordinate identification, notification and management. Applicable restrictions include conversion/encroachment, solid-waste dumping and untreated-effluent discharge. The 2017 Rules apply to Ramsar wetlands and notified wetlands or complexes, **but expressly exclude wetlands in areas already covered by specified forest, wildlife and coastal-zone laws**; identify the actual governing instrument for a sanctuary instead of applying both sets of rules indiscriminately.
3. **Keep three statuses apart.** Internationally **designated** ≠ domestically **notified or otherwise regulated** ≠ **managed effectively**. A site might already have sanctuary or forest protection. Ask which instrument applies to which parcel and whether a financed plan is implemented; do not treat a notification alone as measured recovery.
4. **Follow policy continuity accurately.** The earlier **National Wetland Conservation Programme (NWCP)** and **National Lake Conservation Plan** were merged into the **National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems (NPCA)** in 2013. NPCA is a conservation support programme, not another Ramsar treaty. **Amrit Dharohar**, announced in the Union Budget 2023–24, links wetland conservation and local livelihoods at Ramsar sites; **MISHTI** is a distinct mangrove initiative, not a different name for Amrit Dharohar. Budget or scheme mention does not prove disbursal or outcome at any named site.
5. **Make management testable.** Compare baseline and later inflow, untreated discharge, habitat and user access; assign responsibilities to the applicable wetland, wildlife, pollution or local bodies. If a fishery is affected, assess both ecological character and distribution of livelihood costs. The Economic Survey 2025–26 adaptation framing supports why this matters, but not an unmeasured claim of success.

**Objection:** If Ramsar is international, shouldn't all restrictions be identical across India? **Reply:** The convention sets shared commitments; Indian rules and other applicable site-specific laws implement them, with different ecological pressures and boundaries. Legal variation is not a licence to ignore ecological character; it makes identifying the actual regulator essential.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

**A.** A notified national park overlaps a biosphere core and part of a Ramsar wetland. Which status tells you the parcel's Indian statutory restrictions, and which two add international recognition?

<details><summary>Worked answer A</summary>

The national-park notification and governing Indian statute establish the park restrictions on the mapped parcel. MAB network status identifies a biosphere landscape with core/buffer/transition functions; Ramsar status identifies a listed wetland for wise use. **Remediation:** Draw all three boundaries; a shared name does not collapse distinct legal extents.

</details>

**B.** A Ramsar wetland is on the Montreux Record while another site gains a new RIS update. Which of these events proves either wetland has recovered?

<details><summary>Worked answer B</summary>

Neither. Montreux flags changed or threatened ecological character; a RIS update supplies information. Recovery requires matched, repeated site measurements and evidence of effective intervention. **Remediation:** Distinguish warning, description, intervention and measured outcome.

</details>

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These questions are newly authored, **not** UPSC PYQs; attempt each before opening its own scored response.

**10 marks, 150 words — Explain** why one Ramsar waterbird criterion cannot be treated as a universal threshold for designation.

<details><summary>Original model 10 and scoring</summary>

**Model (about 107 words):** The Ramsar system identifies internationally important wetlands through alternative criteria, not a cumulative bird-count test. Criterion 5 concerns regular support for 20,000 or more waterbirds, while Criterion 6 concerns 1% of a population of a waterbird species or subspecies. Other criteria cover representative wetland types, threatened taxa, ecological communities and fish. An Indian wetland could qualify through its habitat or threatened-species value without meeting the Criterion 5 number. Its Ramsar Information Sheet should identify the criteria actually used and the relevant site boundary. Designation then brings a wise-use commitment, not evidence that a particular population is increasing or an automatic new Indian prohibition on every use.

**Scoring (10):** Alternative-criteria reasoning 3; precise C5/C6 3; other types and RIS 2; post-listing qualification 2.

</details>

**15 marks, 250 words — Critically examine** whether Montreux listing is a sanction sufficient to restore a degraded Indian wetland.

<details><summary>Original model 15 and scoring</summary>

**Model (about 137 words):** The Montreux Record is a register within the Ramsar List for sites where human interference has changed, is changing or is likely to change ecological character. It focuses attention and may facilitate advisory help; it is neither automatic delisting nor an Indian criminal sanction. Keoladeo and Loktak illustrate Indian entries, while Chilika's historical removal followed restoration. These examples show that international accountability can stimulate reassessment, not that every entry has the same cause or remedy. A lake exposed to altered inflow and untreated waste needs a diagnosis of its hydrology, pollution sources and livelihoods, followed by competent domestic authorities, an applicable legal instrument, resources and repeated ecological measurements. A past RIS or one bird census cannot establish the intervention's effect without considering season and baseline. Montreux is useful precisely as an alert within a continuing conservation relationship; its weakness lies in dependence on implementation after the alert.

**Scoring (15):** Correct definition/status 4; nuanced Indian examples 3; critique of sanction claim 3; institution/measurement response 4; conclusion 1.

</details>

**20 marks, 250 words — Analyse** how India could integrate biosphere zoning, wetland wise use and climate adaptation without confusing international recognition with statutory control.

<details><summary>Original model 20 and scoring</summary>

**Model (about 218 words):** Biosphere-reserve zoning places strict biodiversity conservation in a core, ecologically compatible work in a buffer and sustainable livelihoods in a transition area. Ramsar's wise use instead addresses a particular wetland's ecological character; where landscapes overlap, the designations need not share boundaries. The *Economic Survey 2025–26* describes protected wetlands as flood-moderation and water-security buffers, giving conservation an adaptation rationale. A managed floodplain may reduce peak runoff while sustaining fisheries, but a Ramsar label alone cannot establish avoided flood losses. India should map legal parcels and hydrological connections first, then apply park and sanctuary law where notified and the Wetlands Rules, 2017, where applicable, alongside pollution controls. State/UT authorities and local users can agree compatible activity limits; NPCA can support aquatic-ecosystem work. Amrit Dharohar adds biodiversity, carbon-stock, ecotourism and community-income objectives, while MISHTI separately targets approximately 540 sq km of mangrove work across nine coastal States and four Union Territories during 2023–2028. Programme targets should be tested through hydrology, habitat, carbon, survival and benefit-sharing evidence rather than treated as outcomes. Publish seasonal inflow, water-quality, habitat, flood and livelihood indicators against a dated baseline. This gives UNESCO/Ramsar recognition and domestic programmes practical roles without mistaking any label or announcement for uniform statutory control or demonstrated resilience.

**Scoring (20):** Accurate zonation/wise-use contrast 5; adaptation causal logic and source limit 4; correct legal, programme and institutional pathway 5; site indicators/livelihoods 4; qualified verdict 2.

</details>

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Wetland mechanisms and climate use

- A wetland's catchment, inundation regime, water quality and habitat mosaic jointly produce ecological character; water surface alone is not a full indicator.
- Floodplain storage can moderate peaks; recharge depends on local geology. Reclamation can shift floodwater and erase shallow-water habitat. The *Economic Survey 2025–26* links protected wetlands with flood moderation and water security, but not an outcome measured for every Ramsar site.
- For an urban-waterbody demand: runoff/inflow → storage and habitats → fill/diversion/pollution → exposed residents and lost biodiversity → prevent conversion, treat sewage, monitor and consult users. Not every urban water body is a Ramsar site.

## Biosphere-reserve spatial grammar

- UNESCO's MAB model: **core** for strict biodiversity conservation; **buffer** for compatible research, education and carefully managed use; **transition** for sustainable livelihoods, settlements and negotiated development. Together serve conservation, development and logistical support.
- Country identifies/nominates a zoned landscape; **WNBR international recognition is separate** from Indian declaration and from notification of a park/sanctuary. Core often overlaps an existing protected area, but other laws may regulate buffer and transition too.
- India has **18 notified Biosphere Reserves**; **13** are in UNESCO's World Network after Cold Desert joined in September 2025.
- Nilgiri: India's first biosphere reserve (1986) and first Indian WNBR member (2000); Gulf of Mannar: first marine biosphere reserve.
- Agasthyamala spans the southern Western Ghats across Kerala and Tamil Nadu and contains distinct statutory protected areas.
- Common wrong move: "all zones equally protected" versus "only core has any law." Both disregard parcel-specific Indian regulation.

## Ramsar listing and use

- The **1971 Convention on Wetlands** was adopted at Ramsar, Iran; World Wetlands Day is **2 February**. Ramsar's Secretariat is hosted at IUCN headquarters in Switzerland; UNESCO MAB and Ramsar are institutionally distinct.
- Nine **alternative** criteria for international importance: representative/rare type and biodiversity, threatened species, fish or waterbirds. Regular ≥**20,000 waterbirds** = Criterion 5; regular **1% of a waterbird species/subspecies population** = Criterion 6; neither is universal.
- India nominates a wetland with mapped boundary and **RIS** (Ramsar Information Sheet) giving qualifying evidence; inscription commits the state to conserving and wisely using wetlands. Wise use maintains ecological character via ecosystem approaches in sustainable development, not universal exclusion.
- **Surha Tal/Jai Prakash Narayan Bird Sanctuary**, Ballia, Uttar Pradesh, was India's **100th Ramsar site on 5 June 2026**. **Glaw Lake**, Arunachal Pradesh, became the **101st on 3 August 2026**.
- Geography recall: Chilika—Odisha; Keoladeo—Rajasthan; Loktak—Manipur; Surha Tal—Uttar Pradesh; Glaw Lake—Arunachal Pradesh. Match a site to a state and to its specific designation independently.
- **Hokera** is a reedbed wetland in **Jammu and Kashmir**, contiguous to the Jhelum basin; its historical RIS discusses fish nurseries and waterbird habitat. **Sasthamkotta** is a freshwater lake in **Kerala** whose springs and Kollam drinking-water role give a different wise-use question. Source descriptions are dated 2005 and 2002, not present-condition audits.

## Warning and implementation

- **Montreux Record** is *within* the Ramsar List: ecological character changed, changing or likely to change through human interference. Keoladeo and Loktak are listed examples; Chilika was historically removed after restoration. Removal from Montreux ≠ Ramsar delisting ≠ permanent security.
- **Ramsar Advisory Mission:** technical assistance requested and consented to by the Contracting Party; agreed scope, expert mission and recommendations; not an enforcement raid or automatic Montreux addition/removal. A site need not already be on Montreux, although the tools often work together.
- RIS = dated description; Montreux = warning; RAM = advice; domestic authority action + repeat monitoring = possible response and status review. Normal seasonality ≠ proved anthropogenic loss; one census ≠ proved recovery.
- **Renuka in Himachal Pradesh**: spring/karst-connected water and Giri River outflow; reserve forest and sanctuary overlap. **Rudrasagar in Tripura**: stream-fed lowland lake with Gomti outlet, fisheries and siltation pressures. Both descriptions come from historical 2005 RIS profiles; neither site belongs to a Montreux conclusion by name alone.
- **2010 Wetlands Rules**, under **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**: central authority; definition included the authority-determined drainage/catchment zone of direct influence. **2017 Rules superseded 2010**: separately defined the zone of influence, constituted State/UT Wetland Authorities and addressed identification, notification and applicable activity restrictions. Their express exclusions for specified wildlife/forest/coastal-law areas require checking the site-specific statute.
- **NWCP + National Lake Conservation Plan → NPCA (2013)**. NPCA supports aquatic conservation.
- **Amrit Dharohar:** Budget 2023–24 announced a three-year wetland programme for optimal use, biodiversity, carbon stock, ecotourism and local income. A MoEFCC order effective 1 July 2026 still listed it as an assigned work area; that confirms administrative continuity, not a new duration or an achieved-outcome claim.
- **MISHTI:** separate mangrove initiative with an intended approximately **540 sq km** across **nine coastal States and four Union Territories** over **2023–2028**. The Survey's **22.8 million person-days** and **4.5 million-tonne carbon-sink** figures are expected benefits, not verified achievements.
- Programme differentiation: Amrit Dharohar concerns wetland/Ramsar stewardship; MISHTI concerns mangrove restoration. Ecotourism needs carrying-capacity limits and local benefit-sharing; carbon claims need baselines and monitoring.
- Four separate columns for a Mains verdict: internationally **designated** | domestically **notified/regulated** | **financed and implemented** | **ecological and livelihood outcomes measured**. A failure in one cannot be erased by success in another.

## Fast answer spines and limits

- Ten marks: define either **core→buffer→transition** or **criterion→listing→wise use**; add one Indian example; distinguish law from label.
- Fifteen marks: explain mechanism and domestic authority; include one objection (livelihood, enforcement or weak monitoring) and a practical reply; date any site count.
- Twenty marks: map overlapping jurisdictions, evaluate hydrology, biodiversity, livelihoods and enforcement, add counterpoint and measurable outcome criterion.
- 2018 GS-III Q7 demands a wetland definition, Ramsar wise use and **two Indian examples**; 2021 GS-I Q6 urban reclamation mechanisms; 2023 GS-III Q17 historical NWCP/current NPCA distinction. Objective 2019 Q27/Q40 and 2022 Q30 remain unanswered; Q40 explicitly tests the **2010** instrument.
