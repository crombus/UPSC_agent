### Visual — trace a monsoon pulse

```text
Monsoon runoff → wetland basin / floodplain → slower downstream peak
                       │                    ↘ recharge where geology permits
                       ├→ sediments + nutrients retained (capacity is finite)
                       ├→ shallow/deep habitats → fish + waterbirds
                       └→ water, reeds and fish → local use → management choices
Reclamation/fill → less storage + habitat → greater exposure to flood / lost livelihood
```

*Caption: Water level, connections and catchment pressures jointly determine wetland function; the arrows are mechanisms, not promises for every site.*

Imagine filling an urban lake for buildings. The land gained is visible, but the space that held floodwater is gone; stormwater must find another path. A lake, marsh, lagoon or floodplain is not interchangeable with a tank of water: seasonal flooding, sediment, vegetation and movement of organisms help determine its **ecological character**—the combination of ecosystem components, processes and benefits that defines the wetland at a given time.

1. **Identify the system before naming a designation.** Inland and coastal wetlands can be natural or human-made. Some are permanent; others are seasonal. A wetland may be important for birds, fisheries, floodwater storage and groundwater interactions. Recharge depends on local substrate and hydrological connection; not every wetland recharges aquifers equally.
2. **Follow the pressure, not merely the surface area.** Upstream diversion changes inflow; untreated wastewater changes water quality; bunding or filling changes flooding; invasive growth can change habitat. An unchanged outline on a map does not prove unchanged ecological character. Conversely, not every seasonal change signals degradation: compare an observed condition with the site's normal seasonal regime.
3. **Distinguish public benefit from legal status.** Flood moderation is a causal ecological argument, not proof that this particular site has Ramsar status or a notified management plan. The Economic Survey's printed p. 385 describes protected wetlands as buffers for flood moderation and water security under climate stress; applying that general mechanism to a named wetland requires site-specific hydrology.

**Objection:** If many wetlands are altered by people, is any human use necessarily destructive? **Reply:** No. Timing, scale and water-quality controls matter. Fishery use can remain compatible with an intact flood pulse; permanently filling the basin destroys the storage function. The management question is which uses maintain the character of *this* wetland.

**UPSC application:** For 2021 GS-I Q6, identify storage, water quality, habitats, heat and access implications of reclaiming urban water bodies, then distinguish a general urban water body from a Ramsar site. A GS-III answer can pair adaptation benefits with the limitation that "designated" does not demonstrate functioning.

**Mini recap:** Wetland ≠ perpetual open water; seasonal change ≠ automatic damage; filling a floodplain removes a hydrological function; ecological benefit ≠ international listing.

**Revision notes**

1. A wetland may be seasonal or permanent, inland or coastal, natural or human-made.
2. Catchment, inflow, inundation and outflow shape ecological character.
3. Floodplain storage can reduce a downstream peak by slowing and holding runoff.
4. Recharge depends on geology and connection; it is not equal at every wetland.
5. Sediment and nutrient retention have finite capacity.
6. Water quality can change even when mapped surface area appears stable.
7. Shallow and deep habitats support different fish, plants and waterbirds.
8. Fisheries, reeds and water access connect ecological function with livelihoods.
9. Normal seasonal drawdown is not automatically degradation.
10. Reclamation can remove storage, habitat and public access while increasing urban exposure.

### Visual — a functional landscape, not three equally strict rings

| From centre outward | Conservation job | Typical compatible use | Governing caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Long-term ecosystem and biodiversity conservation | Monitoring, low-impact research subject to applicable law | Strict protection relies on an actual protected-area/forest-law basis where one applies. |
| **Buffer** | Support and shield the core | Education, research, ecologically compatible tourism or use | Compatibility requires specified limits and enforcement, not a blanket permission. |
| **Transition** | Demonstrate sustainable development with residents | Settlements, farming and other sustainable livelihoods | It is a negotiated management landscape, not a second core. |

*Caption: MAB uses complementary functions across space; the actual Indian legal restrictions must be checked zone by zone.*

```text
Core conservation → buffer investigation of pressures → transition-area
livelihood agreements → feedback to conservation decisions
```

Think of a landscape with a sensitive forest centre and villages outside. A single rule excluding people everywhere would miss the livelihoods; treating the forest as ordinary production land would miss irreplaceable habitat. The **Man and the Biosphere (MAB) Programme** uses a **biosphere reserve** to connect three functions: conservation, sustainable development and **logistic support** (research, monitoring and education).

1. **Core protects the ecological reference area.** "Strict" describes the management aim. Many Indian cores overlap national parks or wildlife sanctuaries, whose specific legal force comes from domestic law. A research permit cannot be inferred from the UNESCO designation alone.
2. **Buffer makes protection workable.** It can host monitoring that reveals pressure on the core and education that explains rules; some ecologically compatible tourism can finance stewardship. If tourism fragments habitat or displaces users, calling it buffer activity does not make it compatible. Ask who sets limits, who monitors effects and who benefits.
3. **Transition tests coexistence.** Sustainable agriculture or pastoralism may continue, but livelihood activity must be planned against water, grazing and biodiversity limits. UNESCO does not turn an entire settled transition area into a national park. Nor does a transition label make every development sustainable.
4. **Names can overlap without being identical.** Nilgiri is an Indian biosphere-reserve example; a national park within a biosphere landscape can retain its own statutory boundary and rules. A biosphere landscape may contain wetland habitat, but wetland presence alone does not confer Ramsar status.

**Compare two different landscapes.** The Nilgiri reserve was India's first biosphere reserve, declared in **1986**, and entered UNESCO's World Network in **2000**; the Gulf of Mannar is India's first **marine** biosphere reserve. The marine label describes the ecosystem setting, not a fourth MAB zone. Agasthyamala spans forests in **Kerala and Tamil Nadu** and includes **Neyyar, Peppara and Shendurney Wildlife Sanctuaries** in Kerala and the **Kalakad Mundanthurai Tiger Reserve** in Tamil Nadu. Those protected areas retain separately notified legal identities; none of their individual boundaries is automatically the entire biosphere landscape.

A meaningful buffer monitors effects and excludes incompatible uses under applicable domestic rules; its value is in preventing pressure displacement, not in declaring any peripheral activity harmless. Transition participation can similarly improve compliance, but requires capacity and consent rather than a ceremonial label.

The three zones work as a system: a prohibition in one location can shift extraction elsewhere unless monitoring and livelihood alternatives are credible. ### Visual — follow designation, review and law separately

```text
National government nominates a zoned biosphere landscape
       ↓ technical assessment under the MAB process
MAB International Coordinating Council designates / decides
       ↓ UNESCO Director-General notifies the State of that decision
World Network membership — international recognition; national sovereignty remains
       ↓ every 10 years: State report → Advisory Committee recommendation → ICC review
satisfactory status recognised OR corrective measures recommended
       ↓ continuing non-conformity after a reasonable period
ICC decision: area ceases to be referred to as a WNBR member → Director-General notifies State
       ↘ separate voluntary route: State may remove the reserve from the Network

ON THE GROUND: national park / sanctuary / forest and other Indian rules
             + local management and budgets → actual restrictions and outcomes
```

*Caption: A domestic reserve and a World Network member are two different statuses; neither substitutes for examining the legal category of each parcel.*

Suppose a landscape was called a biosphere reserve domestically yesterday and appears on UNESCO's World Network tomorrow. Residents do not wake up in a wholly new national park. The changed status is **international recognition**, not automatic conversion of its entire landscape into a single Indian statutory protected area.

1. **Follow the formal international chain.** India's Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) coordinates national biosphere-reserve work and international proposals. A national government nominates a reserve that demonstrates conservation, sustainable development, representative ecosystems and functional zonation. Under Article 5 of the Statutory Framework, the **MAB International Coordinating Council (MAB-ICC)** designates the reserve for inclusion in the Network; UNESCO's **Director-General** notifies the State concerned of the ICC decision. A domestic declaration does not guarantee admission.
2. **Inspect the two counts.** India has **18 domestically notified Biosphere Reserves**. After Cold Desert in Himachal Pradesh joined in September 2025, **13** Indian reserves are in UNESCO's World Network. The UNESCO number is a subset of the domestic total; neither may be substituted for the other.
3. **Preserve sovereignty and legal scale.** A WNBR member remains under the sovereign jurisdiction of its State and is governed by national legislation. A core overlapping a National Park or Wildlife Sanctuary can be protected by the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 and relevant notifications; other forest and environmental instruments may also apply depending on parcel. The biosphere label alone is not a new uniformly applicable Indian statutory category. Buffer and transition management can be influenced by other binding domestic restrictions: do not claim *only* core zones ever have enforceable law.
4. **Review the reserve every ten years.** Article 9 requires the responsible national authority to submit a report against the designation criteria. The Advisory Committee examines it and recommends action to MAB-ICC. The ICC examines the report and formally recognises satisfactory status; where the reserve no longer meets the criteria, it may recommend corrective measures within a reasonable period. If non-conformity continues, the ICC may decide that the area ceases to be referred to as a Network member, and the Director-General notifies the State of that decision. Separately, a State may voluntarily remove its reserve from the Network. Review, cessation or withdrawal does not replace Indian enforcement.

### Do not merge four different designations

| Label | International or Indian decision | Core test | Direct legal meaning in India |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MAB Biosphere Reserve / WNBR** | National nomination; MAB-ICC designation decision; Director-General notification to the State | Three functions, zonation and sustainable-development demonstration | International network status; parcel-specific Indian law still controls |
| **UNESCO World Heritage property** | World Heritage Committee under the **1972 World Heritage Convention** | **Outstanding Universal Value (OUV)** and at least one World Heritage criterion | Separate treaty inscription; it is not a biosphere zone or Indian park notification |
| **National Park / Wildlife Sanctuary** | Indian statutory notification | Wildlife and habitat protection under the governing Indian law | Enforceable restrictions on the notified parcel |
| **Ramsar Site** | Contracting Party designation under the wetlands treaty | At least one Ramsar criterion and an RIS/site boundary | International wetland status; domestic restrictions depend on applicable Indian law |

Geographic overlap is possible—a natural World Heritage property may lie within a biosphere landscape or protected area—but one decision never proves the other.

**Objection:** Why pursue UNESCO designation if it cannot prosecute an encroacher? **Reply:** Shared monitoring norms, research cooperation, visibility and the ten-year review can change incentives and reveal weaknesses. But recognition is an input to, not proof of, local enforcement. A strong Mains answer traces *recognition → responsible authority → applicable law → measured outcomes*.

**UPSC application:** An objective question might swap "Indian biosphere reserve" with "UNESCO network biosphere reserve." Ask which institution grants each status before checking a numerical assertion. For the 2019 Agasthyamala demand, compare each proposed group by geography and statutory identity.

**Mini recap:** Domestic declaration ≠ WNBR inscription ≠ World Heritage inscription ≠ Indian National Park notification. WNBR status remains under national sovereignty and faces a ten-year review; it adds scrutiny and exchange, not blanket statutory prohibition.

**Revision notes**

1. Countries identify and nominate biosphere reserves for UNESCO consideration.
2. MoEFCC coordinates India's biosphere-reserve programme and international proposals.
3. MAB-ICC designates the reserve for WNBR inclusion; UNESCO's Director-General notifies the State of the ICC decision.
4. India has **18 notified Biosphere Reserves**.
5. **13** Indian reserves are in UNESCO's World Network after Cold Desert joined in September 2025.
6. WNBR sites remain under national sovereignty and national legislation.
7. Article 9 requires a periodic review every ten years.
8. The Advisory Committee recommends to ICC; ICC recognises satisfactory status or recommends corrective action. Continued non-conformity can end WNBR reference by ICC decision and Director-General notification; voluntary State removal is a separate route.
9. A UNESCO inscription does not convert the entire landscape into a national park.
10. Park, sanctuary, forest and other Indian laws continue to govern their notified parcels.
11. Buffer and transition zones may also be subject to binding domestic restrictions.
12. World Heritage uses OUV and a separate 1972 Convention/Committee process.
13. Domestic BR count, WNBR count, World Heritage status and legal protected-area count answer different questions.

### Visual — four distinct steps

```text
Candidate wetland
   ↓ meet at least one Ramsar international-importance criterion
Contracting Party designation + Ramsar Information Sheet (RIS) / boundary
   ↓ Secretariat checks the submission and adds the site to the Ramsar List
Listed-site conservation + Article 3.1 wise-use planning for all wetlands
   ↓ Indian authorities + management plan + monitoring → actual results
```

*Caption: A site qualifies for listing by evidence; continuing wise use requires management after the announcement.*

The **Convention on Wetlands**, adopted at Ramsar, Iran, in **1971**, is an intergovernmental treaty, not a UNESCO biosphere programme. Its Secretariat is hosted at the headquarters of IUCN in Gland, Switzerland; this does not make Ramsar an IUCN species-status assessment. **World Wetlands Day, 2 February**, recalls the treaty's adoption. It supports wetland conservation and **wise use**—maintaining ecological character through ecosystem approaches in the context of sustainable development. Under **Article 3.1**, a Contracting Party formulates and implements planning to promote conservation of its listed sites and, as far as possible, the wise use of wetlands in its territory. That all-wetland planning duty is not a treaty-created blanket ban or identical strict-protection regime for every wetland.

1. **Understand designation accurately.** A Contracting Party designates a suitable site and supplies a **Ramsar Information Sheet (RIS)** describing its boundary, ecology and the criteria met. The Secretariat checks the submitted information and map before the site appears on the Ramsar List. **Any one qualifying criterion can suffice**; nine is not a checklist that every site must pass.

### The current nine Ramsar criteria — Resolution XI.8 Annex 2, Rev. COP15 (2025)

| Criterion | International-importance test |
|---:|---|
| **1** | Representative, rare or unique natural/near-natural wetland type in the appropriate biogeographic region |
| **2** | Supports vulnerable, endangered or critically endangered species, or threatened ecological communities |
| **3** | Supports plant/animal populations important for maintaining a biogeographic region's biological diversity |
| **4** | Supports species at a critical life-cycle stage or provides refuge during adverse conditions |
| **5** | Regularly supports **20,000 or more waterbirds** |
| **6** | Regularly supports **1%** of a population of one waterbird species or subspecies |
| **7** | Supports a significant share of indigenous fish diversity, life-history stages or interactions |
| **8** | Is important for fish food, spawning, nursery or migration pathways on which stocks depend |
| **9** | Regularly supports **1%** of a population of a wetland-dependent **non-avian animal** species or subspecies |

The framework groups Criterion 1 around wetland type and Criteria 2–9 around biological diversity. Criteria 5–6 are waterbird tests, 7–8 are fish tests and 9 prevents the one-percent rule from being treated as bird-only.

2. **Don't turn bird thresholds into every site's entrance exam.** Criterion 5 concerns regular support of **20,000 or more waterbirds**; Criterion 6 concerns **1% of a population of a waterbird species/subspecies**. Others concern different ecological values. A fish-important or representative wetland need not meet a bird-number threshold to be listed.
3. **Interpret the RIS correctly.** The information sheet records the evidence and ecological baseline used to describe the site; it is not an Indian permit, a final proof of ongoing recovery or a substitute for site monitoring. Changing water regimes or outdated population data call for updates and site-level inquiry.
4. **Separate Indian input from international listing.** State/UT institutions develop site evidence, but the **National Wetlands Committee** is the body assigned by the 2017 Rules to recommend designation of wetlands of international importance under the Ramsar Convention. MoEFCC serves as India's Ramsar Administrative Authority for transmitting the designation package. This route is separate from domestic wetland notification, which Lesson 6 traces.
5. **Locate the current example precisely.** **Jai Prakash Narayan Bird Sanctuary (Surha Tal), Ballia, Uttar Pradesh**, became India's 100th Ramsar site on 5 June 2026. **Glaw Lake, Arunachal Pradesh**, became the 101st on 3 August 2026. A live official check on **5 October 2026** found no later Indian designation; these are wetland designations, and neither event itself creates a sanctuary or proves wise-use performance.

An information sheet is like a medical history; a watch-list is like an alert for complications. Neither performs a treatment. The **Montreux Record** is maintained as part of the Ramsar List for sites where ecological character *has changed, is changing, or is likely to change* owing to technological development, pollution or other human interference. The point is to prompt attention and assistance, not to erase international status.

1. **Use an Indian contrast without claiming a trend today.** Ramsar's historical record lists **Keoladeo National Park, Rajasthan**, and **Loktak Lake, Manipur** as Indian Montreux entries; **Chilika Lake, Odisha**, previously listed, was later removed following restoration. A removal records a specific management assessment, not proof that a lake will remain free of every future threat.
2. **Separate ecological change from ordinary seasonality.** A declining waterbird count in one dry-season observation cannot alone show persistent human-caused change; compare multi-season information about hydrology, water quality and habitat with the RIS and relevant monitoring. Conversely, management should not dismiss an untreated-effluent trend as normal monsoon variation.
3. **Close the accountability loop.** Recognition → site evidence → warning if warranted → diagnosis and management → repeated monitoring. The record signals a problem; national and local agencies must decide what intervention is feasible and lawful. Ramsar can facilitate advisory expertise, but it does not replace Indian enforcement.

4. **Use a Ramsar Advisory Mission for technical help, not punishment.** A RAM is undertaken at the formal request and with the consent of the Contracting Party. The Secretariat and the Party agree the terms of reference and assemble appropriate expertise; the mission can examine the site, consult stakeholders and recommend preventive or remedial action. Its report is advisory. Implementation remains with the country and its competent authorities.

5. **Relate RAM to Montreux without merging them.** Montreux is the warning register; RAM is a technical-assistance procedure. A site need not be on the Montreux Record before a Party can request a RAM, although RAMs are often used for Montreux sites. For a listed site, the mission report can identify actions or conditions relevant to eventual removal from the Record. The mission itself neither removes the entry nor certifies recovery.
6. **Use transboundary status for cooperation, not legal merger.** Article 5 requires Contracting Parties to consult where a wetland or water system is shared. A formal **Transboundary Ramsar Site** exists when an ecologically coherent system extends across national borders, the responsible Ramsar authorities on both or all sides agree to collaborate, and they notify the Secretariat. Each component remains separately designated and governed under its country's law; the transboundary label records cooperation rather than creating a supranational protected area.

These are historical provisions of the 2010 instrument, not quotations from the later 2017 definition. The 2010 framework's treatment of wetlands of international importance did not turn the Ramsar Convention itself into an Indian prohibition covering every water body.
2. **Locate the current enforceable rules and decision-maker.** The **2017 Rules expressly superseded the 2010 Rules**. They define wetland and separately define a **zone of influence** as the part of its catchment where development induces adverse change; do not claim that the 2010 definition's exact catchment wording survives unchanged in the 2017 definition. The concerned State/UT department prepares a mapped **Brief Document**; the State/UT Wetland Authority **recommends** notification; the State Government or UT Administration considers objections and **notifies** the wetland in the Official Gazette. For a trans-boundary wetland, the Central Government coordinates the Brief Document, the National Wetlands Committee recommends and the Central Government notifies. Applicable restrictions include conversion/encroachment, solid-waste dumping and untreated-effluent discharge. The Rules apply to Ramsar wetlands and notified wetlands or complexes, **but expressly exclude wetlands in areas already covered by specified forest, wildlife and coastal-zone laws**; identify the actual governing instrument for a sanctuary instead of applying both sets of rules indiscriminately.
3. **Keep Ramsar designation institutionally separate.** The National Wetlands Committee is assigned to recommend designation of wetlands of international importance under the Ramsar Convention and to review integrated management of Ramsar and trans-boundary wetlands. 4. **Keep three statuses apart.** Internationally **designated** ≠ domestically **notified or otherwise regulated** ≠ **managed effectively**. A site might already have sanctuary or forest protection. **A.** A notified national park overlaps a biosphere core and part of a Ramsar wetland. Which status tells you the parcel's Indian statutory restrictions, and which two add international recognition?

<details><summary>Worked answer A</summary>

The national-park notification and governing Indian statute establish the park restrictions on the mapped parcel. MAB network status identifies a biosphere landscape with core/buffer/transition functions; Ramsar status identifies a listed wetland for wise use. **Remediation:** Draw all three boundaries; a shared name does not collapse distinct legal extents.

</details>

**B.** A Ramsar wetland is on the Montreux Record while another site gains a new RIS update. Which of these events proves either wetland has recovered?

<details><summary>Worked answer B</summary>

Neither. Montreux flags changed or threatened ecological character; a RIS update supplies information. Recovery requires matched, repeated site measurements and evidence of effective intervention. **Remediation:** Distinguish warning, description, intervention and measured outcome.

</details>

**C.** Two neighbouring countries have separately listed the two halves of one wetland and cooperate informally, but have not formally agreed through their Ramsar authorities or notified the Secretariat. May the arrangement already be presented as a formal Transboundary Ramsar Site?

<details><summary>Worked answer C</summary>

Not yet. Article 5 encourages consultation on shared wetlands, but formal Transboundary Ramsar Site recognition requires the responsible authorities to agree to collaborate and notify the Secretariat. The national listings and domestic laws remain separate. **Remediation:** Cooperation in fact, formal TRS recognition and supranational legal control are three different propositions.

</details>

**10 marks, 150 words — Explain** why one Ramsar waterbird criterion cannot be treated as a universal threshold for designation.

<details><summary>Original model 10 and scoring</summary>

**Model (about 125 words):** The Ramsar system identifies internationally important wetlands through alternative criteria, not a cumulative bird-count test. Criterion 5 concerns regular support for 20,000 or more waterbirds, while Criterion 6 concerns 1% of a population of a waterbird species or subspecies. Criterion 9 separately applies a 1% threshold to a wetland-dependent non-avian animal population. The remaining criteria cover representative wetland types, threatened taxa, regional biodiversity, critical life stages and fish diversity or life-cycle functions. An Indian wetland could therefore qualify through habitat, fish or threatened-species value without meeting the Criterion 5 number. Its Ramsar Information Sheet should identify the criteria actually used and the relevant site boundary. Designation then brings listed-site conservation and wise-use commitments, not evidence that a particular population is increasing or an automatic new Indian prohibition on every use.

**Scoring (10):** Alternative-criteria reasoning 3; precise C5/C6/C9 3; other types and RIS 2; post-listing qualification 2.

</details>

**15 marks, 250 words — Critically examine** whether Montreux listing is a sanction sufficient to restore a degraded Indian wetland.

<details><summary>Original model 15 and scoring</summary>

**Model (about 137 words):** The Montreux Record is a register within the Ramsar List for sites where human interference has changed, is changing or is likely to change ecological character. It focuses attention and may facilitate advisory help; it is neither automatic delisting nor an Indian criminal sanction. Keoladeo and Loktak illustrate Indian entries, while Chilika's historical removal followed restoration. These examples show that international accountability can stimulate reassessment, not that every entry has the same cause or remedy. A lake exposed to altered inflow and untreated waste needs a diagnosis of its hydrology, pollution sources and livelihoods, followed by competent domestic authorities, an applicable legal instrument, resources and repeated ecological measurements. A past RIS or one bird census cannot establish the intervention's effect without considering season and baseline. Montreux is useful precisely as an alert within a continuing conservation relationship; its weakness lies in dependence on implementation after the alert.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Wetland mechanisms and climate use

- A wetland's catchment, inundation regime, water quality and habitat mosaic jointly produce ecological character; water surface alone is not a full indicator.
- Floodplain storage can moderate peaks; recharge depends on local geology. Reclamation can shift floodwater and erase shallow-water habitat. The *Economic Survey 2025–26* links protected wetlands with flood moderation and water security, but not an outcome measured for every Ramsar site.
- For an urban-waterbody demand: runoff/inflow → storage and habitats → fill/diversion/pollution → exposed residents and lost biodiversity → prevent conversion, treat sewage, monitor and consult users. Not every urban water body is a Ramsar site.

## Biosphere-reserve spatial grammar

- UNESCO's MAB model: **core** for strict biodiversity conservation; **buffer** for compatible research, education and carefully managed use; **transition** for sustainable livelihoods, settlements and negotiated development. Together serve conservation, development and **logistic support**.
- Country nominates a zoned landscape; MAB-ICC designates it for WNBR inclusion and UNESCO's Director-General notifies the State of that decision. The reserve remains under national sovereignty and national legislation.
- Article 9 requires a national report and periodic review every **ten years**. The Advisory Committee examines the report and recommends to ICC; ICC recognises satisfactory status or recommends corrective measures. If non-conformity continues after a reasonable period, ICC may decide that the area ceases to be referred to as a Network member and the Director-General notifies the State. Voluntary State removal is a separate Article 9 route.
- **WNBR recognition is separate** from Indian declaration, World Heritage inscription and notification of a park/sanctuary. World Heritage uses OUV and a World Heritage Committee decision under the 1972 Convention.
- A PIB explainer dated **3 November 2025** records **18 notified Biosphere Reserves**; **13** are in UNESCO's World Network after Cold Desert joined in September 2025.
- Nilgiri: India's first biosphere reserve (1986) and first Indian WNBR member (2000); Gulf of Mannar: first marine biosphere reserve.
- Agasthyamala spans Kerala and Tamil Nadu and includes Neyyar, Peppara and Shendurney Wildlife Sanctuaries and Kalakad Mundanthurai Tiger Reserve; each retains its own statutory identity.
- Common wrong move: "all zones equally protected" versus "only core has any law." Both disregard parcel-specific Indian regulation.

## Ramsar listing and use

- The **1971 Convention on Wetlands** was adopted at Ramsar, Iran; World Wetlands Day is **2 February**. Ramsar's Secretariat is hosted at IUCN headquarters in Switzerland; UNESCO MAB and Ramsar are institutionally distinct.
- Nine **alternative** criteria: C1 wetland type; C2 threatened taxa/communities; C3 regional biodiversity; C4 critical life stage/refuge; C5 ≥**20,000 waterbirds**; C6 **1% waterbird population**; C7 indigenous fish diversity; C8 fish food/spawning/nursery/migration; C9 **1% wetland-dependent non-avian animal population**.
- A Contracting Party designates a wetland with mapped boundary and **RIS**. Article 3.1 joins conservation of listed sites with wise-use planning for wetlands across its territory; neither obligation means universal strict exclusion.
- In India, the National Wetlands Committee recommends Ramsar designation and MoEFCC transmits the package. This is separate from State/UT domestic notification.
- **Surha Tal/Jai Prakash Narayan Bird Sanctuary**, Ballia, Uttar Pradesh, was India's **100th Ramsar site on 5 June 2026**. **Glaw Lake**, Arunachal Pradesh, became the **101st on 3 August 2026** and remained the latest official addition found on 5 October.
- Geography recall: Chilika—Odisha; Keoladeo—Rajasthan; Loktak—Manipur; Surha Tal—Uttar Pradesh; Glaw Lake—Arunachal Pradesh. Match a site to a state and to its specific designation independently.
- **Hokera** is a reedbed wetland in **Jammu and Kashmir**, contiguous to the Jhelum basin; its historical RIS discusses fish nurseries and waterbird habitat. **Sasthamkotta** is a freshwater lake in **Kerala** whose springs and Kollam drinking-water role give a different wise-use question. Source descriptions are dated 2005 and 2002, not present-condition audits.

## Warning and implementation

- **Montreux Record** is *within* the Ramsar List: ecological character changed, changing or likely to change through human interference. Keoladeo and Loktak are listed examples; Chilika was historically removed after restoration. Removal from Montreux ≠ Ramsar delisting ≠ permanent security.
- **Ramsar Advisory Mission:** technical assistance requested and consented to by the Contracting Party; agreed scope, expert mission and recommendations; not an enforcement raid or automatic Montreux addition/removal. A site need not already be on Montreux, although the tools often work together.
- **Transboundary Ramsar Site:** separately designated national components of one coherent cross-border wetland system; responsible authorities formally agree to collaborate and notify the Secretariat under the Article 5 cooperation logic. It does not merge sovereignty or domestic law.
- RIS = dated description; Montreux = warning; RAM = advice; domestic authority action + repeat monitoring = possible response and status review. Normal seasonality ≠ proved anthropogenic loss; one census ≠ proved recovery.
- **Renuka in Himachal Pradesh**: spring/karst-connected water and Giri River outflow; reserve forest and sanctuary overlap. **Rudrasagar in Tripura**: stream-fed lowland lake with Gomti outlet, fisheries and siltation pressures. Both descriptions come from historical 2005 RIS profiles; neither site belongs to a Montreux conclusion by name alone.
- **2010 Wetlands Rules**, under **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**: central authority; definition included the authority-determined drainage/catchment zone of direct influence. **2017 Rules superseded 2010**: the State/UT department prepares a Brief Document, the Wetland Authority recommends and the State/UT government or administration notifies. The National Wetlands Committee separately recommends Ramsar designation. Express exclusions for specified wildlife/forest/coastal-law areas require checking the site-specific statute.
- **NWCP + National Lake Conservation Plan → NPCA (2013)**. NPCA supports aquatic conservation.
- **Amrit Dharohar:** Budget 2023–24 announced a three-year wetland programme for optimal use, biodiversity, carbon stock, ecotourism and local income. A MoEFCC order effective 1 July 2026 still listed it as an assigned work area; that confirms administrative continuity, not a new duration or an achieved-outcome claim.
- **MISHTI:** separate mangrove initiative with an intended approximately **540 sq km** across **nine coastal States and four Union Territories** over **2023–2028**. The Survey's **22.8 million person-days** and **4.5 million-tonne carbon-sink** figures are expected benefits, not verified achievements.
- Programme differentiation: Amrit Dharohar concerns wetland/Ramsar stewardship; MISHTI concerns mangrove restoration. Ecotourism needs carrying-capacity limits and local benefit-sharing; carbon claims need baselines and monitoring.
- Four separate columns for a Mains verdict: internationally **designated** | domestically **notified/regulated** | **financed and implemented** | **ecological and livelihood outcomes measured**. A failure in one cannot be erased by success in another.
