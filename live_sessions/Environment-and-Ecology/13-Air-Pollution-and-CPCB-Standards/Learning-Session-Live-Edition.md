*The emitted substance and the pollutant finally measured in ambient air may be different.*

### 1. Begin with the ordinary-language problem

Air pollution is the presence of solid particles, liquid droplets or gases at concentrations and for durations capable of harming health, ecosystems, materials or welfare. The important word is **pathway**. A factory may emit sulphur dioxide; sunlight and atmospheric oxidants can transform it; humidity can help form sulphate particles; wind can carry them; a monitor records an ambient concentration; a person inhales only the fraction present where and when exposure occurs.

### 2. Primary and secondary pollutants

- A **primary pollutant** is emitted directly: carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion, sulphur dioxide from sulphur-containing fuel, nitrogen oxides from high-temperature combustion, dust from construction, or soot from burning.
- A **secondary pollutant** forms in the atmosphere: ground-level ozone, peroxyacetyl nitrate, much of sulphate and nitrate particulate matter, and part of secondary organic aerosol.

This distinction matters because controlling a secondary pollutant requires controlling its **precursors**, not searching for an imaginary ozone pipe.

### 3. Particles by size and behaviour

| Class | Exam-safe meaning | Typical behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| PM10 | Particles with aerodynamic diameter up to 10 micrometres | Includes coarse and fine fractions; enters the respiratory tract |
| PM2.5 | Fine particles up to 2.5 micrometres | Penetrates deeper; remains suspended longer; often has secondary components |
| Ultrafine particles | Commonly below 0.1 micrometre | High number concentration and surface area; not a separate Indian NAAQS pollutant category |
| Settling dust | Larger particles | Deposits faster and has strong local effects |

Aerodynamic diameter describes how a particle behaves in air, not merely what a ruler would show. Composition also matters: an equal mass of crustal dust, soot, metal-rich particles and secondary sulphate does not imply identical toxicity.

### 4. Sources: natural does not mean harmless, anthropogenic does not mean uniform

Natural sources include dust storms, sea salt, wildfires, pollen and volcanic emissions. Anthropogenic sources include power generation, industry, transport, households, waste burning, construction and agriculture. The legal and policy focus is largely on controllable human sources, but natural contributions still affect measured concentration and source apportionment.

**Example and limit:** crop-residue burning can sharply influence downwind pollution during particular windows. It is not a sufficient year-round explanation for every Delhi-NCR episode; local transport, dust, industry, combustion and meteorology remain important.

### 5. Pollutant-source pairs worth distinguishing

| Pollutant | Major source logic | Close-option trap |
|---|---|---|
| SO2 | Sulphur-containing fuel combustion and some smelting | Not primarily a sunlight-created pollutant |
| NOx | High-temperature combustion | Precursor to ozone, nitrate aerosol and acid deposition |
| CO | Incomplete combustion | Toxic gas; not a principal acid-rain precursor |
| VOCs | Fuels, solvents, combustion and biogenic sources | Reactivity varies; "VOC" is not one chemical |
| NH3 | Fertiliser, livestock and waste | Important precursor of secondary inorganic PM |
| Lead/metals | Particular industries, fuels/processes and resuspension | Source profile changes with regulation and location |
| Benzene | Fuel evaporation, combustion, tobacco smoke and solvents | Hazardous VOC; do not equate all VOCs with ozone |

### 6. Mixture challenge: objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** pollutant lists encourage fragmented regulation when people inhale a mixture.

**Reply:** pollutant-specific chemistry, health evidence and control technologies are indispensable. A mixture cannot be managed without knowing its components and sources.

**Residual:** single-pollutant standards may still miss mixture effects, particle composition, ultrafine-particle number and cumulative exposure. Good governance therefore combines pollutant standards with source and health evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can reducing directly emitted PM fail to deliver the expected fall in ambient PM2.5?

**Model answer:** Ambient PM2.5 includes both primary particles and secondary particles formed from SO2, NOx, NH3 and VOC precursors. If policy controls only direct dust or soot while precursor emissions and meteorological conditions remain unfavourable, secondary aerosol can keep measured PM2.5 high.

**Misconception to avoid:** Primary emissions and ambient concentration are not interchangeable; atmospheric formation, transport and removal intervene between them.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Classify major air pollutants by formation and explain why the primary-secondary distinction changes pollution-control strategy.

**Model:** Air pollutants may be primary—emitted directly—or secondary—formed through atmospheric reactions. SO2, NOx, CO, dust and soot are important primary pollutants, while ground-level ozone, PAN and substantial sulphate, nitrate and organic aerosol are secondary. The distinction changes control because an end-of-pipe device can directly capture fly ash, but no source emits urban ozone as such. Ozone control instead requires coordinated reduction of NOx and reactive VOC precursors, while secondary inorganic PM requires attention to SO2, NOx and agricultural NH3. Delhi-NCR illustrates the additional need for season-specific source apportionment: episodic residue burning can combine with persistent local emissions and stagnant weather. However, classification should not become fragmentation; people inhale mixtures, and precursor controls can produce nonlinear effects. Policy must therefore link emission inventories, atmospheric chemistry and ambient monitoring rather than treating measured PM or ozone as a direct mirror of one source.

### Reaction board: two ways polluted air becomes more harmful

```text
PHOTOCHEMICAL PATH
NO2 + sunlight → NO + O
O + O2 → O3
VOCs + radicals alter the cycle → ozone and other oxidants accumulate

ACID / SECONDARY-PARTICLE PATH
SO2 → oxidation → sulphuric species ┐
NOx → oxidation → nitric species    ├→ acids + sulphate/nitrate aerosol
NH3 → neutralisation ────────────────┘

WEATHER MODULATOR
mixing height + wind + stability + humidity + rain → dilution, transformation or removal
```

### 1. Emission is not destiny

Once emitted, a pollutant may react, dilute, deposit, be washed out or travel. **Residence time**, chemical reactivity and meteorology determine the eventual concentration. This is why the same daily emissions can produce different AQI readings on two days.

### 2. Photochemical smog

Photochemical smog is an oxidising mixture formed when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight. Its emblematic pollutant is ground-level ozone. Peroxyacetyl nitrate and secondary organic aerosols may accompany it.

The NOx-VOC-ozone relationship is nonlinear. Reducing one precursor in one chemical regime can have a different short-run effect from reducing it elsewhere. The safe policy lesson is not "NOx reduction is bad"; it is that precursor strategy should be based on atmospheric chemistry rather than a slogan.

### 3. Classical smog and photochemical smog

| Dimension | Sulphurous/classical smog | Photochemical smog |
|---|---|---|
| Favoured setting | Cool, humid, coal-smoke conditions | Sunny conditions with NOx and VOCs |
| Broad chemistry | Reducing, sulphur-rich aerosol mixture | Oxidising mixture |
| Signature concerns | SO2, smoke, sulphate | Ozone, PAN, oxidants, secondary aerosol |
| Control emphasis | Cleaner fuel, SO2 and particle control | NOx/VOC management plus transport/solvent controls |

### 4. Acid deposition

Sulphur and nitrogen compounds can form acids and acidic particles. **Acid deposition** includes wet deposition through rain, snow or fog and dry deposition of gases and particles. Effects include soil and freshwater acidification, nutrient leaching, metal mobilisation, vegetation stress and material corrosion.

### 5. Meteorology: why winter episodes persist

- A low mixing height compresses pollutants into a smaller air volume.
- Temperature inversion can suppress vertical mixing.
- Weak winds reduce horizontal dispersion.
- Humidity can accelerate aerosol growth and impair visibility.
- Rain can remove some pollutants, but washout is temporary if emissions continue.
- Regional transport can import and export pollution across administrative borders.

**Example and limit:** an inversion behaves like a lid, but it creates neither vehicles nor smoke. Weather amplifies or disperses emissions; it does not absolve sources.

### 6. Model challenge: what weather does to attribution

**Objection:** because weather dominates daily concentration, emission controls cannot be evaluated from observed air quality.

**Reply:** meteorology complicates attribution but does not make evaluation impossible. Weather-normalised trends, chemical transport models, long time series and matched comparisons help separate emission change from weather.

**Residual:** models depend on inventories, boundary conditions and assumptions. Results should carry uncertainty ranges rather than false precision.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a rain-making intervention an incomplete air-pollution strategy even if it temporarily lowers particle concentration?

**Model answer:** Precipitation can scavenge some particles and soluble gases, but it neither eliminates emission sources nor works without suitable clouds and meteorological conditions. Concentrations can rebound after the episode; ozone and some gases may not respond in the same way.

**Misconception to avoid:** Removal from air during one episode is not equivalent to permanent emission reduction.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain how atmospheric chemistry and meteorology convert an emission-control problem into an airshed-management problem.

**Model:** Emissions are only the first stage of air pollution. Primary SO2, NOx, VOCs, NH3 and particles undergo oxidation, neutralisation, condensation and photochemistry, producing ozone and secondary sulphate, nitrate and organic aerosols. Their concentration then depends on mixing height, stability, wind, humidity, rainfall and regional transport. Thus a city can experience severe pollution when persistent local emissions coincide with inversion and imported pollution, while a windy day can show lower concentration without any structural source improvement.

This breaks the administrative assumption that each city controls only what it emits. Upwind power, industry, agriculture and biomass burning can affect downwind populations; conversely, city emissions travel outward. Policy therefore needs an airshed-scale inventory, seasonal source apportionment, interoperable monitoring and coordinated precursor controls. Delhi-NCR's CAQM represents an institutional response to this coordination problem, while GRAP addresses episodes rather than the full source system.

Yet modelling is not omniscient. Inventories may be outdated and source shares vary by method and season. Decisions should combine measurements, receptor studies, satellite and meteorological evidence, report uncertainty, and update controls as evidence changes. Airshed governance is therefore scientific coordination across space, sectors and time—not a claim that local accountability disappears.

### Exposure journey: concentration is not the final outcome

```text
AMBIENT CONCENTRATION
        ↓ modified by time-location-activity
PERSONAL EXPOSURE
        ↓ modified by breathing rate and protection
INHALED DOSE
        ↓ modified by particle size, chemistry and susceptibility

        ↓
```

### 1. Exposure is personal; standards are ambient

An ambient monitor measures outdoor air at a location. It does not directly measure every person's exposure. A traffic police worker, child near a road, cook using smoky fuel and office worker with filtered air can receive different doses during the same citywide reading.

### 2. Major health pathways

- PM2.5 can reach deep lung regions; some constituents can trigger inflammation and systemic effects.
- Ozone is a strong oxidant affecting respiratory function and vegetation.
- NO2 is associated with combustion exposure and respiratory harm and also participates in secondary pollution.
- SO2 irritates the respiratory system, especially for susceptible persons.
- CO binds haemoglobin and reduces oxygen delivery.
- Benzene and certain toxic air pollutants create chronic risks distinct from AQI's immediate communication categories.
- Lead and other metals can affect neurological and other systems.

The correct statement is **risk increases with exposure**, not that every exposed individual will develop an identical disease.

### 3. Vulnerability and environmental justice

Children, older persons, pregnant persons, people with cardiopulmonary disease and outdoor workers may face higher susceptibility or exposure. Low-income households can face a double burden: proximity to roads, dumps or industry and fewer resources for avoidance, healthcare or filtration.

**Analytical distinction:** equal ambient standards are necessary, but equal standards do not automatically produce equal protection when baseline health and exposure differ.

### 4. Ecological and material effects

| Pollutant/process | Ecological/material pathway |
|---|---|
| Ozone | Enters leaf stomata, impairs photosynthesis and can reduce crop/forest productivity |
| Sulphur/nitrogen deposition | Acidification, eutrophication and nutrient imbalance |
| Particles | Coat leaves, alter radiation and soil/water deposition |
| Nitrogen deposition | May fertilise some systems but shift species competition and biodiversity |
| Acidic/oxidising pollution | Corrodes metals and degrades stone, paint and heritage surfaces |
| Black carbon | Warms the atmosphere and darkens snow/ice after deposition |

### 5. Standards versus WHO guidelines

WHO air-quality guidelines are health-based recommendations, not Indian legal limits. India's NAAQS are notified national ambient standards shaped by legal and policy context. Comparing them is useful, but one must not label a WHO guideline as an enforceable Indian limit.

### 6. Causation challenge: multifactor disease

**Objection:** disease has multiple causes, so attributing health harm to air pollution is speculative.

**Reply:** epidemiology estimates population-level risk using multiple designs, exposure models and confounder controls. Multifactor causation does not mean no causal contribution.

**Residual:** burden estimates depend on exposure data, baseline rates and concentration-response functions. Quote a study, year and method before using a precise mortality number.

### Concept check

**Question:** Can a city meet an annual ambient standard while some groups remain seriously overexposed?

**Model answer:** Yes. Spatial hotspots, occupational exposure, indoor sources, commuting routes and episodic peaks can produce high personal exposure even when a citywide or station-specific annual average meets a standard. Distribution and time pattern therefore matter.

**Misconception to avoid:** Compliance at one aggregation level is not proof that every person, place or hour is safe.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** "Air pollution is simultaneously a public-health, ecological and distributive-justice problem." Analyse.

**Model:** Air pollution harms through linked but distinct pathways. Fine particles and gases raise cardiopulmonary and other health risks; ozone injures lungs and vegetation; sulphur and nitrogen deposition can acidify or eutrophy ecosystems; particles and oxidants damage crops, visibility and materials. Black carbon additionally connects local combustion with regional climate and cryosphere effects.

The burden is distributive. Ambient concentration is not personal exposure: roadside workers, children, residents near industry or dumps, and households dependent on polluting fuels may receive larger doses. Pre-existing illness and weak healthcare increase susceptibility. Thus formally uniform standards can coexist with unequal protection.

Policy should first prevent emissions through clean energy, transport, industrial and waste controls; then reduce exposure through hotspot monitoring, occupational safeguards, clean household energy, school and hospital protection, and public warnings. NAAQS provide an ambient benchmark, AQI communicates short-term conditions, and health surveillance tests whether cleaner readings improve welfare. Crop-loss and ecosystem indicators should complement urban-health metrics.

However, precise mortality or yield claims require study-specific attribution, and a single monitor cannot represent an entire city. A just clean-air strategy therefore combines universal ambient improvement with targeted protection for high-exposure groups, transparent local data and health/ecological outcome evaluation.

### 1. National Ambient Air Quality Standards

India's 2009 NAAQS cover 12 pollutants: PM10, PM2.5, SO2, NO2, ozone, CO, lead, ammonia, benzene, benzo(a)pyrene, arsenic and nickel. A standard specifies a pollutant, averaging period, concentration, area class where applicable and compliance method. Annual and short-term standards answer different questions.

| NO2 | 40 | 30 | 80, 24-hour | µg/m³ |

The lower annual SO2 and NO2 values for notified ecologically sensitive areas are a frequent close-option trap. Units and averaging periods are inseparable: CO is in mg/m³; benzo(a)pyrene, arsenic and nickel are in ng/m³; the remaining displayed values are in µg/m³.

### 2. Monitoring and compliance data rules

Manual stations can provide scheduled integrated samples and laboratory analysis; **Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Stations (CAAQMS)** generate high-frequency data. A network requires:

1. representative and purpose-specific siting;
2. calibrated instruments and standard methods;
3. quality assurance and quality control;
4. sufficient data capture;
5. metadata on downtime and maintenance;
6. transparent validation before trend claims.

A reading is an observation at a site and time. A city's air quality is an inference from a network.

The 2009 NAAQS notification also fixes minimum compliance logic:

- an annual arithmetic mean requires at least **104 measurements in a year at a site**, taken twice a week as 24-hour samples at uniform intervals;
- the applicable 24-hour, 8-hour or 1-hour values should comply **98% of the time in a year**;
- exceedance is permitted up to 2% of the time, but not on two consecutive monitoring days;
- two consecutive exceedances are adequate reason for regular or continuous monitoring and further investigation.

These are data-sufficiency and compliance rules, not a claim that 104 observations represent every neighbourhood or exposure.

### 3. India's AQI

The national AQI communicates eight pollutants—PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, ozone, ammonia and lead—through six categories:

| AQI | Category |
|---:|---|
| 0-50 | Good |
| 51-100 | Satisfactory |
| 101-200 | Moderately polluted |
| 201-300 | Poor |
| 301-400 | Very poor |
| 401-500 | Severe |

Pollutant concentrations are converted into sub-indices using breakpoint functions. The CPCB calculation protocol requires valid data for at least **three pollutants**, and at least one must be **PM10 or PM2.5**. A pollutant sub-index requires at least **16 hours of valid data**. PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, ammonia and lead use 24-hour concentration averages for daily AQI; CO and ozone use 8-hour averages. Once those sufficiency conditions are met, the overall AQI is the highest qualifying sub-index, not a simple arithmetic average of pollutant concentrations.

CAAQMS data availability is therefore not identical to AQI availability. A station can report some concentrations while lacking enough valid pollutant-hours to publish an overall AQI.

### 4. The six distinctions UPSC can test

| Term | What it is | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| Ambient standard | Permissible outdoor concentration benchmark | Stack-emission limit |
| Emission standard | Limit at a source/technology/fuel | City ambient concentration |
| Measurement | Observed concentration with method and QA | Proof of source attribution |
| AQI | Communication index | The legal standard itself |
| Compliance | Determination under stated rule | One favourable reading |
| Outcome | Observed environmental/health change | Money spent or machines installed |

### 5. Example and limit

If a power plant meets its stack PM emission limit, nearby ambient PM may still exceed NAAQS because of other sources, secondary formation and weather. Conversely, an ambient station may meet NAAQS while a particular stack violates its emission standard. The two control points are connected but not interchangeable.

### 6. Communication trade-off: one index versus a mixed atmosphere

**Objection:** a single AQI number hides pollutant mixtures and spatial inequality.

**Reply:** that simplification is its communication purpose; pollutant-wise concentrations and sub-indices should remain available for diagnosis.

**Residual:** sparse siting, missing data and category-focused media reporting can create false confidence. AQI must be accompanied by station coverage, dominant pollutant and raw concentration.

### Prelims distinction drill

Prelims close-option logic often turns on five pairs: annual versus 24-hour/8-hour/1-hour average; µg/m³ versus mg/m³ versus ng/m³; 12 NAAQS pollutants versus eight AQI pollutants; NAAQS compliance rules versus AQI data-sufficiency rules; ambient concentration versus source emission.

### Standards-and-monitoring recall

1. NAAQS are pollutant- and averaging-time-specific ambient benchmarks.
2. India's current NAAQS were notified in 2009 and cover 12 pollutants.
3. AQI uses eight pollutants and six categories.
4. AQI communicates risk; it does not replace NAAQS.
5. Overall AQI is driven by the highest qualifying sub-index.
6. Annual and short-term standards cannot be swapped.
7. PM values use µg/m³; CO uses mg/m³; benzo(a)pyrene, arsenic and nickel use ng/m³.
8. Monitoring requires siting, calibration, completeness and QA/QC.
9. A station reading is not automatically a citywide average.
10. Ambient and emission standards regulate different points in the causal chain.
11. Compliance needs a defined rule, not an isolated reading.
12. Installation of monitors is an input, not an air-quality outcome.
13. Pollutant-wise data should accompany the headline AQI.
14. WHO guidelines and Indian NAAQS have different legal status.
15. Annual compliance needs at least 104 site measurements; short-period NAAQS values use the 98% rule and consecutive-exceedance trigger.

### Concept check

**Question:** A city reports AQI 280 while every industrial stack checked that day meets its emission limit. Is there a contradiction?

**Model answer:** No. Stack limits apply to specified sources, while AQI reflects ambient concentrations influenced by all sources, secondary formation, transport and weather. Source compliance can coexist with poor ambient air, especially if standards are weak, coverage incomplete or other sources dominate.

**Misconception to avoid:** Source compliance is neither logically identical to nor sufficient proof of ambient attainment.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish NAAQS, ambient monitoring, AQI and emission standards. Why does conflating them weaken accountability?

**Model:** NAAQS specify permissible outdoor concentrations for pollutants and averaging periods. Ambient monitoring supplies site- and time-specific measurements used to assess those standards. AQI converts selected pollutant concentrations into sub-indices and a public health category; it is a communication device. Emission standards instead regulate releases from a source such as a stack or vehicle.

Conflation weakens accountability because each answers a different question. A compliant stack does not prove citywide attainment; a lower AQI day may reflect wind rather than emission reduction; monitor installation does not prove cleaner air; and an AQI category cannot substitute for pollutant-wise legal assessment. India therefore needs calibrated and representative networks, transparent raw data, clearly stated compliance rules and linked source inventories. Reporting should separate input—funds and stations; activity—inspections; compliance—violations corrected; emissions—mass reduced; ambient outcome—weather-normalised concentration; and health outcome—exposure or disease change.

### 1. Statutory basis

The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 establishes the prevention, control and abatement framework. It followed the Stockholm Conference context and was enacted using Parliament's Article 253 power. The existing Central and State Boards created under the Water Act perform functions under the Air Act.

The current statute must be read with the Jan Vishwas amendments. **G.S.R. 757(E), 18 October 2023**, brought the Air Act entries in the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023 into force on **1 April 2024**. **G.S.R. 701(E), 12 November 2024**, then notified the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) (Manner of Holding Inquiry and Imposition of Penalty) Rules, 2024 for adjudication of covered contraventions. Monetary adjudication changed the sanction route for specified provisions; it did not repeal pollution standards, consent, inspection or direction powers.

### 2. CPCB and SPCB functions

Under the Air Act, CPCB's national role includes advising the Union, planning nationwide programmes, coordinating State Boards, technical assistance, research, training, information and air-quality functions. Section 16(2)(h) assigns CPCB the function of laying down standards for the quality of air. SPCBs plan state programmes, advise states, inspect control areas and plants, assess emissions and administer on-ground control; section 17(1)(g) places source-emission standard setting with the State Board, in consultation with CPCB and having regard to CPCB's ambient-air standards.

Many sector-specific source-emission limits are separately notified by the Central Government under sections 3 and 6 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and rule 3/Schedule I of the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986. The authority map is therefore:

The exam-safe distinction is:

```text
CPCB: national ambient-air standards / coordination / technical architecture
SPCB: consulted source standards + state consent / inspection / enforcement
Central Government under EPA/Rules: many industry/process emission standards
```

This is a functional emphasis, not a claim that CPCB has no enforcement role, that SPCBs make policy in isolation, or that every national source-emission limit is a CPCB notification.

### 3. Air-pollution-control areas and industrial consent

The statutory chain allows declaration of air-pollution-control areas and regulates industrial plants within the framework. Consent to Establish and Consent to Operate translate general law into source-specific conditions. Conditions may concern fuel, technology, emission controls, monitoring and operation.

The **Control of Air Pollution (Grant, Refusal or Cancellation of Consent) Guidelines, 2025**, notified through **G.S.R. 84(E), 29 January 2025**, created a nationally structured consent procedure under section 21A. The **2026 Amendment Guidelines, G.S.R. 62(E), 23 January 2026**, now provide that:

1. once granted, **Consent to Operate continues until it is cancelled** under the guidelines;
2. a State/UT may set a one-time CTO fee for a chosen **5-25 year** period, with a further fee for the next chosen period;
3. Registered Environment Auditors may perform specified verification functions;
4. CPCB must develop a unified consent-and-authorisation portal, after which consent processing is to occur through it; and
5. eligible micro and small units in duly notified industrial estates/areas receive deemed Consent to Establish on submission of the prescribed self-certified application.

**Critical distinction:** a consent order is permission subject to conditions. It is not a certificate that continuous compliance has already occurred.

### 4. Compliance architecture

| Stage | Evidence | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Standard/condition | Legal limit or consent term | Ambiguous/outdated condition |
| Measurement | Stack test, CEMS or inspection | Poor calibration, tampering, missing data |
| Comparison | Limit versus valid observation | Selective period or wrong averaging |
| Finding | Compliant/non-compliant under rule | No due process or weak documentation |
| Response | Direction, penalty, closure/correction | Delay or low deterrence |
| Verification | Follow-up measurement | Assuming an order equals correction |

### 5. Regulatory reform without category confusion

The Jan Vishwas reform and the 2024 inquiry rules create a monetary-penalty/adjudication route for specified Air Act contraventions. This is decriminalisation of listed contraventions, not repeal of emission standards, consent duties, inspection powers or serious environmental accountability. The policy trade-off is precise: simpler, trust-based compliance may reduce procedural criminalisation, but penalties and verification must remain sufficiently certain to deter profitable non-compliance.

CPCB's revised industrial classification adds **Blue** to Red, Orange, Green and White. Blue identifies industries providing essential environmental services for managing domestic waste. The Economic Survey 2025-26 recorded a two-year extension in the then category-linked consent-validity framework, based on information retrieved on 14 January 2026. That duration statement must not be used as the current CTO rule: **G.S.R. 62(E), issued on 23 January 2026, now makes CTO continuous until cancellation**, with one-time fee periods of 5-25 years. Blue is a pollution/service classification, not an AQI class, proof of zero pollution or a current two-year CTO.

### 6. CPCB/SPCB, CAQM and NGT are not substitutes

- CPCB/SPCBs are pollution regulators.
- CAQM is a regional statutory coordinator with overriding air-quality directions in NCR and adjoining areas.
- NGT adjudicates substantial environmental questions and appeals under scheduled laws, including the Air Act.
- MoEFCC is the policy ministry.

One institution can check or coordinate another without inheriting every function.

### 7. Capacity challenge: law on paper versus performance

**Objection:** India's problem is not missing law but weak regulator capacity, so more standards achieve little.

**Reply:** capacity is a serious last-mile constraint, but clear standards, measurement protocols and enforceable conditions remain necessary. Capacity reform and substantive regulation are complements.

**Residual:** staffing and laboratory capacity vary across states; aggregated national claims should not erase this variation. Transparent board-level performance data is needed.

1. The Air Act was enacted in 1981 in the Stockholm/Article 253 context.

**Question:** Why is the number of closure directions issued an inadequate standalone measure of SPCB effectiveness?

**Model answer:** Closure directions measure enforcement activity, not whether violations were detected comprehensively, emissions actually fell, compliant production resumed, ambient exposure improved or enforcement was proportionate and durable. Follow-up verification and outcome indicators are needed.

**Misconception to avoid:** More punitive orders do not automatically mean better environmental performance.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine the CPCB-SPCB regulatory chain under the Air Act and identify the points at which formal standards can fail to become cleaner air.

**Model:** The Air Act creates a multi-level chain. CPCB advises the Union, coordinates State Boards and lays ambient-air-quality standards; SPCBs set source-emission standards in consultation with CPCB, administer consent, inspect plants, monitor emissions and issue enforcement directions. Many industry/process emission limits are separately notified under the Environment (Protection) Act/Rules. Consent conditions translate these general standards into source obligations.

Failure can occur at each link. Standards may lag health evidence or omit important pollutants. Emission inventories and permits may not reflect actual operating conditions. Sparse monitoring, poor calibration or missing CEMS data weaken detection. Staffing, laboratories and legal capacity differ across SPCBs. Penalties may arrive late or be cheaper than compliance. Finally, a corrected stack may not deliver ambient attainment when transport, dust, household fuel, agriculture or secondary aerosols dominate.

Reform should therefore link legal limits to risk-based inspection, tamper-resistant monitoring, accredited laboratories, public data, time-bound adjudication and verified corrective action. Since G.S.R. 62(E) makes CTO continuous until cancellation, ongoing verification matters more—not less. CPCB can standardise methods and compare board performance; states must strengthen staff and enforcement; airshed bodies are needed where sources cross borders. Yet centralisation alone is not a cure: local knowledge and accountable state implementation remain essential.

The chain succeeds only when permission, measurement, compliance, emission reduction and ambient outcomes are reported separately and connected causally.

### 1. NCAP

The National Clean Air Programme, launched in 2019, is a national framework for improving air quality through action plans, monitoring and inter-agency coordination. Its target architecture evolved from an initial tentative **20-30% reduction in particulate concentration by 2024 against the 2017 base year** to **up to 40% reduction in annual PM10 concentration, or attainment of the PM10 NAAQS of 60 µg/m³, by 2025-26**. The end-year target is not proof that every city attained it.

Current official programme language must be separated from the historical identification shorthand. MoEFCC's Annual Report 2025-26 defines a **non-attainment city** as one exceeding the annual average concentration of **any notified NAAQS parameter consecutively for five years**. The current 130-city NCAP/grant universe also includes identified million-plus cities; it is not simply the original PM10/NO2 list.

**Official outcome status, 7 September 2026:** PIB reported that, in FY 2025-26, **108 of 130 cities** recorded lower annual PM10 than in **2017-18**; **72** recorded reductions above 20%, **26** above 40%, and **14** met the PM10 NAAQS. These are government-reported city outcomes for a specified year and baseline, not proof of uniform national attainment, health improvement or causal attribution to one intervention.

### 2. City action plans

2. seasonal source apportionment;
3. sector measures with responsible agencies;
4. budgets and milestones;
5. emission and ambient indicators;
6. public disclosure;
7. review and revision when measures underperform.

A list of activities without quantified source logic is an administrative checklist, not a strategy.

### 3. GRAP

Stages may be invoked in advance when IMD/IITM forecasts show a sustained projected breach; an actual episodic breach can trigger immediate action; and higher-stage restrictions are cumulative with lower stages. GRAP is valuable for acute exposure reduction but remains reactive. Construction restrictions, vehicle controls or industrial curbs during an episode cannot substitute for year-round structural change.

### 4. CAQM and the coordination problem

CAQM was first created by ordinance in 2020 and placed on a statutory footing by the 2021 Act. Its field covers the NCR and **adjoining areas**—areas in Haryana, Punjab, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh where a pollution source causes adverse impact on NCR air quality. This does not convert the entirety of all four states into NCR. Its design recognises that an airshed crosses municipal and state borders.

Supreme Court supervision remains institutionally distinct from CAQM administration. In its **17 December 2025** order in *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India*, W.P.(C) No. 13029/1985, the Court treated Delhi air quality as a live issue, sought CAQM's expert opinion and requested it to revisit long-term measures across mobility, clean industry/energy, stubble, construction, household pollution, green cover, public transport and inter-state coordination. CAQM's **29 May 2026** GRAP order expressly states that the modified 21 November 2025 schedule operates in pursuance of Supreme Court directions. The Court supervises and issues case-specific directions; CAQM remains the statutory expert commission that invokes, revokes and coordinates GRAP.

### 5. What is an airshed?

An **airshed** is a geographic area within which emissions, terrain and meteorology interact to shape air quality. Its boundary is scientific and functional; it need not match an administrative border.

| Governance scale | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| City | Local implementation and exposure hotspots | Cannot control all imported pollution |
| State | Sector and land-use authority | Upwind/downwind externalities persist |
| Regional airshed | Matches transport and shared sources better | Coordination and accountability are harder |
| International/transboundary | Addresses cross-border transport | Sovereignty, data and burden-sharing constraints |

### 6. Federalism trade-off: coordination versus accountability

**Objection:** regional bodies dilute elected-state accountability.

**Reply:** cross-border externalities require a coordinating authority; otherwise each jurisdiction can under-control emissions whose costs fall elsewhere.

**Residual:** statutory override cannot manufacture trust, finance or farmer/municipal alternatives. Regional authority needs transparent burden-sharing, state participation and appeal/accountability mechanisms.

```text
CAQM ≠ CPCB for the whole country
airshed ≠ administrative boundary

```

3. NCAP action plans are structural and multi-year.

5. NAAQS define ambient benchmarks; neither NCAP nor GRAP replaces them.
6. CAQM is statutory under the 2021 Act.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can GRAP be well implemented during severe episodes while a city's long-run PM trend remains poor?

**Model answer:** GRAP temporarily restricts activities after pollution reaches trigger levels. Long-run trends depend on permanent changes in transport, fuels, industry, dust, waste, agriculture and regional precursor emissions. Episodic control cannot substitute for structural source reduction.

**Misconception to avoid:** Effective crisis response is not the same as successful prevention.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Critically evaluate India's shift from city-based air-pollution control toward airshed and regional governance.

**Model:** City governments control roads, waste, construction and local enforcement, but air moves across their borders. Secondary particles form from precursors emitted across sectors and states; seasonal biomass burning, power, industry and transport can affect downwind cities. City-only plans therefore create a jurisdictional mismatch.

India's response combines NCAP city plans with regional coordination, most clearly through the statutory CAQM for NCR and adjoining areas. An airshed approach can harmonise inventories, monitoring, episode forecasts, industrial and transport controls, and agricultural alternatives. It also reduces blame-shifting by locating sources and receptors in one analytical frame.

However, scale alone does not guarantee effectiveness. Source-apportionment results vary by season and method; regional bodies can obscure who must deliver; states face unequal costs; farmers and municipalities need funded alternatives; and override powers can weaken cooperative legitimacy if decisions are opaque. GRAP further shows the risk of substituting visible emergency bans for durable prevention.

India should define airsheds scientifically, publish common inventories and model uncertainty, assign sector milestones to named governments, use fiscal transfers for unequal abatement burdens, and retain local hotspot action. Independent outcome review should compare weather-normalised concentration and emissions, not merely directions issued.

The appropriate model is nested governance: strong local delivery inside accountable state and regional coordination, rather than either municipal isolation or unreviewable central command.

### Sector workshop: choose the control point before the device

```text
AVOID ACTIVITY/DEMAND
        ↓

        ↓
IMPROVE EFFICIENCY AND MAINTENANCE
        ↓
```

### 1. Power and large combustion

Controls include lower-sulphur fuel or fuel switching, energy efficiency, electrostatic precipitators/bag filters for particles, flue-gas desulphurisation for SO2 and combustion/after-treatment measures for NOx. Each device has operating conditions, energy/water implications and maintenance needs.

An installed device is not an emission reduction unless it operates effectively. Stack CEMS can improve temporal coverage, but quality assurance and independent verification remain essential.

### 2. Industry

Industrial strategy combines cleaner production, process enclosure, fugitive-emission control, fuel standards, common infrastructure where suitable, stack controls, consent conditions and credible measurement. Small dispersed units create a different enforcement challenge from a few large stacks.

The Surat particulate-matter emissions trading scheme supplies quantitative Indian evidence rather than a decorative market example. It covered **317 industrial plants**, used mandatory **Continuous Emissions Monitoring Systems (CEMS)** for large particulate sources, and achieved near-universal compliance in the experimental evaluation. Participating plants recorded **20-30% lower particulate emissions** than plants under the status-quo regime, while achieving a given emissions level at **11-14% lower abatement cost**. The result supports a bounded inference: credible measurement can make a cap-and-trade instrument work in a lower-capacity setting. It does not prove that every pollutant, city or weakly monitored market will replicate Surat.

### 3. Transport

Transport controls operate through:

- avoiding unnecessary motorised trips through land-use planning;
- shifting trips to reliable public and non-motorised transport;
- cleaner fuels and vehicle technologies;
- inspection and maintenance;
- scrappage/retirement rules with equity safeguards;
- freight logistics and cleaner commercial fleets;
- road-dust and congestion management.

For this direction, N1 light goods vehicles have gross vehicle weight up to **3.5 tonnes** and the covered N2 light goods vehicles are above **3.5 tonnes and up to 7.5 tonnes**.

### 4. Construction and road dust

Controls include covering material, wheel washing, paved access, on-site barriers, mechanised sweeping where appropriate, prompt debris removal and enforcement tied to site conditions. Water sprinkling can suppress dust temporarily but may be wasteful or ineffective if used mechanically without source containment.

### 5. Household energy, waste and biomass

Clean household energy reduces both indoor and ambient exposure. Waste segregation, collection and disposal reduce open burning. Crop-residue policy needs machinery access, viable residue markets, altered incentives and agronomic alternatives; punishment alone ignores the narrow harvest window and farm economics.

**Residual:** subsidies can lock in weak technology, and exemptions can become permanent. Support must be conditional on measured performance.

### 1. Reactive response versus structural source reduction

GRAP and NCAP operate on different clocks. GRAP restricts activity after air quality reaches a severity trigger; it can reduce acute exposure but does not by itself alter vehicle-fleet composition, industrial fuel choice, farm-residue economics or construction practice. NCAP is the structural counterpart: it uses multi-year plans, source diagnosis, monitoring and reduction targets. The advanced conclusion is complementarity without substitution—an emergency shield cannot become evidence of source elimination, while a long-run plan still needs an episode-response mechanism.

### 2. CAQM as institutional design for a coordination failure

CAQM exists because Delhi-NCR pollution crosses municipal and state boundaries. A single city or SPCB cannot unilaterally control upwind agricultural burning, regional industry, freight or power-sector emissions. Statutory regional authority can align directions and data, but legal override alone cannot supply farmer alternatives, municipal capacity, finance or political trust.

**Institutional residual:** regional coordination must identify the actor responsible for each measure, publish burden-sharing and preserve review and accountability. Otherwise, a larger jurisdiction can merely relocate blame.

### 3. Season-aware source apportionment

Source apportionment must specify receptor, season, study period and method. Crop-residue burning may be important during a narrow harvest window without being the dominant year-round source. Vehicles, road and construction dust, industry, combustion and secondary aerosols can dominate in other periods. A fixed universal source-share claim is therefore analytically unsafe.

This is why blanket restrictions can misfire: the measure must target the source and precursor mix actually producing the episode or long-run concentration.

### 4. NCAP implementation and data limits

NCAP's target-and-plan architecture is stronger when city plans contain inventories, responsible agencies, finance and outcome milestones. Its recurring implementation risks are uneven monitoring density, changing station coverage, inconsistent data quality, variable municipal/SPCB capacity and the absence of one empowered regional coordinator in many airsheds.

Real-time AQI comparison also needs caution. A densely monitored city may reveal hotspots that a sparse network misses. Health-burden figures require named epidemiological evidence, while NAAQS values must be sourced to the current notification. Neither a dashboard nor an announced target establishes attainment.

### 5. Emissions inventory versus source apportionment

An **emissions inventory** estimates pollutant mass emitted by source categories using activity data and emission factors or direct measurements. **Source apportionment** estimates contributions to measured ambient pollution using receptor models, dispersion/chemical transport models or combined approaches.

They answer different questions:

- inventory: what was emitted, where and by whom?
- apportionment: what contributed to concentration at the receptor during the studied period?

### 6. Measurement, reporting and verification

MRV requires:

1. a defined indicator and baseline;
2. a method and frequency;
3. calibrated instruments and data validation;
4. responsible reporting entity;
5. independent or credible verification;
6. treatment of missing data and uncertainty;
7. public disclosure;
8. corrective consequences.

Technology does not eliminate governance. Automated systems can drift, fail or be manipulated.

### 7. Outcome hierarchy

| Claim | Evidence needed |
|---|---|
| "The city acted" | Budget and completed activity |
| "Sources complied" | Valid source measurements against conditions |
| "Emissions fell" | Comparable mass-emission estimate |
| "Ambient air improved" | Quality-assured, weather-normalised network trend |
| "Exposure fell" | Population-weighted or personal-exposure evidence |
| "Health/ecology improved" | Appropriate outcome study with lag/confounder control |

Do not jump from the first row to the last.

### 8. Counterfactual and attribution

Suppose PM10 falls after road sweeping. A causal claim still asks: did rain increase, economic activity fall, a monitor move, construction decline, fuel change, or upwind emissions alter? Useful designs include interrupted time series, matched cities, randomised or phased roll-outs, model-based weather normalisation and direct source measurement.

### 9. The difficult case of ozone

Particle controls can improve PM while ozone worsens if precursor chemistry and sector strategy are ignored. A single "air quality improved" claim can therefore hide pollutant substitution. Multi-pollutant evaluation is essential.

### 10. Urgency versus evaluation

**Objection:** demanding causal evaluation delays urgent action.

**Reply:** precaution can justify action before perfect evidence, especially for severe harm. Evaluation should accompany implementation so ineffective measures are corrected quickly.

**Residual:** not every intervention can be randomised, and health outcomes may take time. Use the strongest feasible design and disclose limitations.

### Examiner-ready verdict

The examiner-ready verdict is:

> India's clean-air architecture should be judged not by plans, funds, monitors or restrictions alone, but by a verified causal chain from compliance and emission reduction to weather-normalised ambient concentration, lower exposure and improved health/ecological outcomes.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** "What gets measured gets managed only when the measurement is connected to accountability." Discuss with reference to India's air-quality governance.

**Model:** India has expanded standards, monitors, AQI communication, source measurements and programme dashboards. Yet measurement improves governance only when the indicator matches the decision. India's next clean-air reform should therefore make public MRV and adaptive correction—not dashboard activity—the bridge between statutory standards, sector compliance and measured health protection.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker — 150 words

**Question:** Why must an air-quality regulator distinguish ambient concentration from emissions?

**Model answer:** Emissions are pollutant mass released by sources over time; ambient concentration is the amount present in outdoor air at a location and time. Between them lie atmospheric chemistry, transport, dilution, deposition and weather. Therefore, a compliant stack may coexist with poor ambient air because other sources, secondary aerosols or stagnant conditions dominate. Conversely, favourable wind may lower concentration without reducing emissions.

The distinction determines accountability. CPCB/SPCB source regulation needs valid emission standards, consent conditions and monitoring; NAAQS and AQI require representative ambient networks. Policy evaluation should connect both through inventories and source apportionment. Inputs such as control devices and activities such as inspections should be reported separately from verified emission reduction and weather-normalised ambient change.

A clean-air regime succeeds when source obligations collectively deliver ambient attainment and lower exposure, not when either stack compliance or a few favourable AQI days is treated as sufficient.

## Final 15-marker — 250 words

**Question:** Assess whether NCAP and GRAP together constitute an adequate response to urban air pollution in India.

**Model answer:** NCAP and GRAP address different time horizons. NCAP supplies a national, multi-year framework for non-attainment-city plans, inventories, source apportionment, monitoring and particulate-reduction targets. # COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Air-pollution causal spine

- Air pollution must be reconstructed as source → emission → transformation → ambient concentration → exposure → effect.
- Primary pollutants are emitted directly; secondary pollutants form through atmospheric reactions.
- PM2.5/PM10 are aerodynamic size fractions; composition and exposure modify toxicity.
- SO2, NOx, VOCs and NH3 are important precursor families.
- Weather changes concentration without necessarily changing emissions.

## Chemistry and meteorology

- NOx + VOCs + sunlight produce photochemical oxidants including ground-level ozone.
- SO2 and NOx oxidation produces acid deposition and secondary particles.
- NH3 helps form ammonium sulphate/nitrate PM.
- Low mixing height, inversion and weak wind favour accumulation.
- Rain washout is temporary and conditional; it is not source control.
- Use weather-normalised trends for policy evaluation.

## Health, ecology and justice

- Ambient concentration ≠ personal exposure ≠ dose ≠ health outcome.
- High-risk groups include children, older persons, people with disease and outdoor workers.
- Ozone affects lungs and vegetation; deposition can acidify/eutrophy ecosystems.
- Black carbon links combustion, health and climate/cryosphere effects.
- Cite precise mortality or yield estimates only with study, year and method.
- Uniform standards can coexist with unequal exposure.

## NAAQS and AQI

- 2009 NAAQS cover 12 pollutants.
- PM10 annual/24-hour: 60/100 µg/m³; PM2.5: 40/60.
- SO2 annual: 50 µg/m³ in industrial/residential/rural/other areas and 20 in notified ecologically sensitive areas; 24-hour: 80.
- NO2 annual: 40 µg/m³ in industrial/residential/rural/other areas and 30 in notified ecologically sensitive areas; 24-hour: 80.
- Ozone: 100 µg/m³ over 8 hours and 180 over 1 hour; CO: 2 mg/m³ over 8 hours and 4 over 1 hour.
- Lead annual/24-hour: 0.50/1.0 µg/m³; ammonia: 100/400 µg/m³.
- Annual-only values: benzene 5 µg/m³; benzo(a)pyrene 1 ng/m³; arsenic 6 ng/m³; nickel 20 ng/m³.
- AQI uses eight pollutants and six categories from Good to Severe.
- The 101-200 AQI category is **Moderately polluted**.
- AQI needs at least three pollutant sub-indices, including PM10 or PM2.5, with at least 16 valid hours for each calculated sub-index.
- Daily AQI uses 24-hour averages for PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, ammonia and lead, and 8-hour averages for CO and ozone.
- Overall AQI is driven by the highest qualifying sub-index after the data-sufficiency gate.
- NAAQS = benchmark; monitoring = evidence; AQI = communication.
- Ambient standards and source-emission standards are not interchangeable.
- Annual NAAQS calculation requires at least 104 site measurements; applicable short-period values use the 98% compliance rule, with no two consecutive monitoring-day exceedances.

## Institutions and law

- Air Act, 1981: prevention, control and abatement; Stockholm/Article 253 context.
- CPCB under section 16(2)(h): national planning, coordination, technical guidance and ambient-air-quality standards.
- SPCBs under section 17(1)(g): source-emission standards in consultation with CPCB, plus state programmes, consent, inspection, source monitoring and enforcement.
- Central Government under the Environment (Protection) Act/Rules: many industry/process source-emission standards.
- Consent is conditional permission, not demonstrated continuous compliance.
- CAQM: statutory regional body under the 2021 Act for NCR/adjoining areas.
- NGT adjudicates; MoEFCC frames/administers policy and programmes.
- Capacity, authority and political cooperation are separate governance variables.
- G.S.R. 757(E): Air Act Jan Vishwas changes effective 1 April 2024; G.S.R. 701(E): 2024 inquiry-and-penalty rules.
- G.S.R. 84(E): 2025 consent guidelines; G.S.R. 62(E): 2026 amendment.
- Current CTO remains valid until cancellation; a 5-25-year period concerns one-time fee payment.
- Blue covers essential environmental services for domestic-waste management; its earlier two-year extra-validity description does not override the current CTO-continuity rule.

## Programme architecture

- NCAP: structural, multi-year national framework launched in 2019.
- Current MoEFCC definition: annual exceedance of any notified NAAQS parameter consecutively for five years; the 130-city universe also includes identified million-plus cities.
- Target chronology: initial 20-30% particulate reduction by 2024 against 2017; revised up to 40% PM10 reduction or attainment of 60 µg/m³ by 2025-26.
- Official 7 September 2026 result: 108/130 cities improved against 2017-18; 72 exceeded 20% reduction, 26 exceeded 40%, and 14 met PM10 NAAQS.
- GRAP: reactive NCR episode response under the operative 21 November 2025 four-stage schedule.
- CAQM: response to cross-jurisdiction pollution; Supreme Court supervision in *M.C. Mehta* does not replace CAQM administration.
- Airshed boundaries follow transport and meteorology, not only administration.
- Clean-air plans need inventory, source apportionment, agencies, budgets, milestones and outcome review.

## Sector-control register

- Power/industry: cleaner fuel/process, efficiency, PM capture, SO2/NOx control, QA-assured CEMS.
- Surat ETS: 317 plants; mandatory CEMS; 20-30% lower particulate emissions and 11-14% lower abatement cost in the experimental comparison.
- Transport: avoid trips, shift mode, improve technology/maintenance, manage freight and dust.
- CAQM Direction No. 102 (19 August 2026) limits **new registrations**, not operation of the existing fleet. Delhi and the five high-density districts exclude new diesel/petrol/CNG N1/N2 registrations on the phased dates; remaining NCR districts exclude new diesel/petrol registration but continue to permit CNG. The direction's covered N2 LGVs are above 3.5 and up to 7.5 tonnes.
- Construction: contain, cover, clean access and remove debris; sprinkling alone is weak.
- Waste: service delivery and enforcement against open burning.
- Household energy: clean fuel/technology reduces indoor and ambient exposure.
- Agriculture: machinery, markets, agronomy and incentive reform; punishment alone is insufficient.
- Cloud seeding is episodic and meteorology-dependent.
- Equity support must be tied to verified cleaner performance.

## Outcome and answer-writing register

- Inventory asks what was emitted; apportionment asks what contributed at a receptor.
- Report separately: input, activity, compliance, emissions, ambient concentration, exposure and health/ecology.
- Use counterfactual reasoning; temporal sequence alone is not attribution.
- Monitor relocation, weather and economic change can distort trends.
- Use multi-pollutant evaluation to detect ozone/PM trade-offs.
- Precaution justifies action; evaluation enables correction.

## PYQ answer routes

- 2020 NCAP: features first, brief evaluation last.
- 2021 WHO AQGs: new guidance → 2005 comparison → NCAP reform.
- 2022 photochemical smog: formation → effects → mitigation → Gothenburg Protocol.
- Objective questions: resolve source-pollutant, precursor-product, guideline-standard and episodic-structural distinctions before considering options.
