*A waste system is a materials system: decisions at design and purchase determine what can later be repaired, separated and recovered.*

### 1. Why "collect and dump" is the wrong mental model

Waste does not begin at the bin. A product's material mix, durability, repairability, toxicity and packaging determine its end-of-life options. A multilayer pouch may be light and useful in distribution but difficult to separate economically. A modular electronic device may cost more initially yet permit component replacement and longer service life.

The central question is therefore not merely, "Where should waste go?" It is:

```text
How can society prevent material from becoming waste,
retain value for as long as possible,
and isolate unavoidable hazards safely?
```

### 2. The waste hierarchy

| Priority | Meaning | Example | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prevention | Avoid material use or toxicity | Eliminate unnecessary packaging | Cannot remove all essential packaging |
| Reduction | Use fewer resources per service | Lightweighting with adequate durability | Excessive thinning may reduce recyclability |
| Reuse | Use the same product or package again | Refillable rigid container | Needs return logistics and hygiene control |
| Repair/refurbishment | Extend product life | Replace a phone battery or refurbish a laptop | Safety and quality standards remain necessary |
| Recycling/composting | Recover material or nutrients | Recycling a clean sorted material; composting clean wet waste | Contamination and mixed materials lower yield |
| Other recovery | Recover energy or useful output | RDF co-processing for suitable residuals | Must not displace prevention or recycling |
| Disposal | Isolate final rejects | Engineered sanitary landfill | Land, leachate and methane burdens remain |

The hierarchy is not an absolute algorithm. Infection-risk waste, contaminated residues or unsafe batteries may require specialised treatment even when ordinary reuse appears preferable.

### 3. Linear and circular material flows

```text
LINEAR:   extract -> make -> use -> discard

CIRCULAR: design for durability
              -> use longer
              -> maintain / repair
              -> reuse / refurbish
              -> recover clean material
              -> return material to production
              -> safely isolate the diminishing residue
```

A circular economy is not "recycling everything." It seeks to slow loops through durability and reuse, narrow loops through resource efficiency, and close loops through recovery. Some material is dissipated, contaminated or technically uneconomic to recover; claims of a perfectly closed loop are therefore unsafe.

### 4. Segregation protects material value

Mixed waste converts recoverable streams into contaminated residue. Food waste soils paper; broken mercury lamps contaminate ordinary dry waste; lithium batteries create fire risk in compactors; biomedical waste exposes workers; construction debris overloads municipal collection.

> **Answer line:** Segregation is not a housekeeping ritual; it is the information architecture of resource recovery.

### 5. Lifecycle thinking and ecodesign

Lifecycle thinking evaluates impacts across raw-material extraction, production, transport, use and end-of-life. Ecodesign can improve:

- durability and repairability;
- modular replacement of parts;
- material identification;
- disassembly;
- avoidance of hazardous additives;
- compatibility with existing recycling;
- recycled-content use;
- refill or take-back systems.

The limit is burden shifting. A heavier reusable package may need many return cycles before it outperforms a lightweight disposable package. A biodegradable label is not proof that an item will safely degrade in every open environment.

### 6. Environmental-health chain

```text
MIXED / OPENLY DUMPED WASTE
   +-> leachate -> soil and groundwater risk
   +-> open burning -> particulate and toxic exposure
   +-> methane -> fire and climate risk
   +-> vectors -> local disease burden
   +-> sharp/toxic components -> worker injury
   +-> blocked drains -> urban flooding
```

The burdens are unequal. Waste facilities, dumps and informal processing often affect low-income communities and workers who consume least but bear concentrated exposure.

### UPSC integration

Use the hierarchy to organise any waste answer: **prevent -> segregate -> collect -> recover -> dispose residuals safely**. Then add actor responsibility, market viability, worker safety and verification.

### Revision notes

1. Waste management begins at product design, not at disposal.
2. Prevention and reduction rank above recycling.
3. Reuse retains product value; recycling usually retains only material value.
4. Refurbishment extends service life but needs safety and quality assurance.
5. Segregation preserves the identity and value of material streams.
6. Circular economy means slowing, narrowing and closing material loops.
7. Recovery is below recycling in the hierarchy.
8. Disposal should receive only unavoidable, treated residuals.
9. Lifecycle thinking prevents burden shifting between stages.
10. Environmental justice asks who receives convenience and who bears exposure.
11. A biodegradable claim is environment-specific, not a licence to litter.
12. Perfect circularity is impossible where materials dissipate or become contaminated.

### Mains application - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain why segregation at source is the foundation of both scientific waste management and a circular economy.

**Model (128 words):** Source segregation preserves the physical identity of materials before collection. When wet waste is separated, it can be composted or biomethanated; clean paper, metal, glass and suitable plastics retain recycling value; sanitary, hazardous and electronic items can enter safer specialised channels. Mixed collection instead contaminates recyclables, raises sorting costs, exposes workers and increases the reject fraction sent to dumps or combustion. Segregation also supplies reliable material-flow data, enabling local bodies to plan capacity and producers to discharge lifecycle obligations. Yet bins alone are insufficient: collection vehicles, transfer points and processing facilities must maintain separation, households need convenient service, and recyclers need stable offtake. Thus segregation is the information and quality-control layer connecting household behaviour, municipal logistics, recycling markets and safe residual disposal.

**Rubric (10):** hierarchy and definition 2 + material-quality mechanism 3 + health/worker link 2 + institutional chain 2 + qualified conclusion 1 = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a city with high collection coverage still have poor resource recovery?

**Model answer:** If separately generated streams are mixed during transport or processing, contamination destroys recycling and composting value; collection coverage measures removal, not circular recovery.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating collection, recycling and safe disposal as interchangeable performance indicators.

### Parent statute and delegated rule-making

The waste-management rules are **subordinate legislation under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**, not stand-alone Acts of Parliament. The parent Act supplies legal authority; Gazette-notified rules convert that authority into stream-specific definitions, duties, standards, registrations, reporting systems and enforcement mechanisms.

```text
ENVIRONMENT (PROTECTION) ACT, 1986
   +-> section 3: Central Government measures to protect and improve the environment
   +-> section 6: rule-making for environmental-pollution regulation
   +-> section 8: safeguards for handling hazardous substances
   +-> section 25: power to make rules
                      |
                      v
OFFICIAL GAZETTE NOTIFICATION
                      |
                      v
SWM / PWM / E-WASTE / BATTERY AND OTHER STREAM-SPECIFIC RULES
                      |
                      v
technical duties + standards + portals + authorisations + compliance
```

The exact enabling provisions are stated in each notification's preamble and can differ with the subject. For example, **G.S.R. 285(E), 2018** expressly invokes sections **6, 8 and 25** of the Act, read with rule 5(4) of the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986. This architecture matters for three reasons:

1. a rule has binding legal force but must remain within the authority delegated by the parent Act;
2. technical obligations can be amended by a fresh Gazette notification without enacting a new parent statute; and
3. the operative law must be read as the principal rules together with all commenced amendments, not from an old isolated notification.

### 1. Exact legal status on 3 October 2026

The **Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026**, notified by MoEFCC through **S.O. 388(E) dated 27 January 2026**, came into force on **1 April 2026**. They superseded the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016, while saving things already done or omitted under the earlier rules.

The 2016 Rules remain historically important for older PYQs, but the operative municipal-solid-waste framework on 3 October 2026 is the 2026 Rules.

### Historical-reading method for superseded rules

Older questions must be answered from the legal position applicable in their own year. Do not
substitute current definitions or rely on a familiar summary of a superseded instrument. Check
separately:

- the source-segregation architecture then in force;
- the territorial and institutional reach of the rules;
- whether facility-siting safeguards appeared in a schedule or other operative provision; and
- how authorised collection, transport, processing and disposal were connected.

A replacement framework may preserve the regulatory purpose while changing categories,
institutions or drafting. Each proposition must therefore be tested against the contemporaneous
text rather than inferred from the 2026 Rules.

### 2. Scope and exclusions

The 2026 Rules apply broadly to urban bodies and rural local bodies and to domestic, institutional, commercial and other non-residential generators within listed jurisdictions. They expressly do not absorb separately regulated industrial, hazardous, biomedical, e-waste, battery and radioactive waste.

| Included in ordinary SWM stream | Divert to separate regime |
|---|---|
| Wet household waste | Biomedical waste |
| Ordinary dry recyclables | E-waste |
| Sanitary waste | Waste batteries |
| Special-care domestic waste | Hazardous industrial waste |
| Horticulture waste | Radioactive waste |
| Residual municipal waste | Sector-specific C&D waste |

### 3. Four-stream segregation

Every waste generator must segregate and store waste at source into:

1. **wet waste**;
2. **dry waste**;
3. **sanitary waste**; and
4. **special care waste**.

Used sanitary waste must be securely wrapped and placed separately. Construction and demolition waste and horticulture waste must also be stored separately and handed into their proper channels.

### 4. Generator duties

The generator must prevent pollution, segregate, hand waste to authorised collectors or waste pickers, avoid littering/open burning/burying, pay prescribed user charges and comply with local directions. Event organisers, gated premises, institutions and other specified entities carry additional duties appropriate to their scale.

### 5. Bulk Waste Generator threshold

A listed establishment is a **Bulk Waste Generator (BWG)** if it satisfies at least one threshold:

| Test | Threshold |
|---|---:|
| Floor area | 20,000 square metres or above |
| Water consumption | 40,000 litres per day |
| Solid-waste generation | 100 kilograms per day |

BWGs must register through the centralised portal, arrange collection and handover of dry, sanitary and special-care streams, and process wet or horticulture waste in a decentralised manner where applicable.

### 6. Institutional architecture

```text
MoEFCC
  -> national rule-making and policy
CPCB
  -> guidelines + central portal + coordination + standards
SPCB/PCC
  -> authorisation, monitoring, inspection, compensation
State / UT government
  -> land, policy, coordination, capacity and review
Urban / rural local body
  -> service delivery, bye-laws, user charges, collection and facilities
Generator
  -> segregation, storage, handover and payment
Operator / authorised collector / waste picker
  -> maintain separated movement to processing
```

The chain demonstrates cooperative regulation: national standards do not collect a household's waste, while a municipality cannot independently create a national EPR or environmental standard.

### Why the 2026 replacement matters now

The replacement of the 2016 Rules by the **SWM Rules, 2026** is the dated legal-status
application. An answer written after 1 April 2026 should not present the 2016 framework as
currently operative. The analytical shift is from the earlier segregation-and-coverage model
towards urban-rural coverage, four-stream segregation, stronger bulk-generator duties, digital
monitoring and explicit environmental compensation.

### Strongest criticism, reply and residual

**Criticism:** More categories and portal duties can burden weak local bodies without changing ground reality.
**Reply:** Separate streams and digital records can improve safety, traceability and planning when backed by infrastructure and enforcement.
**Residual:** Legal precision is necessary but not sufficient; unfunded mandates and fragmented capacity can convert formal compliance into mixed collection.

### Mains application - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine whether the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026 convert municipal waste management from a sanitation function into a shared regulatory responsibility.

**Model (190 words):** The 2026 Rules retain sanitation as a local-body function but distribute responsibility across the waste chain. Generators must segregate wet, dry, sanitary and special-care waste and use authorised channels. Bulk Waste Generators are identified through floor-area, water-use or waste-generation thresholds and receive enhanced registration and decentralised-processing duties. Urban and rural local bodies must create collection, transfer, processing and disposal systems, frame bye-laws and recover user charges. SPCBs/PCCs oversee authorisation, compliance and environmental compensation, while CPCB supplies guidelines and centralised digital monitoring.

This design corrects the old tendency to treat every discarded material as a municipal burden. It links household conduct, institutional scale, processing capacity and regulatory oversight. However, shared responsibility can become diffused responsibility. Rural bodies and small municipalities may lack engineers, land, laboratories and contract-management capacity; portals cannot prove segregation or processing without physical inspection; and user charges can be inequitable if basic service is unreliable. The reform therefore succeeds only when fiscal transfers, common regional facilities, worker integration, service benchmarks and auditable material balances accompany legal duties. The Rules create a stronger responsibility chain, but implementation capacity determines whether it operates as one.

**Rubric (15):** legal transition 2 + actor-duty chain 4 + four-stream/BWG provisions 3 + capacity critique 3 + reforms 2 + verdict 1 = 15.

### Concept check

**Question:** Does the 2026 framework make CPCB responsible for door-to-door collection?

**Model answer:** No. CPCB supplies national guidance, standards, coordination and portal functions; urban and rural local bodies organise ordinary collection and service delivery.

**Misconception to avoid:** Confusing national regulatory oversight with municipal operations.

### 1. Collection is only the first operational test

Scientific management requires continuity of segregation. Separate bins followed by a common compactor merely hide mixing downstream. Route design, compartmentalised vehicles, transfer stations, weighbridges and processor receipts must preserve the material balance.

### 2. Wet waste

Clean wet waste can be composted or biomethanated. Decentralised treatment reduces transport of water-heavy material and can produce compost or biogas. It fails when feedstock contains plastic, glass or metals, when odour and leachate are unmanaged, or when there is no market for the output.

### 3. Dry waste and Material Recovery Facilities

A **Material Recovery Facility (MRF)** temporarily stores and sorts non-wet waste so recyclables can be transferred to authorised recyclers and non-recyclables to suitable processors. An MRF is not a licence for indefinite dumping; throughput, fire safety, worker protection and offtake contracts matter.

### 4. RDF and waste-to-energy boundary

The 2026 Rules create a fuel-substitution schedule for suitable segregated combustible fraction and RDF. The schedule begins at **at least 6% of fuel intake when the Rules take effect**, rises to **10% after three years**, and **15% after six years** for covered industrial units within specified distances. RDF grades intended for cement kilns have calorific-value specifications; material supplied to cement plants must reach at least **3,000 kcal/kg**.

> **Qualification:** Energy recovery is for suitable non-recyclable combustible residue. Burning mixed, wet or recyclable waste violates the hierarchy and can create poor combustion and emissions.

### Thermal conversion requires a process test

```text
SUITABLE PRE-PROCESSED FEEDSTOCK
        +-> oxygen-limited heating
        |      -> gas + liquid fuel fraction + char
        |
        +-> controlled-oxidant conversion at high temperature
               -> fuel gas + solid residue
                       |
                       +-> an ionised-gas variant can reach still higher temperatures
                           and vitrify part of the residue
```

Classify a thermal-conversion route by its feedstock, oxygen condition, useful output and
residual-control system. Suitable waste may yield fuel or energy, but performance still depends
on segregation, feed quality, gas cleaning, energy balance and safe handling of char, slag and
other residues. A thermal label does not make mixed-waste treatment automatically clean or
circular.

### 5. Landfill is a residual facility

Sanitary landfills isolate post-processing residuals and inert material. Open dumps are uncontrolled accumulations. Biomining excavates, screens and separates old dump material to recover fractions and reclaim land; it remediates a legacy failure but does not replace daily segregation.

### 6. Delhi and Indore: two governance narratives

| Dimension | Delhi | Indore |
|---|---|---|
| Central problem | Large legacy dumps and continuing metropolitan scale | Preventing mixed waste through disciplined service |
| Salient response | Biomining and remediation of Ghazipur, Bhalswa and Okhla | Door-to-door collection, source segregation and decentralised processing |
| Governance lesson | Corrective investment is necessary but costly and prolonged | Prevention needs citizen compliance and consistent municipal routines |
| Safe verdict | Remediation-in-progress, neither total success nor total failure | Strong prevention-oriented model, not automatically transferable without local adaptation |

The contrast is **legacy correction versus prevention-first design**, not "technology versus no technology." Indore also uses technology; Delhi also needs behaviour and collection reform.

### Federal and local capacity

Municipal performance depends on predictable finance, suitable land, technical staffing, enforceable contracts, regional facilities for smaller bodies and data that reconcile generation, collection, processing and disposal. A city may report high processing capacity while plants are underused, feedstock is contaminated or rejects return to dumps.

### Mains application - 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Compare prevention-first and remediation-first approaches to urban solid-waste management, using Indore and Delhi as illustrations.

**Model (220 words):** Prevention-first management seeks to stop mixed waste and legacy dumps from arising. Indore illustrates this logic through routine door-to-door collection, source segregation, citizen participation and differentiated processing. Clean wet waste can enter biological treatment, dry waste can reach sorting and recycling, and only residuals require disposal. Its principal strength is avoided contamination and lower future remediation liability.

Delhi illustrates remediation-first necessity. Ghazipur, Bhalswa and Okhla accumulated mixed waste over decades. Biomining can screen legacy deposits, recover usable fractions and reclaim land, but it is capital-intensive, creates large secondary flows and proceeds while fresh waste still requires daily management. It is therefore a recovery from historical system failure, not a substitute for prevention.

The comparison should not become a moral binary. Metropolitan scale, land constraints, institutional fragmentation and inherited waste differ. Delhi's remediation can register real gains, while Indore's model depends on sustained household compliance, collection reliability and output markets. A national strategy needs both: enforce four-stream segregation and processor accountability to prevent new dumps; finance time-bound biomining, leachate control and landfill-fire management for old dumps; integrate waste workers; and publish reconciled mass-balance data. Prevention is cheaper and higher in the hierarchy, but justice requires remediation where communities already bear legacy exposure.

**Rubric (20):** define both approaches 3 + Indore mechanism 4 + Delhi mechanism 4 + comparison/qualification 4 + integrated reforms 3 + judgement 2 = 20.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does biomining not prove that a city's waste system has become circular?

**Model answer:** Biomining treats an accumulated stock of mixed legacy waste; circularity requires prevention, continuing source segregation, viable recovery and minimal new residual disposal.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating land reclaimed from an old dump with successful management of current daily flows.

### 2. What G.S.R. 285(E), 2018 actually changed

The amendment made five distinct changes:

1. inserted **alternate use**, meaning a beneficial use different from the material's originally conceived purpose that promotes resource efficiency;
2. inserted **energy recovery**, covering conversion of waste into usable heat, electricity or fuel through specified processes;
3. in Rule 9(3), replaced the words **"non-recyclable multilayered plastic if any"** with **"multi-layered plastic which is non-recyclable or non-energy recoverable or with no alternate use"**;
4. revised Rule 13(2), sending producer or brand-owner registration to the concerned SPCB/PCC when operating in one or two States/Union territories and to CPCB when operating in more than two; and
5. **omitted Rule 15**.

Rule 15 of the 2016 principal rules had been titled **"Explicit pricing of carry bags"**. It required willing shopkeepers and street vendors to register with the local body under a fee-based arrangement and display that plastic carry bags were supplied on payment. Its deletion means that this original Rule 15 mechanism must not be quoted as a continuing provision after the 2018 amendment.

The multilayered-plastic change should be stated through the exact substituted criteria. Calling it simply a "narrowing" or "broadening" hides the legal text and its three separate tests: recyclability, energy recoverability and alternate use.

### 3. Identified single-use plastic prohibition

From **1 July 2022**, manufacture, import, stocking, distribution, sale and use were prohibited for specified items including:

- ear buds with plastic sticks;
- plastic sticks for balloons, plastic flags, candy and ice-cream sticks;
- polystyrene/thermocol for decoration;
- plates, cups, glasses, forks, spoons, knives, straws, trays and stirrers;
- wrapping or packing films around sweet boxes, invitation cards and cigarette packets;
- plastic or PVC banners below 100 microns.

This is an item-specific prohibition. Plastic packaging outside the list remains regulated through standards, collection and EPR.

### 4. Carry-bag thickness

The 2021 amendment raised the minimum thickness to **75 microns from 30 September 2021** and **120 microns from 31 December 2022**. The logic is to discourage very thin, low-value bags and improve collectability, but thickness alone does not guarantee reuse or recycling.

### 5. Packaging categories after the 2024 amendment

| Category | Coverage |
|---|---|
| I | Rigid plastic packaging |
| II | Flexible single-layer or all-plastic multilayer packaging, sheets, covers, bags, sachets and pouches |
| III | Multilayer packaging with at least one plastic layer and at least one non-plastic material layer |
| IV | Packaging, carry bags and commodities made of compostable plastic |
| V | Packaging, carry bags and commodities made of biodegradable plastic |

The 2024 definition requires biodegradable plastic, other than compostable plastic, to degrade through biological processes in specified environments without leaving harmful microplastic, visible/distinguishable or toxic residue.

### Polymer literacy: products do not reveal composition by appearance

| Evidence step | General material principle | Waste-policy implication |
|---|---|---|
| Identify the article | Trade names, familiar labels and visual appearance may not disclose formulation. | Begin with verified composition rather than assumption. |
| Characterise the formulation | Additives, fillers, coatings and blended constituents can alter the behaviour of the base material. | Treat the finished article, not an idealised resin, as the regulatory object. |
| Test the claimed function | Performance depends on the service condition and the applicable technical standard. | Avoid universal claims drawn only from a material-family name. |
| Trace the lifecycle | Collection conditions, contamination, available processing and residual handling shape the practical route. | A theoretical material property does not prove a real-world recovery outcome. |
| Separate the findings | Composition, environmental effect and legal classification answer different questions. | Establish each proposition independently before drawing a policy conclusion. |

Material identification and legal classification are separate steps. Discovering polymer content
does not by itself prove that an article is on the identified single-use-plastic prohibition list
or that every component follows the same EPR stream.

### 6. Marking and traceability

The 2023 changes require specified plastic packaging to carry the name and portal-generated registration certificate number of the producer, importer or brand owner. For listed rigid and multilayer packaging this requirement applied from **1 July 2024**; specified compostable-plastic packaging carried a later **1 January 2025** trigger and certificate particulars.

Marking connects a market item to a registered obligated entity. It does not prove that the particular package was collected or recycled.

### 7. What the 2025 and 2026 amendments changed

**G.S.R. 73(E), 23 January 2025** inserted Rule 11(1A). From **1 July 2025**, a
producer, importer or brand owner may provide the information required by Rule 11(1)
through:

1. a barcode or QR code printed on the packaging;
2. a product-information brochure; or
3. a unique number issued under another law where Rule 11 information is fulfilled before
   that number is issued.

The entity must inform CPCB of the chosen route; CPCB must publish the relevant list on its
website and update it quarterly. The amendment also inserted **Rule 19**, linking
contravention to the penalty under section 15 of the Environment (Protection) Act.

**G.S.R. 237(E), 31 March 2026** is the latest located amendment at the status cut-off. It:

- recast definitions of end-of-life disposal, recycling and plastic-waste processor, and
  added definitions for registered environment auditor, reuse and seller;
- required recycled plastic packaging/commodities to conform to **IS 14534:2023** and
  applicable food-contact marking/labelling;
- assigned specified enforcement functions to local bodies, Gram Panchayats and
  district-level Panchayats and revised the State-Level Monitoring Committee;
- allowed designated agencies or registered environment auditors in the relevant verification
  route; and
- substituted the recycled-content and Category-I reuse schedules, including the three
  size/use-specific reuse rows taught in Lesson 5.

These are amendments to the 2016 principal Rules. They do not create a new standalone
Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2026 instrument.

### 8. Institutions

Local bodies manage collection systems and enforce prohibited-item controls in urban areas; Panchayats have corresponding rural responsibilities. CPCB operates national registration/EPR systems and guidelines. SPCBs/PCCs enforce within their jurisdictions and regulate processors.

### 1. What EPR changes

Extended Producer Responsibility shifts part of post-consumer management upstream to entities that introduce packaging into the market. It does not abolish municipal responsibility; rather, it adds financing, design and recovery obligations that municipalities alone cannot discharge.

### 2. Obligated entities

The amended framework covers producers, importers, brand owners, plastic-waste processors, manufacturers/importers of plastic raw material, and manufacturers of compostable or biodegradable plastic items. The 2024 amendment allocates certain micro and small producer EPR quantities to their raw-material suppliers while retaining specified declarations and recycled-content duties.

### 3. Main obligation bundle

The Guidelines cover:

1. reuse;
2. recycling;
3. use of recycled plastic content; and
4. end-of-life disposal.

The general category-wise EPR target rose from **25% in 2021-22**, to **70% in 2022-23**, and **100% from 2023-24** of the calculated eligible quantity. This does not mean 100% material-to-material recycling: the obligation bundle distinguishes recycling, reuse and permitted treatment of non-recyclable residue.

### 4. Minimum recycling level applicable in 2026-27

| Packaging category | Minimum recycling as % of EPR target in 2026-27 |
|---|---:|
| I - rigid | 70% |
| II - flexible all-plastic | 50% |
| III - plastic/non-plastic multilayer | 50% |
| IV - compostable | 70% through industrial composting |

The notified schedule rises further in 2027-28. A percentage must always be named with its denominator: it may be a share of eligible quantity, EPR target or manufactured plastic, which are not interchangeable.

### 5. Recycled-content obligation applicable in 2026-27

| Category | Required recycled content |
|---|---:|
| I | 40% |
| II | 10% |
| III | 5% |

Where a law or standard prohibits the use of recycled plastic content, the corresponding recycled-content target does not apply; the producer, importer or brand owner must report the supporting law or standard annually. This is statutory inapplicability, not case-specific relief or compliance granted through a CPCB certificate.

### 6. Reuse obligation for rigid packaging

G.S.R. 237(E), 2026 divides the brand owner's Category-I rigid-packaging reuse obligation
by **both size and use**:

| Category-I rigid packaging placed on the market | Minimum reuse in 2026-27 |
|---|---:|
| volume/weight **>=0.9 but <4.9 litres/kg** | **15%** |
| volume/weight **>=4.9 litres/kg used for drinking-water packaging** | **75%** |
| volume/weight **>=4.9 litres/kg used for products other than drinking water** | **10%** |

The **75%** figure therefore applies only to large drinking-water packaging. It must not be
generalised to every container of 4.9 litres/kg or above. A large edible-oil, detergent or
other non-water container falls in the **10%** row for 2026-27, while a one-litre Category-I
container falls in the **15%** row.

The table does not override another law or mandatory standard that prohibits reuse for a
particular application. A brand owner claiming such an exemption must provide the applicable
legal or standards basis in its annual return. The amendment also permits the unfulfilled
2025-26 reuse target to be carried forward for up to three consecutive years from 2026-27,
with at least one-third of the carried shortfall fulfilled in each year until cleared.

### 7. Certificates and compliance accounting

Registered plastic-waste processors generate the basis for EPR certificates according to eligible processing recorded under the Guidelines. Producers, importers and brand owners may acquire and use category-compatible certificates to meet quantified obligations. The portal adjusts the certificate against liability, while surplus and carry-forward treatment follows the notified rules. This separates the producer's legal obligation from a requirement to recover only its own branded package.

### 8. Collection and recycling markets

Low-value flexible packaging often costs more to collect and sort than the recovered resin is worth. The policy response combines producer finance, aggregation, long-term offtake, recycled-content demand and support for collection networks.

### 9. What the portal record connects

Entity registration identifies the obligated producer, importer, brand owner or processor. Category-wise declarations establish liability; processor records support certificate generation; certificate transfer and retirement adjust the obligation. The system therefore connects legal actors and quantities even though it is not a product-by-product return system.

### 1. Legal status

The **E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022**, notified through **G.S.R. 801(E) on 2 November 2022**, came into force on **1 April 2023**.

They were amended by:

- **G.S.R. 61(E), 30 January 2023**, effective 1 April 2023;
- **G.S.R. 534(E), 24 July 2023**, effective on Gazette publication, including refrigerant-management duties; and
- **G.S.R. 164(E), 8 March 2024**, effective on publication, clarifying dismantlers, permitting limited return-filing relaxation and enabling regulated certificate-exchange platforms.

As on 3 October 2026, these were the latest operative e-waste amendments in the official rule set.

### Historical-regime and assurance-standard method

EPR obligations have evolved through successive waste-specific instruments. A historical
question should therefore identify the date, regulated stream and legal function of each
instrument, distinguishing the first creation of a producer obligation from later redesign of
targets, registration, certificates and institutional roles.

Named industry codes must also be separated from statutory compliance accounting. Classify each
tool by its issuing body, covered actors, regulated activity and legal effect rather than
inferring its sector from an acronym or label. A voluntary assurance framework may evaluate
operational practices, whereas a statutory EPR certificate adjusts a quantified producer
obligation under the applicable rules.

### 2. Scope and exclusions

The Rules apply to manufacturers, producers, refurbishers, dismantlers and recyclers dealing with listed electrical and electronic equipment, components, consumables, parts and spares. Waste batteries, plastic packaging, micro-enterprises as specified, and radioactive waste are separately treated under the applicable law.

### 3. EPR target

For established producers, the recycling target is:

| Financial year | Target by weight of equipment placed in market in the relevant historical year |
|---|---:|
| 2023-24 | 60% |
| 2024-25 | 60% |
| 2025-26 | 70% |
| 2026-27 | 70% |
| 2027-28 onward | 80% |

New producers follow Schedule IV until their years of operation equal the product's average life. Importers of used equipment carry 100% end-of-life EPR if the material is not re-exported. Solar photovoltaic modules, panels and cells have a distinct storage/recovery path and are not simply inserted into the ordinary target table.

### 4. Producer

The producer's EPR remains legally its own even if collection centres, producer-responsibility organisations or other third parties assist. Producers meet obligations through online purchase of EPR certificates from registered recyclers, with portal cross-checking and environmental audit.

### 5. Recycler

A recycler must register, follow CPCB standards, record collection/dismantling/recycling and onward transfers, send residues to authorised treatment-storage-disposal facilities, file returns and account for non-recycled quantities. CPCB generates certificates in favour of registered recyclers according to eligible output and conversion factors.

### 6. Dismantler

The 2024 amendment defines a dismantler as a person or entity dismantling used equipment/components according to CPCB guidelines. A dismantler can support a recycler, but dismantled material must flow to a registered recycler and records must be maintained. Dismantling is not equivalent to final recycling.

### 7. Refurbisher

A refurbisher registers, channels process waste to a registered recycler, meets applicable product standards and files returns. A refurbishing certificate defers producer liability for the extended life of the product. On expiry, only **75% of the deferred quantity** is added back for recycling, but final EPR extinguishment occurs only through registered recycling and a recycling certificate.

### 8. The 2024 certificate platform amendment

The Central Government may establish platforms for exchange or transfer of EPR certificates under CPCB guidelines and regulation. CPCB fixes highest and lowest exchange prices by reference to environmental compensation. This formalises a market but heightens the need for audit.

**Mains prompt:** Explain how the E-Waste (Management) Rules distinguish recycling from refurbishment.

**Model (139 words):** Recycling and refurbishment perform different lifecycle functions. ### 1. Why the informal sector cannot be treated as peripheral

Waste pickers, itinerant buyers, scrap dealers and small aggregators provide collection density and material knowledge that formal systems often lack. They recover value from dispersed waste and reduce municipal burdens. Their contribution is economic infrastructure, not merely charity.

### 2. Why informality is not automatically circular

Unsafe e-waste operations may use open burning, crude heating, acid leaching and unprotected dismantling. Workers can face sharp injuries, smoke, heavy metals and persistent chemical exposure; residues contaminate neighbourhood air, soil and water. High collection efficiency does not excuse unsafe processing.

### 3. Official integration hook

Under the E-Waste Rules, State/UT labour authorities are assigned roles in recognition and registration of workers, assistance in forming groups and dismantling facilities, skill development, and annual safety and health monitoring. Local bodies must separate e-waste found in municipal waste and channel it to registered recyclers or refurbishers.

The legal direction is therefore integration and safer transition, not simple displacement.

|---|---|
| No legal identity | Worker registration and portable identity |
| Unstable price | Transparent floor/service payments and producer-financed collection |

| Informal-to-formal break | Authorised aggregation centres linked to recyclers |

Three questions improve analysis:

1. **Distribution:** who receives products and who receives pollution?
2. **Procedure:** do workers and host communities influence facility siting and contracts?
3. **Recognition:** are waste workers treated as service providers with knowledge and rights?

Justice also has an intergenerational dimension. Toxic residues and contaminated sites transfer costs to people who did not benefit from the original consumption.

- Plastic waste: prevent open burning and fumes; control washing effluent.
- E-waste: controlled dismantling, ventilation, chemical management and residue disposal.
- Batteries: fire prevention, electrical isolation and chemistry-specific handling.
- Biomedical waste: no mixing with ordinary municipal material.

One discarded product can contain several regulated components. An electric vehicle has electronics, a traction battery, plastics, tyres and metal. The correct approach is component-wise routing, not forcing the entire object into whichever rule name appears most familiar.

### 2. Battery waste


### 3. Construction and demolition waste

The **Environment (Construction and Demolition) Waste Management Rules, 2025**, notified through **G.S.R. 219(E) on 2 April 2025**, commenced on **1 April 2026** and superseded the 2016 C&D rules. They apply to construction, demolition, remodelling, renovation and repair, subject to stated exclusions, and introduce EPR, environmental compensation and centralised monitoring for this stream.

The SWM Rules require generators to keep C&D waste separate; they do not convert rubble into ordinary dry waste.

### 4. Biomedical waste

Infectious, anatomical, contaminated recyclable, sharps and pharmaceutical waste require the colour-coded and treatment architecture of the **Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016, as amended**. Mixing it with municipal dry waste endangers collectors and destroys safe recycling conditions.

### 5. Hazardous and other wastes

Industrial hazardous residues and transboundary movement are governed by the **Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016, as amended**, including the official **G.S.R. 177(E) amendment dated 12 March 2024**. This regime also connects India to Basel Convention controls.

Household "special care" waste under municipal rules should not be confused with the complete industrial hazardous-waste classification.

### 6. Basel interface

The Basel Convention controls transboundary movement and environmentally sound management of hazardous and other wastes. It is relevant to e-waste and certain plastic-waste movements, but it does not replace domestic EPR, collection or recycler duties.

### 8. End-of-life vehicles: one object, several material regimes

The **Environment Protection (End-of-Life Vehicles) Rules, 2025**, notified through **S.O. 98(E) on 6 January 2025**, came into force on **1 April 2025**. Vehicle producers carry annual EPR scrapping obligations; producers register with CPCB, while bulk consumers and Registered Vehicle Scrapping Facilities (RVSFs) register with the relevant SPCB/PCC. Owners channel an end-of-life vehicle through a producer collection point, designated sales outlet or RVSF.

An RVSF depollutes, dismantles, segregates and scraps the vehicle, supports portal-based certificates and sends recovered material to authorised recyclers. The vehicle is not one undifferentiated waste stream:

```text
ELV at RVSF
  +-> steel / non-ferrous metal -> authorised material recovery
  +-> battery -> Battery Waste Management Rules
  +-> electronic equipment -> E-Waste Rules
  +-> plastic parts -> Plastic Waste Management Rules
  +-> oils / hazardous residue -> Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules
  +-> final non-recyclable residue -> authorised treatment/disposal
```

EPR proves a producer obligation for vehicle scrapping; it does not merge component-specific safety rules.

### 9. Contaminated sites: governing the historic stock

The **Environment Protection (Management of Contaminated Sites) Rules, 2025**, notified through **S.O. 3401(E) on 24 July 2025**, commenced on Gazette publication. They address land affected by hazardous contaminants above prescribed response levels—a historic **stock** problem, unlike EPR's management of continuing material **flows**.

The pathway is:

```text
suspected site
  -> preliminary assessment
  -> probable site / detailed assessment
  -> declaration as contaminated site
  -> identify responsible person and liability
  -> risk-based remediation plan
  -> remediation to site-specific level
  -> completion order + post-remediation monitoring
```

SPCBs/PCCs lead identification, liability and remediation action; CPCB maintains the central portal and the Central Remediation Committee reviews the framework. An **orphan site** is one where the responsible person cannot be traced. The Rules permit preliminary and detailed assessment funding, to the extent feasible, initially in whole or part from the **Environmental Relief Fund** established under the Public Liability Insurance Act, alongside government sharing. If a responsible person is identified, public expenditure is recoverable; orphan-site assessment, planning and remediation follow the prescribed Central–State funding ratios. The Fund is therefore a financing bridge and public backstop, not a release from polluter-pays liability.

| EPR stream | Lifecycle responsibility and material route | Scope distinction |

### 2. Ten Circular Economy Action Plan categories

The ten action-plan categories are a wider policy inventory than the eight legally named EPR streams:

| Category | Circular-economy task |
|---|---|
| **Lithium-ion batteries** | Recover critical minerals, improve safe collection and reduce dependence on virgin extraction. |
| **E-waste** | Extend product life, formalise dismantling and recover metals through authorised recycling. |

An action plan can coordinate ministries, standards, markets and technology even where a dedicated EPR rule is absent or differently scoped. Therefore **ten action plans ≠ eight EPR rules**.

### 3. The market problem

Virgin material may be cheaper or more predictable than recycled material because environmental damage is externalised, quality varies and collection costs are high. A recycler cannot buy expensive mixed waste, produce uncertain output and survive without demand.

Industrial by-products show why a secondary-material market needs both technical evidence and
standards. Ashes, slags, gypsum-rich residues and other mineral by-products differ in chemistry,
particle size, expansion behaviour, alkalinity and contaminant mobility. A proposed secondary use
therefore needs representative sampling, material characterisation, suitable pre-treatment,
compliance with the relevant product standard and evidence of long-term performance. Approval for
one material and service condition cannot be transferred automatically to another.

This is controlled utilisation, not permission for dumping. Quality assurance, leachability,
grading, exposure conditions and long-term performance must be checked for the particular
material and application.

### 4. Demand-side instruments

- mandatory recycled content;
- public procurement of compliant recycled products;
- product standards for secondary material;
- long-term industry offtake;
- consumer information where verifiable.

Demand matters because collection targets without output markets create stockpiles.

### 5. Supply-side instruments

- source segregation;
- convenient return systems;
- deposit-refund where suitable;
- MRFs and aggregation centres;
- EPR finance;
- concessional finance for authorised processing;
- shared regional facilities.

### 6. Ecodesign and fee modulation

EPR can be improved by charging higher fees for packaging or products that are hard to collect, toxic, inseparable or incompatible with existing recycling, while rewarding durability, repairability, standardised parts and safe recycled content.

This is stronger than a uniform per-kilogram obligation because one kilogram of clear mono-material packaging is not equivalent to one kilogram of contaminated multilayer packaging.

### 7. Repair and right-to-repair logic

Longer product life can reduce material throughput, but repair must preserve electrical safety, cybersecurity, warranties and availability of genuine information/parts. Planned obsolescence and non-replaceable batteries can shift costs to consumers and waste systems.

### 8. Reform design matrix

| Failure | Instrument | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Products difficult to repair | ecodesign/repairability standards | safety and warranty rules |
| Low-value collection | EPR service payment | worker inclusion and transparent weighing |
| Weak recycled-material demand | recycled-content mandate/procurement | quality standards |
| Small local-body capacity | regional facilities | accountable inter-municipal contracts |
| Legacy contamination | remediation finance | polluter pays plus public backstop for orphan sites |

### 9. Qualified circular-economy verdict

Circularity should reduce virgin extraction and pollution, not merely increase the number of transactions labelled "recycling." Outcome indicators should include virgin-material displacement, actual yield, product longevity, safe residue disposal and worker health.

The Core answer is complete once it explains hierarchy, the three rule systems, actor duties, EPR, worker integration and reform. The following refinements improve evaluation.

### 2. Verification is the central EPR problem

Certificate markets separate the obligated producer from the processor. This lowers transaction costs but creates principal-agent and information problems. A regulator must verify not just documents but the causal chain from waste receipt to recovered output and safe residue.

The certificate is credible only when the evidence chain establishes that:

- input waste existed and was lawfully received;
- the processor had plausible installed and operating capacity;
- conversion factors and recovery yield were technically possible;
- the same quantity was not counted twice;
- recovered output entered a legitimate material market; and
- non-recycled residue reached an authorised destination.

Use a material balance:

```text
opening stock
+ waste received
- recovered output
- authorised residue disposal
- closing stock
= unexplained difference, if any
```

This is the Advanced distinction between a **compliance record** and a **verified environmental outcome**. Registration numbers identify actors and certificates adjust liability; physical reconciliation tests whether the recorded claim corresponds to material movement.

### 3. Risk-based audit

Inspection resources should concentrate on:

- certificates far above plausible installed capacity;
- abrupt output spikes;
- identical invoices or transport records;
- implausible conversion yields;
- repeated cross-state transfers;
- high stock with low energy or water use;
- processors generating certificates but little residue;
- related-party trading.

Random audit remains necessary because a purely predictable system can be gamed.

### 4. Federal enforcement gap

CPCB can build portals and issue guidance, but SPCBs/PCCs conduct much physical oversight. Their staffing, laboratories, travel budgets and prosecution capacity vary. Local bodies control collection contracts and land. Data integration must therefore preserve clear accountability rather than centralise information while diffusing action.

### 5. Environmental compensation

Compensation operationalises polluter pays but should not become a routine fee for continuing violation. It needs:

- transparent calculation;
- recovery mechanisms;
- escalation for repetition;
- restoration linkage;
- prosecution or closure where harm persists.

| Waste received | input recorded | material recovered |
| Certificate generated | eligible claim recorded | closed-loop use |
| Certificate purchased | liability adjusted | producer's own waste recycled |
| Material sold | output transaction | displacement of virgin material |

### 7. Outcome-based dashboard

A stronger dashboard would publish:

- generation estimates with method and uncertainty;
- separately collected quantity;
- processor-wise capacity and utilisation;
- verified recovery yield;
- residue and authorised disposal;
- fire, accident and worker-health indicators;
- recycled-content uptake;
- legacy stock remediated;
- enforcement orders and compensation recovered.

**Criticism:** Extensive verification destroys the cost advantage of market-based EPR.
**Reply:** Risk-based rather than universal audit protects integrity while retaining flexibility.
**Residual:** Some physical uncertainty is unavoidable; claims should use confidence ranges and transparent methods rather than false precision.

### 2019 Civil Services (Preliminary), GS-I, Question 26

> **In the context of which one of the following are the terms 'pyrolysis and plasma gasification' mentioned?**
>
> (a) Extraction of rare earth elements
> (b) Natural gas extraction technologies
> (c) Hydrogen fuel-based automobiles
> (d) Waste-to-energy technologies

#### Post-question teaching note

Pyrolysis heats suitable waste with little or no oxygen and may yield gas, oil and char.
Gasification uses a controlled oxidant at high temperature to produce synthesis gas and solid
residue; plasma gasification is a very-high-temperature variant using ionised gas. These are
thermal waste-conversion routes, but their environmental performance still depends on feedstock,
gas cleaning, energy balance and safe residue management.

**Question:** A municipality sends clean recyclable plastic directly to a waste-to-energy plant. What is the conceptual error?

## Check 4 - e-waste

**Question:** Why is dismantling not the same as recycling?

**Model:** Dismantling separates components; recycling performs material recovery and supports final EPR discharge.

## Check 5 - justice

**Question:** What does a just formalisation policy preserve and what does it eliminate?

**Model:** It preserves collection livelihoods, knowledge and reach while eliminating unsafe processing and unprotected exposure.

**Model:** Implausibly low residue can indicate overstated recovery or unreported disposal; recovery claims require a complete material balance.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Differentiate product prohibition, design standard and Extended Producer Responsibility as plastic-policy instruments.

**Model (143 words):** A product prohibition removes a specified item from manufacture, import, stocking, sale and use; India's identified single-use-plastic list is the example. A design standard changes a product characteristic without prohibiting the category, as with minimum carry-bag thickness. Extended Producer Responsibility retains market access but imposes lifecycle duties on producers, importers and brand owners through registration, reuse, recycling, recycled-content and residual-treatment obligations. The instruments solve different problems: prohibition addresses dispensable high-litter items, design standards improve durability or recoverability, and EPR internalises collection and processing costs. None is sufficient alone. Prohibition needs alternatives and surveillance; a thicker bag can still be littered; and EPR certificates can become paper compliance without physical audit. A coherent policy uses each instrument according to utility, hazard, recyclability and enforcement capacity.

**Rubric (10):** three precise definitions 3 + distinct examples 2 + problem-instrument fit 2 + limitations 2 + synthesis 1 = 10.

## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Analyse the role of Urban Local Bodies in an EPR-dominated waste-governance system.

**Model (203 words):** EPR does not replace Urban Local Bodies. ULBs still organise household collection, preserve segregation, operate or contract transfer and processing facilities, enforce bye-laws, manage user charges and prevent littering or burning. They also encounter e-waste and plastic leakage before producer systems do, making them indispensable to last-mile recovery.

EPR changes the financing and accountability environment. Producers and brand owners must support end-of-life management, while registered processors and certificates can create national recovery markets. ULBs can contract waste-picker organisations, provide segregated material to authorised facilities and receive transparent service payments for collection attributable to obligated products. Portal data can improve planning if reconciled with municipal weighbridge and route records.

Tension arises where producer systems cherry-pick valuable material while municipalities retain costly low-value residue, or where parallel collection confuses households. Weak contract capacity and uncertain revenue can deepen inequality between cities. Reform requires standard cost-sharing agreements, interoperable data, differentiated EPR finance for difficult materials, regional facilities, service-level disclosure and protection of worker livelihoods. ULBs remain the territorial service backbone; EPR supplies upstream responsibility and finance. The system works only when the two are designed as complements.

**Rubric (15):** continuing ULB duties 4 + EPR interaction 3 + conflict analysis 3 + institutional reforms 3 + balanced conclusion 2 = 15.

## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** India's waste laws increasingly use digital portals and tradable certificates. Critically examine whether this transition can deliver environmental and social outcomes.

**Model (231 words):** Digital portals make dispersed obligations administratively possible. They register producers and processors, calculate liabilities, record certificate transfers, cross-check returns and create a national trail across state borders. Tradable certificates add flexibility: recovery can occur through registered processors rather than requiring every producer to build its own chain.

Yet digitisation can certify documents more easily than physical outcomes. False invoices, overstated capacity, duplicate waste, implausible yields and unreported residue can generate "paper recycling." Large firms may dominate platforms while waste pickers who supply collection remain outside formal value chains. Uneven SPCB staffing and municipal data quality further weaken physical verification.

The solution is regulated digitalisation. Portals should incorporate processor-capacity registers, material-balance and conversion-factor checks, anomaly detection, tax/transport reconciliation and transparent certificate retirement. Risk-based and random inspections must verify stock, machinery, output and residue. Recycled-content standards and procurement should ensure that recovered material has a real market. Producer finance should pay authorised collection and worker organisations, with registration, safety protection and grievance redress. Public dashboards should distinguish registration, reported throughput, certificate generation, safe disposal and actual environmental outcomes.

Thus portals and certificates are enabling infrastructure, not self-executing regulation. Their legitimacy depends on auditable material flow, capable federal enforcement and a just transition for workers.

**Rubric (20):** digital/certificate gains 4 + integrity risks 5 + social/federal critique 3 + verification design 4 + market/worker reforms 2 + reasoned verdict 2 = 20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Material-flow fundamentals

- Waste begins at design; disposal is the final stage, not the governing idea.
- Hierarchy: prevent -> reduce -> reuse -> repair/refurbish -> recycle/compost -> recover -> dispose.
- Segregation preserves material identity, worker safety and processing value.
- Circular economy slows, narrows and closes loops; it is not synonymous with recycling.
- Lifecycle analysis must check burden shifting across extraction, use and end-of-life.

## Parent statute and rule-making architecture

- The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 is the parent statute; the waste rules are binding subordinate legislation made through delegated rule-making.
- Section 3 empowers Central Government environmental-protection measures; section 6 supports pollution-control rules; section 8 governs hazardous-substance safeguards; section 25 supplies rule-making power.
- The exact notification preamble controls which enabling provisions are invoked; G.S.R. 285(E), 2018 cites sections 6, 8 and 25 with rule 5(4) of the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986.
- Principal rules must be read with all commenced Gazette amendments; an old principal-rule clause may have been substituted or omitted.
- Delegated legislation allows technical updating without a new Act, but a rule must remain within the parent Act's authority.

## Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026

- **S.O. 388(E), 27 January 2026; effective 1 April 2026.**
- Superseded SWM Rules, 2016 with savings for past acts/omissions.
- Applies to urban and rural local bodies and broad classes of generators.
- Four source streams: wet, dry, sanitary, special-care.
- C&D and horticulture waste stored separately; hazardous, biomedical, e-waste, battery and radioactive waste remain separately regulated.
- BWG threshold: floor area >=20,000 sq m **or** water >=40,000 L/day **or** waste >=100 kg/day.
- Local bodies provide service; CPCB guides/coordinates; SPCBs/PCCs authorise, inspect and enforce.
- MRF sorts non-wet material; landfill takes inert and post-processing residue.
- RDF fuel substitution starts at 6%, rises to 10% after three years and 15% after six years for covered units.
- Delhi = legacy remediation/biomining; Indore = prevention-first segregation and service discipline.
- **2026 GS-III Mains Q17 — Solid waste challenges, policy framework, and Delhi vs Indore:**
  structure the answer as challenges → operative 2026 Rules → Delhi legacy correction versus
  Indore prevention-first design → qualified national lesson.

## Plastic Waste Management Rules

- Principal Rules: **G.S.R. 320(E), 18 March 2016**, currently read through
  **G.S.R. 237(E), 31 March 2026**.
- **G.S.R. 285(E), 27 March 2018:** added alternate-use and energy-recovery definitions; in Rule 9(3), substituted "multi-layered plastic which is non-recyclable or non-energy recoverable or with no alternate use"; divided registration jurisdiction between SPCB/PCC and CPCB; and omitted Rule 15.
- **G.S.R. 73(E), 23 January 2025:** from 1 July 2025 permits Rule 11 information through
  barcode/QR code, brochure or an eligible unique number; requires CPCB intimation and a
  quarterly updated list; inserts Rule 19's section 15 penalty linkage.
- **G.S.R. 237(E), 31 March 2026:** latest located amendment; revises definitions,
  IS 14534:2023 marking, enforcement/monitoring and auditor provisions, recycled-content
  schedules and Category-I reuse obligations.
- The omitted Rule 15 had governed fee-based local-body registration and explicit pricing of carry bags; it must not be cited as a continuing post-2018 provision.
- Specified SUP items prohibited from **1 July 2022**; not a blanket plastic ban.
- Carry-bag thickness: 75 microns from 30 September 2021; 120 microns from 31 December 2022.
- Categories: I rigid; II flexible all-plastic; III plastic/non-plastic multilayer; IV compostable; V biodegradable.
- Marking links packaging to portal registration; it does not prove physical recovery.
- EPR bundle: reuse, recycling, recycled content and authorised residual treatment.
- General EPR eligible-quantity target reached 100% from 2023-24.
- 2026-27 minimum recycling: I 70%, II 50%, III 50%, IV 70%.
- 2026-27 recycled content: I 40%, II 10%, III 5%.
- 2026-27 Category-I reuse: 15% for >=0.9 and <4.9 litre/kg; 75% only for >=4.9
  litre/kg drinking-water packaging; 10% for >=4.9 litre/kg other products.
- Certificate integrity requires capacity, input, yield, output and residue checks.

## E-Waste Management Rules

- **G.S.R. 801(E), 2 November 2022; effective 1 April 2023.**
- Amendments: G.S.R. 61(E), 30 January 2023; G.S.R. 534(E), 24 July 2023; G.S.R. 164(E), 8 March 2024.
- Established-producer recycling target: 70% in 2026-27; 80% from 2027-28.
- Producer retains EPR even when third parties assist.
- Registered recyclers generate the basis for EPR certificates and account for residue.
- Dismantlers separate components under CPCB guidance; material must reach registered recyclers.
- Refurbishing certificates defer and adjust obligation; only recycling finally extinguishes it.
- State/UT authorities should recognise/register workers, support groups, build skills and monitor safety.
- Waste batteries and plastic packaging follow their own regimes.

## Interfaces

- Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022 establish the battery-specific EPR route.
- **S.O. 4669(E), 25 October 2023:** expanded battery/producer coverage, included imports, self-use and pre-consumer waste, recast registration/returns and tightened certificate accounting.
- **G.S.R. 190(E), 14 March 2024:** set the EPR-certificate price band at 30-100% of applicable environmental compensation and standardised carry-forward.
- **S.O. 2374(E), 20 June 2024:** prescribed phased recycled-content minima—portable/EV 5-20% and automotive/industrial 35-40% from 2027-28 onward.
- **S.O. 5210(E), 3 December 2024:** aligned contraventions with the amended section 15 penalty regime without deleting EPR or environmental-compensation duties.
- **S.O. 958(E), 24 February 2025:** allowed specified barcode/QR routes for communicating the EPR registration number.
- Environment (C&D) Waste Management Rules, 2025: G.S.R. 219(E), 2 April 2025; effective 1 April 2026.
- Environment Protection (End-of-Life Vehicles) Rules, 2025: S.O. 98(E), 6 January 2025; effective 1 April 2025. Producers meet scrapping obligations through RVSFs, while batteries, electronics, plastics, oils and hazardous residues retain their own rule routes.
- Environment Protection (Management of Contaminated Sites) Rules, 2025: S.O. 3401(E), 24 July 2025; effective on publication. They govern assessment, liability, risk-based remediation and post-remediation monitoring of contaminated land.
- The Environmental Relief Fund may initially support assessment to the extent feasible; orphan-site assessment/planning/remediation uses prescribed public cost-sharing, while expenditure remains recoverable from an identified responsible person.
- Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016 as amended: infection-risk stream; never mix with municipal recyclables.
- Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules, 2016 as amended, including G.S.R. 177(E), 12 March 2024: industrial hazardous and transboundary controls.
- Basel Convention complements, but does not replace, domestic collection and EPR law.

## Cross-stream EPR and circular-economy scope

- **Eight EPR streams:** plastic packaging; batteries; e-waste; waste tyres; used oil; end-of-life vehicles; construction and demolition waste; non-ferrous metal scrap.
- Plastic, battery and e-waste systems assign lifecycle duties to product/packaging producers but use different processors, targets and safety controls.
- Waste tyres and used oil operate through the hazardous-and-other-wastes framework; ELVs use RVSFs before components enter separate rules.
- C&D EPR addresses large construction producers and mineral recovery; non-ferrous scrap EPR addresses covered metal-product chains from 1 April 2026.
- **Ten Circular Economy Action Plan categories:** lithium-ion batteries; e-waste; toxic and hazardous industrial waste; ferrous and non-ferrous scrap metal; tyre and rubber; end-of-life vehicles; gypsum; used oil; solar panels; municipal solid waste.
- The action-plan inventory is broader than the EPR inventory: it coordinates industrial strategy, standards and markets and is not itself one combined legal rule.
- Ecodesign, repair/refurbishment, take-back, secondary-material standards, recycled-content demand and safe residual treatment connect the categories into a materials policy.

## Optional advanced verification and enforcement

- Digital portals scale registration and certificate accounting; physical audits establish truth.
- Distinguish registrations, reported recycling, certificates and productive material use.
- Use material balance: opening stock + input - output - authorised residue - closing stock.
- Create demand through recycled-content standards, quality standards and procurement.
- Use differentiated EPR fees for difficult or hazardous design.
- Integrate waste workers through contracts, cooperatives, safety and social protection.
- Pool regional facilities for small local bodies.
- Treat environmental compensation as corrective enforcement, not a licence fee.
- Measure product life, virgin displacement, verified yield, residue safety, leakage and worker health.

## Final qualified conclusion

India has moved from municipal disposal towards lifecycle responsibility, stream-specific law and certificate-based EPR. The decisive test is not the number of rules, portals or certificates but whether design improves, segregation survives logistics, workers become safer, recovered material displaces virgin inputs and only a small, controlled residue reaches final disposal.
