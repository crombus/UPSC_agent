## Lesson 1 — Carbon-accounting grammar: what exactly changed?

Progress: 1 / 14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Avoidance, reduction, removal, allowances and credits

### Visual: follow the carbon atom

```text
REFERENCE WORLD                         PROJECT / POLICY WORLD

Coal plant emits 100 tCO2               Efficiency retrofit emits 80 tCO2
                     \                  /
                      \--> REDUCTION = 20 tCO2 against a defensible baseline

Planned plant never built               Renewable supply displaces its generation
                     \                  /
                      \--> AVOIDED EMISSION = counterfactual non-release

100 tCO2 already in air                 DAC captures and durably stores 100 tCO2
                     \                  /
                      \--> REMOVAL = atmosphere-to-reservoir transfer

At a regulated installation:
ALLOWANCE = permission to emit under a scheme

From a quantified mitigation activity:
CREDIT = certified reduction or removal relative to rules/baseline
```

The first exam trap is linguistic. **Avoiding an emission, reducing an emitting flow and
removing carbon already in the atmosphere are not interchangeable physical outcomes.**

### 1. Start with stocks and flows

The atmosphere contains a **stock** of accumulated carbon dioxide. Human activities add a
**flow** of new emissions. Climate policy can therefore act in three different ways:

1. **Avoidance:** prevent a projected new flow from occurring.
2. **Reduction:** lower an existing flow relative to a defined reference.
3. **Removal:** withdraw CO2 from the atmospheric stock and store it.

An efficiency measure at a cement plant may reduce emissions per tonne of cement. Closing an
unnecessary emitting process may avoid future emissions. Direct air carbon capture and storage
may remove atmospheric CO2. These outcomes can all be valuable, but their accounting,
additionality and permanence questions differ.

### 2. Gross capture is not net climate benefit

Suppose a facility captures 100 tonnes of CO2 but uses energy whose production emits 25 tonnes,
loses 3 tonnes in transport and stores only 60 tonnes durably while 37 tonnes enter a short-lived
product. It cannot honestly report 100 tonnes of durable net mitigation.

```text
Gross captured CO2
    - upstream energy emissions
    - transport and processing emissions
    - uncaptured/released fraction
    - reversal or short-lived product release
    = net atmospheric benefit over the stated boundary and period
```

The example establishes why lifecycle boundaries matter. Its limit is that actual projects need
technology-specific data; the arithmetic above is illustrative, not a reported project result.

### 3. Allowance versus credit

| Test | Allowance | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Basic meaning | Authorisation to emit a stated quantity under a regulatory system | Unit representing a verified reduction or removal under a crediting rule |
| Created by | Scheme authority under a cap or allocation framework | Performance beyond a baseline or an eligible project activity |
| Reference | The scheme's emissions budget or allocation | A baseline/counterfactual and an approved methodology |
| Typical use | Compliance surrender by covered entities | Compliance where permitted, voluntary retirement, or international transfer |
| Integrity question | Is the overall cap/target stringent? | Is the credited outcome additional, conservatively quantified and durable? |

One allowance surrendered does not prove that a separate project removed one tonne from the
atmosphere. One project credit does not grant an unrestricted right to emit everywhere. Scheme
rules decide fungibility.

### 4. Carbon price, certificate and claim

A **carbon price** is the monetary signal attached to emitting or mitigating a tonne of
greenhouse-gas emissions, usually expressed in carbon-dioxide equivalent. A **certificate** is
the tradable legal or registry instrument. A **claim** is what an entity says after buying,
surrendering or retiring that unit. These layers must not be collapsed:

```text
physical outcome --> quantified result --> issued unit --> transfer/retirement --> public claim
```

A unit may be valid for compliance under one scheme but unsuitable for a broad claim such as
"carbon neutral." Claim quality depends on boundaries, residual emissions, unit quality,
retirement, corresponding adjustments where relevant and honest communication.

### 5. Carbon-dioxide equivalent is a common accounting unit

Different greenhouse gases have different warming effects and atmospheric lifetimes. Schemes
often convert them into **tonnes of carbon-dioxide equivalent (tCO2e)** using specified global
warming potential values. This aids comparison and trading; it does not make methane, nitrous
oxide and carbon dioxide physically identical.

### 6. Comparison with an ordinary commodity

A tonne of steel is a tangible product. A carbon unit is a **rule-created environmental
attribute**. Its quality therefore depends unusually heavily on:

- the measurement boundary;
- the baseline;
- the monitoring method;
- independent verification;
- registry control;
- rules against duplicate issuance and claims;
- treatment of reversal.

The market cannot discover these facts merely through price. Regulation and methodology create
the commodity before trading can allocate it.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish allowance, credit, avoided emission, reduction and removal.
- **Mains:** begin an answer by separating physical mitigation from the instrument used to
  finance or account for it.
- **Answer-use:** evaluate any carbon claim through the chain from atmospheric outcome to final
  retirement, not through the certificate's name.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A carbon allowance represents regulated permission to emit, whereas
> a carbon credit represents a quantified reduction or removal against specified rules; neither
> label alone proves atmospheric integrity.

### Revision notes

1. Avoidance prevents a projected emission; reduction lowers an emitting flow.
2. Removal withdraws CO2 from ambient air and requires storage to produce a lasting benefit.
3. Standard point-source capture is normally avoided emission, not atmospheric removal.
4. Gross capture must be corrected for lifecycle emissions and later release.
5. An allowance is created within a regulatory emissions budget or allocation framework.
6. A credit is issued against a baseline or approved project methodology.
7. Carbon price, tradable certificate and corporate claim are separate layers.
8. tCO2e is an accounting unit, not a claim that all greenhouse gases behave identically.
9. Fungibility exists only when scheme rules recognise units for a specified use.
10. Registry issuance and retirement do not substitute for sound physical quantification.
11. A project's boundary and time horizon determine what enters the net-benefit calculation.
12. The atmosphere, not transaction volume, is the ultimate integrity test.

### Concept check

**Question:** A cement plant captures part of its process CO2 before release, while a separate
facility captures ambient CO2 and injects it into a verified geological reservoir. How should
the two outcomes be classified?

**Model answer:** The cement intervention is primarily point-source emissions avoidance or
reduction because it prevents newly generated CO2 from entering the atmosphere. The ambient-air
intervention can qualify as carbon dioxide removal if lifecycle emissions are deducted and the
captured CO2 is durably stored. Calling both simply "negative emissions" would erase the
source-versus-atmosphere distinction.

**Misconception to avoid:** "Captured" does not automatically mean "removed from the
atmosphere"; the origin of the CO2 and the durability of storage decide the category.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish carbon allowances from carbon credits, and explain
why the distinction matters for climate integrity.

#### Model response (139 words)

A carbon allowance is a regulator-created permission to emit a specified quantity within a
compliance system. Its environmental value depends chiefly on the stringency and declining
trajectory of the overall cap or target. A carbon credit, by contrast, certifies a quantified
emission reduction or removal against a baseline and approved methodology. Its value therefore
depends on additionality, conservative baselines, MRV, permanence and exclusive claiming.

The distinction matters because units can perform different legal and atmospheric functions.
Surrendering an allowance demonstrates compliance but not atmospheric removal. Buying a project
credit may finance mitigation, yet it does not justify continued emissions if the credited
activity would have happened anyway or can reverse. Regulators must therefore define eligibility,
fungibility and retirement rules, while users should state whether a unit represents permission,
reduction or removal. Climate integrity begins with accurate classification before trading.

## Lesson 2 — Carbon tax, cap-and-trade and baseline-and-credit

Progress: 2 / 14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Price certainty, quantity certainty and intensity targets

Book context: Carbon-pricing foundation available

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: three ways to create a carbon incentive

```text
CARBON TAX
Government fixes PRICE per tCO2e
Firms decide how much to emit/abate
=> price clearer; exact emissions quantity uncertain

CAP-AND-TRADE / ETS
Government fixes TOTAL ALLOWANCE QUANTITY
Market discovers allowance price
=> aggregate quantity clearer; price may fluctuate

BASELINE-AND-CREDIT
Authority fixes PERFORMANCE BASELINE / INTENSITY TARGET
Below target earns credits; above target needs credits
=> rewards relative outperformance; absolute emissions may still rise with output
```

### 1. One common purpose, different control variables

All three instruments try to make emissions costly and cleaner choices comparatively attractive.
They differ in what the public authority fixes.

| Instrument | Authority fixes | Market/firm determines | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon tax | Tax rate | Emissions response | Tax liability per tCO2e |
| Cap-and-trade | Allowance quantity/cap | Allowance price and abatement location | Allowance |
| Baseline-and-credit | Baseline or performance target | Credits generated/needed and price | Performance credit |

No instrument automatically guarantees ambition. A very low tax, an over-allocated cap and a
generous baseline can all produce weak incentives.

### 2. Carbon tax: transparent price, uncertain quantity

If the tax is ₹X per tCO2e, a firm compares:

```text
marginal cost of reducing the next tonne
                 versus
tax payable for emitting that tonne
```

It abates when abatement is cheaper than the tax, subject to technology, finance and operational
constraints. Advantages include administrative simplicity where emissions can be measured and
more predictable revenue. Limitations include political resistance, distributional effects and
uncertainty about the exact emissions outcome.

A tax can recycle revenue into household transfers, green infrastructure or transition support.
Without such design, it may disproportionately affect energy-poor households or trade-exposed
industries.

### 3. Cap-and-trade: quantity architecture

The authority determines a cap and issues fewer allowances over time. Covered entities surrender
allowances equal to verified emissions. Trading directs reductions toward firms with lower
marginal abatement costs.

```text
Firm A: abatement costs ₹500/t --> reduces and sells allowance
Firm B: abatement costs ₹1,500/t --> buys allowance if price is below ₹1,500
                    |
                    `--> same cap, lower aggregate compliance cost
```

This example shows the cost-efficiency logic. It does not prove fairness or innovation: free
allocation, market concentration, price volatility and windfall profits require separate design.

### 4. Baseline-and-credit: performance relative to output

Suppose a steel unit's target is 2.0 tCO2e per tonne of output. If it produces 100 tonnes:

- actual intensity 1.8 gives performance 20 tCO2e better than target;
- actual intensity 2.2 gives a 20 tCO2e shortfall.

The exact certificate calculation depends on scheme rules, verified output and emissions.

```text
eligible performance difference
= (target intensity - actual intensity) x verified output
```

The critical qualification is that intensity can improve while total emissions increase:

| Year | Output | Intensity | Absolute emissions |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 100 | 2.0 | 200 |
| B | 150 | 1.6 | 240 |

Year B is cleaner per unit but emits more in total. Intensity systems can suit a growing economy,
yet they are not equivalent to an absolute cap.

### 5. Allocation and auctioning

In an ETS, allowances may be:

- auctioned;
- allocated free using historical emissions;
- allocated free using output-based benchmarks; or
- distributed through a hybrid.

Auctioning raises revenue and reduces windfall gains. Free allocation may address carbon-leakage
risk but can weaken the polluter-pays signal if excessive. Output-based allocation protects
production incentives but may subsidise continued output.

### 6. Price-stability tools

An ETS may use a price floor, auction reserve price, allowance reserve, borrowing limits or
banking rules. A tax may include a scheduled escalator and review mechanism. A baseline system
may tighten benchmarks over time. These are not decorative details: they determine whether firms
expect a durable incentive to invest.

### 7. Choosing an instrument

| Policy condition | Design implication |
|---|---|
| Reliable facility-level MRV | Trading becomes more credible |
| Urgent fixed emissions budget | Cap architecture gains appeal |
| Need for predictable investment signal | Tax or price corridor may help |
| Rapid output growth | Intensity baseline may ease transition but needs absolute-emissions tracking |
| High distributional concern | Revenue recycling and targeted protection are essential |
| Trade exposure | Transitional, benchmarked support may be justified; permanent over-allocation is not |

The choice is not tax **or** market in the abstract. A hybrid can combine an ETS with a price
floor, sectoral standards, public investment and border measures.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** tax fixes price; a cap fixes allowance quantity; baseline-and-credit rewards
  performance against a benchmark.
- **Mains:** compare effectiveness, efficiency, equity, administrative feasibility and
  innovation rather than declaring one instrument universally superior.
- **India link:** India's compliance architecture uses greenhouse-gas emission-intensity targets,
  so it is closer to baseline-and-credit than economy-wide absolute cap-and-trade.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Carbon taxes provide greater price certainty, cap-and-trade provides
> greater aggregate quantity control, and baseline-and-credit controls relative performance but
> may permit absolute emissions to rise with output.

### Revision notes

1. Carbon tax fixes the price signal; emissions response remains uncertain.
2. Cap-and-trade fixes allowance quantity; the market discovers the price.
3. Baseline-and-credit fixes a benchmark or intensity target.
4. A weak tax, loose cap and generous baseline can all underperform.
5. Trading lowers compliance cost when marginal abatement costs differ.
6. Cost efficiency does not by itself establish equity or environmental integrity.
7. Intensity improvement is not the same as absolute-emissions decline.
8. Auctioning can raise revenue for transition and reduce windfall gains.
9. Free allocation may address leakage but should be targeted and transitional.
10. Banking aids intertemporal efficiency but excessive surplus can depress future prices.
11. Price floors, reserves and escalators can strengthen investment confidence.
12. Instrument choice must consider MRV capacity, growth, trade exposure and justice.
13. Complementary regulation is needed where price response is weak or infrastructure is missing.

### Concept check

**Question:** An industry reduces emissions intensity by 20% while its output grows by 40%. Why
can a baseline-and-credit system record improvement even if total emissions rise?

**Model answer:** The system compares verified emissions per unit of output with an intensity
target. A lower intensity can beat that target and generate compliance value. Absolute emissions,
however, equal intensity multiplied by output; sufficiently rapid output growth can therefore
raise total emissions. The system must track both indicators and tighten targets if it is to
support an absolute climate pathway.

**Misconception to avoid:** A declining emissions-intensity figure is not automatic evidence that
the atmosphere receives fewer total emissions.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Compare a carbon tax, cap-and-trade and baseline-and-credit as
instruments for an industrialising economy.

#### Model response (206 words)

Carbon-pricing instruments differ mainly in the variable controlled. A carbon tax fixes the
price per tonne, allowing firms to choose their emissions response. It offers a predictable
investment signal and can generate revenue for household protection and green infrastructure,
but the final emissions quantity is uncertain and tax increases may face political resistance.

Cap-and-trade fixes an aggregate allowance quantity and lets the market discover the price.
Trading can equalise marginal abatement costs, while auction revenue can fund transition.
However, over-allocation, price volatility, free-allocation windfalls and weak market oversight
can undermine performance.

Baseline-and-credit sets output-linked emission-intensity targets. Efficient facilities earn
credits and laggards buy them. This can accommodate industrial growth and reward cleaner
production, as relevant to India's developmental context. Yet absolute emissions can rise when
output growth exceeds intensity improvement, and generous baselines create surplus credits.

For an industrialising economy, the appropriate design is therefore a policy package: stringent
and progressively tightened benchmarks for trade-exposed sectors, transparent MRV, an eventual
shift toward stronger absolute constraints where feasible, and complementary standards,
infrastructure and innovation support. Revenue or compliance-cost relief should protect
vulnerable households and genuinely exposed firms without freezing incumbent technology. The
best instrument is the one whose ambition, administration and justice provisions remain credible
over time.

## Lesson 3 — Compliance and voluntary carbon markets

### Visual: two markets, different sources of demand

```text
COMPLIANCE MARKET                              VOLUNTARY MARKET

Law creates obligation                        Buyer chooses to purchase
       |                                                |
Covered entity measures emissions             Project claims reduction/removal
       |                                                |
Surrenders eligible units                     Standard/methodology validates activity
       |                                                |
Penalty for shortfall                         Credit issued in registry
       |                                                |
Regulator controls eligibility                Buyer retires credit for stated purpose

Shared integrity needs:
MRV + registry control + clear boundaries + no duplicate issuance/claim
```

### 1. Compliance market

A compliance market exists because law requires covered entities to meet a cap, intensity
target, fuel standard or another quantified obligation. Demand is therefore regulatory. The
scheme defines:

- covered gases, sectors and installations;
- compliance period;
- monitoring plan and reporting boundary;
- eligible units;
- surrender deadline;
- verification and enforcement;
- penalties or make-good obligations.

Compliance does not necessarily mean that every unit is an allowance. A scheme can allow
limited project credits, performance credits or imported units, each with eligibility limits.

### 2. Voluntary market

In a voluntary market, a company, institution or person buys credits without being compelled by
the same compliance obligation. Motives include internal climate targets, contribution claims,
procurement commitments or preparation for future regulation.

Voluntary demand can channel finance to mitigation, but it creates a special risk: a buyer may
use cheap credits to make a claim broader than the underlying evidence. Good practice therefore
separates:

```text
inventory reduction inside the buyer's value chain
             from
finance provided to mitigation outside that value chain
```

An outside credit does not physically erase the buyer's reported emissions. It can compensate,
contribute or support, depending on claim rules and unit quality.

### 3. Crediting project cycle

```text
1. Define activity and boundary
        |
2. Select approved methodology
        |
3. Establish baseline and demonstrate additionality
        |
4. Validate project design
        |
5. Register project
        |
6. Monitor actual performance
        |
7. Verify monitored report independently
        |
8. Issue serialised credits
        |
9. Transfer and retire
        |
10. Monitor reversal / fulfil liability where applicable
```

Validation asks whether the proposed design and methodology are acceptable. Verification asks
whether monitored outcomes actually occurred. The two functions should not be confused.

### 4. MRV is the measurement constitution of a market

**Monitoring** collects activity, fuel, process, output, leakage and storage data. **Reporting**
presents them under a prescribed format and boundary. **Verification** uses an independent,
competent body to test the report and evidence.

MRV must answer:

| Question | Example |
|---|---|
| What source or sink is covered? | Kiln process emissions, not only purchased electricity |
| Which gases count? | CO2 alone or a specified GHG basket |
| What period applies? | Annual compliance year or crediting period |
| Which factors are used? | Measured carbon content or approved emission factor |
| How is uncertainty handled? | Conservative deductions or uncertainty analysis |
| Who verifies? | Accredited independent verifier with conflict controls |

Digital monitoring can improve frequency and traceability, but bad sensors, opaque algorithms or
uncontrolled data access can digitise error rather than eliminate it.

### 5. Registry, issuance, transfer and retirement

A registry should give every unit a unique serial number and record:

- project or allocation origin;
- vintage;
- methodology;
- ownership changes;
- status: active, surrendered, cancelled or retired;
- authorisation where relevant;
- corresponding-adjustment information where relevant.

**Retirement** removes a unit from further circulation for the stated purpose. It prevents resale
of that unit but does not independently prove additionality, permanence or honest marketing.

### 6. Relationship between the two markets

Compliance schemes sometimes admit selected voluntary-standard credits. Governments may also
create a domestic offset mechanism whose participation is voluntary but whose units can later
serve regulated uses. Therefore, "voluntary participation" and "unregulated quality" are not
synonyms. The controlling questions are legal oversight, methodology and use.

### 7. Corporate hierarchy

A credible organisation normally follows:

```text
measure full inventory
    -> reduce avoidable operational and value-chain emissions
    -> disclose residual emissions and target boundary
    -> use high-integrity credits only for a clearly stated residual/contribution purpose
    -> retire units and report their attributes
```

Credits should complement, not replace, a transition plan. Where a firm depends indefinitely on
cheap offsets while expanding high-carbon assets, the market may enable mitigation deterrence.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** compliance demand is legally created; voluntary demand is buyer-initiated.
- **Mains:** explain the project cycle and why retirement is necessary but insufficient.
- **Governance:** focus on accredited verification, transparent registries, conflict-of-interest
  control and claims discipline.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Compliance and voluntary markets differ in the source of demand, but
> both require rigorous MRV, serialised registries and truthful claims because trading cannot
> convert an unproved outcome into climate mitigation.

### Revision notes

1. Compliance demand arises from a legal obligation.
2. Voluntary demand arises from a buyer's chosen climate or contribution objective.
3. A compliance scheme may admit different unit types subject to eligibility rules.
4. Voluntary participation does not necessarily mean absence of public regulation.
5. Validation examines project design; verification examines monitored performance.
6. MRV means monitoring, reporting and verification.
7. The monitoring boundary must include material direct and lifecycle sources.
8. Conservative treatment protects against measurement uncertainty and over-crediting.
9. Registry serial numbers control custody and unit status.
10. Retirement prevents reuse of a unit but does not cure a weak baseline.
11. A credit outside a company's value chain does not reduce its inventory emissions.
12. Claims must identify purpose, scope, period, unit type and treatment of residual emissions.
13. Credit use should follow, not replace, direct decarbonisation.
14. Verifier competence and independence are core governance requirements.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is retiring a carbon credit necessary but not sufficient for a credible climate
claim?

**Model answer:** Retirement prevents the same registry unit from being sold or used again by
that holder. Credibility still depends on whether the underlying activity was additional,
correctly baselined, conservatively measured, free from material leakage, durable where storage
is claimed and not counted elsewhere. The buyer must also describe the claim honestly rather
than treating an outside credit as an internal emissions reduction.

**Misconception to avoid:** Registry retirement solves reuse of the unit; it does not retroactively
create a real or additional mitigation outcome.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why is MRV central to both compliance and voluntary carbon
markets?

#### Model response (143 words)

Monitoring, reporting and verification convert a claimed climate action into an auditable
quantity. Monitoring defines and measures emissions, output, leakage or stored carbon. Reporting
applies prescribed boundaries, factors and uncertainty rules. Independent verification tests the
data and methodology before allowances are surrendered or credits issued.

MRV is indispensable in compliance markets because regulated entities must be compared fairly
and penalties require defensible evidence. It is equally important in voluntary markets, where
buyers otherwise cannot know whether a project is additional, conservatively quantified or
durable. A serialised registry can prevent duplicate use, but it cannot correct bad measurements
or an inflated baseline.

Effective design therefore needs approved methodologies, calibrated instruments, accredited
verifiers, conflict-of-interest safeguards, transparent reports and conservative deductions for
uncertainty. MRV is not clerical paperwork: it is the measurement constitution that turns a
carbon unit from a promise into a contestable environmental claim.

## Lesson 4 — Integrity tests: additionality to corresponding adjustments

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

CA found: No distinct news linkage; official accounting concepts are treated as institutional rules

### Visual: the carbon-credit integrity funnel

```text
Claimed tonnes
     |
     v
Is the baseline credible? -------- no --> inflated quantity
     |
    yes
     v
Would it happen anyway? ---------- yes -> non-additional
     |
     no
     v
Did emissions move elsewhere? ---- yes -> deduct leakage
     |
     no/materially deducted
     v
Can stored carbon reverse? ------- yes -> buffer / monitoring / replacement
     |
     controlled
     v
Is the tonne counted twice? ------ yes -> reject or adjust accounts
     |
     no
     v
CONSERVATIVELY QUANTIFIED, UNIQUELY CLAIMED OUTCOME
```

### 1. Baseline: the reference world

A baseline estimates emissions or removals without the credited intervention. It may be:

- historical;
- performance-benchmark based;
- modelled;
- standardised for a sector or region;
- updated periodically.

If a baseline assumes unrealistically high future emissions, the project can appear to reduce
more than it truly does. Baselines should therefore be conservative, transparent and capable of
tightening as common practice changes.

### 2. Additionality: causation, not mere chronology

An activity is additional when the credited mitigation would not occur in the relevant
counterfactual without the incentive or framework being tested. Common tests examine:

- legal requirements;
- financial viability;
- investment alternatives;
- technological or institutional barriers;
- common practice;
- prior consideration of carbon revenue.

No single test is infallible. A profitable project may still face a real barrier; an unprofitable
project may have been planned for another subsidy. Additionality requires documented causal
reasoning, not a ritual checklist.

### 3. Baseline and additionality are related but distinct

```text
Additionality asks: "Would the activity occur without the crediting incentive?"
Baseline asks:      "What emissions/removals would occur without the activity?"
```

A project may be additional but over-credited through an inflated baseline. Conversely, a
baseline may be accurately calculated for a project that is legally mandatory and therefore
non-additional for crediting.

### 4. Leakage

Leakage occurs when an intervention changes emissions outside its accounting boundary.

| Activity | Possible leakage |
|---|---|
| Forest protection | Timber extraction or agriculture shifts to another area |
| Industrial closure | Production and emissions move to an unregulated jurisdiction |
| Bioenergy expansion | Land-use change or displaced food production raises emissions elsewhere |
| CO2 transport | Compression energy and transport losses omitted from project boundary |

Leakage is not proof that every project fails. It means the boundary must follow material causal
effects and deduct them conservatively.

### 5. Permanence and reversal

Avoiding one tonne of fossil CO2 prevents a long-lived addition. Storing carbon in a forest,
soil, product or geological formation has different reversal risks and timescales.

| Reservoir | Typical integrity issue |
|---|---|
| Forest biomass | Fire, harvest, pests, land-use change |
| Soil carbon | Management reversal and measurement variability |
| Long-lived product | Eventual disposal, decay or combustion |
| Geological formation | Well integrity, faults, plume migration and long-term liability |

Possible safeguards include buffer pools, discounting, monitoring obligations, replacement
requirements, insurance and long-duration liability. A temporary store may still have value, but
it should not be represented as permanent without qualification.

### 6. Double counting has several forms

1. **Double issuance:** more than one unit is issued for the same outcome.
2. **Double use:** the same unit is surrendered or retired more than once.
3. **Double claiming:** two entities or countries both claim the same mitigation outcome.
4. **Overlapping programmes:** the same activity earns incompatible certificates without
   reconciliation.

A registry mainly addresses issuance and use. International accounting rules are needed when a
host country and acquiring country might both apply the same transferred mitigation outcome
toward targets.

### 7. Corresponding adjustment

Under Paris Agreement cooperative approaches, an authorised international transfer used toward
another country's NDC or another approved international mitigation purpose requires accounting
that prevents both sides from claiming the same outcome.

```text
Host authorises transfer of mitigation outcome
            |
Host applies an emissions-balance adjustment
            |
Acquiring Party applies the paired adjustment for authorised use
            |
One outcome is not counted toward two NDC achievements
```

A corresponding adjustment is an accounting operation, not a new physical emission reduction.
It protects exclusive claim. It also affects host-country strategy: exporting low-cost outcomes
may make the host's own future NDC harder or costlier to achieve.

### 8. Conservative quantification

Where data are uncertain, integrity normally favours:

- lower credited quantity;
- uncertainty deduction;
- shorter renewal period;
- updated baseline;
- exclusion of unverifiable sources;
- reversal reserve.

This can reduce credit supply but protects trust and prevents a nominal tonne from exceeding the
underlying atmospheric benefit.

### 9. Integrity hierarchy

```text
Real outcome
  + additional
  + conservatively baselined
  + leakage deducted
  + durable or reversal-covered
  + independently verified
  + uniquely issued, used and claimed
  = high-integrity unit
```

High price does not prove this chain. Low price does not necessarily disprove it. Price reflects
supply, demand, rules and market power; integrity requires evidence.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** additionality, leakage, permanence and double counting test different defects.
- **Mains:** make MRV and corresponding adjustments the core of Article 6 evaluation.
- **Policy:** host countries should authorise exports selectively after considering their own NDC.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Carbon-market integrity requires causal additionality, conservative
> baselines, leakage deductions, durable storage and exclusive claiming; a corresponding
> adjustment prevents double counting but does not create extra mitigation.

### Revision notes

1. A baseline is the defensible reference scenario without the intervention.
2. Inflated baselines cause over-crediting even when an activity occurs.
3. Additionality asks whether crediting caused mitigation beyond the counterfactual.
4. Legal, investment, barrier and common-practice tests support additionality assessment.
5. Baseline accuracy and additionality are separate tests.
6. Leakage shifts emissions outside the chosen boundary.
7. Leakage should be prevented, measured or conservatively deducted.
8. Permanence concerns the duration and reversal risk of stored carbon.
9. Buffer pools and replacement obligations manage but do not erase reversal risk.
10. Double issuance, double use and double claiming are distinct.
11. Registries control unit identity and status.
12. Corresponding adjustments address authorised international double claiming.
13. A corresponding adjustment is accounting, not another physical reduction.
14. Exporting cheap outcomes can raise a host country's future NDC cost.
15. Conservative uncertainty treatment protects atmospheric integrity.

### Concept check

**Question:** A host country authorises a mitigation outcome for transfer, and the buyer uses it
toward its NDC. What problem does a corresponding adjustment solve, and what does it not prove?

**Model answer:** The paired accounting adjustment prevents the host and buyer from both applying
the authorised outcome toward their NDC achievement. It does not prove that the project was
additional, that its baseline was conservative, that leakage was deducted or that storage was
durable. Those require separate methodology, MRV and permanence safeguards.

**Misconception to avoid:** Corresponding adjustment is a double-counting safeguard, not a
complete quality seal for the underlying activity.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "The hardest part of a carbon market is not trading but
constructing the tonne being traded." Explain.

#### Model response (202 words)

A carbon unit is not a self-evident physical commodity. It is produced through rules that define
the source, boundary, baseline, methodology, monitoring period and eligible claim. Trading can
allocate such units efficiently only after this environmental attribute has been credibly
constructed.

First, additionality must show that mitigation exceeds what law, normal investment or common
practice would already deliver. Second, a conservative baseline must avoid crediting an
implausibly high counterfactual. Third, MRV must quantify actual emissions, output, leakage and
uncertainty through competent independent verification. Fourth, biological, product or
geological storage needs reversal monitoring and replacement or liability rules. Finally,
serialised registries must prevent duplicate issuance and use, while corresponding adjustments
must prevent two countries from claiming an authorised international transfer.

Weakness at any stage creates paper mitigation. A liquid market may then spread rather than
correct the defect, because price cannot reveal an omitted leakage source or fictitious
counterfactual. Conversely, excessively complex rules can exclude small actors and raise costs.
Policy should therefore combine conservative standardised methodologies, transparent data,
risk-based verification, accessible grievance systems and periodic baseline tightening.

Thus, exchange infrastructure is secondary. The primary public task is to define a tonne whose
causal, quantified, durable and exclusive atmospheric benefit can survive scrutiny.

## Lesson 5 — Integrity is also a justice question

Progress: 5 / 14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Distribution, participation, land, labour and transition

Book context: Climate-justice foundation available
CA search: "carbon market safeguards local communities benefit sharing just transition official"
CA found: No distinct current-affairs linkage; justice principles are integrated as design criteria
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: one carbon price, many incidence channels

```text
CARBON POLICY
    |
    +--> producer cost --> prices / profits / investment
    |
    +--> worker impact --> job loss / reskilling / new employment
    |
    +--> household impact --> energy and transport affordability
    |
    +--> place impact --> coal districts / industrial clusters / forest landscapes
    |
    +--> project impact --> land, tenure, consent, livelihood, benefit-sharing
    |
    `--> fiscal effect --> auction/tax revenue and its recycling
```

Climate efficiency asks whether mitigation is achieved at lower cost. Climate justice asks
**whose cost, whose land, whose livelihood, whose voice and whose benefit**.

### 1. Distributional incidence

The entity that formally pays is not always the actor that ultimately bears the cost. An
industrial carbon price may be passed through to consumers, absorbed in profits, shifted to
workers, or avoided through cleaner investment. Incidence depends on competition, trade exposure,
technology and demand.

Low-income households often spend a larger share of income on energy and transport. A carbon
price can therefore be regressive unless revenue recycling, lifeline consumption, public
transport and targeted transfers protect basic needs.

### 2. Just transition in emitting regions

A market may reveal that an old plant is expensive to decarbonise. Closure can lower emissions
but destabilise a district dependent on wages, local procurement and public revenue.

```text
credible transition plan
= advance notice
+ worker and community participation
+ income and pension protection
+ reskilling linked to actual jobs
+ regional diversification
+ remediation of polluted sites
```

Carbon revenue can help finance this package. Merely promising "green jobs" without location,
skill and wage matching is not sufficient.

### 3. Land and tenure in nature-based crediting

Afforestation, avoided deforestation and soil-carbon projects can produce ecological and
livelihood benefits. They can also create conflict if developers treat customary use as vacant
land, restrict access, exaggerate sequestration or distribute revenue unequally.

Integrity therefore needs:

- clear land and carbon rights;
- lawful tenure recognition;
- meaningful participation;
- accessible grievance and remedy;
- transparent contracts and benefit-sharing;
- biodiversity and water safeguards;
- no coercive displacement.

Carbon additionality cannot legitimise an unjust land process.

### 4. Procedural, distributive and recognitional justice

| Dimension | Core question |
|---|---|
| Procedural | Who participates, receives information and can challenge a decision? |
| Distributive | Who receives benefits and who bears costs or risks? |
| Recognitional | Are customary rights, identities, knowledge and unequal vulnerability respected? |
| Restorative | Is remedy available when harm, reversal or misrepresentation occurs? |

These dimensions are analytically separate. A project can distribute money while excluding
communities from decisions; it can consult formally while ignoring customary tenure.

### 5. Global equity and low-cost mitigation

International markets can lower global compliance cost by funding lower-cost mitigation in
developing countries. Yet a host country should ask:

- Does the transfer finance new technology and sustainable development?
- Is the price commensurate with the outcome and future NDC cost?
- Are the easiest domestic reductions being exported?
- Does the contract share risk fairly?
- Can the host retain enough mitigation space for development and stronger future targets?

Cheap credits are not automatically equitable credits.

### 6. Market access and concentration

Complex methodologies, validation fees and registry costs can exclude small farmers, urban local
bodies and micro-enterprises. Aggregation can reduce transaction costs, but aggregators may hold
information and bargaining power. Public templates, standardised methods, transparent fees and
legal assistance can improve access.

### 7. Carbon leakage versus justice

Protecting a trade-exposed industry from relocation may justify transitional free allocation or
output-based support. But protection should be conditional on measurable decarbonisation. Using
"leakage" as a permanent excuse for free pollution transfers costs to citizens and delays
innovation.

### 8. A justice-screen for policy design

```text
Environmental integrity
        +
affordability protection
        +
worker and regional transition
        +
rights-based project safeguards
        +
transparent benefit sharing
        +
grievance and remedy
        =
socially durable carbon policy
```

Social durability matters for mitigation: a policy perceived as unfair is vulnerable to
rollback, evasion and distrust.

### UPSC integration

- **GS-III:** link carbon pricing with industrial transition, energy affordability and regional
  development.
- **GS-II/Essay linkage:** procedural fairness, rights, accountability and cooperative federalism.
- **Evaluation:** pair cost efficiency with atmospheric integrity and distributive legitimacy.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** A carbon market is environmentally incomplete and politically fragile
> unless its price signal is joined to affordability protection, worker transition, secure land
> rights, benefit-sharing and effective remedy.

### Revision notes

1. Formal payer and ultimate cost-bearer may differ.
2. Carbon pricing can be regressive without targeted recycling.
3. Auction or tax revenue can finance transfers, infrastructure and just transition.
4. Plant closure affects workers, suppliers, local government and place-based identity.
5. Reskilling must connect to real jobs, locations and wage conditions.
6. Nature credits require secure tenure and lawful rights.
7. Consultation is not equivalent to meaningful participation or consent where law requires it.
8. Procedural, distributive, recognitional and restorative justice are distinct.
9. Low-cost international mitigation can still create an unfair host-country bargain.
10. Exporting cheap outcomes may raise the host's future NDC cost.
11. High transaction costs can exclude small actors.
12. Aggregators reduce costs but create bargaining and transparency risks.
13. Leakage protection should be targeted, conditional and temporary.
14. Grievance and remedy are integrity mechanisms, not public-relations additions.
15. Fairness strengthens the long-term political durability of climate policy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a technically additional forest-carbon project still fail a high-integrity
test?

**Model answer:** Additionality addresses whether the credited sequestration exceeds the
counterfactual. The project can still fail if its baseline is inflated, reversal risk is unmanaged,
leakage shifts deforestation, customary rights are ignored, access is restricted without lawful
process, benefits are captured by intermediaries, or affected people lack grievance and remedy.
Environmental and social integrity must both be satisfied.

**Misconception to avoid:** A real carbon gain does not cancel violations of tenure, participation
or livelihood rights.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the justice dimensions that should shape carbon-market
design in India.

#### Model response (200 words)

Carbon markets seek least-cost mitigation, but their social legitimacy depends on how costs,
rights and benefits are distributed. First, carbon costs may pass to electricity, transport and
material prices. Targeted transfers, efficient public transport and lifeline protection are
therefore preferable to broad subsidies that blunt the price signal.

Second, industrial transition is place-specific. Workers and coal- or industry-dependent
districts need advance notice, income and pension security, credible reskilling, regional
diversification and site remediation. Auction or compliance revenue can support this transition.

Third, nature-based credits raise land and livelihood questions. Clear tenure, recognition of
customary use, meaningful participation, transparent benefit-sharing, biodiversity safeguards
and accessible grievance mechanisms are as necessary as carbon MRV. An additional project can
still be unjust.

Fourth, market access matters. High validation costs may exclude small farmers, municipalities
and enterprises, while aggregators can acquire excessive bargaining power. Standardised
methodologies, public data and transparent fees can widen participation.

Finally, India should authorise international transfers selectively. Selling low-cost mitigation
too cheaply may increase the future cost of its own NDC. A just market must therefore combine
environmental integrity with distributive, procedural and recognitional justice. This makes
climate action not only fairer but also more durable against backlash and policy reversal.

## Lesson 6 — Kyoto mechanisms and the Clean Development Mechanism legacy

Progress: 6 / 14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Emissions trading, Joint Implementation and CDM

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: UNFCCC and Kyoto treaty foundation available
CA search: "UNFCCC Kyoto Protocol flexibility mechanisms CDM transition official"
CA found: No distinct news linkage; the focus is treaty evolution and institutional legacy
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: Kyoto's three flexibility mechanisms

```text
KYOTO PROTOCOL: quantified commitments for Annex I Parties
                           |
        +------------------+------------------+
        |                  |                  |
International         Joint               Clean Development
Emissions Trading     Implementation      Mechanism
        |                  |                  |
trade assigned        project in an       project in a
amount units among    Annex I host         non-Annex I host
eligible Annex I      generates ERUs       generates CERs
Parties/entities                           for Annex I use
```

Kyoto created the first large treaty-based experiment in using markets and projects to reduce
the cost of meeting quantified international commitments.

### 1. Why flexibility mechanisms emerged

Greenhouse gases mix globally. A tonne reduced in one location has a similar direct atmospheric
effect to a tonne reduced elsewhere, while abatement costs differ. Kyoto therefore allowed
eligible Parties to use market and project mechanisms alongside domestic action.

The efficiency proposition was:

```text
same aggregate commitment
+ reductions undertaken where marginal cost is lower
= lower total compliance cost
```

The political concern was that excessive external units might substitute for structural domestic
decarbonisation. Supplementarity and eligibility rules sought to manage that tension.

### 2. International Emissions Trading

Parties with assigned amounts could transfer eligible Kyoto units under treaty accounting. This
was allowance/accounting-unit trading rooted in national commitments, not the same as a voluntary
project-credit market.

A key lesson is that surplus units can weaken scarcity. If initial commitments or allocations
are loose, trading efficiently reallocates a weak ambition rather than strengthening it.

### 3. Joint Implementation

Joint Implementation enabled a project in an Annex I host to generate **Emission Reduction
Units (ERUs)** for use by another eligible Annex I participant. Because both host and buyer were
inside quantified commitment accounting, issuance needed an adjustment to the host's assigned
amount to avoid double counting.

This prefigured the Paris concern with paired accounting, though Paris operates in a universal
NDC architecture rather than Kyoto's Annex-I-only commitment system.

### 4. Clean Development Mechanism

The CDM allowed an eligible project in a developing-country host to generate **Certified
Emission Reductions (CERs)** that could be purchased for compliance by developed-country actors.
It pursued two stated purposes:

- assist non-Annex I hosts in achieving sustainable development;
- assist Annex I Parties in meeting Kyoto commitments.

```text
project design
 -> host-country approval
 -> validation
 -> registration
 -> monitoring
 -> verification/certification
 -> CER issuance
```

India became a major host of CDM activities, creating experience in project documentation,
designated authority approval, verification and carbon revenue.

### 5. CDM strengths

The mechanism:

- established global project methodologies;
- created independent validation and verification practices;
- channelled finance to mitigation projects;
- made additionality and baselines central policy concepts;
- built registries and serialised units;
- developed institutional capacity in host countries.

It also demonstrated that rules can evolve through methodology panels, executive oversight and
project-level review.

### 6. CDM criticisms

| Criticism | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Non-additional projects | Credits may pay for outcomes that would occur anyway |
| Inflated baselines | More CERs issued than real mitigation |
| Uneven geography | Finance concentrated in countries and sectors with capacity |
| Industrial-gas incentives | Crediting could distort production incentives |
| Weak sustainable-development scrutiny | Host approval did not always ensure broad local benefit |
| Price collapse/oversupply | Low prices weakened investment signal |
| Limited structural change | Project-by-project gains could coexist with carbon-intensive development |

The strongest balanced view is neither "CDM solved climate finance" nor "CDM produced no value."
It created genuine institutions and some mitigation, while exposing how baseline crediting can
over-credit and distribute benefits unevenly.

### 7. Kyoto-to-Paris transition

Paris Article 6.4 is not a simple renaming of the CDM. Important changes include:

- all Parties have NDCs;
- host-country accounting and authorisation matter;
- overall mitigation in global emissions is built into the mechanism;
- a share of proceeds supports adaptation;
- the mechanism includes both reductions and removals under agreed rules;
- eligible CDM activities and units face transition conditions and deadlines.

As of 3 October 2026, the CDM-to-Article-6.4 transition is an active institutional process. It is
safer to describe eligible transitions and registry arrangements than to assume every old CER
automatically becomes a Paris unit.

### 8. Lessons for India

India's CDM experience provides:

- project-development and verification capacity;
- evidence on additionality and baseline risk;
- familiarity with international buyers;
- a warning against overreliance on cheap external demand;
- a basis for stronger sustainable-development and benefit-sharing safeguards.

Domestic CCTS credits, voluntary credits, CDM CERs and Article 6 units remain legally distinct
unless rules explicitly connect them.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** CDM = projects in non-Annex I hosts; JI = Annex I project mechanism; emissions
  trading = assigned-amount unit trading.
- **Mains:** treat CDM as both institutional legacy and integrity warning.
- **Article 6 bridge:** universal NDC accounting makes host authorisation and corresponding
  adjustments more central under Paris.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The CDM's enduring contribution is not merely a stock of CERs but a
> global methodology-and-verification infrastructure whose additionality, baseline and equity
> failures shaped the stricter accounting ambitions of Paris Article 6.

### Revision notes

1. Kyoto placed quantified commitments mainly on Annex I Parties.
2. Its three flexibility mechanisms were emissions trading, JI and CDM.
3. Emissions trading exchanged eligible Kyoto accounting units.
4. JI generated ERUs from projects in Annex I hosts.
5. CDM generated CERs from eligible projects in non-Annex I hosts.
6. CDM combined sustainable-development and compliance-assistance purposes.
7. India was a major CDM host and gained project-market capacity.
8. CDM institutionalised methodologies, validation, verification and registries.
9. Non-additionality and inflated baselines created over-crediting risk.
10. Uneven access concentrated projects in capable countries and sectors.
11. Oversupply and demand weakness contributed to low CER prices.
12. Project crediting did not guarantee economy-wide structural transition.
13. Article 6.4 is a Paris mechanism, not an automatic continuation of every CDM unit.
14. Transition depends on eligibility, host approval, rules and deadlines.
15. CDM experience should strengthen India's domestic integrity and justice safeguards.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is Paris Article 6.4 not simply the CDM under a new name?

**Model answer:** The CDM operated between Annex I buyers with Kyoto commitments and non-Annex I
hosts without such quantified commitments. Under Paris, every Party has an NDC, so host
authorisation, accounting and possible corresponding adjustments become central. Article 6.4
also includes Paris-specific governance, overall mitigation and transition conditions. Some CDM
activities may transition, but not automatically.

**Misconception to avoid:** Institutional continuity and project transition do not erase the
different treaty architecture.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Assess the legacy of the Clean Development Mechanism for the
Paris carbon-market architecture.

#### Model response (143 words)

The Clean Development Mechanism allowed projects in developing-country hosts to generate
Certified Emission Reductions for developed-country compliance. Its positive legacy is
institutional: methodologies, project design documents, validation, verification, registries and
host-country approval created the first large international crediting infrastructure. India
gained significant project-development and verification experience.

Its weaknesses shaped Paris reforms. Contested additionality, inflated baselines, uneven
geographical access, industrial-gas incentives, weak sustainable-development scrutiny and
oversupply showed that a traded unit can diverge from real mitigation. Project crediting also
could not substitute for economy-wide transition.

Article 6.4 therefore inherits useful capacity but operates in a different system where all
Parties have NDCs. Host authorisation, stronger accounting, overall mitigation, adaptation
finance and transition conditions are consequently more important. The CDM should be viewed as
both an institutional foundation and an integrity warning, not as either a complete failure or
an automatically transferable credit stock.

## Lesson 7 — Paris Agreement Article 6: cooperation with accounting

Progress: 7 / 14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Article 6.2, 6.4 and 6.8 with status as of 3 October 2026

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Paris Agreement Article 6 foundation available
CA search: "UNFCCC Article 6.2 6.4 6.8 operational status 3 October 2026"
CA found: No separate news item; the lesson records the official institutional status
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: one Article, three cooperation routes

```text
PARIS AGREEMENT ARTICLE 6
          |
          +--> 6.2 COOPERATIVE APPROACHES
          |       decentralised/bilateral or plurilateral
          |       ITMOs + authorisation + reporting + corresponding adjustments
          |
          +--> 6.4 PARIS AGREEMENT CREDITING MECHANISM
          |       central UN mechanism under CMA supervision
          |       registered activities -> A6.4 emission reductions
          |
          `--> 6.8 NON-MARKET APPROACHES
                  cooperation without transfer of mitigation units
                  finance + technology + capacity + coordinated policy
```

Article 6 is voluntary. It does not require every country to trade, and it is broader than carbon
markets because Article 6.8 expressly covers non-market cooperation.

### 1. Article 6.2: cooperative approaches

Article 6.2 provides accounting guidance for Parties that use **Internationally Transferred
Mitigation Outcomes (ITMOs)** toward NDCs or other authorised international mitigation purposes.
The cooperation can be bilateral, regional or plurilateral; the UN does not run a single
project-credit programme for every 6.2 approach.

Core controls include:

- participation requirements;
- authorisation by participating Parties;
- tracking of ITMOs;
- corresponding adjustments;
- initial, annual and regular reporting;
- technical expert review;
- consistency with sustainable development and environmental integrity.

ITMOs may be measured in tCO2e or another permitted non-GHG metric when rules are met. They are
accounting outcomes, not a single branded credit type.

### 2. Article 6.4: centralised crediting

Article 6.4 establishes a mechanism under the authority and guidance of the Conference of the
Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement, supervised by the Article
6.4 Supervisory Body. Activities must follow approved standards, methodologies, validation,
registration, monitoring, verification and issuance processes.

The mechanism is commonly called the **Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism (PACM)**. Units are
Article 6.4 emission reductions. Their accounting treatment depends on host authorisation:

| Treatment | Basic meaning |
|---|---|
| Authorised outcome | May be used for an authorised international purpose; corresponding adjustment applies |
| Mitigation contribution outcome | Contributes to host mitigation without authorisation for such external use; host retains the accounting benefit |

Terminology in operational documents must be used carefully; authorisation status matters more
than a buyer's marketing label.

### 3. Overall mitigation and adaptation share

Article 6.4 includes two features beyond ordinary bilateral trading:

- a cancellation arrangement intended to deliver **overall mitigation in global emissions**;
- a **share of proceeds** from mechanism activities to assist particularly vulnerable developing
  countries with adaptation costs.

These do not remove the need for strong baselines and additionality.

### 4. Article 6.8: non-market approaches

Article 6.8 recognises integrated, holistic and balanced cooperation that does not rely on
transfer of mitigation outcomes. Examples can combine:

- mitigation and adaptation;
- finance;
- technology development and transfer;
- capacity-building;
- coordinated fiscal, regulatory or resilience measures.

It is incorrect to call Article 6 entirely a carbon-market article.

### 5. Corresponding adjustment in practice

```text
Host mitigation occurs
    -> host decides whether to authorise transfer/use
    -> authorised outcome enters 6.2/authorised 6.4 accounting
    -> paired corresponding adjustments prevent double claim
    -> reports enter Article 6 transparency and review systems
```

Authorisation is a sovereign policy decision. A host can reserve sectors or technologies for its
own NDC, attach conditions, limit quantities or decline authorisation.

### 6. Current institutional status — 3 October 2026

The safe status description is:

1. The Article 6 rulebook and accounting guidance are adopted and being implemented.
2. Article 6.2 reporting operates through UNFCCC infrastructure including the Centralized
   Accounting and Reporting Platform, with technical review and registry arrangements continuing
   to mature.
3. The Article 6.4 Supervisory Body has adopted core standards and procedures. PACM activity
   registration, methodology work, verifier accreditation and CDM transition are operational
   processes.
4. An interim mechanism registry supports functions while the full registry architecture is
   completed and migrated.
5. Eligible CDM transitions and eligible CER transfers are governed by specific deadlines and
   conditions; no blanket conversion should be assumed.
6. Article 6.8 continues through its work programme and Glasgow Committee rather than through a
   tradeable-credit registry.

This status does **not** justify describing Article 6 as a mature, deep and fully liquid global
market. Legal rules, national authorisations, methodologies, infrastructure and actual
transactions advance at different speeds.

### 7. Integrity and ambition risks

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Double counting | Authorisation, registries, corresponding adjustments |
| Weak baseline | Conservative methodology and review |
| NDC overselling | Host strategy and quantitative limits |
| Rights harm | Safeguards, consultation, grievance and appeal |
| Mitigation deterrence | Strong domestic action and careful claims |
| Inconsistent reporting | Common formats and technical expert review |

### 8. India and Article 6

India has identified eligible activity categories for Article 6.2 cooperation through its
national authority. This is separate from automatic eligibility under the domestic CCTS.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain the architecture of Paris Agreement Article 6 and

from claiming the same outcome.

Hence Article 6 requires conservative methodologies, transparent registries, independent review,
safeguards and strategic national authorisation. As of October 2026, rules and institutions are
operationalising, but infrastructure and transaction depth remain uneven; success should be
judged by atmospheric outcomes, not market volume.

The amendment came into force on 1 January 2023. This is a statutory framework, not a purely

### 2. CCTS notification and two mechanisms

The Ministry of Power notified the **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023** on 28 June 2023. The
framework now contains:

- a **compliance mechanism** for obligated entities with notified greenhouse-gas emission
  intensity targets; and
- an **offset mechanism** for eligible non-obligated activities to register and seek certificates

The 2023 amendment to the scheme incorporated the offset mechanism. Participation in an offset
project may be voluntary, but issuance remains within a government framework.

### 3. National Steering Committee for Indian Carbon Market

The National Steering Committee provides oversight and recommends procedures, targets, sectors,
methodologies and market development. Its inter-ministerial structure links the Ministry of Power

not an economy-wide absolute cap.

### 2. Offset mechanism

The offset mechanism allows eligible non-obligated entities to register mitigation activities

The mechanism should exclude reductions already required by law unless the methodology
demonstrates eligible performance beyond that obligation.

### 3. Compliance credits and offsets are not identical

Compliance performance credits arise because an obligated entity beats its notified intensity

methodologies. They can coexist, but scheme rules must prevent:

- the same reduction being credited twice;
- an obligated entity turning mandatory performance into an offset;
- overlapping PAT and CCTS claims;
- duplicate renewable attributes;
- a domestic use and international export claim without reconciliation.

### 4. Green Credit Programme boundary

Green credits reward specified environmental actions under separate Green Credit Rules. The
underlying action may have climate co-benefits, but a green credit is not automatically
denominated as a verified tCO2e reduction or removal. Converting or linking attributes requires
explicit rules and carbon quantification.

```text
tree-planting action
    -> may earn a green credit under its rules
    -> may create carbon sequestration over time
    -> does not automatically create a carbon credit for the same action
```

This protects against attribute stacking without proof.

### 5. Article 6 export is a sovereign layer

A domestic CCC does not automatically become an internationally transferred mitigation outcome.
For Article 6 use, India must consider:

- activity eligibility;
- national authorisation;
- NDC accounting;
- corresponding adjustment where required;
- sustainable-development safeguards;
- registry interoperability;
- whether export serves technology and finance goals.

India has published eligible Article 6.2 activity categories, including selected emerging
technologies. Eligibility is a gateway, not guaranteed issuance or authorisation.

### 6. Carbon-border relationship

India's domestic carbon price may support measurement capability and industrial transition, but
creditability against a foreign border charge depends on that foreign measure's rules. A domestic
certificate should not be assumed to eliminate the border liability.

The strategic response includes:

- product-level emissions data;
- cleaner electricity and fuels;
- material efficiency;
- green hydrogen and process innovation;
- CCUS for genuine residual process emissions;
- diplomacy on equivalence and equity.

### 7. Federal and sectoral implementation

Industry, power, waste, agriculture and forestry involve different ministries and State
authorities. Harmonisation requires common data definitions while preserving sector-specific
methods. State pollution-control data, electricity accounts, industrial production data and land

## Lesson 10 — CCUS chain: from separation to storage

Progress: 10 / 14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Capture, conditioning, transport, utilisation and geological storage

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: CCUS technical foundation available
CA search: "IEA IPCC CCUS capture transport utilisation storage chain official"
CA found: No additional current-affairs item; the lesson teaches the technical chain
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: CCUS is a chain, not a single machine

```text
CO2 SOURCE
cement kiln / steel / refinery / fertiliser / power
      |
      v
CAPTURE AND SEPARATION
post-combustion / pre-combustion / oxy-fuel / process stream
      |
      v
DEHYDRATION + PURIFICATION + COMPRESSION
      |
      v
TRANSPORT
pipeline / ship / road or rail for smaller volumes
      |
      +--> UTILISATION --> fuels / chemicals / concrete / other products
      |                    | climate value depends on lifecycle and duration
      |
      `--> STORAGE --> deep saline formation / depleted hydrocarbon field
                           |
                           v
                     injection + monitoring + closure + liability
```

If one link is absent, the chain fails. A capture plant without transport and verified storage
does not deliver durable sequestration.

### 1. What CCUS means

**Carbon Capture, Utilisation and Storage** covers technologies that separate CO2 from an
industrial or energy-related stream, condition and transport it, then use it or inject it into a
storage formation. Standard point-source CCUS normally prevents a portion of newly generated CO2
from reaching the atmosphere. It is therefore emissions avoidance/reduction, not automatically
carbon dioxide removal.

### 2. Capture routes

| Route | Basic mechanism | Suitable context |
|---|---|---|
| Post-combustion | Separates CO2 from flue gas after fuel combustion | Retrofit or conventional combustion streams |
| Pre-combustion | Converts fuel to a synthesis gas, separates CO2 before final combustion/use | Integrated gasification or hydrogen/ammonia routes |
| Oxy-fuel | Burns fuel in oxygen-rich conditions, producing a CO2-rich exhaust after water removal | Selected power/industrial designs |
| Process-stream separation | Captures CO2 already present at relatively high concentration | Natural-gas processing, ammonia/fertiliser and some industrial processes |

Capture cost generally falls when CO2 concentration and pressure are higher, though impurities,
plant design and integration also matter.

### 3. Solvent example

In common post-combustion systems, an absorbent binds CO2. Heat then regenerates the solvent and
releases a concentrated CO2 stream.

```text
flue gas -> absorber -> cleaned gas
              |
          CO2-rich solvent
              |
          regeneration heat
              |
          concentrated CO2 + recycled solvent
```

The example explains the energy penalty: solvent regeneration and CO2 compression consume heat
and power. It does not imply that every capture system uses the same solvent.

### 4. Conditioning and compression

Captured CO2 may contain water, oxygen, sulphur compounds or other impurities. Specifications
protect pipelines, ships, wells and storage performance. Compression increases density for
transport and injection. These steps add cost and lifecycle emissions and therefore belong inside
the system boundary.

### 5. Transport

Pipelines suit large, stable volumes and clusters. Ships can connect dispersed coastal sources to
shared storage. Road and rail can serve smaller or demonstration volumes but usually have higher
unit costs and logistics limits.

Shared hubs can reduce cost:

```text
many industrial sources -> common CO2 network -> shared storage basin
```

They also create coordination risk: one delayed source, pipeline or storage permit can affect the
whole cluster.

### 6. Utilisation is not automatically storage

CO2 use can produce:

- urea or chemicals;
- synthetic fuels;
- building materials;
- beverages or industrial applications;
- enhanced hydrocarbon recovery.

The climate result depends on:

1. whether the CO2 is later re-released;
2. what product or process is displaced;
3. energy source;
4. additional emissions from feedstocks;
5. storage duration;
6. whether increased fossil production occurs.

Synthetic fuel may recycle carbon rather than store it. Mineralised CO2 in suitable material can
be more durable. Enhanced oil recovery requires full lifecycle accounting and cannot be assumed
climate-positive.

### 7. Geological storage

CO2 is injected into deep porous rock beneath an impermeable sealing layer. Major storage types
include deep saline formations and depleted oil or gas fields. Security arises through:

- structural trapping beneath caprock;
- residual trapping in pore spaces;
- dissolution into formation fluid;
- gradual mineral trapping.

Site characterisation must assess capacity, injectivity, faults, pressure, old wells and induced-
seismicity risk.

### 8. Capture rate versus system avoidance

A unit may report a high capture rate at the capture equipment while upstream fuel extraction,
extra energy use and uncaptured plant emissions lower system-wide avoidance. Answers should
distinguish:

```text
equipment capture rate
        from
net lifecycle emissions avoided
```

### 9. Hard-to-abate role

CCUS is strongest where emissions arise from process chemistry or where alternatives remain
difficult:

- calcination in cement;
- selected iron and steel routes;
- hydrogen/ammonia from fossil feedstock during transition;
- refinery and chemical streams;
- waste-to-energy residuals.

It is weaker as a blanket justification for new high-carbon capacity when efficient renewable
electricity, electrification or material substitution can cut emissions more directly.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** point-source capture differs from ambient-air capture.
- **Mains:** structure CCUS as capture → conditioning → transport → use/store → MRV.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish utilisation, temporary recycling and durable geological storage.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** CCUS is a linked infrastructure system whose climate value equals net
> lifecycle emissions avoided and durably stored, not the gross CO2 entering a capture unit.

### Revision notes

1. CCUS captures CO2 from a point source.
2. Standard fossil/process CCUS is normally emissions avoidance, not atmospheric removal.
3. Capture routes include post-combustion, pre-combustion, oxy-fuel and process separation.
4. Higher-concentration streams are generally easier to separate.
5. Solvent regeneration and compression contribute to the energy penalty.
6. Dehydration and impurity control protect transport and storage.
7. Pipelines suit large stable flows; shipping can connect coastal clusters.
8. Shared hubs reduce cost but create coordination dependence.
9. Utilisation does not necessarily provide durable storage.
10. Synthetic fuels usually recycle captured carbon that is later released.
11. Mineralisation can provide longer-lived storage where lifecycle accounting supports it.
12. Geological storage uses porous reservoir rock and sealing caprock.
13. Structural, residual, dissolution and mineral trapping act over different timescales.
14. Site characterisation must examine faults, wells, pressure and injectivity.
15. Equipment capture rate is not the same as net lifecycle avoidance.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can two projects that each capture one million tonnes of CO2 have very different
climate value?

**Model answer:** Their net outcomes can differ because of capture source, energy supply,
upstream emissions, capture rate, transport losses, utilisation pathway, storage duration,
additional fossil production, leakage risk and displacement of another product. Gross captured
tonnage is only the first measurement; lifecycle emissions and durable containment determine the
atmospheric value.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equal gross capture does not imply equal net mitigation or permanence.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** What is CCUS? Explain its potential role and principal
limitations in climate mitigation.

#### Model response (137 words)

Carbon Capture, Utilisation and Storage separates CO2 from a concentrated industrial stream,
transports it, and uses or injects it into a geological reservoir. Its strongest role is in
residual process emissions from cement, steel, fertiliser, refinery and chemical operations where
efficiency, electrification or green hydrogen cannot eliminate every emission.

However, standard point-source CCUS avoids new emissions; it does not remove historical CO2 from
ambient air. Capture and compression consume energy and require coordinated transport and storage.
Utilisation may release CO2 quickly, while storage needs suitable geology, monitoring and
liability. Equipment capture rates can overstate lifecycle avoidance if upstream emissions are
omitted.

CCUS should therefore complement rapid renewable deployment, efficiency, material substitution
and direct electrification. It is most defensible for residual process emissions with transparent
lifecycle MRV and durable storage, not as a general licence for fossil lock-in.

## Lesson 11 — Geological storage: MRV, liability, energy penalty and economics

Progress: 11 / 14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Reservoir performance, long-term responsibility and business models

CA found: No additional current-affairs item; advanced engineering and governance are the focus

### Visual: storage assurance across time

```text
BEFORE INJECTION
basin screening -> seismic survey -> wells/faults -> capacity/injectivity -> permit
       |
DURING INJECTION
mass metering -> pressure control -> plume tracking -> well integrity -> leakage response
       |
AFTER INJECTION
closure test -> continued monitoring -> financial security -> liability transfer conditions
       |
LONG TERM

```

### 1. Capacity is not the same as usable storage

Theoretical pore volume can greatly exceed practically bankable capacity. A usable site needs:

- adequate porosity and permeability;
- injectivity at acceptable pressure;
- competent caprock;
- manageable faults and old wells;
- access and permits;
- proximity or transport connection;
- public acceptance;
- monitoring feasibility;
- defined liability.

National storage-resource estimates should therefore distinguish theoretical, effective,
practical and permitted capacity.

### 2. How geological trapping strengthens over time

Initially, buoyant CO2 is retained beneath caprock through structural trapping. As injection
continues and stops:

1. some becomes immobilised as disconnected droplets through residual trapping;
2. some dissolves in formation water;
3. a smaller fraction may react to form minerals over longer periods.

The increasing role of residual, solubility and mineral trapping can improve security, but
monitoring remains necessary because wells and faults can provide pathways.

### 3. Storage MRV

| Stage | Measurement or evidence |
|---|---|
| Capture/transfer | Calibrated mass flow and CO2 composition |
| Injection | Wellhead pressure, temperature and injected mass |
| Reservoir | Seismic, pressure, tracer or other site-specific monitoring |
| Surface | Soil gas, groundwater or atmospheric monitoring where risk warrants |
| Accounting | Reconcile captured, transported, injected, leaked and retained quantities |

Monitoring plans should be risk-based. Repeating every possible measurement everywhere can be
costly without improving assurance; omitting a known migration pathway is unsafe.

### 4. Leakage and remediation

Potential pathways include defective injection wells, abandoned legacy wells, transmissive
faults and caprock failure. Responses may include reducing injection pressure, repairing wells,
changing injection location, extracting fluids or CO2, and compensating affected parties.

The policy test is not "zero imaginable risk." It is whether the site is selected, operated and
regulated so that expected retention is high, deviations are detected and responsibility is
clear.

### 5. Long-term liability

Liability covers:

- monitoring and remediation;
- environmental or health damage;
- reversal of credited tonnes;
- closure and post-closure costs;
- stewardship after an operator ceases to exist.

A possible model keeps operator responsibility through injection and a post-closure stability
period, requires financial security, and permits conditional transfer to the State only after
specified evidence. Automatic early transfer can socialise private risk; indefinite private
liability may deter investment or become unenforceable.

### 6. Energy penalty

Capture equipment needs heat and power. Compression, pumping and injection add further energy.
For a power plant, this can reduce net output or increase fuel use for the same delivered
electricity. For an industrial facility, it can raise product cost and upstream emissions.

```text
net avoided emissions
= uncaptured reference emissions
- residual stack emissions
- extra energy emissions
- transport/storage emissions
- leakage
```

The exact penalty is technology- and site-specific. Avoid universal percentages without a dated,
comparable source.

### 7. Economics

CCUS cost varies with:

- CO2 concentration and pressure;
- retrofit difficulty;
- capture rate;
- energy price and carbon intensity;
- distance and transport mode;
- storage appraisal and injection rate;
- financing cost;
- operating reliability;
- carbon price, tax credit or contract support.

High-purity process streams can be cheaper initial opportunities. Cement process emissions are
strategically important but can be costly because kilns require integrated capture and heat
management.

### 8. Network economics and cross-chain risk

Shared transport and storage lower average cost but need coordination:

| Risk | Example |
|---|---|
| Volume risk | Fewer emitters connect than planned |
| Timing risk | Storage ready after capture plant |
| Counterparty risk | Anchor emitter closes |
| Monopoly risk | Single pipeline/storage operator charges excessive tariff |
| Stranded-asset risk | Faster direct decarbonisation reduces CO2 supply |

Regulated-access rules, phased capacity, public co-investment and transparent tariffs can address
these risks.

### 9. Utilisation hierarchy

An exam-safe hierarchy asks:

1. Is the use durable?
2. Does it displace a more carbon-intensive product?
3. Is clean energy used?
4. Is the carbon later released?
5. Does it stimulate additional fossil extraction?

The word "utilisation" is not itself a climate benefit.

### 10. Qualified policy role

CCUS can be justified when:

- residual process emissions remain after direct mitigation;
- storage and MRV are credible;
- lifecycle avoidance is material;
- the facility is compatible with a transition pathway;
- public support buys verifiable learning or infrastructure rather than indefinite fossil lock-in.

### UPSC integration

- **Mains depth:** add usable-versus-theoretical capacity, long-term liability and network risk.
- **Prelims trap:** saline formations are not underground lakes; CO2 occupies pore spaces in rock.
- **Policy evaluation:** compare public support per net tonne and strategic industrial value.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Geological storage becomes a climate asset only when usable capacity,
> measured injection, plume control, long-term liability and reversal accounting are integrated
> across the full CO2 chain.

### Revision notes

1. Theoretical pore volume is not equal to bankable storage capacity.
2. Porosity stores fluids; permeability permits flow and injection.
3. Caprock, faults and old wells shape containment risk.
4. Structural trapping dominates early containment.
5. Residual and dissolution trapping can strengthen security over time.
6. Mineral trapping is generally slower.
7. Storage MRV reconciles captured, transported, injected, leaked and retained CO2.
8. Monitoring should follow site-specific risk.
9. Financial security supports closure and remediation.
10. Liability transfer should be conditional, not automatic.
11. Energy penalty reduces net avoidance and raises cost.
12. Cost depends strongly on source concentration, retrofit and infrastructure.
13. Shared networks create economies of scale and cross-chain dependence.
14. Utilisation requires lifecycle and duration analysis.
15. Public support should target strategic residual emissions and verifiable learning.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a country with a large theoretical geological storage resource still face a
CCUS deployment constraint?

**Model answer:** Theoretical capacity does not guarantee injectivity, secure caprock, acceptable
pressure, absence of problematic wells, proximity to sources, permits, public acceptance,
monitorability or clear liability. Appraisal and transport infrastructure take time and capital.
Only characterised, accessible and governable capacity can support bankable storage.

**Misconception to avoid:** A national pore-volume estimate is not the same as a permitted,
connected and commercially injectable reservoir.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Discuss the technical and regulatory conditions required for
secure geological storage of carbon dioxide.

#### Model response (199 words)

Secure geological storage requires an integrated reservoir and governance system. Technically,
the formation must have adequate porosity, permeability and injectivity, while competent caprock
limits upward migration. Site characterisation should map faults, pressure behaviour, groundwater
and abandoned wells. Deep saline formations and depleted hydrocarbon fields may be suitable, but
theoretical capacity must be converted into appraised, accessible capacity.

During operation, calibrated meters should reconcile captured, transported and injected CO2.
Wellhead pressure, plume movement and well integrity require risk-based monitoring through
seismic, pressure, tracer or other site-specific methods. Operators need response plans for
unexpected migration, including pressure reduction, well repair and remediation.

Regulation must define pore-space rights, permits, transport access, closure criteria and
financial security. The operator should retain responsibility through injection and a
post-closure stability period. Any transfer of long-term stewardship to the State should occur
only after evidence of stable containment, complete records and funded residual risk. Credited
reversals must trigger replacement or accounting correction.

Finally, lifecycle MRV must deduct capture energy, compression, transport and leakage. Public
support should depend on net tonnes securely stored and strategic value in hard-to-abate sectors.
Thus, geology supplies the reservoir, but monitoring, liability and financial assurance convert
it into a credible climate service.

### 1. CDR definition

Carbon dioxide removal comprises human activities that remove CO2 from the atmosphere and store
it durably in geological, terrestrial, oceanic or product reservoirs. Removal is not identical to:

- avoided emissions;
- conventional point-source CCS;
- natural uptake that would occur without human intervention;
- temporary capture followed by rapid release.

### 2. Why removal appears in net-zero pathways

Even after deep mitigation, some residual emissions may remain from agriculture, aviation,
industrial processes or other difficult sources. Removal can balance those residuals at net zero
and may later reduce atmospheric CO2. The sequence matters:

```text
rapid gross-emissions reduction first
      -> minimise residual emissions
      -> use high-integrity removal for the residual
```

Using speculative future removal to justify avoidable present emissions increases scale, cost and
delivery risk.

### 3. Direct Air Capture process

Ambient air passes through a contactor containing a chemical sorbent or solvent that selectively
binds CO2. Energy regenerates the material and releases a concentrated stream.

```text
ambient air
   -> fan/contactor
   -> CO2 binds to solid sorbent or liquid solvent
   -> heat, vacuum, moisture or chemical regeneration
   -> concentrated CO2
   -> compression
   -> storage or use
```

Solid-sorbent and liquid-solvent systems use different regeneration conditions. The governing
challenge is the same: atmospheric CO2 is dilute, so large air volumes and significant energy
are needed.

### 4. DAC versus DACCS

**DAC** describes separation from ambient air. **DACCS** adds durable storage. If captured CO2
becomes synthetic aviation fuel and is soon combusted, the process recycles atmospheric carbon
and may lower fossil demand, but it does not provide durable removal. If it is injected and
securely stored with lifecycle deductions, it can provide removal.

| Path | Carbon outcome |
|---|---|
| DAC + geological storage | Potential durable removal |
| DAC + mineralised durable product | Potential removal, subject to lifecycle/duration |
| DAC + synthetic fuel | Carbon recycling/avoidance relative to fossil fuel, not durable storage |
| DAC + short-lived product | Temporary use followed by release |

### 5. Energy and location

DAC's net value depends on low-carbon heat and electricity. A plant powered by high-emission
energy can remove much less net CO2 than its gross capture. Location should consider:

- clean energy;
- water needs of the chosen process;
- climate effects on sorbents;
- storage proximity;
- transport;
- land and community impacts.

DAC is modular in principle, but storage and energy systems remain place-bound.

### 6. BECCS

Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage uses biomass, captures the resulting biogenic CO2 and
stores it. It can be net-negative only after accounting for:

- whether biomass regrows;
- land-use change;
- fertiliser and farm emissions;
- harvesting and transport;
- foregone ecosystem carbon;
- capture rate and energy;
- storage permanence.

Bioenergy from residues can differ greatly from dedicated crops that displace food, forests or
communities.

### 7. Nature-based removal

Afforestation, reforestation, ecosystem restoration, agroforestry and soil-carbon management can
remove carbon while supporting biodiversity, water and livelihoods. Their advantages include
near-term availability and co-benefits. Limitations include:

- saturation;
- fire, drought, pests and harvest reversal;
- land competition;
- measurement uncertainty;
- dependence on continuing management;
- non-equivalence with permanent fossil-carbon storage.

Protecting existing high-carbon ecosystems avoids emissions and preserves sinks; it should not
always be described as a new removal.

### 8. Enhanced weathering and ocean methods

Spreading suitable reactive minerals can accelerate chemical reactions that consume atmospheric
CO2. Ocean alkalinity approaches seek to increase the ocean's capacity to store carbon. These
methods require careful assessment of mining, grinding, transport, energy, ecological effects,
monitoring and governance. Their scientific promise does not remove the need for field evidence.

### 9. Removal portfolio

| Method | Storage | Main strength | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forest/soil | Biological/terrestrial | Co-benefits and current availability | Reversal, land and measurement |
| BECCS | Geological after biomass route | Energy plus potential removal | Biomass sustainability and land |
| DACCS | Geological | Measurable atmospheric source; limited land coupling | High energy and cost |
| Enhanced weathering | Mineral/ocean bicarbonate pathways | Potential durability | Material scale and MRV |

No method is unlimited or impact-free. Portfolios reduce dependence on one uncertain route.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** DAC captures ambient CO2; DACCS stores it; synthetic fuel use is not durable removal.
- **Mains:** use residual-emissions logic and compare technology, storage, land and permanence.
- **Evaluation:** always subtract lifecycle emissions and state storage duration.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Carbon dioxide removal is an atmosphere-to-reservoir service; Direct
> Air Capture becomes durable removal only when low-carbon capture is followed by secure storage
> and full lifecycle accounting.

### Revision notes

1. CDR removes CO2 from ambient air and stores it.
2. Conventional point-source CCS is not by itself CDR.
3. Net zero requires gross reductions plus balancing of residual emissions.
4. Removal should not substitute for feasible near-term mitigation.
5. DAC uses solid sorbents or liquid solvents to separate dilute atmospheric CO2.
6. Regeneration and compression require substantial energy.
7. DACCS means DAC followed by durable storage.
8. DAC-derived synthetic fuel recycles carbon rather than storing it permanently.
9. Clean energy determines net DAC removal.
10. BECCS needs sustainable biomass and geological storage.
11. Land-use change can erase BECCS benefits.
12. Forest and soil methods offer co-benefits but face reversal and saturation.
13. Protecting an existing sink may be avoided emission, not a new removal.
14. Enhanced weathering and ocean methods need lifecycle and ecological MRV.
15. A diversified removal portfolio reduces technological and ecological dependence.

### Concept check

**Question:** A company uses DAC-derived CO2 to make aviation fuel. May it describe the full
captured amount as permanent carbon removal?

**Model answer:** No. The fuel returns the CO2 to the atmosphere when combusted. The pathway may
recycle atmospheric carbon and avoid some fossil extraction if powered cleanly, but it does not
durably store the captured carbon. Any mitigation claim must use lifecycle comparison with the
displaced fuel and must not label temporary use as permanent removal.

**Misconception to avoid:** Atmospheric origin alone does not create durable removal; destination
and storage time are decisive.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Distinguish carbon dioxide removal from emissions avoidance,
and compare DACCS, BECCS and nature-based removal.

#### Model response (205 words)

Emissions avoidance prevents a new greenhouse-gas flow, whereas carbon dioxide removal withdraws
CO2 already present in the atmosphere and stores it. Conventional point-source CCS is therefore
normally avoidance; Direct Air Carbon Capture and Storage, sustainably designed BECCS and
additional ecosystem sequestration can qualify as removal.

DACCS chemically separates dilute atmospheric CO2 and stores it geologically. Its advantages are
a measurable atmospheric feedstock, modularity and limited dependence on fertile land. Its
constraints are high energy and cost, clean-power requirements and the need for transport and
storage.

BECCS uses biomass for energy, captures the biogenic CO2 and stores it. It can combine energy and
removal, but only if regrowth, land-use change, fertiliser, transport, capture and storage are
accounted for. Dedicated biomass at large scale may compete with food, water, biodiversity and
community rights.

Forests, soils and ecosystem restoration are available now and can provide biodiversity,
livelihood and adaptation co-benefits. Yet biological carbon can saturate or reverse through
fire, drought, harvest and management change, and measurement is difficult.

Accordingly, removals are not interchangeable tonnes. Policy should reduce gross emissions
rapidly, reserve removals for residuals, match claims to storage duration, protect rights and use
a diversified portfolio. Removal complements mitigation; speculative future scale must not
justify avoidable emissions today.

## Lesson 13 — Removal integrity, limits and moral hazard

### Visual: the removal claim must survive five filters

```text
GROSS REMOVAL CLAIM
      |
      +--> lifecycle emissions deducted?
      |
      +--> additional to baseline?
      |
      +--> storage duration matched to claim?
      |
      +--> reversal and liability covered?
      |
      +--> harms, rights and opportunity costs assessed?
      |
      `--> only then: NET, DURABLE, GOVERNABLE REMOVAL
```

### 1. Durability is not binary

Carbon can be stored for years, decades, centuries or much longer. A crediting system must decide
how storage duration relates to a fossil CO2 emission whose climatic influence is long-lived.

Possible approaches include:

- tonne-for-tonne claims only for highly durable storage;
- temporary crediting with renewal;
- discounting shorter-lived storage;
- buffer pools and replacement;
- separate unit classes by durability.

Treating all storage durations as perfectly equivalent hides reversal risk.

### 2. Measurement asymmetry

Geological injection can often be metered directly, though storage integrity still needs
monitoring. Landscape removals involve heterogeneous biomass and soils, uncertain counterfactuals
and natural variability. This does not make nature valueless; it means uncertainty deductions and
different monitoring designs are necessary.

### 3. Reversal under correlated climate risk

A buffer pool works best when project failures are independent. Wildfire, drought or pest
outbreak can affect many projects simultaneously, creating correlated loss. Climate change can
therefore threaten the very biological stores used to compensate for fossil emissions.

Risk management should include geographical diversity, conservative crediting, dynamic buffers,
insurance and a backstop liability rule.

### 4. Physical and social scale limits

| Method | Scaling constraint |
|---|---|
| DACCS | Clean energy, sorbents, capital, storage and infrastructure |
| BECCS | Sustainable biomass, land, water, nutrients and transport |
| Afforestation | Land rights, ecosystem suitability, permanence and saturation |
| Soil carbon | Measurement, saturation and continued practice |
| Enhanced weathering | Mining, grinding, logistics, ecology and verification |

A modelled potential is not a project pipeline. Policy should distinguish technical, sustainable,
economic and deployable potential.

### 5. Moral hazard and mitigation deterrence

Moral hazard arises when expectation of future removal weakens current pressure to reduce gross
emissions. It appears through:

- delayed retirement of high-carbon assets;
- inflated "net-zero" claims with no gross-emissions pathway;
- reliance on technologies not yet deployable at assumed scale;
- use of cheap temporary removals against continued fossil emissions;
- lobbying for public removal subsidy without direct-abatement obligations.

Removal is still necessary in many pathways. The solution is governance, not rejection:

```text
separate gross-reduction target
+ separate residual-emissions estimate
+ separate removal target by method/durability
+ transparent progress and contingency plan
```

### 6. Substitution versus contribution claims

A **substitution/offset claim** says a removal compensates for an emission. This requires strong
equivalence in quantity, additionality, durability and exclusive claim. A **contribution claim**
says finance supports mitigation beyond the buyer's value chain without asserting that its own
emissions have disappeared.

Contribution claims can be more honest where exact equivalence is weak, but they still require
real outcomes and transparent finance.

### 7. Intergenerational liability

Future generations may inherit monitoring and remediation obligations after present firms earn
credits. Financial assurance, durable records and institutional stewardship should therefore be
funded before closure, not left as an unfunded promise.

### 8. Portfolio governance

A public removal strategy should:

1. prioritise ecosystem protection and direct emissions cuts;
2. support learning across multiple removal methods;
3. require lifecycle MRV;
4. classify storage durability;
5. cap exposure to any one method;
6. protect food, water, biodiversity and tenure;
7. create long-term liability and remedy;
8. avoid counting the same removal toward multiple targets.

### 9. Net-zero integrity

```text
credible net zero
= deep gross emissions reduction
+ small, explicitly defined residual
+ high-integrity removal matched to residual
+ no double claim
+ continuing post-target monitoring
```

The word "net" is not permission to conceal the gross side of the equation.

### UPSC integration

- **Mains:** use moral hazard as a qualification, not as a reason to dismiss all removals.
- **Essay/Ethics:** intergenerational liability and claims honesty.
- **Policy:** demand separate reporting of gross emissions, residuals and removals.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Removal governance must prevent the promise of tomorrow's negative
> emissions from becoming a licence for today's avoidable emissions, while still building the
> durable capacity needed for genuine residuals.

### Revision notes

1. Storage duration varies across removal methods.
2. Short-lived and geological storage should not be assumed perfectly equivalent.
3. Temporary crediting, discounting and replacement are possible responses.
4. Landscape measurement faces heterogeneity and counterfactual uncertainty.
5. Biological reversals can be correlated across projects.
6. Dynamic buffers and backstop liability improve risk management.
7. Modelled potential exceeds sustainable and deployable potential.
8. DAC scale depends on clean energy and storage infrastructure.
9. BECCS and afforestation face land, water and rights limits.
10. Moral hazard means future removal expectations can weaken present mitigation.
11. Gross-reduction and removal targets should be reported separately.
12. Offset claims demand stronger equivalence than contribution claims.
13. Contribution finance still requires real and transparent outcomes.
14. Financial assurance should precede long-term liability transfer.
15. Net zero must disclose gross emissions, residuals and removal composition.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a buffer pool less reliable when climate change causes regional wildfire
across many forest projects?

**Model answer:** A buffer assumes that losses from some projects can be covered by units reserved
from others. Regional wildfire creates correlated reversal, so many projects may fail together
and exhaust the common reserve. Conservative issuance, geographically diversified risk,
dynamic buffers, insurance and a backstop replacement obligation are therefore needed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Pooling removes idiosyncratic risk more easily than system-wide
climate risk; it does not guarantee permanence.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Carbon dioxide removal is necessary for net zero but can also
weaken mitigation. Critically analyse.

#### Model response (202 words)

Carbon dioxide removal is necessary because deep decarbonisation may still leave residual
emissions from agriculture, aviation and industrial processes. DACCS, sustainably designed
BECCS, ecosystem restoration and mineral routes can balance such residuals and, eventually,
lower atmospheric CO2.

However, necessity can become mitigation deterrence. Firms or governments may postpone closure
of avoidable high-carbon assets by assuming large future removals. Modelled potentials may ignore
clean-energy, land, water, storage and social constraints. Short-lived biological storage may be
treated as equivalent to long-lived fossil emissions, while wildfire or land-use change reverses
the credited carbon. Gross capture claims may also omit lifecycle emissions.

Governance should therefore impose a mitigation hierarchy. Net-zero plans must disclose separate
gross-reduction trajectories, residual-emissions estimates and removal targets by method and
durability. Credits need additionality, conservative baselines, lifecycle MRV, reversal buffers
and replacement liability. Nature-based methods require tenure, biodiversity and food-water
safeguards; engineered methods require clean energy, geological storage and financial assurance.
Contribution claims should be preferred where tonne-for-tonne equivalence is weak.

The correct conclusion is neither to reject removals nor to use them as an unlimited offset.
Policy must reduce avoidable emissions immediately while building a diversified, governed
removal portfolio for genuine residuals. This preserves both present ambition and future net-zero
feasibility.

```text
         |       material efficiency + process change + CCUS for residuals
         |
         +--> methane/waste
         |       prevention + capture + utilisation with leakage control
         |
         `--> residual economy-wide emissions
                 high-integrity CDR, including future DACCS where justified

Carbon market = incentive/accounting layer across the physical transition
```

### 1. Cement

Cement emits CO2 from fuel and from calcination of limestone. Efficiency, alternative fuels,
clinker substitution, material efficiency and renewable power can reduce emissions. Residual
process emissions make capture strategically relevant.

Policy evaluation should ask:

- Is the plant efficient before capture support?
- What capture rate applies to total kiln emissions?
- Is low-carbon energy available?
- Is there transport to durable storage?
- Does public support reward net tonnes and learning?
- Can low-clinker materials reduce demand first?

### 2. Iron and steel

Routes differ. Scrap-based electric arc furnaces, direct reduced iron using progressively greener
hydrogen, efficiency and material circularity can reduce emissions. CCUS may support selected
blast-furnace or gas-based routes and residual process streams, but should not freeze a route when
a lower-carbon replacement is feasible.

The carbon market should use route-sensitive benchmarks so that fundamentally different
processes are not rewarded or penalised through an unfair average.

### 3. Fertiliser and hydrogen

Ammonia production can produce concentrated CO2 during hydrogen manufacture, making capture
technically easier than dilute flue-gas capture. Yet fossil-based hydrogen with CCS remains
dependent on:

- methane leakage;
- capture coverage and rate;
- energy source;
- geological storage;
- comparison with green hydrogen.

The correct transition choice varies by cost, infrastructure, fertiliser security and net
emissions.

### 4. Refineries and petrochemicals

These complexes contain multiple stacks and process streams. High-purity capture opportunities
may coexist with diffuse combustion emissions. Clustering can improve transport economics, but
long-lived capture subsidies should not shield products whose demand must decline in a net-zero
pathway.

### 5. Waste and biomass

Capturing biogenic CO2 from ethanol, biomass processing or waste streams may offer lower-cost
removal opportunities if feedstock sustainability and storage are secure. Waste-to-energy with
capture can contain fossil and biogenic fractions; accounting must separate them and prioritise
waste prevention, reuse and recycling.

### 6. Industrial clusters

Potential Indian cluster logic includes co-locating:

- several CO2 sources;
- common conditioning;
- pipeline or shipping terminals;
- characterised storage;
- shared monitoring and emergency response.

```text
capture-ready sources
   -> open-access network
   -> storage operator
   -> common MRV data layer
   -> regulated tariff and liability allocation
```

Cluster planning should begin with storage appraisal; building capture capacity before a secure
sink creates stranded equipment.

### 7. DAC in India

DACCS may eventually serve dispersed residual emissions and provide measurable removal. Near-term
policy should be cautious because India also needs abundant clean electricity for development,
electrification, cooling and green hydrogen. Pilot support is strongest when it:

- uses additional low-carbon energy;
- tests sorbents under Indian heat, humidity and dust;
- links to verified storage or durable mineralisation;
- discloses water use;
- produces open performance data;
- avoids extravagant offset claims.

### 8. Carbon-market design for sectoral transition

India's intensity-based CCTS can encourage efficient production, but policy should also track
absolute emissions and technology pathways.

| Design lever | Good use |
|---|---|
| Tightening benchmark | Rewards continued intensity improvement |
| Differentiated route benchmark | Avoids unfair comparison across production technologies |
| Banking limits | Prevents old surplus from weakening later ambition |
| Offset limit | Preserves direct action by obligated entities |
| CCUS methodology | Credits only net captured and durably stored tonnes |
| Revenue/support contract | Pays for verified learning and transition outcomes |
| Public disclosure | Shows intensity, output, absolute emissions and certificate position |

### 9. Evaluation framework

Every Indian proposal should be tested against:

```text
EFFECTIVENESS: net tCO2e reduced/removed?
EFFICIENCY: cost per net tonne and infrastructure value?
INTEGRITY: baseline, MRV, leakage, permanence, no double claim?
EQUITY: household, worker, regional and land-right impacts?
FEASIBILITY: energy, storage, finance, skills, institutions?
TRANSITION FIT: complements or delays direct decarbonisation?
```

### 10. Policy sequence

1. Reduce energy and material demand where socially desirable.
2. Improve efficiency and circularity.
3. Electrify with cleaner power.
4. Use green hydrogen or process substitution where suitable.
5. Apply CCUS to credible residual process emissions.
6. Build storage appraisal, networks, MRV and liability law.
7. Develop removals for genuine residuals.
8. Use carbon markets to support, not replace, this physical sequence.

### UPSC integration

- **Mains:** organise India applications by sector and residual-emission logic.
- **Policy question:** begin with alternatives before selecting capture.
- **Conclusion:** carbon pricing, infrastructure and technology policy must be mutually consistent.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** India's optimal strategy is not "markets versus technology" but a
> mitigation hierarchy in which carbon pricing rewards direct decarbonisation, CCUS addresses
> verified residual process emissions, and durable removals balance only the remaining residual.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should a CCUS subsidy for an Indian cement plant be conditioned on more than the

**Model answer:** The policy should reward net lifecycle avoidance after energy and transport

### Original Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Design an integrated policy framework for carbon markets,
CCUS and carbon dioxide removal in India's net-zero transition.

#### Model response (231 words)

India needs a mitigation hierarchy rather than interchangeable climate instruments. First,
efficiency, material circularity, renewable electricity, electrification, public transport and
green hydrogen should reduce gross emissions directly. The CCTS can reinforce this through
progressively tightened, route-sensitive intensity benchmarks, transparent MRV and disclosure of
both intensity and absolute emissions.

Second, CCUS should target residual process emissions in cement, selected steel, fertiliser and
chemical streams after direct alternatives are assessed. Support should be conditional on net
lifecycle avoidance, high capture coverage, low-carbon energy and durable storage. Government
should appraise storage basins, clarify pore-space rights and long-term liability, and develop
open-access cluster transport with regulated tariffs. Contracts can reward verified tonnes and
learning rather than capital expenditure alone.

Third, removal policy should distinguish DACCS, BECCS and ecosystem methods by lifecycle,
durability and social impact. India should pilot DAC with additional clean energy and transparent
water-performance data, use sustainable biogenic streams where available, and protect forests
and soils through rights-based restoration. Gross-reduction, residual-emissions and removal
targets must be reported separately to prevent mitigation deterrence.

Across all instruments, registries must prevent overlap among CCCs, ESCerts, green credits and
Article 6 outcomes. International transfers should require strategic authorisation and
corresponding adjustments. Revenue should support vulnerable households, workers and industrial
regions.

Thus markets provide incentives, CCUS handles selected residual point sources, and removals
balance only genuine residuals. Integrity, justice and transition fit—not certificate volume—
should determine success.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Instrument identification

**Question:** A regulator fixes the total number of tradable permissions to emit and reduces that
number annually. Which policy family is this, and what remains market-determined?

**Model answer:** It is cap-and-trade or an emissions trading system based on allowances. The
authority fixes the aggregate allowance quantity, while trading discovers the allowance price
and allocates abatement among covered entities.

## Check 2 — Intensity versus absolute emissions

**Question:** Why should India's CCTS disclose absolute emissions in addition to GEI performance?

**Model answer:** GEI shows emissions per unit of output and is the compliance metric, but output
growth can raise total emissions despite intensity improvement. Absolute disclosure reveals the
atmospheric trajectory and helps evaluate consistency with long-term net zero.

## Check 3 — Credit quality

**Question:** Can a conservative baseline compensate for a non-additional project?

**Model answer:** No. A conservative baseline may prevent inflated quantity, but additionality is
a separate causation test. If the project would occur anyway, issuing credits can still displace
real mitigation elsewhere.

## Check 4 — Paris accounting

**Question:** When is a corresponding adjustment central, and why is it not an MRV substitute?

**Model answer:** It is central when an authorised mitigation outcome is internationally
transferred for another approved use and must not be claimed by both Parties. It reconciles
accounts; it does not establish the project's baseline, additionality, leakage or permanence.

## Check 5 — CCUS classification

**Question:** A gas-processing facility captures a naturally concentrated CO2 stream and stores
it. Is this automatically carbon dioxide removal?

**Model answer:** No. If the CO2 comes from a fossil or geologic process stream before atmospheric
release, storage mainly avoids an emission. Removal requires CO2 to be withdrawn from ambient air
or through a sustainably accounted biogenic route.

## Check 6 — Utilisation

**Question:** Why should CO2-to-fuel be assessed differently from geological storage?

**Model answer:** Fuel use normally releases the CO2 during combustion, so the pathway recycles
carbon and may avoid fossil feedstock rather than store carbon durably. Geological storage aims
at long-term containment and therefore has a different permanence claim.

## Check 7 — Nature permanence

**Question:** What two conditions make a forest-removal claim especially vulnerable under climate
change?

**Model answer:** Biological carbon can reverse through fire, drought, pests or land-use change,
and such events can affect many projects simultaneously. Conservative crediting and replacement
liability are needed alongside ecosystem and tenure safeguards.

## Check 8 — India scheme boundary

**Question:** Why can a domestic CCC not be presented automatically as an Article 6 ITMO?

**Model answer:** Domestic issuance and international authorisation are separate legal and
accounting steps. Article 6 use requires national authorisation, reporting, tracking and a
corresponding adjustment where applicable.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain additionality, leakage and permanence in carbon
crediting.

### Model response (146 words)

Additionality asks whether the credited mitigation would occur without the carbon-finance
incentive or programme. A legally mandatory, already profitable or common-practice activity may
fail this causation test even if it lowers emissions.

Leakage is the increase in emissions outside the project boundary caused by the intervention.
Forest protection may shift extraction elsewhere; industrial regulation may relocate production.
Material leakage must be prevented, measured or conservatively deducted.

Permanence concerns how long removed carbon remains stored and whether reversal is possible.
Forest, soil and product carbon can return through fire, harvest, management change or disposal;
geological storage requires well, pressure and plume monitoring. Buffer pools, discounting,
insurance and replacement obligations can manage, but not eliminate, reversal risk.

These tests are complementary. A project can be additional yet over-credited through leakage or
temporary storage. High-integrity crediting therefore needs a credible baseline, conservative
MRV, exclusive claims and liability for reversal.

## Final 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically evaluate India's intensity-based Carbon Credit
Trading Scheme as an instrument of industrial decarbonisation.

### Model response (250 words)

India's CCTS creates a statutory price incentive under the Energy Conservation Act. Obligated
entities receive greenhouse-gas emission-intensity targets; outperformance can earn Carbon Credit
Certificates, while shortfalls require purchase and surrender. The design builds on BEE's PAT
experience and can direct abatement toward lower-cost facilities, improve industrial data and
reward efficient production without imposing an immediate economy-wide cap.

Intensity targets accommodate output growth and can
be differentiated by subsector or production route. By January 2026, entity-level coverage had
expanded to 490 notified obligated entities. G.S.R. 517(E) of 26 June 2026 separately proposed
FY2026-27 targets for 255 iron-and-steel entities, but remained a draft at the cut-off and cannot
be counted as operative coverage.

However, intensity can fall while absolute emissions rise. Generous baselines, weak verification,
excess banking or unrestricted offsets can depress the signal. Route-insensitive benchmarks may
penalise cleaner processes or protect incumbents. Notified targets and a registry also do not
prove active liquidity or a stable price.

The scheme should therefore publish intensity, output and absolute emissions; tighten benchmarks
predictably; accredit independent verifiers; limit offset use; prevent overlap with PAT, RECs and
green credits; and impose penalties for non-compliance. Support for trade-exposed sectors should
be conditional on transition investment. Auction or fee revenue, where available, should assist
workers, vulnerable consumers and industrial clusters.

CCTS can be a valuable transition instrument, but it cannot alone deliver net zero. Its success
depends on alignment with renewable power, green hydrogen, material efficiency, CCUS for residual
process emissions and eventual absolute-emissions discipline.

## Final 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Carbon markets and carbon-removal technologies can complement
deep decarbonisation, but neither can substitute for it. Discuss with reference to Article 6,
CCUS and Direct Air Capture.

### Model response (242 words)

Carbon markets and removal technologies perform different functions. Markets attach value to
verified mitigation and can lower compliance cost by directing action toward cheaper
opportunities. Article 6.2 enables Party-designed ITMO cooperation, Article 6.4 creates a central
UN crediting mechanism, and Article 6.8 supports non-market cooperation. Their benefit depends on
additionality, conservative baselines, MRV, corresponding adjustments, safeguards and host-country
control over NDC assets.

Technology supplies the physical outcome. Point-source CCUS can avoid residual process emissions
from cement, steel, fertiliser and chemical production, but capture energy, transport, storage,
liability and fossil lock-in constrain it. It is not automatically atmospheric removal.

Direct Air Capture withdraws dilute CO2 from ambient air. When followed by geological storage,
DACCS can balance dispersed residual emissions. Yet clean-energy needs, cost and storage
infrastructure limit scale. DAC-derived fuels recycle carbon rather than store it permanently.
BECCS and ecosystem methods broaden the removal portfolio but introduce land, water, biodiversity
and reversal risks.

Substitution occurs when firms buy weak credits instead of cutting feasible emissions or invoke
speculative future removals to preserve high-carbon assets. Policy must therefore follow a
hierarchy: reduce demand and gross emissions, transform energy and industry, use CCUS for verified
residual point sources, and match durable removals to a small disclosed residual. Gross,
residual and removal trajectories should be reported separately.

Thus markets can mobilise finance and technologies can address difficult emissions, but climate
integrity rests on direct decarbonisation first, conservative accounting, social safeguards and
long-term liability.

## Repair drill 1

**Faulty line:** "India has an ETS cap that guarantees falling industrial emissions."

**Repair:** India has an intensity-based baseline-and-credit compliance mechanism. It can improve
emissions per unit of output, but absolute industrial emissions need separate tracking and may
rise with production.

## Repair drill 2

**Faulty line:** "DAC removes carbon permanently and therefore solves aviation emissions."

**Repair:** DAC separates atmospheric CO2. Permanent removal requires durable storage and
lifecycle deductions. Using DAC CO2 in aviation fuel recycles carbon and may reduce fossil use,

## Repair drill 3

**Faulty line:** "Article 6 credits automatically reduce both buyer and seller emissions."

**Repair:** One physical mitigation outcome cannot satisfy two NDC claims. Authorisation and
corresponding adjustments allocate the accounting benefit; they do not create a second reduction.

## Repair drill 4

**Faulty line:** "CCUS is undesirable because it supports fossil fuels."

**Repair:** CCUS can create lock-in if used indiscriminately, but it may be valuable for residual
process emissions in cement or selected industrial streams. Evaluate alternatives, lifecycle
avoidance, storage and transition fit.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Carbon-outcome grammar

- **Avoided emission:** projected release prevented.
- **Reduction:** an emitting flow is lowered against a defensible reference.
- **Removal:** atmospheric CO2 is withdrawn and stored.
- **Allowance:** regulatory permission to emit a stated quantity.
- **Credit:** quantified reduction/removal against a baseline or performance rule.
- **Gross capture ≠ net benefit:** subtract energy, transport, leakage and later release.
- **Exam line:** identify source, boundary, destination and storage duration before naming the
  outcome.

## 2. Carbon-pricing designs

| Design | Authority fixes | Crucial trap |
|---|---|---|
| Tax | Price | Emissions quantity uncertain |
| Cap-and-trade | Allowance quantity | Price volatile; loose cap weakens scarcity |
| Baseline-and-credit | Intensity/performance target | Absolute emissions can rise with output |

- Formula: **absolute emissions = emissions intensity × output**.
- Free allocation may address leakage but risks windfalls.
- Auction/tax revenue can support households, infrastructure and just transition.
- Price signal needs standards, innovation, grids and finance.

## 3. Compliance and voluntary markets

- Compliance demand is created by law.
- Voluntary demand is buyer-initiated.
- Validation tests design; verification tests monitored performance.
- Project cycle: boundary → methodology → baseline/additionality → validation → registration →
  monitoring → verification → issuance → transfer/retirement.
- Retirement prevents unit reuse; it does not cure a weak project.
- Outside credits do not physically reduce the buyer's inventory emissions.

## 4. Integrity doctrine

- **Baseline:** reference emissions/removals without intervention.
- **Additionality:** credited outcome would not occur anyway.
- **Leakage:** emissions shift outside boundary.
- **Permanence:** storage duration and reversal risk.
- **Double issuance/use/claim:** different duplication defects.
- **Corresponding adjustment:** paired international accounting to prevent two NDC claims.
- Integrity equation:

```text
real + additional + conservative baseline - leakage
+ durable/reversal-covered + verified + uniquely claimed
= high-integrity outcome
```

## 5. Justice doctrine

- Protect energy affordability through targeted recycling.
- Build worker and regional transition before closures.
- Recognise land, tenure, customary use and benefit-sharing.
- Provide accessible grievance and remedy.
- Distinguish procedural, distributive, recognitional and restorative justice.
- International transfer price should reflect host NDC opportunity cost.
- High transaction costs can exclude small actors; transparent aggregation is needed.

## 6. Kyoto and CDM

- Kyoto mechanisms: International Emissions Trading, Joint Implementation and CDM.
- JI: Annex I host project → ERUs.
- CDM: non-Annex I host project → CERs for eligible developed-country use.
- CDM contribution: methodologies, validation, verification, registries and capacity.
- CDM warning: non-additionality, inflated baselines, uneven access, oversupply and weak
  sustainable-development scrutiny.
- Article 6.4 is a new Paris mechanism; transition is conditional, not automatic.

## 7. Paris Article 6

| Provision | Function |
|---|---|
| 6.2 | Party-designed cooperative approaches using ITMOs |
| 6.4 | Central UN-supervised PACM crediting mechanism |
| 6.8 | Non-market approaches |

- Authorisation and corresponding adjustments are central for internationally used outcomes.
- Article 6.4 includes adaptation share and overall-mitigation cancellation.
- As of 3 October 2026: rulebook adopted; 6.2 reporting/review and registries developing;
  PACM standards, accreditation, activity processes and CDM transition operating; interim
  registry arrangements remain relevant; do not claim a fully mature global market.

## 8. India CCTS legal spine

- Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022; effective 1 January 2023.
- **Section 14(w):** power to specify CCTS.
- **Section 14AA:** issuance and purchase/sale of CCCs under the scheme.
- CCTS notified 28 June 2023.
- Dual mechanism: compliance + offset.
- NSCICM: oversight.
- BEE: administrator.
- Grid Controller of India: registry.
- CERC: trading regulator.
- Accredited Carbon Verification Agencies: validation/verification.
- Compliance design: entity-level GEI baseline-and-credit.
- PIB, 22 January 2026: coverage reached 490 obligated entities.
- G.S.R. 517(E), 26 June 2026: draft Third Schedule proposed FY2026-27 GEI targets for 255
  iron-and-steel entities using FY2023-24 baselines; it was not an operative addition to the
  notified entity count at the cut-off.
- Status trap: notified targets ≠ liquid trading ≠ stable discovered price.

## 9. Certificate boundaries in India

| Instrument | Attribute |
|---|---|
| CCC | tCO2e outcome under CCTS |
| ESCert | Energy saving under PAT |
| REC | Renewable electricity attribute |
| Green credit | Specified environmental action |
| ITMO/A6.4ER | Paris international accounting unit/outcome |

- No automatic fungibility.
- Avoid double issuance across PAT, CCTS, RECs, green credits and Article 6.
- International transfer needs national authorisation and accounting.

## 10. CCUS chain

```text
source -> capture -> purification/dehydration -> compression
-> transport -> use or storage -> monitoring -> closure/liability
```

- Capture routes: post-combustion, pre-combustion, oxy-fuel, process-stream separation.
- High concentration generally lowers separation difficulty.
- Utilisation may be temporary; fuels usually re-release CO2.
- Geological options: saline formations and depleted hydrocarbon fields.
- Trapping: structural → residual → dissolution → mineral.
- Equipment capture rate ≠ net lifecycle avoidance.
- Best role: residual process emissions after direct mitigation.

## 11. Storage and economics

- Usable storage needs injectivity, caprock, well/fault control, permits and access.
- MRV reconciles captured, transported, injected, leaked and retained mass.
- Liability must cover monitoring, remediation, reversal and closure.
- Energy penalty comes from separation, regeneration, compression and injection.
- Costs depend on concentration, retrofit, energy, distance, storage and finance.
- Shared hubs lower cost but create timing, volume, monopoly and stranded-asset risks.

## 12. CDR and DAC

- CDR = atmosphere-to-reservoir transfer.
- DAC separates CO2 from ambient air.
- DACCS = DAC plus durable storage.
- DAC-to-fuel = recycling/possible avoidance, not permanent removal.
- BECCS needs sustainable biomass, full land-use accounting and storage.
- Forest/soil removal gives co-benefits but can saturate and reverse.
- Enhanced weathering/ocean approaches need mining, energy, ecological and MRV scrutiny.
- Mitigation hierarchy: deep gross cuts → small residual → high-integrity removal.

## 13. Removal integrity and moral hazard

- Match claim to storage duration.
- Correlated wildfire can exhaust biological buffer pools.
- Technical potential ≠ sustainable ≠ deployable potential.
- Separate gross-reduction, residual and removal targets.
- Offset claims require stronger equivalence than contribution claims.
- Fund intergenerational monitoring and liability before closure.
- Do not let speculative future removal justify avoidable present emissions.

## 14. India sector-and-policy spine

- Cement: efficiency, clinker substitution, material efficiency, then residual CCUS.
- Steel: circularity, scrap-EAF, green hydrogen and route-specific CCUS choices.
- Fertiliser: concentrated streams can ease capture; include methane and hydrogen route.
- Refineries/petrochemicals: cluster opportunities but lock-in risk.
- Waste/biogenic streams: separate fossil and biogenic carbon; prevention remains prior.
- Storage appraisal before large capture investment.
- DAC pilots: additional clean energy, water disclosure, Indian-condition testing, verified sink.
- CCTS should disclose absolute emissions beside intensity.
- Evaluation: effectiveness, efficiency, integrity, equity, feasibility and transition fit.

## 16. Final examiner traps

1. Carbon tax is not an ETS.
2. India's CCTS is not economy-wide cap-and-trade.
3. Allowance is not the same as credit.
4. Reduction is not the same as removal.
5. Point-source CCS is not automatically negative emissions.
6. DAC is not automatically DACCS.
7. CO2 use is not automatically permanent storage.
8. Corresponding adjustment is not additionality proof.
9. Retirement is not complete integrity proof.
10. Green credit is not automatically a CCC.
11. Domestic CCC is not automatically an ITMO.
12. Intensity decline is not necessarily absolute decline.
13. Notified target is not the same as completed trade.
14. Modelled removal potential is not deployable capacity.
15. A "hard-to-abate" label does not remove the duty to compare alternatives.
