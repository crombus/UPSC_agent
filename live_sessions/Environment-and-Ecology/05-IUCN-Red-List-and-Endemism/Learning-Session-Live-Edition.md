# IUCN Red List and Endemism — Live Session Edition

What should conservationists do when a species is scarce, poorly surveyed, or found only in one part of India? A risk label, a geographical fact and a legal safeguard answer three different questions. Learn to keep them separate before joining them in a conservation strategy.

### Visual — sort the labels before ranking risk

| Assessment state | Category | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Not assessed | Not Evaluated (NE) | The criteria have not yet been applied. |
| Insufficient evidence | Data Deficient (DD) | Available information does not allow a risk estimate. |
| Evaluated below threatened threshold | Least Concern (LC) → Near Threatened (NT) | LC does not currently meet higher-risk thresholds; NT is close to meeting threatened thresholds or likely to do so soon. |
| **Threatened** | Vulnerable (VU) → Endangered (EN) → Critically Endangered (CR) | Increasing risk of extinction **in the wild**. |
| Persistence outside a wild population / none remaining | Extinct in the Wild (EW) → Extinct (EX) | EW survives only in cultivation, captivity or as a naturalised population well outside its former range; EX means no reasonable doubt that the last individual has died. |

*Caption: Only LC → NT → VU → EN → CR forms a useful increasing-risk comparison among extant assessed wild populations; NE and DD are **not** low-risk rungs.*

Imagine two forest birds. One has been surveyed sufficiently and its population appears secure at the assessment scale; the other has almost no reliable sightings. Putting the second bird beside the first under "low risk" would confuse **absence of evidence** with **evidence of low risk**. IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature), working with its Species Survival Commission's specialist expertise, maintains a standardised, criteria-based Red List; the label records an assessed *extinction risk*, not an entitlement to legal protection.

1. **Separate the information states.** NE means no Red List assessment; DD means evidence is inadequate for a direct or indirect assessment after examination. DD warrants investigation and may justify precaution; it does not itself prove that the species is endangered. LC is an actual assessment, not a synonym for "unprotected."
2. **Name the collective term precisely.** "Threatened" comprises exactly **VU, EN and CR**. NT is near the threshold but not a threatened category; EW and EX describe loss of a wild population or of the taxon, not ever-higher subdivisions of threatened *living* wild populations.
3. **Read category together with scope and date.** A **taxon** is the species or eligible infraspecific entity whose extinction risk is assessed. Its label is attached at a defined assessment scale. The scientific classification may change with new evidence or changing conditions. Never supply a current population estimate or assert a category change without its dated assessment.
4. **Keep scale and recovery distinct from the category ladder.**

| Assessment or category | Question answered | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| Global Red List assessment | What is the taxon's extinction risk across its entire natural range? | It is not automatically a description of risk inside every country or region. |
| Regional preliminary category | What category follows when the global criteria are first applied to the regional population? | This is step two, not always the final regional result. |
| Regional final category | How does interaction with conspecific populations outside the region change risk? | For a non-endemic taxon, credible immigration or a **rescue effect** may justify downlisting. Declining external populations can prevent that downlisting; uplisting is warranted when the regional population is a demographic sink and immigration is expected to decrease. |
| Regionally Extinct (RE) / Not Applicable (NA) | Does the taxon persist in the region, and is it eligible for regional assessment? | RE means regional loss despite survival elsewhere; NA covers ineligible cases such as vagrants, introduced populations or taxa outside the authority's assessment scope. |
| Green Status of Species | How recovered is the species, and what contribution has conservation made? | It complements rather than replaces the Red List. Recovery progress can coexist with substantial remaining extinction risk. |

A regional assessment follows three steps: decide which taxa and populations are eligible; apply the global criteria to obtain a **preliminary category**; then, where the taxon is not endemic or demographically isolated, examine populations outside the boundary and adjust up or down only when the evidence supports it. If the entire natural population is endemic to, or effectively isolated within, the region, no outside rescue adjustment is available. A widespread species may therefore be globally secure enough for a lower-risk category while declining severely inside one region. Conversely, an improvement recorded through Green Status does not silently downlist the species. **Red List status measures extinction risk; Green Status measures recovery and conservation impact.** Neither should be quoted without its assessment date and scope.

**Objection:** If the ladder ends in EX, why not call EW simply "more threatened than CR"? **Reply:** The threat thresholds for CR concern risk to a taxon still living in the wild; EW states that wild persistence has already ended. The diagram orders possible states of wild persistence without making them identical kinds of forecast.

**UPSC application:** GS-III biodiversity questions reward the phrase "risk assessment, not statute." For Prelims, separate three similar-looking expressions: **assessed low risk**, **assessed but information insufficient**, **not assessed**. In the 2026 official GS-I Q22, an IUCN-status claim appears alongside habitat and anatomy claims: each needs its own evidentiary test.

**Mini recap:** NE ≠ DD ≠ LC; VU + EN + CR = threatened; EW ≠ EX; global ≠ regional; Red List risk ≠ Green Status recovery; category without scale and date is an incomplete citation.

**Revision notes**

1. IUCN is an international scientific assessment body, not a legislature.
2. Species Survival Commission specialist groups contribute taxon expertise.
3. NE means the criteria have not been applied.
4. DD means available evidence cannot support a risk estimate; it is not a low-risk finding.
5. LC is an assessed category below higher-risk thresholds.
6. NT lies outside the collective threatened group.
7. Threatened means exactly VU, EN and CR.
8. CR is the most severe threatened category for a taxon still surviving in the wild.
9. EW means survival only outside a natural wild population; EX means no reasonable doubt the last individual has died.
10. Every category citation should identify the taxon, assessment scale and date.
11. A regional assessment first assigns a preliminary category and then considers populations beyond the boundary.
12. Endemic or isolated regional populations have no outside rescue adjustment; non-endemic populations may be adjusted only on evidence.
13. RE means regional extinction; NA means the taxon is not eligible for that regional assessment.
14. Global and regional/national assessments can differ without contradiction.
15. Green Status measures recovery and conservation impact; it complements rather than replaces Red List extinction risk.

**Question:** A survey cannot support a defensible estimate of extinction risk for a forest mammal. A classmate puts it below LC because nobody proved a decline. What is wrong with that placement?

<details><summary>Scored model and marking notes</summary>

**Model (about 118 words):** DD describes a failure of available information to support an extinction-risk assessment, not a finding that risk is low. In India, poorly surveyed freshwater and invertebrate taxa may have narrow distributions exposed to land-use change. Treating missing surveys as safety would channel research and protection only to conspicuous species. By contrast, LC follows an evaluation against IUCN criteria; NE means evaluation has not taken place. A sensible response is to prioritise surveys where restricted habitat and ongoing threats make hidden risk plausible, then reassess on improved evidence. ### Visual — alternative evidence, not five compulsory hurdles

```text
Population falls? ─────────────── A: reduction over a specified period
Range contracts? ──────────────── B: restricted range + required threat indicators
Few mature individuals declining? C: small population + continuing decline
Very few / very restricted? ───── D: very small or restricted population
Extinction risk model? ────────── E: quantitative extinction probability
                 \   |   |   |   /
                  Assess EACH relevant test
                           ↓
             ONE satisfied threshold can qualify
                VU / EN / CR, as applicable
```

*Caption: The criteria are independent possible routes to a category, but each criterion has its **own** numerical thresholds and subsidiary conditions.*

Suppose a species is hard to count but confined to a shrinking cluster of forest patches. A simple headcount may miss the danger; a geographical test can capture the risk. Conversely a widespread species can decline sharply enough to warrant concern. The five criteria therefore measure *different failure pathways* rather than voting on one common statistic.

The official thresholds below come from IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria v3.1, second edition, printed pp. 16–22. Read every number together with its condition; a threshold fragment is not a category.

| Criterion | CR | EN | VU | Conditions that must travel with the number |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **A1: past reduction; causes reversible, understood and ceased** | ≥90% | ≥70% | ≥50% | Over the longer of **10 years or 3 generations**. |
| **A2–A4: causes may persist, be unclear or irreversible** | ≥80% | ≥50% | ≥30% | A2 is an observed, estimated, inferred or suspected past reduction using evidence (a)–(e); A3 is a projected or suspected future reduction using (b)–(e); A4 is observed, estimated, inferred, projected or suspected across a period spanning past and future using (a)–(e). The same 10-year/3-generation rule applies, with a 100-year future cap where specified. |
| **B1: EOO** | <100 km² | <5,000 km² | <20,000 km² | Plus **at least two** of: (a) severe fragmentation or no more than 1/5/10 locations respectively; (b) continuing decline; (c) extreme fluctuations. |
| **B2: AOO** | <10 km² | <500 km² | <2,000 km² | The same two-of-three B subconditions are required. EOO and AOO are not interchangeable. |
| **C: small mature population + decline** | <250 | <2,500 | <10,000 | C1 decline: ≥25% in 3 years/1 generation; ≥20% in 5 years/2 generations; ≥10% in 10 years/3 generations. Or C2: continuing decline plus specified subpopulation concentration/size or extreme fluctuations. |
| **D: very small population** | <50 mature individuals | <250 | <1,000 | VU alone also has D2: typically AOO <20 km² or ≤5 locations, exposed to a plausible rapid threat capable of producing CR or EX quickly. |
| **E: modelled probability of extinction in the wild** | ≥50% within 10 years/3 generations | ≥20% within 20 years/5 generations | ≥10% within 100 years | Use the longer generation-based window where stated, subject to the official 100-year cap. The quantitative analysis and assumptions must be defensible. |

Under C2, the category-specific structure tests also matter: no subpopulation may exceed **50 / 250 / 1,000** mature individuals for CR / EN / VU, or at least **90% / 95% / 100%** respectively must occur in one subpopulation; extreme fluctuations are the alternative C2 route. **Mature individuals** are those capable of reproduction; a **subpopulation** has little demographic or genetic exchange with others; **generation length** describes the average age of parents in the current cohort and sets the biological time window. **Extent of occurrence** (EOO) is the area contained within the shortest continuous imaginary boundary encompassing all known, inferred or projected sites of present occurrence, excluding vagrancy; **area of occupancy** (AOO) is the area actually occupied, conventionally measured through the prescribed grid. A **location** is defined by a single threatening event, not merely by counting place names. A1 and A2 may use direct observation among their evidence types; A3 is future-facing and uses projected or suspected reduction based on evidence types (b)–(e); A4 spans past and future.

The move from observation to category is: define taxon and assessment scale → assemble dated observations, estimates and uncertainty → test criteria against the category-specific thresholds → identify the *highest* qualifying threat category → publish the complete criterion and subcriterion code → reassess when evidence changes. One qualifying criterion suffices, **not** one isolated suggestive symptom. For example, an AOO below 10 km² does not by itself establish CR under B2, and VU D2 cannot be silently converted into a CR range rule.

**India application:** The Great Indian Bustard illustrates why population size, distribution, fragmentation and particular hazards need to be examined together. Do not reverse-engineer the precise Red List criterion, threshold or assessment year from a newspaper description of its plight. A survey estimate is an observation at a specified time, not a continuously updated headcount. ⚠️ **Inference:** Monitoring range and mature individuals together gives a better management signal than one headline total.

**Objection:** Won't fragmented habitat automatically satisfy B? **Reply:** No. Fragmentation is one possible subcondition. A sufficiently small EOO or AOO and the required combination of subconditions must be substantiated at the proper category threshold; the *assessment*, not a classroom vignette, assigns the label.

**UPSC application:** Distinguish Criterion A from C (decline alone versus small population with decline), B from D (range with extra conditions versus very small/restricted population), and E from an informal prediction. Mains should explain a causal pathway rather than merely recite A–E.

**Mini recap:** Alternative routes, category-specific thresholds; EOO ≠ AOO; B requires more than small range; C needs continuing decline; D is not necessarily evidence of decline; E needs a defensible model.

**Revision notes**

1. Criterion A uses the longer of 10 years or three generations; A1 thresholds are 90/70/50%, while A2–A4 use 80/50/30% for CR/EN/VU.
2. Criterion B thresholds are EOO <100/<5,000/<20,000 km² or AOO <10/<500/<2,000 km² for CR/EN/VU.
3. B always requires at least two additional subconditions; area alone is insufficient.
4. EOO encloses known, inferred or projected present occurrence; AOO is area occupied on the prescribed grid.
5. Criterion C requires <250/<2,500/<10,000 mature individuals plus continuing decline and the applicable rate or structure test.
6. C counts mature individuals, not every observed individual.
7. Criterion D1 uses <50/<250/<1,000 mature individuals; D2 is a VU-only rapid-threat route for very restricted populations.
8. Criterion E thresholds are 50% within 10 years/3 generations, 20% within 20 years/5 generations and 10% within 100 years.
9. One fully satisfied criterion can qualify a taxon; one fragment of a criterion cannot.
10. The published code, uncertainty, scale, date and taxonomic boundary remain essential.

**Model (about 208 words):** The Red List assesses extinction risk through independent evidence routes rather than one headcount. A still-numerous, widespread species may qualify under Criterion A if its reduction reaches the applicable threshold over the longer of ten years or three generations. The threshold also depends on whether the causes have ceased and are reversible: A1 is stricter than A2–A4. ### Visual — which geographical proposition is being tested?

```text
                    SPECIES' NATURAL RANGE
                      /               \
       wholly within defined region   crosses region boundary
           endemic TO that region       not endemic TO that region
                    │
        measure threats and trends separately
                    ↓
      stable / declining / poorly known are DIFFERENT findings
```

*Caption: Endemism describes where a species naturally occurs; a Red List category estimates extinction risk. The named region's scale matters.*

Call a species "endemic to the Western Ghats" only if its natural range is confined to that region. An organism confined to India need not be confined to one Indian state; an animal present in an Indian sanctuary need not be endemic to India. Captive or introduced occurrences are not a substitute for its natural range.

**Types of endemism**

| Spatial scale | Meaning | Exam caution |
|---|---|---|
| Point or site endemism | Natural range confined to an exceptionally small locality, island, ridge, cave or stream system | One record does not prove that the whole range is known |
| Regional endemism | Range confined to a named biogeographic region such as the Western Ghats or Nicobar group | State borders and ecological regions need not coincide |
| National endemism | Natural range confined within one country's boundary | Useful for policy, but the political boundary may not explain the evolutionary process |

**Why endemics arise:** physical isolation on islands or separated mountains can reduce gene flow; specialised dependence on one soil, stream, host or microclimate can confine a taxon; and local speciation coupled with weak dispersal can produce a narrow natural range. These are possible mechanisms, not conclusions that can be inferred from a small map alone. Survey gaps can imitate endemism, so distribution claims must remain open to new records and taxonomic revision.

**Indian centres and examples**

| Region or centre | Illustrative taxa / habitats | What the example teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Western Ghats | Lion-tailed macaque in rainforest; Nilgiri tahr in high-elevation grassland; narrowly ranged amphibians | One centre contains several distinct habitat dependencies; “Western Ghats endemic” does not mean one common remedy |
| Eastern Himalaya and north-eastern hills | High turnover across elevation, ridges and valleys; western hoolock gibbon supplies a transboundary canopy-connectivity caution | Rich mountain diversity does not make every species Indian-endemic |
| Andaman and Nicobar islands | Concentrated plant and small-vertebrate endemism in isolated island habitats | Island isolation can create very small natural ranges and weak recolonisation options |
| Eastern Ghats hill and scrub systems | The Papikonda rusty-spotted-cat record supplies an occurrence-versus-endemism caution | Local records, restricted habitat and whole-range endemism are separate propositions |

Consider the **lion-tailed macaque**, naturally restricted to the Western Ghats' rainforest landscape, and the **Nilgiri tahr**, a Western Ghats mountain specialist. These are illustrations of habitat specificity, *not* interchangeable habitat requirements. Western Ghats amphibians may have extremely narrow ranges: a surviving patch in one valley cannot be replaced by a similarly sized forest anywhere in India if the species' conditions occur only there. In the islands, isolation that produces distinctive endemics can also make natural recolonisation after local extinction difficult. "Island" does not, by itself, prove a taxon's global category.

**Why mountain endemics can be especially vulnerable**

```text
warming / land-use pressure
          ↓
upslope shift into a narrower belt
          ↓
less area + isolated ridges/valleys + summit limit
          ↓
smaller connected habitat and fewer recolonisation routes
```

High mountains compress climate zones over short horizontal distances. IPCC AR6 WGII's mountain assessment reports observed upslope shifts and warns that mountain species can undergo range contraction where losses at lower limits outpace dispersal upward or where no higher suitable elevation remains. It also defines **micro-refugia** as small places where topography, microclimate or cold-water influence preserves locally suitable conditions. Roads and other linear infrastructure can further divide narrow habitat belts. Phenological change is documented for some mountain plants, but it must not be generalised to every endemic. Upslope movement and collapse are therefore possibilities to test against species-specific climate tolerance, habitat continuity, dispersal and trend—not automatic predictions.

| Label | Exact question answered | Frequent error |
|---|---|---|
| **Native / indigenous** | Does the species occur naturally in the named region? It may also occur naturally elsewhere. | Treating “native to India” as “endemic to India.” |
| **Endemic** | Is the *entire* natural range confined to the named region? | Inferring whole-range restriction from one sanctuary or record. |
| **Rare** | Is abundance, frequency or occupancy low at the stated scale? | Treating local scarcity as proof of global extinction risk. |
| **Threatened** | Does an assessment meet VU, EN or CR criteria? | Treating every endemic or rare species as threatened. |
| **Keystone** | Does the species have an ecological effect disproportionate to its abundance? | Assuming keystone means rare, endemic or legally protected. |
| **Flagship** | Is the species used to mobilise public support for conservation? | Treating communication value as an ecological role or risk category. |

**Why vulnerability can compound:** If habitat is lost throughout the only natural range, the species cannot fall back on unaffected populations elsewhere. Fragmentation can obstruct movement and recolonisation. Yet range restriction alone does not automatically make an endemic endangered: habitat stability, actual threat and qualifying assessment criteria still matter. Nor are all hotspot species endemic: a hotspot is a region defined by exceptional endemic plant diversity and heavy habitat loss, not a label for each species inside it.

### Visual — one taxon, three different measurements

```text
GLOBAL RED LIST ── extinction risk across the entire natural range

REGIONAL METHOD
eligibility (RE / NA?) → preliminary category → outside-population test
                                               ├─ endemic/isolated: retain
                                               └─ non-endemic: adjust only on evidence

```

*Caption: A category for one taxon, recovery of that taxon and the aggregate trend of a species set are not interchangeable.*

Think of a species plentiful outside India but rare at the edge of its Indian range. The regional process first decides eligibility, then applies the global criteria to the regional population to obtain a **preliminary category**. It next examines conspecific populations beyond the boundary. If immigration is likely to persist and can produce a genuine **rescue effect**—immigrants lowering regional extinction risk—downlisting may be justified. Declining external populations can prevent that downlisting. Uplisting is appropriate when the regional population is a demographic sink and immigration is expected to decrease. For a species endemic to, or effectively isolated within, the assessed region, outside populations cannot alter the result.

Regional guidelines also add **Regionally Extinct (RE)** and **Not Applicable (NA)**. RE records disappearance from the region despite possible survival elsewhere. NA means the taxon is ineligible for that assessment—for example, because it is outside its natural range, introduced, merely vagrant or excluded by the authority's stated filter. These are not extra global threat rungs.

The IUCN **Green Status of Species** therefore remains alongside the Red List rather than above or below it on one ladder. A population can improve while still facing substantial extinction risk. A conservation programme should report the direction and dependence of recovery together with remaining risk, instead of treating one favourable short-term trend as proof of security.

The **Red List Index (RLI)** asks a fourth question: is overall extinction risk improving or worsening for a defined set of species assessed repeatedly? It weights Red List categories and counts only **genuine** improvements or deteriorations. Category changes caused solely by improved knowledge, revised taxonomy or altered interpretation are corrected or excluded. An RLI of **1.0** represents all included species in LC; **0** represents all extinct. A rising RLI indicates improving aggregate status and a falling RLI worsening status. It is not a category for a species, a headcount, or a direct abundance index. Because categories are broad, it can miss substantial decline that occurs without a category crossing.

**Data friction:** DD taxa may include understudied freshwater organisms, fungi and invertebrates. Survey geography and taxonomy influence apparent endemism; publishing a distribution map with unsampled gaps can mislead. Population estimates for charismatic species also depend on survey design, date and uncertainty. ⚠️ **Inference:** allocate some resources to resolving unknowns and reassess after survey; neither discard DD nor announce all DD taxa threatened.

**Dated India anchor:** IUCN reported on **31 July 2026** that a fourth training workshop brought together BSI and ZSI regional staff and other specialists to contribute toward assessment of species under a National Red List vision. This is a useful illustration of *assessment capacity*. It does not establish that the target has been attained, that any named species has changed category, or that Indian legal schedules have been revised.

**Objection:** If recovery or the RLI improves, does that replace a worrying species category? **Reply:** No. Green Status concerns recovery of a species; the RLI aggregates genuine category movement across a defined species set; a Red List category estimates one taxon's extinction risk. Improvement on one axis can coexist with serious risk on another.

**UPSC application:** Prefix category claims with scale, year and assessor. For policy discuss monitoring as well as protection; do not treat training targets as achieved biodiversity outcomes.

**Mini recap:** Global ≠ regional; preliminary ≠ final regional category; RE/NA are regional; Green ≠ Red; RLI ≠ abundance or a species category; DD ≠ LC; training ≠ completed assessments.

**Revision notes**

1. A global assessment addresses extinction risk across the taxon's entire natural range.
2. A regional assessment first assigns a preliminary category using the global criteria.
3. Step three tests interaction with populations outside the region and adjusts only on evidence.
4. Endemic or isolated populations cannot receive an outside rescue adjustment.
5. RE means regionally extinct; NA means ineligible for that regional assessment.
6. A rescue effect is immigration that genuinely lowers regional extinction risk.
7. Green Status evaluates recovery and the contribution of conservation action.
8. The RLI tracks aggregate extinction-risk trends for a repeatedly assessed species set.
9. RLI calculations use genuine category changes, excluding knowledge-only and taxonomic changes.
10. RLI 1 means all included species LC; RLI 0 means all extinct.
11. RLI is not abundance, a population count or a species category.
12. DD records an evidence gap, not a favourable category.
13. Survey effort and taxonomic boundaries affect apparent range and abundance.
14. Training is capacity, not completed assessments or biological recovery.
15. Category claims should state taxon, scale, year and assessor.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## The category grammar

- IUCN Red List = scientific extinction-risk assessment; no statutory protection follows automatically. A **taxon** is the species or eligible infraspecific entity being assessed. Distinguish taxon, assessment **scale** and **year** before quoting a status.
- NE: never assessed; DD: inadequate evidence to estimate risk; LC: evaluated below higher-risk thresholds; NT: close to threatened; **threatened = VU + EN + CR**. CR is highest threatened category for a taxon still in the wild. EW survives only outside natural wild range in captivity/cultivation or as a far-off naturalised population; EX means no reasonable doubt last individual died.
- Do **not** draw NE → DD → LC as an increasing-risk ladder. DD can conceal serious risk; neither DD nor NE is proof of safety.

## Criteria without shortcut

- **A:** over the longer of 10 years/3 generations, A1 reduction thresholds are **90/70/50%** and A2–A4 are **80/50/30%** for CR/EN/VU. A1 requires causes reversible, understood and ceased; A2–A4 cover persistent/unclear/irreversible causes and past, future or combined windows.
- **B:** EOO thresholds are **<100/<5,000/<20,000 km²** and AOO **<10/<500/<2,000 km²** for CR/EN/VU, always with at least two of fragmentation/few locations, continuing decline and extreme fluctuations.
- **C:** mature population **<250/<2,500/<10,000** plus continuing decline and the category-specific decline-rate or subpopulation-structure/extreme-fluctuation test.
- **D:** mature population **<50/<250/<1,000**; VU D2 alone covers a very restricted population, typically AOO <20 km² or ≤5 locations, exposed to rapid threat.
- **E:** modelled extinction probability **≥50% in 10 years/3 generations; ≥20% in 20 years/5 generations; ≥10% in 100 years**.
- EOO encloses known, inferred or projected present occurrence; AOO is occupied area on the prescribed grid. One *fully satisfied* criterion can qualify; one number or subcondition cannot.
- The direct 2018 bustard record supplies A2a+4acd; C1; do not derive that code from news or turn the assessment into a current total. Reassessment is not a continuous headcount.

## Range, Indian habitats and evidence

- Endemic **to named region** = entire natural range lies there. Occurring in India, being protected here and having one sanctuary population do not establish Indian endemism. Current range ≠ place of evolutionary origin.
- **Do not merge labels:** native = naturally occurs here; rare = low abundance/occupancy; threatened = VU/EN/CR assessment; keystone = disproportionate ecological effect; flagship = public-support role.
- **Spatial types:** point/site endemic, regional endemic and national endemic use different denominators. Isolation, specialised habitat, weak dispersal and local speciation can restrict range; apparent endemism can shrink when surveys or taxonomy improve.
- **Indian centres/examples:** Western Ghats (lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri tahr and narrowly ranged amphibians); Eastern Himalaya and north-eastern hills (high elevational turnover, while many ranges remain transboundary); Andaman–Nicobar islands (concentrated island plant and small-vertebrate endemism); isolated Eastern Ghats hills (Papikonda occurrence evidence as a warning that one record is not endemism).
- **Mountain vulnerability:** IPCC AR6 WGII documents upslope shifts and risk of range contraction where upward dispersal cannot offset lower-range losses or no higher suitable elevation remains. **Micro-refugia** are small locally suitable climatic pockets that may permit persistence. Species-specific evidence remains necessary; some mountain plants show phenological shifts, but this is not universal.
- Western Ghats: lion-tailed macaque in rainforest and Nilgiri tahr in mountain habitat illustrate different dependencies. Habitat loss across the only natural range can eliminate spatial buffers; endemism itself is not an IUCN threat category. Islands can constrain natural recolonisation; do not assert recovery is impossible without assessing surviving sources and interventions.
- Hotspots combine exceptional endemic plant diversity and habitat loss, but no hotspot label categorises every resident animal. Unstudied fungi, invertebrates and freshwater taxa need surveys, not invented threat counts.
- PYQ evidence: Asiatic lion now wild in Gir, one-horned rhino India **and Nepal**, Bactrian camel Central Asia; distinct Cauvery blue-finned and Himalayan golden mahseers are fishes; Irrawaddy dolphin Chilika versus Ganges dolphin Chambal; **2016 Papikonda camera-trap record places rusty-spotted cat in the northern Eastern Ghats of Andhra Pradesh** without making it region-endemic; Indian nightjar, spoonbill and the question's “white ibis” are birds; marsupials occur naturally in Australasia and the Americas, not India. Indri Madagascar; bonobo DR Congo; elk North America and parts of Asia.
- Peacock/Gooty tarantula: direct IUCN record **CR, assessed 1 January 2008**, with restricted Andhra Pradesh forest range; it is an arboreal arachnid.
- Western hoolock gibbon: direct IUCN record **EN, assessed 12 September 2017 and published 2019**; WII Spring 2025 records Hollongapar habitat and brachiation. Its transboundary range defeats Indian endemism; the report's 2019 sanctuary census is not current abundance.

## Institutions, recovery and action

- IUCN/SSC: scientific assessment; BSI/ZSI: Indian botanical/zoological field evidence; WII: habitat and wildlife research; Wildlife (Protection) Act: domestic legal safeguards; CITES: international trade controls. One instrument does not automatically reclassify the others.
- **Assessment scale:** regional method = eligibility (including RE/NA) → preliminary category → evidence-based outside-population adjustment. Endemic/isolated populations have no outside rescue adjustment. For non-endemic populations, credible continuing immigration may justify downlisting; external decline may prevent downlisting, while a sink population with immigration expected to decrease may require uplisting.
- **Recovery axis:** Green Status assesses how recovered a species is and the contribution of conservation action. It complements rather than replaces Red List extinction risk; recovery progress can coexist with a threatened category.
- **Trend axis:** RLI tracks genuine category-change trends for a repeated species set; **1 = all LC, 0 = all extinct**. It is not abundance, a population count or a category for one species.
- IUCN's **31 July 2026** India training story documents assessment *capacity*, not completed nationwide categories or conservation outcomes. Do not substitute a proposed programme target for measured species change.
- Bustard: IUCN/BirdLife **CR A2a+4acd; C1, assessed 17 August 2018**, domestically Schedule I; arid grassland + overhead-line collision. **21 March 2024**, *M.K. Ranjitsinh*: expert, area-sensitive review and Articles 14/21 climate right. **19 December 2025**: revised priority areas **14,013 sq km Rajasthan / 740 sq km Gujarat**, project restrictions, Rajasthan 80 km urgent 33 kV and 250 km WII critical-line directions, specified rerouting and scientific diverter review; accepted Gujarat recommendations include 79.2 km of 33 kV and 64.9 km of critical 66 kV lines. PIB **14 June 2026**: **94 captive birds; 26 chicks**. Court order and captive output ≠ completed mitigation, wild estimate or recovery.
- Mains spine: define range and date → qualify risk under valid criterion → identify habitat and direct threat → verify Indian legal rule and trade rule separately → choose spatially feasible remedy → measure mortality, habitat and population trend → revise. If evidence is DD, combine precaution with targeted research.
