# IUCN Red List and Endemism — Live Session Edition

What should conservationists do when a species is scarce, poorly surveyed, or found only in one part of India? A risk label, a geographical fact and a legal safeguard answer three different questions. Learn to keep them separate before joining them in a conservation strategy.

### Visual — sort the labels before ranking risk

| Assessment state | Category | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Not assessed | Not Evaluated (NE) | The criteria have not yet been applied. |
| Insufficient evidence | Data Deficient (DD) | Available information does not allow a risk estimate. |
| Evaluated below threatened threshold | Least Concern (LC) → Near Threatened (NT) | LC does not currently meet higher-risk thresholds; NT is close to meeting threatened thresholds or likely to do so soon. |
| **Threatened** | Vulnerable (VU) → Endangered (EN) → Critically Endangered (CR) | Increasing risk of extinction **in the wild**. |
| Persistence outside a wild population / none remaining | Extinct in the Wild (EW) → Extinct (EX) | EW survives only in cultivation, captivity or as a naturalised population well outside its former range; EX means no reasonable doubt that the last individual has died. |

*Caption: Only LC → NT → VU → EN → CR forms a useful increasing-risk comparison among extant assessed wild populations; NE and DD are **not** low-risk rungs.*

Imagine two forest birds. One has been surveyed sufficiently and its population appears secure at the assessment scale; the other has almost no reliable sightings. Putting the second bird beside the first under "low risk" would confuse **absence of evidence** with **evidence of low risk**. IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature), working with its Species Survival Commission's specialist expertise, maintains a standardised, criteria-based Red List; the label records an assessed *extinction risk*, not an entitlement to legal protection.

1. **Separate the information states.** NE means no Red List assessment; DD means evidence is inadequate for a direct or indirect assessment after examination. DD warrants investigation and may justify precaution; it does not itself prove that the species is endangered. LC is an actual assessment, not a synonym for "unprotected."
2. **Name the collective term precisely.** "Threatened" comprises exactly **VU, EN and CR**. NT is near the threshold but not a threatened category; EW and EX describe loss of a wild population or of the taxon, not ever-higher subdivisions of threatened *living* wild populations.
3. **Read category together with scope and date.** A label is attached to a taxon at a defined assessment scale. The scientific classification may change with new evidence or changing conditions. Never supply a current population estimate or assert a category change without its dated assessment.
4. **Keep scale and recovery distinct from the category ladder.**

| Assessment | Question answered | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| Global Red List assessment | What is the taxon's extinction risk across its entire natural range? | It is not automatically a description of risk inside every country or region. |
| Regional or national Red List assessment | What is the taxon's extinction risk within the stated region? | Its category may differ from the global result because the spatial denominator and regional condition differ; always name the scale. |
| Green Status of Species | How recovered is the species, and what contribution has conservation made? | It complements rather than replaces the Red List. Recovery progress can coexist with substantial remaining extinction risk. |

A widespread species may be globally secure enough for a lower-risk category while declining severely inside one region. That does not make either scale meaningless: each assessment answers a question about its stated area. Conversely, an improvement recorded through Green Status does not silently downlist the species. **Red List status measures extinction risk; Green Status measures recovery and conservation impact.** Neither should be quoted without its assessment date and scope.

**Objection:** If the ladder ends in EX, why not call EW simply "more threatened than CR"? **Reply:** The threat thresholds for CR concern risk to a taxon still living in the wild; EW states that wild persistence has already ended. The diagram orders possible states of wild persistence without making them identical kinds of forecast.

**UPSC application:** GS-III biodiversity questions reward the phrase "risk assessment, not statute." For Prelims, separate three similar-looking expressions: **assessed low risk**, **assessed but information insufficient**, **not assessed**. In the 2026 official GS-I Q22, an IUCN-status claim appears alongside habitat and anatomy claims: each needs its own evidentiary test; the answer-neutral first attempt appears before Lesson 1.

**Mini recap:** NE ≠ DD ≠ LC; VU + EN + CR = threatened; EW ≠ EX; global ≠ regional; Red List risk ≠ Green Status recovery; category without scale and date is an incomplete citation.

**Revision notes**

1. IUCN is an international scientific assessment body, not a legislature.
2. Species Survival Commission specialist groups contribute taxon expertise.
3. NE means the criteria have not been applied.
4. DD means available evidence cannot support a risk estimate; it is not a low-risk finding.
5. LC is an assessed category below higher-risk thresholds.
6. NT lies outside the collective threatened group.
7. Threatened means exactly VU, EN and CR.
8. CR is the most severe threatened category for a taxon still surviving in the wild.
9. EW means survival only outside a natural wild population; EX means no reasonable doubt the last individual has died.
10. Every category citation should identify the taxon, assessment scale and date.
11. Global and regional/national assessments can differ because they evaluate different spatial extents.
12. Green Status measures recovery and conservation impact; it complements rather than replaces Red List extinction risk.

**Question:** A survey cannot support a defensible estimate of extinction risk for a forest mammal. A classmate puts it below LC because nobody proved a decline. What is wrong with that placement?

<details><summary>Scored model and marking notes</summary>

**Model (about 118 words):** DD describes a failure of available information to support an extinction-risk assessment, not a finding that risk is low. In India, poorly surveyed freshwater and invertebrate taxa may have narrow distributions exposed to land-use change. Treating missing surveys as safety would channel research and protection only to conspicuous species. By contrast, LC follows an evaluation against IUCN criteria; NE means evaluation has not taken place. A sensible response is to prioritise surveys where restricted habitat and ongoing threats make hidden risk plausible, then reassess on improved evidence. Precaution does not require claiming every DD species is CR. Ultimately the aim is to convert uncertainty into testable population, distribution and threat data, while protecting vulnerable habitat where immediate loss would be irreversible.

### Visual — alternative evidence, not five compulsory hurdles

```text
Population falls? ─────────────── A: reduction over a specified period
Range contracts? ──────────────── B: restricted range + required threat indicators
Few mature individuals declining? C: small population + continuing decline
Very few / very restricted? ───── D: very small or restricted population
Extinction risk model? ────────── E: quantitative extinction probability
                 \   |   |   |   /
                  Assess EACH relevant test
                           ↓
             ONE satisfied threshold can qualify
                VU / EN / CR, as applicable
```

*Caption: The criteria are independent possible routes to a category, but each criterion has its **own** numerical thresholds and subsidiary conditions.*

Suppose a species is hard to count but confined to a shrinking cluster of forest patches. A simple headcount may miss the danger; a geographical test can capture the risk. Conversely a widespread species can decline sharply enough to warrant concern. The five criteria therefore measure *different failure pathways* rather than voting on one common statistic.

| Criterion | Plain-language test | Technical caution |
|---|---|---|
| A | How much has the population shrunk over the prescribed time? | A reduction needs suitable observation, inference or projection; do not guess a time window. |
| B | How small is the mapped range? | **Extent of occurrence** (EOO: outer envelope of occupied locations) and **area of occupancy** (AOO: area actually occupied, measured on the prescribed grid) are not interchangeable. A small EOO/AOO alone is insufficient: the relevant category's **at least two** qualifying subconditions among severe fragmentation/few locations, continuing decline and extreme fluctuations are also required. |
| C | Are there few mature individuals **and** continuing decline? | Additional structure/rate conditions apply; never replace "mature individuals" with all individuals. |
| D | Is the mature population very small, or (for the applicable VU subcriterion) very restricted? | The restricted-area route is **not** an automatic CR finding. |
| E | What extinction probability follows from an accepted quantitative analysis? | A model depends on its assumptions and inputs, not on a guessed percentage. |

The move from observation to category is: define taxon and assessment scale → assemble dated observations, estimates and uncertainty → test criteria against the category-specific thresholds → identify the *highest* qualifying threat category → publish rationale and reassess when evidence changes. One qualifying criterion suffices, **not** one isolated suggestive symptom. For example, a tiny mapped range with neither the required additional B subconditions nor evidence for D does not by itself establish CR. This qualification repairs a common but tempting shortcut.

**India application:** The Great Indian Bustard illustrates why population size, distribution, fragmentation and particular hazards need to be examined together. Do not reverse-engineer the precise Red List criterion, threshold or assessment year from a newspaper description of its plight. A survey estimate is an observation at a specified time, not a continuously updated headcount. ⚠️ **Inference:** Monitoring range and mature individuals together gives a better management signal than one headline total.

**Objection:** Won't fragmented habitat automatically satisfy B? **Reply:** No. Fragmentation is one possible subcondition. A sufficiently small EOO or AOO and the required combination of subconditions must be substantiated at the proper category threshold; the *assessment*, not a classroom vignette, assigns the label.

**UPSC application:** Distinguish Criterion A from C (decline alone versus small population with decline), B from D (range with extra conditions versus very small/restricted population), and E from an informal prediction. Mains should explain a causal pathway rather than merely recite A–E.

**Mini recap:** Alternative routes, category-specific thresholds; EOO ≠ AOO; B requires more than small range; C needs continuing decline; D is not necessarily evidence of decline; E needs a defensible model.

**Revision notes**

1. Criterion A tests population reduction over a prescribed period.
2. Criterion B tests restricted EOO or AOO together with the required subsidiary conditions.
3. EOO is the outer spatial envelope; AOO is the area actually occupied on the prescribed grid.
4. Criterion C requires a small mature population and continuing decline plus additional structure conditions.
5. Criterion D addresses a very small population or the applicable restricted-population route.
6. Criterion E uses a quantitative extinction-probability analysis.
7. One fully satisfied criterion can qualify a taxon; one fragment of a criterion cannot.
8. A small range alone does not establish CR under Criterion B.
9. Counts must distinguish mature individuals from all individuals.
10. Assessment scale, date, uncertainty and taxonomic boundary remain essential.

**Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words.** Analyse how two species with different abundance trends could both qualify as threatened under the IUCN framework.

<details><summary>Scored model and marking notes</summary>

**Model (about 150 words):** The Red List assesses extinction risk by several independent evidence routes, not by headcount alone. A widespread species undergoing the requisite reduction over a prescribed period may qualify under Criterion A even if it still has many individuals. Another species could have no measured steep reduction yet meet Criterion B if its EOO or AOO falls below the relevant threshold **and** at least two required subconditions, such as severe fragmentation and continuing decline, are established. Alternatively, Criterion D can identify a very small mature population; quantitative Criterion E can model extinction probability. These are conditional illustrations, not category assignments to a named taxon. In India, monitoring the Great Indian Bustard therefore needs both abundance and spatial/hazard evidence. Survey effort and date affect apparent trends; a small range without the B subconditions cannot prove CR. Assessment should state the scale, qualifying criterion and uncertainty before habitat or infrastructure decisions are derived from the risk signal.

### Visual — which geographical proposition is being tested?

```text
                    SPECIES' NATURAL RANGE
                      /               \
       wholly within defined region   crosses region boundary
           endemic TO that region       not endemic TO that region
                    │
        measure threats and trends separately
                    ↓
      stable / declining / poorly known are DIFFERENT findings
```

*Caption: Endemism describes where a species naturally occurs; a Red List category estimates extinction risk. The named region's scale matters.*

Call a species "endemic to the Western Ghats" only if its natural range is confined to that region. An organism confined to India need not be confined to one Indian state; an animal present in an Indian sanctuary need not be endemic to India. Captive or introduced occurrences are not a substitute for its natural range.

**Types of endemism**

| Type | Meaning | Exam caution |
|---|---|---|
| Point or site endemism | Natural range confined to an exceptionally small locality, island, ridge, cave or stream system | One record does not prove that the whole range is known |
| Regional endemism | Range confined to a named biogeographic region such as the Western Ghats or Nicobar group | State borders and ecological regions need not coincide |
| National endemism | Natural range confined within one country's boundary | Useful for policy, but the political boundary may not explain the evolutionary process |
| Paleoendemism | An old lineage survives in a restricted remnant of a formerly wider distribution | Present restriction may reflect historical contraction and refugia |
| Neoendemism | A comparatively recent lineage evolved locally and has not spread far | Recent origin must be supported by evolutionary evidence, not inferred from a small map |

**Why endemics arise:** physical isolation on islands or separated mountains can reduce gene flow; river valleys, ridges and habitat fragmentation can reinforce separation; long-term climatic refugia can preserve old lineages; specialised dependence on one soil, stream, host or microclimate can confine a taxon; and local speciation coupled with weak dispersal can produce a new narrow-range lineage. Survey gaps can imitate endemism, so distribution claims must remain open to new records and taxonomic revision.

**Indian centres and examples**

| Region or centre | Illustrative taxa / habitats | What the example teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Western Ghats | Lion-tailed macaque in rainforest; Nilgiri tahr in high-elevation grassland; narrowly ranged amphibians | One centre contains several distinct habitat dependencies; “Western Ghats endemic” does not mean one common remedy |
| Eastern Himalaya and north-eastern hills | High turnover across elevation, ridges and valleys; western hoolock gibbon supplies a transboundary canopy-connectivity caution | Rich mountain diversity does not make every species Indian-endemic |
| Andaman and Nicobar islands | Concentrated plant and small-vertebrate endemism in isolated island habitats | Island isolation can create very small natural ranges and weak recolonisation options |
| Eastern Ghats hill and scrub systems | The Papikonda rusty-spotted-cat record supplies an occurrence-versus-endemism caution | Local records, restricted habitat and whole-range endemism are separate propositions |

Consider the **lion-tailed macaque**, naturally restricted to the Western Ghats' rainforest landscape, and the **Nilgiri tahr**, a Western Ghats mountain specialist. These are illustrations of habitat specificity, *not* interchangeable habitat requirements. Western Ghats amphibians may have extremely narrow ranges: a surviving patch in one valley cannot be replaced by a similarly sized forest anywhere in India if the species' conditions occur only there. In the islands, isolation that produces distinctive endemics can also make natural recolonisation after local extinction difficult. "Island" does not, by itself, prove a taxon's global category.

**Why mountain endemics can be especially vulnerable**

```text
warming / land-use pressure
          ↓
upslope shift into a narrower belt
          ↓
less area + isolated ridges/valleys + summit limit
          ↓
smaller connected habitat and fewer recolonisation routes
```

High mountains compress climate zones over short horizontal distances. A species tied to a cool, moist elevation belt may be pushed upslope as temperature or cloud conditions change; available land often narrows toward summits, producing a **summit trap**. Deep valleys and separated ridges can limit gene flow, while roads, transmission lines, tourism facilities or plantations can sever already narrow habitat bands. Extreme rainfall, landslides, fire, invasive species and altered flowering or breeding timing can compound the pressure. Yet upslope movement is not automatic, and a mountain species may persist in microrefugia; measure its climatic tolerance, habitat continuity and population trend before predicting collapse.

| Claim | What must be tested | Frequent error |
|---|---|---|
| Endemic to India | Entire *natural* range within India | "Occurs in India" or "has a protected site here." |
| Endemic to a subregion | Range wholly inside that subregion | Assuming every Indian endemic is a Western Ghats endemic. |
| Threatened | Meets VU, EN or CR assessment threshold | Treating every endemic as threatened. |
| Locally rare | Scarce at one location | Treating scarcity at one site as proof of global extinction risk. |

**Why vulnerability can compound:** If habitat is lost throughout the only natural range, the species cannot fall back on unaffected populations elsewhere. Fragmentation can obstruct movement and recolonisation. Yet range restriction alone does not automatically make an endemic endangered: habitat stability, actual threat and qualifying assessment criteria still matter. Nor are all hotspot species endemic: a hotspot is a region defined by exceptional endemic plant diversity and heavy habitat loss, not a label for each species inside it.

The **western hoolock gibbon (*Hoolock hoolock*)** adds three different lines of evidence. The Wildlife Institute of India's **Spring 2025** Hollongapar report calls it **Endangered** and records a population in **Hollongapar Gibbon Sanctuary, Jorhat, Assam**. The same account describes small apes moving by **brachiation**, swinging through the middle and upper canopy with elongated forelimbs, and why a railway-created canopy gap fragments their habitat. The gibbon is an ape, not a heavy-built gorilla; **neither ape status nor Indian sanctuary presence proves Indian endemism**. Its natural range also extends beyond India; check scale and assessment date before quoting its risk. The report's historical sanctuary census is **not** a current population count, and reported bridge installation is **not** proof of population recovery. Sanctuary, Red List category and locomotion require separate evidence.

**Objection:** If an animal is absent outside India today, isn't that proof it evolved here? **Reply:** No. Present natural-range endemism is a distribution statement, not a conclusion about evolutionary origin. **Revision notes**

1. Endemism describes the whole natural range relative to a named region; it is not a risk category.
2. Point, regional and national endemism use different spatial denominators.
3. Paleoendemics are old restricted remnants; neoendemics are recently evolved narrow-range lineages.
4. Isolation, refugia, specialised habitat, limited dispersal and local speciation can generate endemism.
5. Survey gaps and taxonomic revision can enlarge or alter a previously asserted range.
6. Western Ghats rainforest, montane grassland and seasonal specialised habitats require different remedies.
7. Nicobar island examples show how isolation can restrict range and recolonisation.
8. Mountain endemics can face upslope compression, shrinking summit area, ridge isolation and fragmented elevation belts.
9. Microrefugia may permit persistence, so warming does not justify an automatic collapse claim.
10. Endemism can multiply exposure when habitat loss covers the only range, but it does not automatically mean VU, EN or CR.
11. A sanctuary population or single camera-trap record proves occurrence, not national endemism.
12. Western hoolock gibbon evidence separates Endangered status, Hollongapar habitat and brachiation; its range crosses India's border.
13. Mahseer, dolphins, rusty-spotted cat, rhino, marsupial, indri, bonobo and elk questions require taxon-specific natural-range checks.

<details><summary>Scored model and marking notes</summary>

**Model (about 120 words):** Endemism means that a species' natural distribution is confined to a defined region. For the lion-tailed macaque, restricted Western Ghats rainforest habitat means that loss and fragmentation across its natural range cannot be buffered by populations in an unaffected distant biome. *Caption: A change of scale, an information shortfall and a recovery measure answer different questions.*

Think of a species plentiful outside India but rare in an Indian range edge. A *regional* assessment may emphasise its local risk; the *global* assessment may see a broader buffer. Conversely a species endemic to India has no external native population to buffer an India-wide collapse, although a valid regional method and taxonomic boundary must still be stated.

Lesson 1 established the core grammar: global and regional assessments use different spatial extents, while Green Status measures recovery rather than extinction risk. The advanced problem is interpreting results that move in different directions. A regional population may improve while the global category remains threatened; a better survey may change an estimate without any biological increase; and conservation action may make a measurable contribution without restoring the species across its indigenous range.

The IUCN **Green Status of Species** therefore remains alongside the Red List rather than above or below it on one ladder. A population can improve while still facing substantial extinction risk. A conservation programme should report the direction and dependence of recovery together with remaining risk, instead of treating one favourable short-term trend as proof of security.

**Data friction:** DD taxa may include understudied freshwater organisms, fungi and invertebrates. Survey geography and taxonomy influence apparent endemism; publishing a distribution map with unsampled gaps can mislead. Population estimates for charismatic species also depend on survey design, date and uncertainty. ⚠️ **Inference:** allocate some resources to resolving unknowns and reassess after survey; neither discard DD nor announce all DD taxa threatened.

**Dated India anchor:** IUCN reported on **31 July 2026** that a fourth training workshop brought together BSI and ZSI regional staff and other specialists to contribute toward assessment of species under a National Red List vision. This is a useful illustration of *assessment capacity*. **Objection:** If recovery is measured, doesn't a positive Green Status replace a worrying Red List label? **Reply:** No. The metrics ask related but different questions; conservation gains can coexist with serious remaining extinction risk and can be reversed if protective action ends.

**UPSC application:** Prefix category claims with scale, year and assessor. For policy discuss monitoring as well as protection; do not treat training targets as achieved biodiversity outcomes.

**Mini recap:** Global ≠ regional; Green ≠ Red; DD ≠ LC; training ≠ completed assessments; a count needs survey method and date.

**Revision notes**

1. A global assessment addresses extinction risk across the taxon's entire natural range.
2. A regional assessment addresses risk inside a defined region and may differ from the global result.
3. Different spatial denominators can produce different categories without contradiction.
4. The Red List estimates extinction risk.
5. Green Status evaluates recovery and the contribution of conservation action.
6. Improvement in recovery metrics need not remove substantial remaining extinction risk.
7. DD records an evidence gap, not a favourable category.
8. Survey geography, effort, method and taxonomic boundaries affect apparent range and abundance.
9. A programme target or training workshop is not a completed assessment total.
10. A changed estimate caused by better detection is not automatically biological population growth.

*Caption: The 2024 remand to expert feasibility led to specific 2025 directions; neither order by itself proves on-ground compliance.*

The Great Indian Bustard is a grassland bird assessed as **Critically Endangered** globally and protected under **Schedule I of India's Wildlife (Protection) Act**: two distinct classifications. A large bird crossing open arid landscapes may collide with overhead transmission lines; protecting individual birds without maintaining grassland and safe movement routes would attack only part of the problem. In **M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India**, the Supreme Court on **21 March 2024** modified its earlier broad undergrounding direction, required expert, area-sensitive feasibility review and recognised a **right against adverse effects of climate change under Articles 14 and 21**. That is the constitutional and procedural stage, **not the end of the remedy**.

The Court's **19 December 2025 operative judgment** then accepted revised priority areas of **14,013 sq km in Rajasthan** and **740 sq km in Gujarat** (paragraph 76(i), judgment pp. 64–65). It directed in-situ and ex-situ conservation in those areas forthwith and immediate monitoring of bustards; accepted a power corridor near the southern boundary of Desert National Park; approved restrictions on future renewable-energy development in revised priority areas; and accepted **immediate undergrounding of 80 km of 33 kV line in Rajasthan**. It also required time-bound rerouting of specified existing **66 kV-and-above** lines, ordered mitigation including undergrounding/rerouting to start immediately and be **completed within two years of 19 December 2025**, and required the competent authority to underground **250 km of WII-identified critical Rajasthan lines within two years** (directions viii–xi, xv, pp. 65–66). The competent authority must assess **bird-flight diverters on scientific evidence**, rather than assuming every line has received one (direction xiv). These are **court orders**, not evidence that all work is complete.

For **future** infrastructure, paragraph 67 accepts the expert committee's location-specific restrictions: no new overhead lines in revised priority areas except dedicated corridors (with an exception for **11 kV and below**), no new wind turbines there, and no new solar parks/plants above **2 MW** or expansion of existing solar parks in those areas. Paragraph 70 permits mitigation of 11 kV-and-below lines through insulated horizontal or bunched cables rather than compelling one universal cable design. The order also accepts an exception to mitigation of 11 kV-and-below lines in the **100-metre settlement buffer** (direction vi, p. 64). Do not generalise priority-area restrictions to all of Rajasthan, Gujarat or India's renewable infrastructure; nor infer installed underground lines, measured mortality reduction or recovery without post-order evidence.

**The hard objection:** If solar and wind projects are climate-friendly, why delay transmission to protect one bird? **Reply:** Climate mitigation and biodiversity protection are both public interests. The later order converted feasibility review into mapped priority areas, targeted undergrounding and rerouting, renewable-project siting restrictions and time-bound monitoring. Its corridors and low-voltage exceptions acknowledge real implementation constraints; its binding measures guard against leaving protection at the level of a promise. Inspect implementation records before evaluating results.

**Institutional roles:** WII's habitat and population research can guide decisions; MoEFCC and relevant authorities must translate legal duties into planning and compliance. Independent Red List reassessment and Indian legal review can run on different timetables. ⚠️ **Inference:** durable conservation needs a protected landscape and monitoring, not only an endangered-species label or a single device on one power line.

**UPSC application:** In GS-III, write the sequence: *scientific assessment → domestic protection → 2024 expert-review stage → operative 2025 area-specific directions → independent evidence of implementation*. Neither 2024 nor 2025 is an event in the last six months as of October 2026.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Try both before expanding the answers; these test synthesis rather than repeat a lesson question.

**A.** A species is endemic to a stable island forest, has not yet been assessed, and is protected under Indian law. Which three statements may be made without claiming a Red List risk category?

<details><summary>Worked answer A</summary>

The natural range lies within the specified island region; the species is **NE**, not an assessed LC/DD/CR taxon; its Indian statutory protection must be established from the applicable law. Stability of one habitat does not confer an IUCN category. **Remediation:** Keep geographical range, evidentiary state and domestic legal status in separate sentences.

</details>

**B.** A taxon satisfies a small EOO threshold but no verified B subconditions; its population also lacks a reliable estimate. What must an assessor avoid asserting?

<details><summary>Worked answer B</summary>

Avoid asserting CR or even a threatened category solely from a small EOO. Verify the required combination of B subconditions, independently check A/C/D/E if evidence permits, and disclose insufficient data when needed. **Remediation:** The "one criterion" rule does not waive conditions *inside* B.

</details>

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

### Original 10 marks — answer in 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish NE, DD and NT when constructing a survey plan for India's lesser-known taxa.

<details><summary>Model solution and marking</summary>

**Model (about 126 words):** NE means criteria have not been applied, DD means attempted assessment cannot determine risk from existing information, and NT is an assessed category close to a threatened threshold. These call for different next steps. For a never-assessed endemic insect, establish taxonomy, natural range and baseline field records before assigning risk. For a DD freshwater organism, target missing trend, habitat and distribution evidence and consider precaution if its habitat faces immediate irreversible pressure. For an NT taxon, monitor the measurements approaching relevant thresholds and intervene against identified pressures. IUCN's July 2026 Indian assessor training indicates capacity building, not completion of all proposed assessments. Indian legal safeguards require separate confirmation under domestic law. None of these three labels alone proves that a species is currently VU, EN or CR.

**Marks / 10:** Three definitions 4; distinct operational actions 4; scientific/legal qualification 2.

</details>

### Original 15 marks — answer in 250 words

**Question:** Analyse why a species' local rarity, endemism and global Red List category cannot be used as synonyms when identifying India's conservation priorities.

<details><summary>Model solution and marking</summary>

**Model (about 156 words):** Local rarity describes abundance at a specified site; endemism describes the entire natural range relative to a named region; a global IUCN category assesses extinction risk across the taxon's natural range under defined criteria. Thus a globally widespread species can be scarce in one Indian landscape without being Indian endemic, and an endemic species can have sufficiently stable habitat to avoid a threatened assessment. The lion-tailed macaque illustrates the spatial exposure of a Western Ghats endemic: habitat losses throughout its natural range leave fewer unaffected source populations. That mechanism does not itself establish a precise Red List threshold. Global and regional risk assessments can also differ. A practical Indian priority framework combines dated taxon-specific risk, habitat pressure and range, data gaps, applicable wildlife law and feasibility of action. Species with DD evidence need surveys as well as precaution where habitat conversion is imminent. Treating local abundance as a global population count, or assuming that an Indian legal schedule copies an IUCN category, would distort both prioritisation and enforcement.

**Marks / 15:** Three-way distinction 6; valid Indian mechanism 3; scale/criteria qualification 3; actionable priority test 3.

</details>

### Original 20 marks — answer in 250 words

**Question:** Propose a stepwise science-to-action strategy for a range-restricted Indian species threatened by new infrastructure, balancing uncertainty, law and monitoring.

<details><summary>Model solution and marking</summary>

**Model (about 212 words):** First delimit the species' natural range and map the infrastructure footprint against breeding and movement habitat. Establish a dated, scale-specific Red List assessment, identifying the qualifying criterion rather than assuming that small range alone proves CR. If data are inadequate, record DD or the actual evidentiary status and commission targeted surveys. Second, identify the applicable Indian wildlife law and any relevant CITES trade rule; scientific and legal classifications are distinct. Third, compare avoidance, route changes and feasible mitigation by location. In the Great Indian Bustard case, the Supreme Court's **March 2024** judgment mandated expert area-specific review rather than universal undergrounding. Its **December 2025** operative judgment then set revised Rajasthan and Gujarat priority areas, restrictions on new infrastructure there, urgent 33 kV undergrounding and specified higher-voltage rerouting; WII-identified critical Rajasthan lines must be undergrounded within the ordered two-year period. These duties allow a practitioner to select actual measures, not to infer they were already completed. Fourth, monitor habitat, mortality, movement and survey uncertainty against a published baseline, and inspect court-directed works on the ground. Include less visible taxa in survey budgets: concentrating solely on charismatic species misses DD endemics. The result is a testable plan, not a declaration that either a risk category, judicial deadline or infrastructure clearance settles the ecological outcome.

**Marks / 20:** Range/criterion and uncertainty 5; domestic/trade-law separation 4; proportionate spatial mitigation 5; dated case fidelity 3; monitoring and qualified conclusion 3.

</details>

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## The category grammar

- IUCN Red List = scientific extinction-risk assessment; no statutory protection follows automatically. Distinguish taxon, assessment **scale** and **year** before quoting a status.
- NE: never assessed; DD: inadequate evidence to estimate risk; LC: evaluated below higher-risk thresholds; NT: close to threatened; **threatened = VU + EN + CR**. CR is highest threatened category for a taxon still in the wild. EW survives only outside natural wild range in captivity/cultivation or as a far-off naturalised population; EX means no reasonable doubt last individual died.
- Do **not** draw NE → DD → LC as an increasing-risk ladder. DD can conceal serious risk; neither DD nor NE is proof of safety.

## Criteria without shortcut

- **A** reduction over prescribed period; **B** restricted **EOO/AOO** plus at least two required subconditions (fragmentation/few locations, continuing decline, extreme fluctuations); **C** few mature individuals plus continuing decline and further conditions; **D** very small population or applicable very restricted VU route; **E** quantitative extinction-probability modelling. A single *fully satisfied* qualifying criterion can determine threat category.
- EOO: outer spatial envelope; AOO: area occupied on prescribed grid. Small area alone ≠ B finding. Document quality, date, threshold and population definition before inferring change.
- The bustard's CR status does not authorise guessing its exact qualifying subcriterion or a current total. Reassessment is not a continuous headcount.

## Range, Indian habitats and evidence

- Endemic **to named region** = entire natural range lies there. Occurring in India, being protected here and having one sanctuary population do not establish Indian endemism. Current range ≠ place of evolutionary origin.
- **Spatial types:** point/site endemic, regional endemic and national endemic use different denominators. **Evolutionary types:** paleoendemics are old restricted remnants; neoendemics are recently evolved narrow-range lineages.
- **Drivers:** island or ridge isolation, vicariance, long-term refugia, specialised soil/stream/host or microclimate, low dispersal and local speciation. Apparent endemism can shrink when surveys or taxonomy improve.
- **Indian centres/examples:** Western Ghats (lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri tahr and narrowly ranged amphibians); Eastern Himalaya and north-eastern hills (high elevational turnover, while many ranges remain transboundary); Andaman–Nicobar islands (concentrated island plant and small-vertebrate endemism); isolated Eastern Ghats hills (Papikonda occurrence evidence as a warning that one record is not endemism).
- **Mountain vulnerability:** warming can compress a cool/moist belt upslope; habitat area often narrows toward summits; valleys and ridges limit gene flow; roads and linear infrastructure sever elevation bands; extreme rainfall, landslides, fire, invasive species and phenological shifts can compound pressure. Microrefugia and surviving source populations prevent automatic collapse claims.
- Western Ghats: lion-tailed macaque in rainforest and Nilgiri tahr in mountain habitat illustrate different dependencies. Habitat loss across the only natural range can eliminate spatial buffers; endemism itself is not an IUCN threat category. Islands can constrain natural recolonisation; do not assert recovery is impossible without assessing surviving sources and interventions.
- Hotspots combine exceptional endemic plant diversity and habitat loss, but no hotspot label categorises every resident animal. Unstudied fungi, invertebrates and freshwater taxa need surveys, not invented threat counts.
- PYQ evidence after the **first attempt**: Asiatic lion now wild in Gir, one-horned rhino India **and Nepal**, Bactrian camel Central Asia; distinct Cauvery blue-finned and Himalayan golden mahseers are fishes; Irrawaddy dolphin Chilika versus Ganges dolphin Chambal; **2016 Papikonda camera-trap record places rusty-spotted cat in the northern Eastern Ghats of Andhra Pradesh** without making it region-endemic; Indian nightjar and spoonbill are birds, and the question's “white ibis” is an ibis common-name formulation, also a bird; marsupials are widespread across Australasia and the Americas in varied habitats and are not naturally recorded from India. Indri Madagascar; bonobo DR Congo; elk North America and parts of Asia. These are natural-history study anchors, **not** marked PYQ choices.
- Western hoolock gibbon: WII's **Spring 2025** report identifies *Hoolock hoolock* as Endangered, records Hollongapar Gibbon Sanctuary in Assam and explains long-forelimb **brachiation** across the canopy; its wider natural range crosses India's border. The official 2026 question tests category, sanctuary and physical adaptation separately. Do **not** invent a 2026 reassessment, current headcount or Indian endemism. Official PYQs are printed unkeyed before Lesson 1.

## Institutions, recovery and action

- IUCN/SSC: scientific assessment; BSI/ZSI: Indian botanical/zoological field evidence; WII: habitat and wildlife research; Wildlife (Protection) Act: domestic legal safeguards; CITES: international trade controls. One instrument does not automatically reclassify the others.
- **Assessment scale:** a global Red List assessment addresses extinction risk across the entire natural range; a regional/national assessment addresses the stated area and may differ because its spatial denominator and regional condition differ. Always state scale and date.
- **Recovery axis:** Green Status assesses how recovered a species is and the contribution of conservation action. It complements rather than replaces Red List extinction risk; recovery progress can coexist with a threatened category.
- IUCN's **31 July 2026** India training story documents assessment *capacity*, not completed nationwide categories or conservation outcomes. Do not substitute a proposed programme target for measured species change.
- Bustard: globally CR, domestically Schedule I; arid grassland + overhead-line collision. **21 March 2024**, *M.K. Ranjitsinh*: the Court substituted the broad 2021 transmission-line directions with expert, area-sensitive feasibility review, kept GIB preservation non-negotiable and articulated the Articles 14/21 right against adverse climate-change effects; it did not create an automatic infrastructure ban or prove implementation. **19 December 2025 operative judgment**: revised priority areas **14,013 sq km Rajasthan / 740 sq km Gujarat**; immediate in-/ex-situ measures and monitoring, accepted future-project restrictions, immediate undergrounding of **80 km of 33 kV Rajasthan line**, time-bound rerouting of specified **66 kV+ lines**, undergrounding of **250 km of WII-identified critical Rajasthan lines within two years**, scientific evaluation of bird-flight diverters. Court order ≠ completed mitigation ≠ recovery; do not extend priority-area restrictions to an entire state.
- Mains spine: define range and date → qualify risk under valid criterion → identify habitat and direct threat → verify Indian legal rule and trade rule separately → choose spatially feasible remedy → measure mortality, habitat and population trend → revise. If evidence is DD, combine precaution with targeted research.
