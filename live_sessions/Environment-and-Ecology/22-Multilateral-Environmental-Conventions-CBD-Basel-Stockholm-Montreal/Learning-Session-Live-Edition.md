*The legal effect depends on the instrument and the treaty clause authorising it, not merely on the label "international decision".*

### 1. Start with a practical question

Suppose countries agree that a pollutant is dangerous. That agreement alone does not answer four different questions:

1. What conduct is legally required?
2. Which countries are bound?
3. Can the rule later be tightened without negotiating a new treaty?
4. Who measures and responds to non-compliance?

The four regimes in this topic answer these questions differently. CBD uses broad objectives and national planning. Basel controls particular transboundary waste movements. Stockholm changes annexes chemical by chemical. Montreal combines control schedules, trade restrictions, finance and an adjustment procedure.

### 2. The basic legal vocabulary

| Instrument | Exact meaning | Binding effect |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Convention or treaty** | The principal negotiated legal instrument, such as CBD, Basel, Stockholm or Vienna | Binding only on a state or regional organisation that becomes a Party, after entry into force for it |
| ✅ **Protocol** | A separate treaty linked to a framework convention, such as Cartagena, Nagoya or Montreal | Requires its own consent to be bound; being Party to the parent convention does not automatically make a state Party to every protocol |
| ✅ **Amendment** | A formal alteration to treaty text or annexes under the treaty's amendment rules | Binding according to the acceptance, ratification, opt-out or entry-into-force rule governing that amendment |
| ✅ **COP decision** | A decision of the Conference of the Parties using authority conferred by the treaty | May adopt budgets, procedures, guidance, programmes or annex listings; it is not automatically equivalent to a newly ratified treaty obligation |
| ✅ **Adjustment under Montreal** | A special expedited change to existing control measures | Once adopted under the Protocol's adjustment procedure, it can bind all Parties without separate ratification in the same way required for a formal amendment |
| ✅ **Declaration, reservation or opt-out** | A legally relevant state act allowed by the instrument | Its effect depends entirely on the treaty clause; environmental agreements do not use one uniform rule |

### 3. COP versus MOP

✅ A **Conference of the Parties (COP)** governs a convention. A **Meeting of the Parties (MOP)** governs a protocol. When the same delegates meet under a parent convention and its protocol, the legal capacities remain distinct.

| Regime | Governing meeting |
|---|---|
| CBD | CBD COP |
| Cartagena Protocol | Conference of the Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Protocol, commonly CP-MOP |
| Nagoya Protocol | COP serving as the meeting of the Parties, commonly NP-MOP |
| Basel Convention | Basel COP |
| Stockholm Convention | Stockholm COP |
| Vienna Convention | Conference of the Parties to the Vienna Convention |
| Montreal Protocol | Meeting of the Parties to the Montreal Protocol |

⚠️ **Why this matters:** a CBD COP decision cannot by itself turn a non-Party to the Nagoya Protocol into a Protocol Party. Similarly, the Kigali Amendment changes the Montreal Protocol for states bound by that amendment; it is not a free-standing climate treaty.

### 4. Four kinds of obligation

```text
SUBSTANTIVE OBLIGATION
  phase out / eliminate / restrict / prohibit

PROCEDURAL OBLIGATION
  notify / obtain consent / report / prepare a plan

SUPPORT OBLIGATION
  finance / technology transfer / capacity building

COLLECTIVE POLICY TARGET
  global framework or COP target translated through national action
```

✅ Basel's prior informed consent is procedural but legally consequential. ✅ Montreal's phase-out or phase-down schedules are substantive and time-bound. ✅ CBD Article 6 requires national biodiversity strategies or equivalent integration, while the KMGBF's global targets are implemented through nationally set targets rather than Montreal-style country control schedules.

### 5. Institutions are not interchangeable

Every regime usually has:

- a governing body;
- a secretariat;
- technical or scientific bodies;
- reporting arrangements;
- finance or technical-assistance channels; and
- some form of implementation review.

But institutional strength differs. A compliance committee may be formal and case-oriented, as under Basel or Montreal, or absent at the parent-treaty level, as under the CBD. Finance may be a designated treaty mechanism, as with the GEF under CBD and Stockholm, or a dedicated fund created for incremental compliance costs, as with Montreal's Multilateral Fund.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** If international environmental law lacks a world police force, calling these instruments "binding" exaggerates their effect.

**Reply:** Bindingness and centralised coercive enforcement are different. Treaty duties can shape domestic laws, reporting, trade permissions, access to finance, diplomatic scrutiny and reciprocal conduct even where sanctions are rare.

**Residual:** ⚠️ Legal form cannot guarantee implementation. Capacity, finance, technology, domestic political economy and measurement determine whether obligations change outcomes.

### UPSC application

- First identify the legal level: convention, protocol, amendment, annex listing or COP decision.
- Then ask whether the tested element is binding, procedural, nationally determined or merely recommendatory.
- Never call the KMGBF a protocol or the Montreal Protocol a stand-alone convention.
- Never infer that every COP decision requires fresh parliamentary ratification; equally, never assume every COP declaration creates a new binding duty.

### Revision notes

1. A treaty binds a state only after valid consent and entry into force for that state.
2. Signature and ratification are legally distinct.
3. A protocol is a separate treaty linked to a parent convention.
4. An amendment follows the parent instrument's amendment rule.
5. A COP decision is not automatically a treaty amendment.
6. Annex amendments can create binding chemical controls where the treaty authorises that route.
7. Montreal adjustments differ from formal amendments because they can tighten existing controls without separate ratification.
8. COP governs a convention; MOP governs a protocol.
9. Substantive, procedural, support and collective-target obligations must be separated.
10. Bindingness does not guarantee domestic implementation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it legally inaccurate to say that the KMGBF, the Nagoya Protocol and the Kigali Amendment are three equivalent "CBD decisions"?

**Model answer:** The KMGBF is a CBD COP-adopted global framework implemented through national targets; the Nagoya Protocol is a separately ratified treaty under the CBD; and the Kigali Amendment is a formal amendment to the Montreal Protocol. Their parent regimes, consent rules and legal effects are therefore different.

**Misconception to avoid:** "Adopted at a COP" does not make every instrument legally identical. The treaty text determines whether a COP is adopting guidance, a framework, an annex change or a formal amendment.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish a treaty, a protocol, an amendment and a COP decision in multilateral environmental governance.

**Model (126 words):** A treaty or convention is the principal legal instrument and binds only Parties for which it has entered into force. A protocol is a separate treaty nested under a framework convention; thus CBD membership does not automatically create Nagoya Protocol obligations. An amendment formally changes treaty text or adds a new control architecture and becomes binding under the instrument's acceptance rules, as with Kigali under Montreal. A COP decision is taken by the treaty's governing body and may adopt budgets, guidance, monitoring systems or annex listings where the treaty delegates that power. Its legal effect therefore depends on the enabling clause. Montreal's adjustments form a special category: they can tighten existing substance-control schedules through an expedited procedure. Precise classification prevents candidates from treating political targets, procedural decisions and ratified obligations as interchangeable.

**Unique rubric (10):** treaty definition 2 · protocol distinction 2 · amendment rule 2 · COP-decision qualification 2 · accurate example and conclusion 2.

### Visual — from a broad bargain to national action

```text

        |
        +--> OBJECTIVE 1: conservation
        +--> OBJECTIVE 2: sustainable use
```

*The CBD is a framework treaty: it sets a comprehensive bargain and procedural duties, while much substantive delivery occurs through national law and policy.*

### 1. Formation and purpose

✅ The Convention on Biological Diversity was adopted on 22 May 1992, opened for signature at the Rio Earth Summit and entered into force on 29 December 1993.

✅ Article 1 contains three co-equal objectives:

1. conservation of biological diversity;
2. sustainable use of its components; and
3. fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the utilisation of genetic resources.

The third objective prevents a conservation-only reading. The Convention recognises state sovereignty over natural resources while linking access, technology, funding and benefit-sharing.

### 2. What the CBD requires

| CBD function | Treaty route | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| National planning | ✅ Article 6 | Prepare an NBSAP or equivalent strategy and integrate biodiversity into sectoral policy |
| In-situ conservation | ✅ Article 8 | Protected areas, ecosystem and habitat measures, alien-species control, traditional knowledge under Article 8(j) |
| Ex-situ conservation | ✅ Article 9 | Conservation outside natural habitats, preferably complementing in-situ action |
| Sustainable use | ✅ Article 10 | Integrate conservation and sustainable use into decision-making |
| Impact assessment | ✅ Article 14 | Assess projects likely to have significant adverse biodiversity effects |
| Access to genetic resources | ✅ Article 15 | Access is subject to national authority; terms should support fair benefit-sharing |
| Technology and finance | ✅ Articles 16, 20 and 21 | Technology access and financial resources support implementation |
| Reporting | ✅ Article 26 | Parties report measures and effectiveness |

⚠️ These provisions vary in precision. "Develop national strategies" is a clearer procedural duty than a numerical biodiversity outcome. This explains why CBD implementation is legally binding in parts but not equivalent to Montreal's substance-by-substance schedules.

### 3. Institutional architecture

```text
CBD COP
  |
  +--> Secretariat: convening, documents, communication, implementation support
  +--> SBSTTA: scientific, technical and technological advice
  +--> SBI: review of implementation, planning, reporting and means of implementation
  +--> ad hoc / thematic bodies and working groups
  +--> GEF: institution operating the financial mechanism under COP guidance
```

✅ The **Global Environment Facility (GEF)** operates the CBD financial mechanism under the authority and guidance of, and accountability to, the COP. It is not the CBD Secretariat and it is not a compliance tribunal.

✅ The CBD COP adopts programmes of work, frameworks, monitoring arrangements, budgets and guidance. National implementation remains central because biodiversity loss is driven by land use, agriculture, infrastructure, consumption, pollution and other domestic sectors.

### 4. Compliance: the exact distinction

✅ The parent CBD does **not** have a formal treaty-wide compliance committee comparable to the Basel Implementation and Compliance Committee or Montreal's Implementation Committee.

Its implementation architecture relies mainly on:

- national reports;
- NBSAPs and national targets;
- COP and SBI review;
- monitoring frameworks;
- peer learning and transparency;
- finance, capacity building and technical cooperation.

✅ Cartagena and Nagoya, however, have protocol-specific compliance procedures. Do not transfer those committees upward to the parent CBD.

### 5. India interface

✅ India's principal domestic interface includes:

- Biological Diversity Act, 2002, as amended in 2023;
- Biological Diversity Rules, 2024;
- National Biodiversity Authority;
- State Biodiversity Boards;
- local Biodiversity Management Committees;
- People's Biodiversity Registers;
- access-and-benefit-sharing procedures; and
- National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan.

```text
GLOBAL CBD BARGAIN
      |
      v
INDIA'S CENTRAL LAW + RULES
      |
      +--> NBA: national approvals, regulation and guidance
      +--> SBBs: state-level functions
      +--> BMCs: local biodiversity knowledge and benefit-sharing interface
      |
      v
community knowledge + conservation + regulated access + benefit-sharing
```

⚠️ Institutional creation is not proof of effective benefit transfer. A Mains answer should separate legal design from evidence of implementation.

### 6. Why biodiversity varies across India — the 2018 PYQ foundation

India's biodiversity varies because several gradients intersect rather than because one climatic factor changes:

| Driver | How it creates variation | Indian expression |
|---|---|---|
| Latitude and monsoon | Tropical energy combines with sharply different rainfall and seasonality | Wet evergreen, deciduous, thorn, grassland and wetland mosaics |
| Relief and altitude | Temperature, moisture and growing season change from plains to high mountains | Tropical foothills, temperate forests, sub-alpine belts and alpine systems |
| Coasts and islands | Salinity, tides, isolation and coral or mangrove habitats create distinct communities | Western/eastern coasts, Lakshadweep and Andaman–Nicobar Islands |
| Geological and biogeographic history | India links Himalayan, Indo-Malayan, peninsular and arid evolutionary histories | Endemism and turnover among regions |
| Habitat and land-use mosaic | Forests, grasslands, deserts, rivers, wetlands, farms and community-managed landscapes maintain different species and genetic pools | Wild biodiversity, crop landraces, livestock breeds and associated knowledge |

✅ India's NR-7 describes India as one of the **17 megadiverse countries**, holding nearly **8% of the world's recorded species** on about **2.4% of global land area**. It uses **10 biogeographic zones and 27 biotic provinces**, while four global biodiversity hotspots extend into India. These are different classifications: biogeographic zones cover the whole country; hotspots identify exceptionally rich and threatened regions.

The Biological Diversity Act helps conserve this variation through several linked functions:

1. **Regulated access and transfer:** access to biological resources, associated knowledge, research results and intellectual-property use is channelled through statutory permissions or intimations.
2. **Access and benefit-sharing:** commercial or research value derived from biological resources should generate fair monetary or non-monetary benefits rather than detach value from provider communities.
3. **Institutions at three levels:** the NBA, State Biodiversity Boards and BMCs divide national, state and local functions.
4. **Local documentation:** BMCs prepare and maintain People's Biodiversity Registers, making local resources and knowledge visible for planning and benefit-sharing.
5. **Conservation tools:** the Act supports Biodiversity Heritage Sites, threatened-species measures, biodiversity funds and integration of conservation with sustainable use.

The limitation is equally important. A statute can create institutions and permissions, but conservation still depends on sound inventories, functioning BMCs, habitat protection, fair benefit transfer, enforcement and coordination with sectoral laws.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The CBD's broad language and nationally mediated implementation make it too weak to halt biodiversity loss.

**Reply:** Biodiversity is inherently cross-sectoral and place-specific. A framework treaty can establish shared objectives, planning duties, finance, reporting, protocols and adaptive COP decisions while leaving ecological choices to national and local levels.

**Residual:** ⚠️ Flexibility can become an accountability gap where national targets, budgets and sectoral policies are insufficient. The absence of Montreal-style schedules and a parent-CBD compliance committee remains institutionally important.

### UPSC application

- Write all three CBD objectives in the correct order.
- Distinguish Article 6 procedural implementation from numerical COP targets.
- Identify GEF as the financial-mechanism operator, not a punitive compliance body.
- Use the NBA-SBB-BMC chain only after explaining the global CBD bargain.
- For the 2018 Mains PYQ, explain climatic, altitudinal, coastal-island, historical and habitat-mosaic variation before evaluating the Act's regulatory, institutional, documentation and benefit-sharing functions.

### Revision notes

1. CBD was adopted in 1992 and entered into force in 1993.
2. Conservation, sustainable use and benefit-sharing are co-equal objectives.
3. CBD is a framework treaty, not a species-trade treaty.
4. Article 6 centres national strategies and integration.
5. Article 8 covers in-situ conservation.
6. Article 15 frames access to genetic resources.
7. Article 26 creates national reporting duties.
8. COP, SBSTTA, SBI and Secretariat perform different functions.
9. GEF operates the financial mechanism under COP guidance.
10. The parent CBD lacks a formal compliance committee.
11. Cartagena and Nagoya have their own compliance mechanisms.
12. India's NBA-SBB-BMC structure translates CBD goals into domestic institutions.
13. India's biodiversity spans 10 biogeographic zones and 27 biotic provinces; hotspots are a separate threatened-richness concept.
14. The Biological Diversity Act links regulated access, ABS, PBRs, conservation tools and biodiversity funds.
15. Legal architecture helps only when inventories, local institutions, habitats, benefit transfer and enforcement function.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the CBD correctly described as legally binding even though the KMGBF's 30x30 target is not a Montreal-style national control schedule?

**Model answer:** The CBD creates binding treaty duties such as national planning and reporting. The KMGBF is a COP-adopted global framework implemented through nationally set targets. Therefore, the treaty is binding while a global target does not automatically become an identical, directly enforceable quota for every Party.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate "non-binding numerical target" with "non-binding convention." Different layers within one regime can have different legal effects.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain why the CBD is a framework convention rather than a Montreal-style control treaty.

**Model (131 words):** The CBD establishes a comprehensive legal bargain around conservation, sustainable use and fair benefit-sharing, but biodiversity loss arises from diverse national sectors and ecosystems rather than one bounded set of chemicals. It therefore relies on national biodiversity strategies, integration into sectoral planning, in-situ and ex-situ measures, access regulation and periodic reporting. Its COP, SBSTTA and SBI develop scientific guidance, frameworks and review systems, while the GEF supports implementation. Unlike the Montreal Protocol, the CBD does not assign each Party a uniform, substance-specific phase-out calendar backed by a dedicated compliance fund. This flexibility enables ecological and developmental differentiation, but it also weakens comparability and accountability. The CBD is thus legally binding in its treaty duties while many collective targets require domestic translation before they can shape conduct.

## Lesson 3 — Cartagena and Nagoya: two protocols, two different transactions

Progress: 3 / 12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Biosafety, LMOs, access and benefit-sharing

### Visual — do not confuse movement with utilisation

```text
CBD
 |
 +--> CARTAGENA PROTOCOL (2000)
 |      transaction: transboundary movement of LMOs
 |      key tool: advance informed agreement + Biosafety Clearing-House
 |      concern: biodiversity risk, also taking human-health risks into account
 |

        concern: fair and equitable benefit-sharing
```

*Both protocols serve the CBD, but they regulate different objects, risks and consent chains.*

### 1. Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety

✅ Adopted in 2000 and in force from 2003, the Cartagena Protocol addresses the safe transfer, handling and use of **living modified organisms (LMOs)** resulting from modern biotechnology that may adversely affect biological diversity, taking account also of risks to human health.

#### Advance informed agreement

```text
first intentional transboundary movement
of an LMO for intentional introduction into environment
        |
        v
export notification -> risk information -> import decision
        |
        v
consent / conditions / refusal under Protocol procedure
```

✅ The **advance informed agreement (AIA)** procedure does not apply identically to every LMO movement. LMOs intended for direct use as food, feed or for processing use a separate information and decision route, strongly linked to the Biosafety Clearing-House.

✅ The **Biosafety Clearing-House (BCH)** exchanges decisions, risk assessments, laws and other implementation information.

✅ The Protocol reflects the precautionary approach: scientific uncertainty does not compel an importing Party to approve a movement where potential adverse effects remain.

✅ The **Nagoya-Kuala Lumpur Supplementary Protocol on Liability and Redress (2010)** addresses response measures where damage from LMOs occurs. It is not the Nagoya ABS Protocol.

### 2. Nagoya Protocol on ABS

✅ Adopted in 2010 and in force from 2014, the Nagoya Protocol operationalises the CBD's third objective.

| Element | Function |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Prior informed consent (PIC)** | Access occurs with the provider country's authorisation where its law requires it |
| ✅ **Mutually agreed terms (MAT)** | Provider and user agree benefit-sharing, permitted use, dispute terms and related conditions |
| ✅ **Monetary benefits** | Fees, royalties, milestone payments or research funding |
| ✅ **Non-monetary benefits** | Technology transfer, training, joint research, data or institutional capacity |
| ✅ **ABS Clearing-House** | Publishes national measures, permits and relevant information to improve transparency and monitoring |
| ✅ **Compliance checkpoints** | Help monitor utilisation and observance of provider-country requirements and MAT |

### 3. Digital sequence information: the hard boundary

Traditional ABS was built around access to a physical genetic resource. Modern research can sequence material, store data digitally and use the information elsewhere.

```text
physical sample model
access -> permit -> utilisation -> benefit-sharing

DSI challenge
sequence -> upload -> remote use -> value creation
                 |
                 v
physical-access checkpoint may be bypassed
```

⚠️ DSI therefore tests whether benefit-sharing can follow information rather than only the physical sample. The CBD COP process has developed a multilateral DSI mechanism and Cali Fund, while the relationship between that collective mechanism and bilateral Nagoya implementation must be stated carefully.

### 4. Compliance architecture

✅ Cartagena has a 15-member Compliance Committee that assists Parties and can recommend supportive measures to the CP-MOP.

✅ Nagoya has a cooperative, non-adversarial compliance procedure and committee under Article 30.

✅ The BCH and ABS Clearing-House are transparency and information infrastructures. They are not courts and do not replace domestic permits, risk assessments or contracts.

### 5. India interface

✅ India's biosafety regulation and genetic-resource ABS are distinct domestic tracks.

- Biosafety decisions involve India's environmental and biotechnology regulatory architecture for genetically engineered organisms.
- ABS operates through the Biological Diversity Act framework, NBA, SBBs, BMCs, Rules and ABS regulations.
- The 2025 ABS Regulations expressly extend the domestic access-and-benefit-sharing interface to digital sequence information.

⚠️ The existence of a BMC does not by itself decide every foreign-access application. UPSC may test local, state and national functions separately.

### 6. BMCs, Nagoya implementation and collection fees

A **Biodiversity Management Committee (BMC)** is constituted by every local body under Section 41. Its local role connects the Nagoya objective of fair benefit-sharing with resources and knowledge held in a real territorial jurisdiction.

The functions must be separated:

```text
local biodiversity and associated knowledge
        -> People's Biodiversity Register
        -> BMC advice when NBA/SBB considers access
        -> record of access, collection fee and benefit-sharing
        -> local conservation and benefit interface
```

✅ Section 41 authorises a BMC to levy charges by way of **collection fees** from a person accessing or collecting a biological resource for **commercial purposes** from areas within its territorial jurisdiction. This is a defined local statutory power, not a general power to tax every resident, every customary use or every movement of biological material.

✅ At the time of the 2023 question, Rule 22 of the Biological Diversity Rules, 2004 also required the BMC's register to record access granted, collection fees imposed, benefits derived and the mode of benefit-sharing. The BMC therefore supplies local knowledge, documentation, consultation and a collection-fee interface; it does not replace the NBA or SBB in every approval decision.

This resolves the 2023 PYQ's institutional distinction without converting the BMC into either a powerless documentation committee or the sole national ABS authority.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Prior consent can slow research, seed exchange and innovation.

**Reply:** Consent, risk assessment and MAT address asymmetric risks: environmental release may be irreversible, and commercial use can detach value from provider communities. Predictable, digitised and time-bound procedures can reduce transaction costs without deleting safeguards.

**Residual:** ⚠️ DSI, long research chains and multiple jurisdictions make traceability difficult. Overly burdensome systems may encourage avoidance; overly weak systems reproduce biopiracy and biosafety risks.

### UPSC application

- Cartagena = LMO biosafety, AIA, BCH.
- Nagoya = genetic resources, PIC, MAT, benefit-sharing, ABS Clearing-House.
- Do not equate AIA under Cartagena with PIC under Nagoya merely because both involve prior information.
- Separate the Nagoya Protocol from the Nagoya-Kuala Lumpur Supplementary Protocol.
- The 2023 BMC PYQ tests local ABS implementation: PBRs, consultation, access records and the statutory collection-fee power must be distinguished from NBA/SBB approval functions.

### Revision notes

1. Cartagena and Nagoya are both protocols to the CBD.

### Visual — Basel's control chain

```text
WASTE GENERATED

      |
      +--> manage as close as practicable to source
      |
      +--> if transboundary movement is proposed:
              classify -> notify -> import/transit consent

```

*Basel does not begin with trade permission; it begins with waste prevention, minimisation and environmentally sound management.*

### 1. Formation and objectives

✅ The Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and their Disposal was adopted on 22 March 1989 and entered into force on 5 May 1992.

Its architecture pursues three linked aims:

1. minimise the generation and hazardousness of covered wastes;
2. ensure environmentally sound management (ESM); and
3. control and reduce transboundary movements, especially where the receiving state lacks capacity.

✅ "Disposal" under Basel is broad enough to include listed final-disposal and recovery/recycling operations. Therefore, sending waste abroad for recovery is not automatically outside the Convention.

### 2. What waste is covered?

Basel coverage is built through:

- waste streams and hazardous constituents;
- hazardous characteristics;
- wastes defined as hazardous under relevant national law;
- "other wastes" specifically brought within the Convention, including household waste and certain plastic and e-waste categories after amendments; and
- exclusions, such as radioactive waste covered by other international control systems and certain ship-generated operational wastes covered elsewhere.

⚠️ A used product and a waste are not legally identical. Classification may turn on whether the holder discards, intends to discard or is required to discard it, and whether claimed reuse is genuine.

### 3. Prior informed consent

```text
EXPORTING STATE
  notification with required information
        |
        v
IMPORTING STATE --------> written consent / conditions / denial
        |
        +--> transit-state responses where required
        |
        v
movement may begin only after legal conditions are met
```

✅ Basel's PIC procedure requires advance notification and written consent. It is not a licence for dumping: movement must also be consistent with ESM and the Convention's restrictions.

✅ An exporting Party must not allow export where it has reason to believe the wastes will not be managed in an environmentally sound manner.

✅ Parties may prohibit imports. Exporting Parties must respect notified prohibitions.

### 4. Illegal traffic and responsibility

✅ Illegal traffic includes movement:

- without notification;
- without required consent;
- with consent obtained through falsification, misrepresentation or fraud;
- materially inconsistent with documents; or
- resulting in deliberate illegal disposal.

The Convention requires Parties to treat illegal traffic as criminal and to take appropriate domestic measures. Responsibility for taking waste back or ensuring ESM depends on whether the exporter/generator, importer/disposer or another actor caused the illegality.

### 5. Institutions and implementation support

| Institution | Function |
|---|---|
| ✅ Basel COP | Governing body; adopts amendments, work programmes, technical guidelines and budget |
| ✅ Secretariat | Notifications, meeting support, legal and technical coordination |
| ✅ Open-ended Working Group | Policy, legal and technical preparatory work |
| ✅ Implementation and Compliance Committee | Specific submissions and general implementation/compliance review |
| ✅ Basel Convention Regional and Coordinating Centres | Training, technology transfer and regional capacity |

✅ Basel does not possess a Montreal-style dedicated assessed Multilateral Fund covering agreed incremental compliance costs. Its core budget, voluntary technical-cooperation resources, regional centres and project finance support implementation, but finance is less predictable and centralised.

### 6. The Ban Amendment

✅ The Ban Amendment entered into force on 5 December 2019.

✅ It prohibits covered hazardous-waste exports from Parties listed in Annex VII—OECD members, the European Union and Liechtenstein—to non-Annex VII Parties for final disposal and for recovery/recycling operations.

⚠️ It is binding for Parties for which the Amendment is in force. It should not be rewritten as "Basel bans all hazardous-waste trade everywhere."

### 7. India interface

✅ India implements Basel principally through the **Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016**, under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, with subsequent amendments.

✅ MoEFCC is the central authority for transboundary-movement decisions; customs, CPCB, ports and SPCBs/PCCs have connected enforcement roles.

⚠️ Ship-breaking, metal scrap and used electronic equipment expose the recurring problem of distinguishing a reusable commodity from waste destined for low-cost dismantling or disposal.

### 8. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** PIC legitimises hazardous-waste export instead of preventing environmental injustice.

**Reply:** The system recognises sovereign import decisions, imposes notification, documentation and ESM duties, and is strengthened by the Ban Amendment for Annex VII-to-non-Annex VII flows.

**Residual:** ⚠️ Consent can be undermined by weak capacity, false classification, illegal routing or unequal bargaining power. The Ban Amendment's uneven acceptance and enforcement mean political-economy incentives remain powerful.

### UPSC application

- Basel = waste movement and disposal, not chemical production generally.
- PIC is necessary but not sufficient; add ESM and minimisation.
- Ban Amendment is stronger than the original PIC architecture but narrower than a universal ban.
- Illegal traffic is a Convention-defined legal category.
- Compare Basel finance cautiously with Montreal's dedicated Fund.

### Revision notes

1. Basel was adopted in 1989 and entered into force in 1992.
2. It minimises waste generation and transboundary movement.
3. ESM protects health and environment through the waste lifecycle.
4. Disposal includes final disposal and recovery operations.
5. PIC requires notification and written consent.
6. Import prohibitions must be respected.
7. Export is barred where ESM cannot reasonably be expected.
8. Illegal traffic includes no consent, fraud and deliberate illegal disposal.
9. COP, OEWG, Secretariat, ICC and regional centres have distinct roles.
10. Basel lacks a Montreal-style dedicated compliance fund.
11. The Ban Amendment entered into force on 5 December 2019.
12. The Ban applies from Annex VII to non-Annex VII Parties for covered hazardous wastes.
13. India's HOWM Rules, 2016 are the principal domestic interface.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is "the importing country consented" not a complete legal defence for a Basel waste shipment?

**Model answer:** Basel also requires correct classification, notification, documentation, respect for prohibitions and environmentally sound management. Consent obtained by fraud, or a movement likely to end in unsound disposal, does not satisfy the Convention.

**Misconception to avoid:** PIC is not a waiver of every other Basel obligation.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain how the Basel Convention combines sovereign consent with environmental protection.

**Model (143 words):** Basel permits only controlled transboundary movement of hazardous and other covered wastes. The exporting state must notify the importing and relevant transit states, and movement ordinarily requires written consent. This respects the importing state's sovereign choice. Yet consent is embedded in a wider environmental framework: waste generation and movement should be minimised, disposal should occur as close as practicable to source, and export must not proceed where environmentally sound management cannot be expected. False documentation, movement without consent and deliberate dumping constitute illegal traffic. The 2019 entry into force of the Ban Amendment further prohibits covered Annex VII-to-non-Annex VII hazardous-waste exports for disposal and recovery for Parties bound by it. Thus Basel is neither unrestricted consent-based trade nor a universal ban; its weakness lies in classification fraud, capacity gaps and enforcement incentives.

*The amendments close loopholes created by calling mixed plastic or used electronics "recyclable goods".*

### 1. Plastic-waste amendments

✅ Adopted in 2019 and effective from 1 January 2021, the plastic-waste amendments created a three-part structure:

| Entry | Basic legal effect |
|---|---|
| ✅ **A3210, Annex VIII** | Hazardous plastic waste; PIC applies |
| ✅ **Y48, Annex II** | Plastic waste requiring special consideration, including many mixed or contaminated streams; PIC applies |
| ✅ **B3011, Annex IX** | Narrow category of almost contamination-free, sorted plastic waste destined for environmentally sound recycling; generally outside PIC |

The exam logic is not "all plastic waste is banned." It is:

```text
hazardous or mixed/contaminated -> stronger control and PIC
clean, sorted, specified recycling stream -> narrow non-PIC route
```

⚠️ The B3011 route depends on quality, separation and destination. A recycling label cannot cure contamination or sham recovery.

### 2. E-waste amendments

✅ Basel COP15 adopted e-waste amendments that took effect on 1 January 2025 for Parties bound by them.

✅ The amendments:

- added **A1181** for hazardous e-waste in Annex VIII;
- added **Y49** for other e-waste in Annex II;
- deleted earlier Annex IX entries that had allowed substantial e-waste to be treated as presumptively non-hazardous; and
- brought transboundary movements of hazardous and non-hazardous e-waste within PIC, subject to the Convention's amendment rules.

```text
before 2025: a larger non-hazardous e-waste route existed
after 2025: A1181 or Y49 -> PIC control for covered e-waste movements
```

✅ Components and wastes from e-waste processing can also fall within the amended entries.

### 3. India's domestic connection

| International movement | Domestic lifecycle |
|---|---|
| Basel controls cross-border waste movement | HOWM Rules govern hazardous and other waste import/export |
| Basel e-waste amendments tighten PIC | E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022 govern producer EPR and authorised recycling |
| Basel plastic amendments classify shipments | Plastic Waste Management Rules govern packaging EPR and specified restrictions |

⚠️ Domestic EPR and Basel PIC solve different stages. EPR allocates lifecycle responsibility within India; Basel controls a cross-border movement. One does not replace the other.

### 4. Rotterdam: connected administration, different object

✅ The Rotterdam Convention applies PIC to international trade in certain listed hazardous chemicals and pesticides.

| Basel | Rotterdam |
|---|---|
| Waste shipment | Chemical or pesticide in trade |
| Disposal/recovery destination matters | Import response for listed chemical matters |
| ESM and illegal waste traffic | Information exchange and import decisions |

✅ Basel, Rotterdam and Stockholm form the **BRS** cluster, with coordinated secretariat arrangements and back-to-back COPs.

⚠️ Joint administration does not merge their legal scopes.

### 5. Minamata: mercury lifecycle, not a fourth BRS convention

✅ The Minamata Convention on Mercury controls anthropogenic mercury across supply, trade, products, processes, emissions, releases, storage and waste.

✅ India deposited its instrument of ratification on 18 June 2018, and the Convention entered into force for India on 16 September 2018.

✅ Mercury waste can interface with Basel's waste-control and ESM architecture.

⚠️ Minamata is not one of the three BRS conventions. Mercury is an element, so it is not a POP under Stockholm merely because it is persistent and toxic.

### 6. Mercury sources and health-risk language

The 2023 PYQ combines two source pathways with one toxicology proposition:

1. **Gold mining:** artisanal and small-scale gold mining may use mercury to form an amalgam with gold. Heating the amalgam releases mercury vapour; spills and tailings contaminate soil and water.
2. **Coal-based thermal power:** coal naturally contains trace mercury. Combustion releases mercury unless captured by pollution-control systems, making coal-fired power and heat a major anthropogenic pathway.
3. **Exposure:** UNEP has stated that there is no known safe exposure level for elemental mercury in humans, while WHO warns that even small amounts may cause serious harm and stresses particular danger to fetal and early-child development.

The forms and pathways still matter. Elemental mercury vapour, inorganic salts and methylmercury have different exposure routes and effects; methylmercury bioaccumulates in aquatic food webs. “No known safe level” is therefore a prevention-oriented health statement, not permission to ignore chemical form, dose, duration or population vulnerability.

This is why Minamata is a lifecycle convention: source control, products, industrial processes, emissions, releases, storage and waste must be connected rather than treated as isolated pollution events.

### 7. Shipments as an examiner's classification test

Consider four consignments:

1. a new listed pesticide for sale;
2. mixed contaminated plastic scrap;
3. discarded computers;
4. mercury-contaminated waste.

The likely legal starting points are Rotterdam, Basel plastic entries, Basel e-waste entries, and the Minamata-Basel interface respectively. The answer should then test precise facts rather than assuming only one treaty can ever be relevant.

### 8. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Expanding PIC to more waste categories obstructs legitimate recycling and circular-economy trade.

**Reply:** Circularity requires traceable, environmentally sound recovery. PIC helps distinguish high-quality feedstock from contaminated waste transferred to jurisdictions with cheaper but unsafe processing.

**Residual:** ⚠️ Excessive paperwork may burden responsible recyclers, while weak inspection leaves illegal operators unaffected. Digital documentation, laboratory capacity and harmonised classification are therefore central.

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish the Basel, Rotterdam and Minamata Conventions using a transboundary pollution lens.

Rotterdam instead applies prior informed consent to trade in listed hazardous chemicals and pesticides; the object is a chemical product in trade, not waste as such. Minamata regulates mercury across supply, trade, products, processes, emissions, releases, storage and waste. ━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual — from candidate chemical to control

```text
PARTY PROPOSES CHEMICAL
        |
        v

        |
        v
risk profile -> risk-management evaluation
        |
        v
POPRC recommendation -> COP decision
        |
        v

```

*Scientific screening informs listing, but the COP adopts the legal annex decision.*

### 1. What makes a POP distinct?

✅ Persistent Organic Pollutants are carbon-based chemicals that:

- resist environmental degradation;
- bioaccumulate in organisms;
- can biomagnify through food webs;
- cause significant adverse effects; and
- can travel far from their source through air, water or migratory species.

⚠️ "Persistent" alone is insufficient. Mercury is persistent and toxic but inorganic; it belongs principally to Minamata, not Stockholm.

### 2. Formation and objective

✅ The Stockholm Convention was adopted on 22 May 2001 and entered into force on 17 May 2004.

✅ Its objective is to protect human health and the environment from POPs, guided by precaution.

The original "dirty dozen" were the starting list, not a permanently closed list. The Convention is designed to add new chemicals through annex amendments.

### 3. The three annexes

| Annex | Control logic | Standard example |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Annex A** | Elimination of production and use, subject to listed specific exemptions | Many intentionally produced POPs |
| ✅ **Annex B** | Restriction to specified acceptable purposes and/or exemptions | DDT for disease-vector control under Convention conditions |
| ✅ **Annex C** | Reduction and, where feasible, ultimate elimination of releases from unintentional production | Dioxins and furans |

#### Annex A is not always an instantaneous zero-use command

Specific exemptions may be registered where the annex and COP decision permit them. They are usually time-limited and recorded in a public register.

#### Annex B uses "acceptable purpose"

DDT demonstrates the difference between prohibition and restriction. Continued use for disease-vector control may be allowed for registered Parties under Convention conditions while alternatives are pursued.

#### Annex C regulates processes and releases

Unintentional POPs arise from combustion and industrial processes. Parties develop action plans, inventories and promote **best available techniques (BAT)** and **best environmental practices (BEP)**.

### 4. POPRC is a staged scientific gate

1. A Party submits a proposal.
2. POPRC screens Annex D information.
3. If criteria are met, it prepares a risk profile under Annex E.
4. It evaluates socio-economic and control options under Annex F.
5. It recommends whether the COP should list the chemical.
6. The COP decides and specifies annex placement and exemptions.

⚠️ Scientific hazard identification does not itself amend the treaty. Recommendation, draft decision and adopted annex amendment are different legal stages.

### 5. Trade and waste controls

✅ Import of Annex A or B chemicals is generally limited to:

- environmentally sound disposal; or
- a use or purpose permitted for the importing Party.

✅ Export is correspondingly restricted, including special certification conditions for permitted export to a non-Party.

✅ POP-containing wastes must be managed so that POP content is destroyed or irreversibly transformed, or otherwise disposed of in an environmentally sound manner where destruction is not the environmentally preferable option.

✅ Basel technical work helps operationalise environmentally sound management of POP wastes. Stockholm does not replace Basel's movement-control system.

### 6. COP12 adoption versus entry into force as of 3 October 2026

✅ Stockholm COP12, meeting from 28 April to 9 May 2025, adopted decisions SC-12/9, SC-12/10 and SC-12/12 to amend Annex A by listing:

- chlorpyrifos;
- medium-chain chlorinated paraffins; and
- long-chain perfluorocarboxylic acids, their salts and related compounds,

with the specific exemptions contained in the adopted decisions.

✅ COP12 also adopted an amendment concerning the UV-328 listing.

The UN depositary communicated the three new Annex A amendments through notification **C.N.672.2025.TREATIES-XXVII.15 on 16 December 2025**. Under Article 22's default annex-amendment rule, the one-year period expires on **16 December 2026**. The amendments then enter into force for Parties that have not submitted a notification of non-acceptance; a Party operating under the separate Article 25(4) declaration route is bound only through its applicable consent procedure.

**Status on 3 October 2026:** the COP had adopted the amendments, but the default 16 December 2026 entry-into-force date had not arrived. Therefore, do not describe the three COP12 listings as already operative treaty annex obligations at the cut-off. Adoption, depositary communication, entry into force for a Party and any registered exemption are separate questions.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Chemical-by-chemical listing is too slow and encourages regrettable substitution.

**Reply:** POPRC's evidence stages protect scientific and legal credibility, while annex amendments allow the treaty to evolve without negotiating an entirely new convention.

**Residual:** ⚠️ A structurally similar substitute may spread before its risk is assessed. Group or class approaches can reduce this lag, but broader controls must still be scientifically and legally defined.

### UPSC application

- Annex A = elimination; Annex B = restriction; Annex C = unintentional releases.
- DDT is the standard Annex B trap.
- POPRC recommends; COP lists.
- Specific exemption and acceptable purpose are not synonyms.
- Import/export controls are purpose-limited.
- POP waste creates a Stockholm-Basel interface.

### Revision notes

1. Stockholm was adopted in 2001 and entered into force in 2004.
2. POPs persist, bioaccumulate and can travel long distances.
3. The Convention is guided by precaution.
4. Annex A concerns elimination.
5. Annex B concerns restriction.
6. Annex C concerns unintentional production and releases.
7. DDT is the classic acceptable-purpose example.
8. Dioxins and furans illustrate Annex C.
9. POPRC screens, prepares risk profiles and evaluates management options.
10. COP—not POPRC—adopts annex listings.
11. Specific exemptions are registered and generally time-limited.
12. BAT and BEP are central to Annex C.
13. POP-waste management links Stockholm and Basel.
14. COP12 adopted three Annex A listing amendments in 2025; C.N.672.2025 communicated them on 16 December 2025.
15. Their default entry date is 16 December 2026, so they were not yet operative by 3 October 2026; Party procedure and exemptions still matter.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a chemical be listed under Annex A while a limited use remains temporarily lawful for a Party?

**Model answer:** Annex A establishes elimination, but the listing decision may contain specific exemptions. A Party that validly registers an available exemption may use it within its scope and duration, while the general obligation remains elimination.

**Misconception to avoid:** "Annex A" does not justify ignoring the exact exemption text and entry-into-force rule.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain how the Stockholm Convention combines precaution with a science-based listing process.

**Model (137 words):** Stockholm aims to protect health and the environment from chemicals whose persistence, bioaccumulation, adverse effects and long-range transport make national regulation insufficient. A Party proposes a chemical, after which the POPs Review Committee screens the evidence, develops a risk profile and evaluates risk-management options. The Committee recommends, but the COP legally decides whether to amend Annex A, B or C and whether exemptions are needed. This staged process makes precaution operational without treating suspicion alone as a completed listing. Annex A seeks elimination, Annex B restriction and Annex C reduction of unintentional releases through inventories, BAT and BEP. The strength is adaptive scientific control; the limitation is delay, during which substitute chemicals may spread. Effective governance therefore requires monitoring and attention to chemical classes as well as individual compounds.

### 1. Formation and legal relationship

✅ The Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer was adopted in 1985 and entered into force in 1988.

✅ The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer was adopted in 1987 and entered into force in 1989.

✅ Montreal is a protocol under Vienna, not a stand-alone convention.

The Vienna Convention established cooperation in research, systematic observation and information exchange. It did not itself prescribe the detailed phase-out schedules later created by Montreal.

### 2. Why ozone depletion required collective action

```text
CFC / halon emission
      |
stable in lower atmosphere
      |
reaches stratosphere
      |
UV releases chlorine/bromine radicals
      |
catalytic ozone destruction
      |
more harmful UV-B reaches surface
```

✅ One radical can participate in repeated ozone-destruction cycles. Because emissions and ozone effects cross borders, national regulation alone could not protect the global stratosphere.

### 3. Montreal's control design

✅ The Protocol controls **production and consumption**, with consumption calculated using production, imports and exports under the treaty formula rather than ordinary retail use.

✅ Controlled substances are listed in annexes. Successive amendments added substances or changed legal architecture; adjustments accelerated existing control schedules.

| Legal technique | Function |
|---|---|
| ✅ Baselines | Establish reference production/consumption |
| ✅ Freeze | Prevent growth above baseline |
| ✅ Step-down | Require staged percentage reductions |
| ✅ Phase-out | Reduce controlled production/consumption to the permitted residual level |
| ✅ Essential/critical-use and other limited exemptions | Address uses where treaty conditions permit |
| ✅ Licensing and reporting | Track production and trade |
| ✅ Article 4 trade controls | Restrict controlled-substance trade with non-Parties |

### 4. Adjustment versus amendment

✅ An **adjustment** tightens control measures for already controlled substances through an expedited process and does not require the same separate ratification process as a formal amendment.

✅ An **amendment** makes a formal legal change, such as adding a new group of controlled substances, and binds states according to ratification and entry-into-force rules.

This is a major reason the regime can respond faster than a treaty that must reopen its full text for every stricter schedule.

### 5. Differentiation and the Multilateral Fund

✅ Article 5 recognises the special situation of developing countries and provides differentiated timing.

✅ Article 10 established the **Multilateral Fund**, operational from 1991, to meet agreed incremental costs of eligible Article 5 Parties.

It supports:

- technology conversion;
- equipment and process change;
- technical assistance and training;
- institutional strengthening;
- preparation and implementation of phase-out plans; and
- data and compliance capacity.

✅ The Fund is governed by an Executive Committee and works through UNEP, UNDP, UNIDO and the World Bank as implementing agencies.

⚠️ This finance-compliance bargain is deeper than merely offering project grants: differentiated deadlines are paired with agreed financial and technological support.

### 6. Compliance and expert bodies

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| ✅ Meeting of the Parties | Governing body; control, replenishment and implementation decisions |
| ✅ Ozone Secretariat | Treaty administration and data/meeting support |
| ✅ Implementation Committee | Reviews possible non-compliance and recommends responses |
| ✅ TEAP | Technical and Economic Assessment Panel; alternatives, exemptions and feasibility |
| ✅ Scientific Assessment Panel | Ozone science and recovery assessment |
| ✅ Environmental Effects Assessment Panel | Effects of ozone change and UV radiation |

✅ The non-compliance procedure is managerial and facilitative. It can combine plans, assistance and cautions, with the MOP retaining authority over responses.

### 7. Evidence of effectiveness

✅ The 2022 WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment projected return of total-column ozone to 1980 values, if current policies remain in place, around:

| Region | Approximate recovery year |
|---|---:|
| Near-global average | 2040 |
| Arctic | 2045 |
| Antarctic | 2066 |

⚠️ These are conditional projections, not declarations that the ozone hole has already disappeared.

### 8. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Montreal succeeded only because the technical problem was easier than biodiversity or waste governance.

**Reply:** Technical boundedness helped, but institutional design also mattered: schedules, reporting, trade controls, adjustable controls, finance, technology transfer and a facilitative compliance process reinforced one another.

**Residual:** ⚠️ Illegal production, feedstock emissions, old equipment banks and the energy efficiency of substitutes remain implementation concerns.

### UPSC application

- Vienna = framework science and cooperation.
- Montreal = operative controlled-substance treaty.
- Production and consumption are treaty-defined quantities.
- Adjustment and amendment are distinct.
- MLF supports Article 5 compliance.
- Article 4 trade controls help prevent free riding.
- Recovery years must be attributed to the 2022 assessment and conditional on continued policies.

1. Vienna was adopted in 1985.
2. Montreal was adopted in 1987.
3. Montreal is a protocol under Vienna.
4. Vienna centres research, observation and cooperation.
5. Montreal controls production and consumption.
6. Annexes organise controlled substances.
7. Adjustments tighten existing controls more rapidly.
8. Amendments require treaty consent under their entry rules.
9. Article 4 restricts trade with non-Parties.
10. Article 5 differentiates developing-country schedules.
11. Article 10 created the Multilateral Fund.
12. TEAP is technical/economic, not the compliance body.
13. The Implementation Committee addresses possible non-compliance.
14. Ozone recovery projections are conditional.
15. Montreal's success combines problem structure and institutional design.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the Vienna Convention alone insufficient to explain the ozone regime's success?

**Model answer:** Vienna created cooperation in science, monitoring and information, but Montreal added binding substance-control schedules, trade controls, reporting, differentiated obligations, finance and compliance review. The operative result comes from the two-level regime.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not attribute Montreal's phase-out schedules to the Vienna Convention itself.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Identify the institutional features that made the Montreal Protocol unusually effective.

**Model (148 words):** Montreal converted ozone science into enforceable production and consumption schedules for a bounded list of substances. Baselines, freezes, staged reductions and reporting made performance measurable. Article 4 trade controls reduced incentives to remain outside the regime. The adjustment procedure allowed existing schedules to be tightened faster than ordinary treaty amendment. Developing countries received differentiated timing under Article 5, while the Multilateral Fund financed agreed incremental costs, technology conversion, training and institutional strengthening. TEAP and scientific panels continually linked control decisions to alternatives and atmospheric evidence. A facilitative Implementation Committee addressed non-compliance through plans, assistance and recommendations to the Meeting of the Parties. The 2022 assessment's conditional recovery projections indicate environmental effect. However, technical substitutability and a relatively narrow problem also aided success; the model cannot be copied mechanically to diffuse problems such as biodiversity loss.

### 1. Legal character

✅ The Kigali Amendment was adopted in 2016 as an amendment to the Montreal Protocol and entered into force internationally in 2019.

✅ It adds specified HFCs in Annex F and establishes differentiated production and consumption phase-down schedules.

✅ A **phase-down** differs from a universal zero phase-out. The schedules reduce the aggregate controlled quantity against a baseline, with specified residual levels.

### 2. Why use the Montreal system?

HFCs are widely used in refrigeration, air conditioning, foams, aerosols, fire protection and other applications. They generally do not deplete stratospheric ozone, but many have high global-warming potential.

⚠️ Kigali illustrates adaptive governance: the ozone regime's reporting, licensing, technical panels, finance and industry networks were extended to a related refrigerant problem.

### 3. HFC application language behind the 2023 PYQ

The listed applications do not all use HFCs in the same physical role:

| Application | How an HFC may be used | Distinction to retain |
|---|---|---|
| Aerosols | Propellant in medical, consumer or technical aerosol products | The HFC expels or carries the formulation; it is not necessarily the active ingredient |
| Foam agents | Blowing agent that creates cells in insulating foams | This is a manufacturing role, distinct from refrigeration |
| Fire protection | Clean gaseous extinguishing or suppression agent in specialised systems | “Fire-suppression agent” is more precise than treating every solid or additive fire retardant as an HFC |
| Lubricant products | Propellant or carrier in some technical aerosol lubricants | The HFC need not be the base lubricating oil; product formulation and delivery role matter |

Thus a close-option question may turn on industrial function, not merely whether the final product name sounds like a gas. The safe method is to test each named product chain—propellant, blowing agent, extinguishing agent or carrier—without converting every lubricant or retardant into an HFC.

### 4. Differentiated schedules

The Amendment uses different baselines, freeze dates and reduction steps for:

- non-Article 5 developed-country groups;
- a first Article 5 developing-country group; and
- a later Article 5 group that includes India.

✅ India's HFC baseline is the average HFC production/consumption for **2024-2026**, plus the treaty-specified **65% of the HCFC baseline** component.

✅ India's schedule is:

| Milestone | Control level relative to baseline |
|---|---:|
| 2028 | Freeze at baseline |
| 2032 | 10% reduction |
| 2037 | 20% reduction |
| 2042 | 30% reduction |
| 2047 | 85% reduction |

⚠️ These are cumulative reductions from the baseline, not reductions from the previous step.

### 5. India's legal and policy route

✅ India ratified the Kigali Amendment on 27 September 2021; it entered into force for India on 26 December 2021.

✅ India's implementation uses:

- MoEFCC's Ozone Cell;
- the Ozone Depleting Substances (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000, as amended and further adapted;
- industry conversion plans;
- refrigerant servicing and technician capacity;
- the India Cooling Action Plan;

✅ An April 2026 MoEFCC/Parivesh memorandum linked environmental-clearance decisions for HFC production to the Kigali schedule and required new or expanded controlled HFC-production projects to complete installation and commissioning by 31 December 2027, ahead of the 2028 freeze.

### 6. HCFC and HFC schedules must not be merged

### 7. Climate benefit and transition risks

Potential gains:

- lower direct refrigerant emissions;
- improved appliance efficiency;
- lower peak electricity demand;

**Residual:** ⚠️ The legal deadline is a ceiling, not an ambition cap. Earlier low-GWP and high-efficiency deployment can produce climate, energy-security and consumer benefits if affordability and safety are addressed.

## 10 marks — 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the trade-control mechanisms of Basel, Stockholm and Montreal.

## 15 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate finance and compliance as determinants of multilateral environmental agreement effectiveness.

**Model (213 words):** Environmental obligations succeed when Parties can identify the duty, finance the transition and address implementation difficulty early. **Question:** "Multilateral environmental law works best when it differentiates without diluting accountability." Discuss with reference to the four regimes.

**Model (231 words):** Differentiation recognises unequal capacity, responsibility and development conditions; accountability ensures that flexibility does not become indefinite delay. Differentiation strengthens legitimacy only when paired with measurable progression and review.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Treaty-family grammar

- Convention/treaty = principal binding instrument for Parties.
- Protocol = separate treaty under a parent convention; separate consent is required.
- Amendment = formal legal change; bindingness follows its entry rule.
- COP/MOP decision = governing-body act whose effect depends on delegated authority.
- Montreal adjustment = expedited tightening of existing controls.
- Always separate proposed, adopted and entered into force.

## CBD bargain and institutions

- Adopted 1992; in force 1993.
- Objectives: conservation; sustainable use; fair and equitable benefit-sharing.
- Article 6: NBSAP/integration; Article 8: in-situ; Article 15: access; Article 26: reporting.
- COP governs; SBSTTA advises on science; SBI reviews implementation; Secretariat supports.
- GEF operates the financial mechanism under COP guidance.
- Parent CBD has no formal compliance committee.
- India: 17-megadiverse-country status; nearly 8% of recorded global species on 2.4% of global land; 10 biogeographic zones and 27 biotic provinces.
- Biological Diversity Act tools: regulated access, ABS, NBA-SBB-BMC institutions, PBRs, conservation measures and biodiversity funds.

## Cartagena and Nagoya

- Cartagena: LMO biosafety; AIA; risk assessment; precaution; BCH.
- LMOs for food/feed/processing use a distinct information route.
- Nagoya-Kuala Lumpur = LMO liability/redress.
- Nagoya: access and benefit-sharing; PIC; MAT; monetary/non-monetary benefits; ABSCH.
- DSI challenges a physical-sample ABS model.
- Cartagena and Nagoya have separate facilitative compliance procedures.
- Section 41 BMCs connect local documentation and consultation with ABS and may levy collection fees for commercial access or collection within their territorial jurisdiction.

## KMGBF and India

- COP15, 2022; four 2050 goals and 23 targets for 2030.
- Goal A ecology/species/genetics; B sustainable use; C benefit-sharing/DSI; D means.
- Target 2 restoration; Target 3 30x30; Target 18 harmful incentives; Target 19 mobilises USD 200 billion annually by 2030, including at least USD 30 billion annually in international finance to developing countries.
- COP16 concluded in Rome on 27 February 2025; finance, monitoring, indicators and DSI machinery completed.
- India: NBSAP 2024-2030; 23 NBTs; NR-7 with 142 indicators.
- Named NR-7 examples: 98 Ramsar sites, 58 Tiger Reserves, 33 Elephant Reserves, 3,682 tigers, 4,014 greater one-horned rhinoceroses, 22,446 wild elephants, 891 Asiatic lions, 718 snow leopards, 6,327 riverine dolphins, 22 agrobiodiversity hotspots and 25.17% forest/tree cover.
- Biological Diversity Act 2002, amended 2023; Rules 2024; ABS Regulations 2025 including DSI.
- COP17 was scheduled for 19-30 October 2026 at the 3 October cut-off.

## Basel Convention

- Adopted 1989; in force 1992.
- Minimise generation and movement; ensure ESM.
- PIC = notification + written consent + lawful movement documentation.
- Illegal traffic includes no consent, fraud, document mismatch and deliberate illegal disposal.
- COP; OEWG; ICC; Secretariat; regional centres.
- No Montreal-style dedicated incremental-cost fund.
- Ban Amendment in force 5 December 2019: covered Annex VII-to-non-Annex VII hazardous-waste exports prohibited for Parties bound.
- India: HOWM Rules, 2016 and institutional border/environment controls.

## Basel amendments and boundaries

- Plastic amendments effective 1 January 2021.
- A3210 hazardous; Y48 special-consideration; B3011 narrow clean sorted recycling.
- E-waste amendments effective 1 January 2025.
- A1181 hazardous e-waste; Y49 other e-waste; covered movements enter PIC.
- Rotterdam = listed hazardous chemicals/pesticides in trade.
- BRS = Basel, Rotterdam, Stockholm.
- Minamata = mercury lifecycle; India ratified in 2018; mercury waste can overlap Basel.
- Gold amalgamation and coal combustion are mercury-release pathways; WHO warns that even small amounts may cause serious harm, while chemical form and exposure route remain relevant.
- Domestic EPR and international PIC are different tools.

## Stockholm Convention

- Adopted 2001; in force 2004.
- POP criteria: persistence, bioaccumulation, adverse effects, long-range transport.
- Annex A elimination; Annex B restriction; Annex C unintentional release reduction.
- DDT = Annex B acceptable-purpose example.
- POPRC screens, profiles and evaluates; COP lists.
- Specific exemptions differ from acceptable purposes.
- Imports/exports are limited to permitted purposes or ESM disposal.
- COP12 in 2025 adopted Annex A amendments for chlorpyrifos, MCCPs and long-chain PFCAs with decision-specific exemptions.
- UN notification C.N.672.2025 dated 16 December 2025 produces a default entry date of 16 December 2026 for Parties not notifying non-acceptance; the amendments were not yet operative on 3 October 2026.

## Stockholm implementation and India

- Article 7 requires NIPs and updates.
- Article 16 requires effectiveness evaluation.
- GEF operates the financial mechanism.
- Compliance Committee established at COP11 in 2023.
- Global Monitoring Plan tracks human and environmental concentrations.
- India's published public NIP is from 2011.
- DDT use is restricted to disease-vector control, not agriculture.
- Update work exists; no final public replacement NIP had displaced the 2011 plan by 3 October 2026.

## Vienna-Montreal regime

- Vienna 1985 = science, observation and cooperation.
- Montreal 1987 = binding control schedules.
- Production and consumption use treaty calculations.
- Article 4 = trade controls with non-Parties.
- Article 5 = developing-country differentiation.
- Article 10 = Multilateral Fund.
- TEAP advises on technology/economics; Implementation Committee reviews non-compliance.
- 2022 assessment recovery projections: near-global 2040, Arctic 2045, Antarctic 2066, conditional on continued policy.

## Kigali and India

- Kigali adopted 2016; international entry into force 2019.
- HFCs are generally ozone-safe but climate-warming.
- Uses include aerosol propellants, foam blowing agents, specialised fire-suppression agents and carriers in some aerosol lubricant products.
- Phase-down, not universal phase-out.
- India ratified 27 September 2021.
- Baseline = average 2024-26 HFC quantity + 65% HCFC-baseline component.
- Freeze 2028; reductions: 10% by 2032, 20% by 2037, 30% by 2042, 85% by 2047.
- HPMP Stage III addresses HCFCs up to 2030.
- Pair refrigerant conversion with energy efficiency, safety and technician training.

2. State mechanism.
3. Name governing/scientific body.
4. State finance and compliance route.
5. Add India interface.
6. Distinguish treaty law from COP target and implementation evidence.
7. Add one limitation.
8. Conclude that design must fit the environmental problem.

## Final trap strip

- KMGBF ≠ protocol.
- Montreal ≠ convention independent of Vienna.
- HFC ≠ ozone-depleting.
- Kigali phase-down ≠ phase-out.
- Basel PIC ≠ total waste ban.
- Stockholm ≠ Basel.
- POPRC recommendation ≠ COP listing.
- Annex A ≠ no possible exemption.
- DDT ≠ unrestricted and ≠ universally eliminated.
- Rotterdam PIC ≠ Basel PIC.
- Minamata ≠ BRS member.
- GEF ≠ Multilateral Fund.
- Scheduled COP ≠ completed COP.
