# CMS (Bonn Convention) and Migratory Species — Live Session Edition

One animal can use several jurisdictions during one life cycle. This session follows that animal first, then asks what law, diplomacy and local conservation can actually do for it. ✅ denotes a sourced fact or treaty provision; ⚠️ denotes an analytical inference or proposed application.

### Visual — one journey, several jurisdictions

```text
breeding region ──> staging / feeding stopover ──> wintering region
   range state A           range state B                 range state C
         \________________ one connected life cycle _____________/
                        offshore leg may cross waters
                        beyond national jurisdiction
```

*The drawing shows jurisdictional exposure, not a literal map of any named species' route.*

Imagine protecting a traveller's destination but leaving the only safe rest stop unprotected. The journey can fail even if the destination is perfect. ⚠️ That is the **weakest-link intuition**, not a theorem that every migrant uses the same route or that the worst site alone determines population size. It explains why the Bonn Convention coordinates countries rather than replacing their domestic laws.

✅ Under CMS Article I, a **migratory species** is an entire population, or geographically separate part of a population, of a wild animal species/lower taxon, a *significant proportion* of whose members **cyclically and predictably** cross one or more national jurisdictional boundaries. Merely moving within a country is not enough for this treaty definition. **Range** includes places inhabited, visited temporarily, crossed or *overflown* along the normal route. A **range state** has jurisdiction over part of that range; Article I also includes a state whose flag vessels take the species beyond national jurisdictional limits. A breeding country is therefore not the only range state; an overflight country need not be a nesting ground. Habitat is an area in that range with suitable living conditions.

✅ CMS is the UNEP-administered *Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals* (Bonn Convention), concluded in 1979. It addresses the conservation of migratory wild animals, **not only birds**: mammals (including marine mammals), fish, reptiles and **insects** can fall within its scope when the treaty's migratory-population test is met. Thus a winged insect is not excluded for being an invertebrate; nor does every insect qualify merely because it flies. Article II calls for research, preventive action, immediate protection for Appendix I and efforts towards agreements for Appendix II. ⚠️ A migratory bird's survival requires domestic habitat and threat control *plus* compatible measures along its route; one agreement on paper does not itself keep a stopover safe.

**India example.** ✅ The Amur Falcon's Doyang/Pangti stopover in Nagaland illustrates why a local community's decision about hunting and roost protection can matter for an internationally moving bird. This example illustrates *one route segment*, not an assertion that Nagaland alone determines its global fate. A waterbird at an Indian wetland similarly needs suitable staging grounds outside India. For a marine migrant, fishing gear and shipping risks may shift between jurisdictions.

**Objection:** Could strong Indian wildlife protection make CMS unnecessary? **Reply:** It helps within India's jurisdiction but cannot compel protection at a foreign stopover or along an ocean crossing. Conversely, ⚠️ international coordination cannot substitute for enforcement and community cooperation within India. **UPSC use:** define the species and range precisely before giving the weakest-link argument; do not equate an overflight with a feeding stopover, or conflate a flyway with a legally designated protected area.

**Revision notes**

1. CMS conserves migratory wild animals across jurisdictions; it does not license trade.
2. Article I requires cyclical and predictable crossing of a national jurisdictional boundary by a significant proportion.
3. A geographically separate part of a population may qualify.
4. Range includes normal-route habitation, temporary stays, crossings and overflight.
5. A range state may be a jurisdiction over that route, not merely the breeding state.
6. Article I also recognises a qualifying flag-vessel state taking the species beyond national jurisdiction.
7. Habitat supplies suitable conditions within the species' range.
8. Weakest-link reasoning warns that another route segment may undermine protection at one site.
9. Doyang/Pangti shows community protection of one Amur Falcon stopover, not global security.
10. Strong domestic protection and cross-border coordination complement rather than replace each other.
11. CMS is not bird-only: eligible mammals, fish, reptiles **and insects** must satisfy its migratory-population definition.

**Next question:** what does a listing actually require of those countries?

### Concept check

**Question:** A bird crosses country B without breeding there. Can B be a range state, and does that fact alone prove it must create a sanctuary?

<details><summary>Open worked answer only after your first attempt</summary>

**Model answer:** Yes, Article I's range includes normal-route overflight, so B can be a range state. Range-state status identifies a jurisdiction relevant to cooperation; sanctuary creation does not automatically follow from that definition. Specific duties depend on treaty listing and applicable domestic measures.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Only breeding and wintering countries count” discards transit and overflight; “every transit point must become a sanctuary” invents a specific treaty duty.

</details>

**Original Mains application (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why conserving one Indian stopover is necessary but insufficient for a migratory bird. **Model (about 100 words):** A migratory bird uses breeding, staging and wintering habitats across jurisdictions; CMS calls each jurisdiction on its normal route a range state. Doyang/Pangti in Nagaland shows how local protection of an Amur Falcon stopover can reduce one threat. Yet safer Indian roosts cannot prevent hunting or habitat loss at another range-state site. Conversely, a flyway agreement cannot protect Doyang without village-level stewardship and enforcement. Range states should coordinate habitat monitoring and threat reduction, while India sustains community participation at its own stopovers. This is a weakest-link **planning** argument, not proof that any particular unmeasured stopover currently limits the species. **Scoring focus (10):** range-state definition 2; Doyang/Pangti example and mechanism 3; other route threats and cooperation 3; example's limit 2.

### Visual — the appendices answer different questions

| If the issue is... | Appendix I | Appendix II |
|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Endangered migratory species | Unfavourable conservation status requiring international agreements, **or** substantial benefit from one |
| **Treaty response** | Range-state parties endeavour to conserve/restore important habitat and mitigate obstacles; taking generally prohibited, subject to narrow exceptions | Parties endeavour to conclude agreements; range states are encouraged to negotiate coordinated conservation |
| **Exam distinction** | Existing Convention duties, including prohibition of taking | Cooperation pathway; listing by itself is **not** an Appendix I taking ban |
| **Can both apply?** | Yes | Yes |

*The table distinguishes the legal function of a listing from the subsequent instrument chosen to implement cooperation.*

An appendix is **not** an IUCN Red List category. ✅ Under CMS Article III, Appendix I applies where reliable evidence, including the best available science, shows danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of the range. Parties that are range states **shall endeavour** to conserve/restore important habitat where feasible and appropriate, minimise activities or obstacles seriously impeding migration, and control endangering factors. Article III separately says such parties **shall prohibit taking**, subject to carefully confined exceptions. “Taking” includes hunting, fishing, capturing, harassing and deliberate killing, or attempts. The duty is not an unconditional claim that every habitat must be restored immediately.

✅ Article III(5) permits taking exceptions for scientific purposes, enhancing propagation or survival, traditional subsistence needs or extraordinary circumstances. Exceptions must be precise, limited in space and time, not disadvantage the species, and reported to the Secretariat. “Strict” therefore does **not** mean “no possible exceptions.” A power line may obstruct migration or kill birds even where nobody is deliberately taking them: treaty protection extends beyond anti-poaching.

✅ Article IV's Appendix II is a cooperation trigger for species needing or significantly benefiting from agreements. Article II(3)(c) says parties **shall endeavour to conclude** such agreements; this is a treaty undertaking to try, **not** an automatic legal requirement to sign a particular agreement, nor is Appendix II itself wholly “non-binding.” ⚠️ Calling both appendices interchangeable “levels of protection” hides the different actions each initiates. CMS permits dual listing: urgent strict protection and coordinated range-wide work can be needed simultaneously.

**Example with limit.** Imagine a listed animal crosses a border where an overhead line disrupts its passage. For Appendix I, Article III directs relevant range-state parties to endeavour to mitigate serious obstacles; if it is also Appendix II-listed, cooperation can coordinate standards across borders. This is a *hypothetical mechanism*, **not** a claim that a named species currently appears in either appendix. Check the amended appendices before attaching a taxon to a precise legal listing.

**Objection:** If Appendix II is merely encouragement, why call CMS a treaty? **Reply:** The Convention itself binds its parties and uses graded verbs; it commits parties to endeavour to conclude agreements, but does not make every proposed instrument binding. Exact words matter more than slogans about “soft law.” **UPSC trap:** equating Appendix II to CITES Appendix II (trade-permit rules) or assuming proposed COP15 listings were already in force at the proposal stage. ✅ CMS reports COP15 changes entered into force on **27 June 2026**.

### Who does what in CMS governance?

| Institution | Convention-text role | What it does not do alone |
|---|---|---|
| **Conference of the Parties (COP)** | Article VII makes it the Convention's decision-making organ. It reviews implementation and conservation status, guides the Scientific Council and Secretariat, recommends measures and decides additional implementation action. | It does not itself implement habitat protection inside every range state. |
| **Scientific Council** | Article VIII provides scientific advice, recommends research, evaluates conservation status, recommends taxa for Appendices I/II with their range, and advises on conservation measures and scientific implementation problems. | Its advice is not the final appendix amendment. |
| **Secretariat** | Article IX services COP and Scientific Council meetings, circulates information, maintains liaison, prepares implementation reports, publishes range-state lists and promotes Agreements under COP direction. | It does not vote a species into an appendix. |

The bodies form a decision chain rather than three competing authorities: science informs the
choice, the COP decides, and the Secretariat circulates and administers the result.

### How an appendix amendment becomes legally effective

```text
Party proposes amendment
      |
      | Article XI: text + reasons based on best available scientific evidence
      | sent to Secretariat at least 150 days before COP
      v
Secretariat circulates proposal to all Parties
      |
      | Party comments due at least 60 days before COP;
      | Secretariat circulates comments
      v
Scientific Council supplies listing advice under Article VIII
      |
      v
COP decides: two-thirds majority of Parties present and voting
      |
      v
90-day interval after the COP meeting
      |
      +--> no reservation: amendment enters into force for the Party
      |
      `--> written reservation to Depositary within 90 days:
           amendment does not bind that Party for the reserved listing
           until withdrawal; it binds 90 days after withdrawal
```

This is why **proposal**, **adoption** and **entry into force** are separate dates. It also explains
the COP15 sequence: adoption in March 2026 did not make the revised appendices operative
immediately; Article XI's ninety-day rule produced the **27 June 2026** effective date. A specific
reservation is legally possible; the Convention does not permit a general reservation to all its
provisions.

**Revision notes**

1. CMS Appendix I covers endangered migratory species on the best available evidence.
2. Article III requires party range states to prohibit taking, subject to restricted exceptions.
3. Taking includes hunting, fishing, capturing, harassment and deliberate killing or attempts.
4. The scientific, survival, traditional-subsistence and extraordinary exceptions are narrow.
5. Exceptions must be precise, limited, non-detrimental and reported.
6. Article III separately says parties *shall endeavour* to protect crucial habitat and mitigate serious obstacles.
7. Appendix II covers species requiring agreements or benefiting significantly from them.
8. Parties *shall endeavour* to conclude Agreements; listing does not compel signature of a specific instrument.
9. A population can appear in both appendices; their functions differ.
10. Listings are date-sensitive; COP15 amendments became effective 27 June 2026.
11. COP is the decision-making organ; the Scientific Council supplies scientific advice.
12. The Secretariat services meetings, circulates proposals/comments and maintains range-state information.
13. An appendix proposal comes from a Party with reasons based on best available science at least 150 days before COP.
14. Adoption needs a two-thirds majority of Parties present and voting.
15. Appendix amendments take effect after 90 days, except for a Party making a timely specific reservation.

**Next question:** what follows an Appendix II listing?

### Concept check

**Question:** If a population is in both CMS appendices but no species-specific agreement has been concluded, which responsibilities remain, and which do not arise automatically?

<details><summary>Open worked answer only after your first attempt</summary>

**Model answer:** Appendix I's Article III duties for party range states remain, including the taking prohibition with its narrow exceptions and habitat/obstacle endeavours. Appendix II supports the endeavour to negotiate international cooperation; listing alone does not create a signed species agreement or impose an invented agreement's provisions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling Appendix II legally empty ignores the Convention's undertaking to endeavour to conclude agreements; calling it a signed agreement invents a separate instrument.

</details>

**Original Mains application (15 marks, 250 words):** Discuss how CMS appendices allocate differentiated responsibilities without making species protection optional. **Model (about 132 words):** CMS's two appendices answer different conservation questions. Appendix I concerns endangered migrants: Article III directs party range states to endeavour to restore crucial habitats and reduce serious migratory obstacles, while prohibiting taking except under tightly bounded, reported circumstances. For a bird exposed to a dangerous overhead line, merely banning hunting leaves a collision risk; obstacle mitigation is also relevant. Appendix II concerns species with unfavourable status requiring cooperation or that would benefit significantly from it: parties endeavour to conclude range-wide agreements, but listing does not itself enact one. Dual listing is possible because domestic protection and transboundary planning solve different problems. These rules neither guarantee recovery nor excuse unilateral inaction pending negotiations. Species identification must follow the appendices actually effective on the cited date; the COP15 changes took effect in June 2026. **Scoring focus (15):** Appendix I/Article III precise duties and exception 5; Appendix II endeavour versus instrument 4; obstacle example and dual listing 3; dated qualification and outcome limit 3.

### Visual — do not confuse the layers

```text
CMS Convention (treaty binding on its Parties)
    ├── Appendix I → Article III duties for Party range states
    └── Appendix II → cooperate / endeavour to conclude agreements
                            ├── binding treaty Agreement for its parties (e.g. AEWA)
                            ├── non-binding species/group MOU for signatories
                            └── plans, decisions, initiatives → their own terms
India's implementation: MoEFCC + states + scientists + communities
```

*The branch shows that the legal status of the parent convention does not transfer automatically to every child instrument.*

Suppose several neighbours agree where to keep wetlands safe. A legally enforceable treaty and a voluntary coordinated plan can both improve outcomes, but the **legal weight** differs. ✅ CMS operates as a framework convention; separate **Agreements** under Articles IV–V may be legally binding on their parties, while CMS **Memoranda of Understanding** are generally non-legally-binding cooperative instruments. A named binding example is the **Agreement on the Conservation of African-Eurasian Migratory Waterbirds (AEWA)**, developed under CMS for its own contracting parties; its action plan is legally binding for those parties. It is **not** the same instrument as the CAF Initiative, and India's CMS membership alone does **not** make India an AEWA party. An MOU signatory's political and practical responsibility is real, yet not identical to a treaty obligation. Neither an Appendix II listing nor a COP decision proves that every range state has signed an instrument.

✅ India has been a CMS party since **1983**, with signatory participation in the **Siberian Crane**, **Indian Ocean–South-East Asian Marine Turtles**, **Dugong** and **Birds of Prey/Raptors** MOUs. These named examples are MOUs, not “binding CMS Agreements signed by India.” Species-specific instruments and plans must be read according to their own signatories and geography rather than projected onto every Indian species.

### Concerted Actions: the bridge between listing and delivery

Under Resolution 12.28 (Rev.COP15), a **Concerted Action** is a priority conservation measure,
project or institutional arrangement for selected Appendix I or Appendix II taxa. It must either
involve measures that are the collective responsibility of Parties acting together, or support
the conclusion of an Article IV instrument while conservation proceeds in the meantime—or serve
as an alternative when a separate instrument is not the better remedy.

```text
Appendix-listed taxon
      |
proposal by a Party or relevant stakeholder
      |
Scientific Council / Sessional Committee assessment
      |
recommendation: accept / amend / reject
      |
COP decision on measures + concerned range states
      |
intersessional implementation by proponents, range states and partners
      |
progress report -> Scientific Council review -> COP continuation or closure
```

The proposal may concern one species, a population, lower taxon or a group with common needs.
It should define the range, activities, timeframe and benefits; consult any entity it proposes
to commit; and may incorporate Indigenous and local knowledge. Scientific assessment uses seven
tests:

1. conservation priority;
2. relevance to migration, collective action and CMS mandates;
3. absence of a better remedy, including whether an Article IV Agreement would be preferable;
4. readiness and feasibility, including leadership and funding prospects;
5. likelihood of achieving the intended outcome;
6. magnitude of likely impact; and
7. cost-effectiveness.

Proponents report before the Scientific Council meeting preceding COP. Range states, including
relevant non-Parties, may provide information; CMS Parties report implementation in National
Reports. The Scientific Council reviews progress and recommends further action, while each COP
reviews effectiveness and decides continuation. If no required report is received, the Action is
treated as closed. The Secretariat assists and maintains the online register; Parties and other
stakeholders provide implementation, information and resources.

**Relationship to other CMS tools.** A Concerted Action can operationalise priorities for either
appendix, but it neither changes appendix status nor displaces Appendix I duties. It may prepare
an Article IV Agreement, keep conservation moving while one is negotiated, or provide a lighter
alternative. It is also distinct from an MOU, an Agreement and an action plan, although its
activities may help produce any of them.

**Examples and status discipline.** The Great Indian Bustard and Indian subspecies of the Asian
Elephant received Concerted Actions at COP13 in 2020. They are useful Indian examples of the
mechanism's project logic. The CMS page labelled **Concerted Actions Ongoing** now presents the
actions adopted by the latest COP; therefore a COP13 adoption should not be called an ongoing
COP15 Concerted Action unless the current register or a continuation decision says so. COP15's
separate multi-species bustard Action Plan is important, but its adoption is not itself proof
that the earlier Great Indian Bustard Concerted Action continued or that the population recovered.

### India's domestic implementation chain

```text
CMS appendix / COP decision / MOU
        |
MoEFCC national coordination, international communication and reporting
        |
Indian law and programmes
Wildlife (Protection) Act + protected/community/conservation reserves
+ Wetlands Rules where relevant + species/flyway action plans
        |
scientific evidence
WII and other research bodies: telemetry, habitat and threat assessment
        |
delivery authorities
State/UT forest and wildlife agencies + wetland/fisheries/infrastructure bodies
        |
site management and community action
        |
monitoring -> national report -> CMS review/cooperation
```

India's 2025 CMS National Report identifies MoEFCC and State-government organisations, the
National Biodiversity Authority, National and State Boards for Wildlife, wetland committees and
the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau within the wider coordination landscape. It also reports
Community Reserves under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, central support to States under wildlife-
habitat programmes, species initiatives and community incentives. This is the domestic chain
through which an international listing can influence actual habitat, hunting, bycatch or
infrastructure decisions; the listing does not enforce itself at a site.

### India's Central Asian Flyway progression

| Milestone | What happened | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | India hosted CMS COP13 at Gandhinagar; the Gandhinagar Declaration emphasised ecological connectivity; India held the ensuing presidency | That the flyway was fully institutionalised |
| 2024 | COP14 at Samarkand established the CAF Initiative and advanced Indian coordination; the Samarkand Strategic Plan covers 2024–2032 | That a plan had already recovered populations |

*The timeline tracks institutional steps; a decision's deadline is not an implementation audit.*

The Central Asian Flyway (CAF) is an ecological network of routes, **not** itself a binding CMS Agreement or a national park. ✅ CMS's CAF history records a waterbird Action Plan adopted in **2008**, a New Delhi range-state meeting in **2023**, and establishment of a broader Initiative at **COP14**. ⚠️ Expanding coordination to diverse migratory bird groups requires priority sites, comparable monitoring, capacity and finance; institutional development does not have the same meaning as proven on-ground protection. Do not confuse the CMS presidency after COP13 with permanent ownership of the Convention by India.

**Objection:** Are voluntary MOUs useless without sanctions? **Reply:** Coordinated monitoring and site management can begin before a difficult binding treaty is negotiated. The residual risk is that commitments may not be delivered; evaluate budget, participants, implementation and species trends instead of equating signature with success. **UPSC use:** when asked “India's role,” name a concrete diplomatic milestone and an implementable domestic action, while separating announcement from verified delivery.

**Revision notes**

1. The CMS Convention binds its parties; a separate instrument has its own legal character.
2. Appendix II creates an endeavour to conclude Agreements, not automatic accession to one.
3. AEWA is a binding Agreement for its own parties; voluntary CMS MOUs are not equivalent.
4. India's named MOUs involve Siberian Crane, marine turtles, dugongs and raptors.
5. Concerted Actions are time-bounded priority measures for selected Appendix I/II taxa.
6. A Party or relevant stakeholder may propose one; the Scientific Council assesses it and the COP decides.
7. The seven tests cover priority, relevance, better remedies, feasibility, success, impact and cost-effectiveness.
8. Concerted Actions may lead to, bridge towards or substitute for an Article IV instrument; they do not change appendix status.
9. Reporting drives continuation or closure; no required report means closure under the current process.
10. Great Indian Bustard and Asian Elephant are COP13 examples, not automatically current COP15 ongoing actions.
11. MoEFCC coordinates India; WII and other scientific bodies inform; States and communities implement.
12. Current status: Asian Elephant, Great Indian Bustard and Bengal Florican are Appendix I; Dugong is Appendix II.
13. Amur Falcon falls under the Appendix II family-level Falconidae entry; Ganges River Dolphin appears in both appendices.
14. COP13, COP14 and COP15 mark distinct Indian leadership, CAF institution-building and current decision stages.

**Question:** Does COP15's request to set up a CAF Coordination Unit in India by end-2026 prove either a new binding agreement or a functioning unit today?

**Original Mains application (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse India's role in CMS-led flyway cooperation without equating leadership with conservation success. **Model (about 135 words):** India can convene range states and protect route segments within its territory. ### Four conventions, four distinct levers

| Instrument | Central question | What it does **not** substitute for |
|---|---|---|
| **CMS** | How do range states conserve migratory wild animals across their routes? | CITES import/export controls |
| **CITES** | How is international trade in listed wild fauna/flora regulated through permits and restrictions? | Habitat protection throughout a migration route |
| **CBD** | How are biodiversity conservation, sustainable use and benefit sharing addressed more broadly? | CMS's species/range-specific appendix duties |
| **Ramsar** | How are wetlands conserved and wisely used, including internationally important sites? | Protection of every non-wetland or marine corridor segment |

*These regimes overlap in a real ecosystem but apply different legal tests and instruments; “all protect wildlife” is not an exam answer.*

A species need not be proven to use *only* the CAF to be discussed as a migratory conservation example; avoid assigning the Amur Falcon to a unique flyway or a specific current appendix without an authoritative route/list check.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## The moving population and the jurisdiction test

- CMS/Bonn Convention (1979), UNEP-administered: conservation of migratory wild animals across range states, not international-trade licensing. Birds are not its sole group; qualifying mammals, fish, reptiles and **insects** may be covered.
- Article I: significant proportion of a wild animal population (or geographically separate part) cyclically and predictably crosses a national jurisdictional boundary. Ordinary local dispersal alone is insufficient.
- Range = inhabited, temporary, crossed and overflown normal-route areas. Range state = state exercising jurisdiction over such area **or qualifying flag-vessel state** taking the species beyond national jurisdiction.
- Weakest-link is an analytical warning about connected stages, not a measured mathematical bottleneck in every case. Breeding, stopover, wintering and transit sites need corresponding domestic measures.

## Appendix law: verbs, exceptions and dual listing

- Appendix I: endangered throughout all or significant portion of range. Article III: party range states *shall endeavour* to conserve/restore important habitat and mitigate serious obstacles; *shall prohibit taking*, subject to narrow, precise, time/space-limited, reported exceptions that do not disadvantage species.
- Appendix II: unfavourable conservation status requiring cooperation **or** significant benefit from international agreement. Under Article II parties *shall endeavour to conclude* agreements; Article IV encourages range states to negotiate. Appendix II **is not legally void** and **does not itself enact a species treaty**.
- A species may be on both appendices. CMS appendices do not correspond to CITES trade appendices or IUCN risk categories. Revised COP15 appendices effective **27 June 2026**; do not treat a proposal as an adopted listing.
- **Governance chain:** a Party sends an evidence-based appendix proposal to the Secretariat at least 150 days before COP; the Secretariat circulates it and later Party comments; the Scientific Council supplies listing advice; COP adopts by a two-thirds majority; the amendment enters into force after 90 days except for a Party making a timely written reservation.
- **Institutional division:** COP decides and reviews implementation; the Scientific Council advises on listings, research and measures; the Secretariat services meetings, communicates material, maintains range-state information and promotes Agreements.

## Cooperation instruments and India's position

- CMS framework treaty binds its parties; **AEWA** is a distinct binding Agreement on African-Eurasian migratory waterbirds for AEWA parties, whereas CMS MOUs are generally voluntary. CAF is not AEWA, and CMS membership is not automatic AEWA membership. Action plans and COP decisions have their own terms; listing, signing, operating and measured recovery are distinct steps.
- **Concerted Actions:** time-bounded priority measures for selected Appendix I/II taxa; Party/stakeholder proposal → seven-criterion scientific assessment → COP decision → implementation/reporting → continuation or closure. They may lead to, bridge towards or substitute for an Article IV instrument, but do not change appendix status or displace Appendix I duties. Great Indian Bustard and Asian Elephant were COP13 examples; do not call them current COP15 ongoing actions without a continuation record.
- India party since **1983**; its named CMS MOUs cover Siberian Crane, Indian Ocean–South-East Asian Marine Turtles, Dugong and Birds of Prey/Raptors.
- **Domestic chain:** MoEFCC coordination/reporting → Wildlife (Protection) Act and habitat/action-plan tools → WII and other scientific evidence → State/UT wildlife, wetland, fisheries and infrastructure implementation → site managers and communities → monitoring and national reporting.
- **Appendices effective 27 June 2026:** Asian Elephant (Indian subspecies), Great Indian Bustard and Bengal Florican (named subspecies) are Appendix I; Dugong is Appendix II; Amur Falcon is covered by the Appendix II family-level Falconidae entry; Ganges River Dolphin is in both Appendices I and II.
- COP13: **Gandhinagar, 2020**, Gandhinagar Declaration on connectivity and India's ensuing presidency (mascot “Gibi,” inspired by the Great Indian Bustard; theme “Migratory species connect the planet and we welcome them home”). COP14: **Samarkand, 2024**, CAF Initiative, global stocktake and 2024–2032 strategic plan. Latest completed COP by this session date: **COP15 Campo Grande, Brazil, March 2026**.
- COP15 Decision 15.152 requests support for India's CAF Coordination Unit **by end-2026** and a programme of work. This is a deadline/request, **not** evidence of operation on 3 October 2026.

## From route threats to a defensible answer

- Indian wetland/roost protection + range-wide collaboration; a Ramsar site is a wetland, not the entire flyway. Doyang/Pangti, Nagaland: Amur Falcon stopover and community protection against trapping; no inference of full-route safety.
- Habitat conversion, hunting, collision, bycatch and river barriers demand different agencies/remedies. GIB line-collision risk in India illustrates domestic implementation even when a CMS framework is relevant.
- CMS = migratory-range cooperation; CITES = international wildlife trade; CBD = wider biodiversity conservation/sustainable use/benefit sharing; Ramsar = wetlands. Legal tools may overlap without becoming interchangeable.
