## Lesson 1 — Four terms that must never be collapsed

### Visual: nested concepts, different clocks

```text
LAND DEGRADATION
decline in land's biological/economic productivity or ecological complexity
        |
        `--> when it occurs in ARID + SEMI-ARID + DRY SUB-HUMID areas
                  because of climatic variations and human activities

```

The first difficulty is that ordinary speech uses *desertification* to mean "a desert
spreading." The Convention does not. It defines a decline in productive dryland systems.
Sand may be involved, but moving dunes are neither necessary nor sufficient.

### 1. Land is a system, not soil alone

The UNCCD treats land as a terrestrial bio-productive system comprising soil, vegetation,
other biota and the ecological and hydrological processes operating within it. Land
degradation can therefore occur without visible soil removal. Falling vegetation
productivity, loss of soil organic matter, impaired infiltration or long-term simplification
of a rangeland may all matter.

In strict Article 1 usage, the Convention defines land degradation within arid, semi-arid
and dry sub-humid areas. In wider environmental policy and scientific usage, **land
degradation** is commonly used for declining land condition across climatic zones. In an
answer, state which usage you are adopting.

### 2. Desertification is dryland-specific

The treaty definition contains four parts:

1. there is degradation, not merely natural aridity;
2. the affected location is a dryland;
3. climatic variations may contribute;
4. human activities may contribute.

This is why a naturally sparse desert ecosystem is not automatically degraded. A healthy
desert or steppe can be productive, biodiverse and resilient within its climatic limits.
Conversely, an irrigated dryland farm can desertify through salinity even while appearing
green for several seasons.

### 3. Drought is a hazard; desertification is a condition and process

Drought begins with abnormally low precipitation and creates water imbalance. It may reduce
greenness and yield temporarily. If rain returns and land functions recover, the episode
does not by itself prove desertification. Repeated drought acting on eroded, compacted or
over-extracted land can, however, push the system across a recovery threshold.

| Test | Drought | Desertification |
|---|---|---|
| Primary character | climatic hazard | land-degradation process and resulting condition |
| Core trigger | significantly below-normal precipitation | climatic variation interacting with land use and management |
| Spatial rule | may occur in many climates | UNCCD term applies to drylands |
| Time | episodic, though it can persist for years | persistent decline assessed over time |
| Reversibility | rainfall can end the event | recovery normally needs management change and/or restoration |

### 4. Dryland does not mean barren land

Drylands include arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid zones. Their defining feature is water
limitation measured against atmospheric demand, not absence of people, livestock,
agriculture or biodiversity. Rangelands, rainfed farms, thorn forests, savannas and
agrosilvopastoral mosaics can all be drylands.

The aridity ratio uses annual precipitation divided by potential evapotranspiration. A value
from 0.05 to 0.65 falls within the Convention's dryland range. Polar and sub-polar regions
are excluded even though they may be climatically dry.

### 5. The 2020 UPSC wording requires a qualification

The question "The process of desertification does not have climate boundaries" should not
be answered by deleting the treaty's dryland boundary. The defensible route is:

```text
strict UNCCD noun: desertification = degradation in drylands
                         +
degrading processes: erosion, salinization, vegetation loss and misuse
can occur across climatic zones
                         +
dryland boundaries shift and human pressure can create desert-like loss
outside popular images of hot deserts
```

Thus the examiner's proposition attacks climatic determinism and the "advancing sand"
image; it does not license an inaccurate treaty definition.

### 6. Example and its limit

Irrigation in a semi-arid command area may raise production initially. Without drainage,
groundwater can rise, salts can accumulate near the surface and yields can fall. This is a
clear pathway to dryland degradation. The example shows that added water can degrade land
when salt and drainage balances are ignored. It does not prove that irrigation is
inherently harmful.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** separate dryland, desert, drought, land degradation and desertification.
- **Mains:** define narrowly, then explain interacting climatic and human causes.
- **Trap:** a natural desert is an ecosystem; desertification is deterioration.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Drought is a rainfall shock, aridity is a climatic condition,
> and desertification is a persistent decline of productive dryland systems.

### Revision notes

1. Land includes soil, vegetation, biota and ecological-hydrological processes.
2. Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas.
3. Both climatic variation and human activity can contribute.
4. Drought begins with significantly below-normal precipitation.
5. A drought-induced fall in greenness is not automatically long-term degradation.
6. Drylands use a precipitation-to-potential-evapotranspiration ratio of 0.05–0.65.
7. Polar and sub-polar regions are excluded from the UNCCD dryland definition.
8. A desert can be ecologically healthy.
9. Desertification is not synonymous with advancing dunes.
10. State clearly whether "land degradation" is being used in treaty or wider policy usage.
11. The 2020 PYQ tests non-determinism but still requires definitional precision.

### Concept check

**Question:** A semi-arid district experiences one failed monsoon, lower vegetation greenness and full recovery after two normal seasons. Which term is established, and which is not?

**Model answer:** The evidence establishes a drought episode and temporary vegetation stress. It does not by itself establish desertification, because persistent loss of dryland productivity or ecological function has not been demonstrated. Multi-year indicators and field evidence are needed.

**Misconception to avoid:** One dry year or one low-greenness satellite image is not conclusive proof of desertification.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish drought, aridity and desertification, and explain why the distinction matters for policy.

#### Model response (134 words)

Aridity is a long-term climatic condition in which precipitation is low relative to
potential evapotranspiration. Drought is an abnormal period of significantly below-normal
precipitation that creates hydrological imbalance. Desertification, under the UNCCD, is land
degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas caused by climatic variations and
human activities.

The distinction changes intervention. Drought calls for early warning, water allocation,
fodder and livelihood protection. Aridity requires permanent adaptation through suitable
crops, water harvesting and demand management. Desertification requires correction of
degrading processes such as erosion, salinity, vegetation loss and unsustainable extraction,
followed by monitored recovery. Treating all three as "lack of rain" encourages emergency
relief while ignoring drainage, grazing institutions, soil cover and tenure. Policy should
therefore match the timescale and mechanism while recognising that repeated drought can
accelerate degradation on already fragile land.

## Lesson 2 — How land loses function

Progress: 2 / 15 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Drivers, processes, indicators and reinforcing feedbacks

### Visual: pressure becomes degradation through a process

```text
DRIVER / PRESSURE
climate variability | intense rain | drought | grazing pressure | tillage
deforestation | mining | poorly drained irrigation | groundwater overuse
                         |
                         v
BIOPHYSICAL PROCESS
water erosion | wind erosion | compaction | crusting | salinization
waterlogging | nutrient/SOC decline | vegetation degradation
                         |
                         v
INDICATOR RESPONSE
cover change | lower productivity | lower soil carbon | runoff/sediment
salts | infiltration loss | groundwater change | species/community change
                         |
                         v
IMPACT
yield and forage decline | unstable water supply | biodiversity loss
poverty risk | migration pressure | greater drought sensitivity
                         |
                         `---- feedback to more pressure
```

### 1. Drivers are not the same as processes

A driver changes behaviour or exposure; a process physically, chemically or biologically
alters land. Population growth, insecure tenure or a subsidy for water-intensive crops are
not erosion. They may produce land-use choices that remove cover, increase extraction or
encourage cultivation on unsuitable slopes, which then activate erosion or salinity.

This distinction matters because policy aimed only at the visible process can fail. A check
dam cannot correct a pricing rule that continually rewards groundwater depletion. A
plantation cannot settle conflict over a pastoral commons.

### 2. Natural variability and human pressure interact

Drylands naturally experience variable rainfall and pulse-like productivity. Human use is
not automatically degrading: mobile pastoralism, fallowing and locally adapted rainfed
farming can conserve resilience. Degradation occurs when pressure exceeds the system's
regenerative capacity or blocks recovery after a shock.

```text
drought alone + intact cover + recovery time
        --> temporary stress, possible recovery

drought + bare soil + compacted surface + debt-driven overuse
        --> runoff/deflation + low infiltration + weak recovery
        --> persistent degradation risk
```

### 3. Major process families

| Process family | Mechanism | Typical field or remote signal |
|---|---|---|
| Water erosion | splash, sheet, rill and gully removal | exposed soil, gullies, sediment plumes |
| Wind erosion | deflation, saltation, abrasion and dust export | mobile sand, blowouts, dust source areas |
| Vegetation degradation | cover, composition or biomass declines | persistent productivity/cover loss |
| Physical soil decline | compaction, crusting, structural damage | runoff rises; infiltration and rooting fall |
| Chemical decline | salinity, sodicity, acidification, nutrient imbalance | salt crust, dispersion, poor crop response |
| Hydrological decline | waterlogging, falling water table, altered flow | saturated root zone or declining wells |
| Direct conversion | mining, quarrying, infrastructure and sealing | abrupt land-cover replacement |

Processes often overlap. Removing vegetation can expose soil to both raindrop impact and
wind. Eroded topsoil loses organic matter, which weakens aggregates and further increases
erosion.

### 4. Indicators are evidence, not causes

An indicator answers "what changed?" rather than "why?" Land cover, land productivity and
soil organic carbon are the UNCCD's minimum global LDN indicators. They need interpretation.
A greener pixel could represent native grass recovery, an irrigated monoculture, invasive
scrub or a plantation on a natural grassland. Similar greenness does not mean equal
ecological function.

Field indicators add texture:

- aggregate stability and bulk density;
- infiltration and runoff;
- soil depth, gullies and sediment;
- electrical conductivity or exchangeable sodium;
- groundwater depth and quality;
- species composition, palatable forage and bare-ground proportion;
- yield stability rather than one-year yield.

### 5. Thresholds create non-linearity

Land decline is not always smooth. Once vegetation cover falls below a locally important
threshold, wind or runoff can connect bare patches. Seed sources may disappear, soil
surfaces may crust and recovery may become much harder. This creates hysteresis: removing
the original pressure may not immediately return the system to its earlier state.

### 6. The poverty-degradation relationship is conditional

Poverty can force short time horizons and resource overuse, but blaming poor communities is
analytically weak. Commercial extraction, infrastructure, mining, poorly designed subsidies
and exclusion from secure tenure can be larger drivers. Communities may also be the
institutions that protect commons and maintain adaptive mobility.

The strongest causal answer separates:

```text
proximate process --> land-use practice --> incentive/institution --> wider economy/climate
```

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Satellite productivity decline may simply reflect rainfall variability.

**Reply:** Correct; therefore diagnosis should use multi-year trajectories, climate context,
the other LDN indicators and field validation. The residual problem is that national-scale
monitoring can still miss local distributional harm or ecological simplification.

### UPSC integration

- Explain a named chain, not a list of causes.
- Distinguish pressure, process, indicator and impact.
- Qualify satellite evidence with trend length and ground verification.

### Revision notes

1. Drivers influence decisions or exposure; processes alter land.
2. Climate and management usually interact rather than operate independently.
3. Water erosion, wind erosion and vegetation degradation are major mapped families.
4. Compaction and crusting reduce infiltration and root growth.
5. Salinity and sodicity are distinct chemical problems.
6. Indicators reveal change but do not automatically identify cause.
7. Cover, productivity and soil organic carbon are the minimum LDN indicator set.
8. Multi-year trends are safer than one-year snapshots.
9. Thresholds can make recovery slow or path-dependent.
10. Poverty is neither a universal cause nor proof of poor stewardship.
11. Diagnose from proximate process upward to institutions and incentives.
12. Ground-truthing prevents false interpretation of remote signals.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a successful increase in satellite greenness still fail to prove land restoration?

**Model answer:** Greenness shows vegetation response, not necessarily native biodiversity, soil recovery, water sustainability or secure livelihoods. It may come from irrigation, invasive plants or a monoculture. Land-cover, productivity and soil-carbon trends therefore need field and social interpretation.

**Misconception to avoid:** More green colour on a map is not automatically more ecological function.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain how land degradation emerges from interacting climatic, biophysical and institutional drivers.

#### Model response (191 words)

Land degradation occurs when pressures reduce the productivity, complexity or resilience of
the land system. Climatic variability supplies shocks: drought suppresses vegetation, while
intense rain raises erosive runoff. These shocks become persistent decline when management
weakens recovery.

Biophysically, cover removal exposes soil to splash, sheet, gully and wind erosion.
Compaction and crusting reduce infiltration, increasing runoff and lowering plant-available
water. Poorly drained irrigation can raise groundwater and salts into the root zone.
Loss of soil organic matter weakens aggregates and nutrient cycling, reinforcing erosion and
yield instability.

Institutions shape these processes. Insecure tenure may discourage long-term investment;
restricted pastoral mobility can concentrate grazing; electricity or crop incentives may
promote groundwater overuse; and fragmented departments may treat soil, water and vegetation
separately. Yet communities are not inherently degrading agents: customary grazing rules,
water harvesting and adaptive mobility can conserve dryland resilience.

The result is a feedback loop: lower productivity reduces income, shortens planning horizons
and increases extraction, while degraded land becomes more drought-sensitive. Monitoring
should therefore combine multi-year land cover, productivity and soil-carbon data with field
evidence and local knowledge. Effective policy must treat the visible process and the
underlying incentive together.

### Visual: four routes to an unproductive root zone

```text
EROSION                 COMPACTION / CRUSTING
topsoil exported        pore space collapses
nutrients + SOC lost    infiltration and rooting fall
        \                    /
         \                  /
          --> ROOT-ZONE FUNCTION DECLINES <--
         /                  \
        /                    \
SALINITY / SODICITY       WATERLOGGING
salt/osmotic stress       air displaced from pores
dispersion in sodic soil  roots become oxygen-starved
```

### 1. Water erosion is a sequence

Raindrop impact detaches particles. Sheet flow removes a thin layer; concentrated flow
creates rills and then gullies. The damage is not limited to the source field. Sediment can
fill reservoirs and channels, carry nutrients or contaminants and impair downstream aquatic
systems.

Slopes, intense rain, bare soil, weak aggregation and poorly aligned cultivation increase
risk. Contour farming, vegetative barriers and cover crops work because they shorten slope
length, reduce flow velocity and protect the surface.

### 2. Aeolian erosion has three transport modes

| Mode | Material | Degradation role |
|---|---|---|
| Suspension | finest dust | long-distance nutrient and carbon export; dust loading |
| Saltation | bouncing sand grains | dislodges other particles and abrades seedlings |
| Surface creep | coarser grains | redistributes material near the ground |

Deflation removes loose particles. Abrasion is sand-blasting by transported grains. Wind
erosion is strongest where dry, erodible soil is exposed and wind velocity crosses a
threshold. Shelterbelts, residues and roughness reduce near-surface wind energy; they do not
eliminate regional drought.

### 3. Salinity and sodicity are related but different

**Salinity** is excessive soluble salt in the root zone. Plants struggle to take up water
because the osmotic gradient becomes unfavourable; specific ions may also be toxic.
**Sodicity** is excessive exchangeable sodium relative to other cations. Sodium disperses
clay, damages structure and can sharply reduce infiltration.

```text
irrigation water brings salts
        +
high evaporation / inadequate drainage
        +
shallow water table and capillary rise
        ↓
water leaves; salts remain
        ↓
salinity and possibly sodicity
```

The ISRO-SAC atlas notes that salinity commonly occurs in cultivated, especially irrigated,
land. It may arise naturally or through excess irrigation, rising groundwater, drought,
evapotranspiration and chemical inputs. Satellite detection is easier for visible salinity
than for alkalinization, which often needs soil analysis and ground evidence.

### 4. Waterlogging is too much water in the wrong place

Waterlogging saturates the root zone, reduces oxygen diffusion and changes soil chemistry.
It can coexist with salinity: a shallow water table supplies salts, and evaporation leaves
them at the surface. Drainage, irrigation scheduling and conjunctive water management are
therefore as important as adding water.

### 5. Soil organic matter is functional infrastructure

Organic matter improves aggregation, nutrient cycling, biological activity and water
retention. The exact gain depends on texture and management, but the direction is important.
The 2018 Prelims question turns this material into three separate propositions concerning
organic matter and water retention, soil in nutrient cycling, and irrigation-related salt
accumulation. Keep the propositions independent at this stage. The exact neutral question
appears in the final PYQ block before any demand clue; apply the mechanisms taught here only
after reading all three statements.

### 6. Restoration must match the process

| Diagnosed problem | Process-matched response | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| sheet/rill erosion | cover, contouring, residue, reduced disturbance | fails if upslope runoff is ignored |
| gully erosion | catchment treatment, vegetative/structural checks | isolated check structures may shift erosion |
| wind erosion | cover, shelter, managed grazing, surface roughness | species and water demand must fit dryland |
| salinity | drainage, leaching where feasible, salt-tolerant systems | water quality and disposal constrain use |
| sodicity | amendments where appropriate plus drainage and organic matter | diagnosis must precede treatment |
| compaction | traffic/grazing control, roots and organic inputs | deep physical damage may recover slowly |

### 7. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Engineering structures can quickly arrest erosion.

**Reply:** They can be essential, but source-area cover, maintenance and catchment hydrology
decide whether gains last. A structure-first programme may report assets while soil function
continues to decline. The residual challenge is maintaining collective action after project
funding ends.

### UPSC integration

- Use the detachment–transport–deposition chain for erosion.
- In salinity answers, mention water balance and drainage, not only "excess salts."
- Never prescribe tree planting as a universal soil remedy.

### Revision notes

1. Water erosion progresses from splash and sheet to rill and gully forms.
2. Erosion harms both source land and downstream systems.
3. Suspension, saltation and surface creep are aeolian transport modes.
4. Deflation removes particles; abrasion wears surfaces.
5. Salinity creates osmotic and sometimes ion-specific stress.
6. Sodicity disperses clay and damages structure.
7. Waterlogging deprives roots of oxygen.
8. Shallow groundwater can connect waterlogging with salinity.
9. Organic matter supports aggregation, water retention and nutrient cycling.
10. Soil participates in biogeochemical cycles, including sulphur cycling.
11. Irrigation can raise productivity or cause degradation depending on drainage and salt balance.
12. Treatment must follow diagnosis.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can lining a canal or supplying more irrigation water fail to solve salinity in a command area?

**Model answer:** Salinity is governed by the whole water-and-salt balance. If drainage remains inadequate, groundwater stays shallow and evapotranspiration leaves salts in the root zone. Supply-side improvement without drainage, scheduling and water-quality management can preserve the cause.

**Misconception to avoid:** Salinity is not cured simply by applying more water; leaching requires an outlet and suitable water.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the pathways through which irrigation can both conserve and degrade land.

#### Model response (132 words)

Irrigation can conserve land by stabilising plant cover, reducing crop failure, supporting
biomass return and permitting protective vegetation during dry periods. Reliable moisture
can also make contour cultivation, agroforestry and diversified farming economically viable.

The same intervention degrades land when water application exceeds crop use and drainage.
The water table rises, capillary action brings dissolved salts upward and evaporation leaves
them in the root zone. Salinity imposes osmotic stress; sodicity can disperse clay and reduce
infiltration. Persistent saturation also causes waterlogging and oxygen stress. Canal
seepage, poor-quality groundwater and water-intensive crop incentives can reinforce these
effects.

Therefore irrigation is not intrinsically restorative or degrading. Its outcome depends on
water quality, drainage, scheduling, crop choice, groundwater balance and soil monitoring.
Command-area policy must evaluate productivity together with salinity, waterlogging and
long-term soil function.

### Visual: the degrading and recovering loops

```text
DEGRADING LOOP
cover loss -> raindrop/wind exposure -> erosion and crusting
          -> lower infiltration and soil moisture -> weak regrowth
          -> hotter, barer surface -> still lower resilience

RECOVERY LOOP
pressure control -> protective cover -> slower runoff and wind
                -> infiltration + soil biota + organic inputs
                -> more stable productivity -> stronger recovery after drought
```

### 1. Vegetation is both an outcome and a regulator

Plants respond to water and soil condition, but they also change both. Canopies intercept
rain, roots bind soil and create pores, litter protects the surface, and roughness slows
wind. Vegetation loss therefore removes several protective functions at once.

Composition matters as much as cover. A shift from palatable perennial grasses to
unpalatable shrubs may maintain some greenness while reducing forage value and altering
fire, water and biodiversity functions.

### 2. Infiltration decides whether rain becomes resource or hazard

On a well-structured surface, rainfall can enter soil and recharge root-zone moisture.
Compaction, crusting and bare patches increase overland flow. Thus even where annual
rainfall does not fall, effective water available to plants can decline.

```text
same rainfall
  + porous, covered soil --> infiltration --> plant use and recharge
  + crusted, bare soil   --> runoff --> erosion, flash flow and low storage
```

This is why watershed treatment begins from ridge and source areas rather than only storing
water at the outlet.

### 3. Drought types describe different points in a cascade

Meteorological drought is rainfall deficiency. Agricultural drought concerns inadequate
soil moisture for crops. Hydrological drought concerns depleted surface or groundwater
stores. Ecological drought concerns water deficit severe enough to impair ecosystem
function. They can follow one another, but not mechanically or at the same speed.

Reservoirs or groundwater can delay agricultural stress after low rainfall. Conversely,
over-extraction can produce hydrological scarcity even without a severe meteorological
drought.

### 4. A resilience approach differs from crisis relief

Relief responds after impact through drinking water, fodder, employment or crop support.
Resilience reduces sensitivity beforehand:

- diversified crops and livelihoods;
- soil cover and moisture conservation;
- aquifer and watershed budgeting;
- fodder banks plus protected grazing reserves;
- drought forecasts linked to decisions;
- contingency plans and social protection;
- institutions that permit pastoral mobility.

Preparedness does not eliminate extreme drought. It reduces the probability that a climatic
shock becomes a livelihood collapse and a new round of land overuse.

### 5. Trees can help or harm

Agroforestry can reduce wind, recycle nutrients, supply fodder and diversify income.
However, dense plantations of unsuitable species may consume scarce water, displace native
grassland, reduce forage or simplify habitat. The correct question is not "more trees?" but
"which vegetation structure fits this ecosystem and livelihood?"

### 6. Feedback to climate and dust

Bare, dry soil can produce dust and alter surface energy and moisture exchanges. Loss of
soil carbon and vegetation also weakens carbon storage. These feedbacks connect land,
climate, biodiversity and drought, but site-specific attribution is essential; one dust
storm does not establish a long-term climatic trend.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Rainfall dominates dryland productivity, so land management has little
leverage.

**Reply:** Rainfall is a major control, yet cover, infiltration, soil depth, grazing timing
and water allocation determine how much rainfall becomes usable and how fast the system
recovers. The residual limit is that restoration cannot manufacture unlimited water or
fully offset intensifying climate hazards.

### UPSC integration

- Show the drought cascade but state that lags and human water use modify it.
- Use paired degrading and recovery feedbacks.
- Treat vegetation type, not tree count, as the ecological variable.

1. Vegetation is both an indicator and a regulator of land condition.
2. Roots, litter and roughness protect soil and aid infiltration.
3. Species composition can deteriorate even when total greenness rises.
4. Crusting converts rainfall from stored moisture into erosive runoff.
5. Meteorological, agricultural, hydrological and ecological drought are distinct.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two catchments receive equal rainfall, but one produces more runoff, erosion and crop stress. What land properties could explain the difference?

**Model answer:** Lower cover, compaction, surface crusting, poorer aggregation and connected bare patches can reduce infiltration and increase rapid runoff. The same rainfall then supplies less root-zone moisture and causes more erosion.

**Misconception to avoid:** Rainfall total alone does not determine effective water availability or erosion risk.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** How can drought resilience policy prevent climatic shocks from becoming long-term land degradation?

#### Model response (184 words)

Drought becomes land degradation when water stress interacts with weak soil, vegetation and
livelihood systems. Resilience policy must therefore act before, during and after rainfall
failure.

Before drought, watershed treatment should protect ridge areas, improve infiltration and
harvest runoff without exceeding basin limits. Soil cover, reduced disturbance, suitable
agroforestry and pasture management lower erosion and conserve moisture. Crop and livelihood
diversification reduce dependence on one rainfall-sensitive activity. Groundwater budgeting
and crop alignment prevent emergency pumping from becoming aquifer depletion.

During drought, forecasts must trigger fodder plans, drinking-water priorities, contingency
cropping, employment and social protection. Pastoral mobility and access to reserve grazing
can spread pressure instead of concentrating animals on shrinking commons. After drought,
assistance should support recovery of soil cover and productive assets rather than
incentivise immediate overstocking or cultivation of marginal land.

Monitoring should connect rainfall, soil moisture, vegetation condition, groundwater and
livelihood indicators. A low-greenness year alone is insufficient evidence of
desertification. The policy objective is not to eliminate drought, which is impossible, but
to maintain ecological function and household options so that a temporary climatic shock
does not produce persistent decline.

## Lesson 5 — Why degraded land is an economy-wide problem

Progress: 5 / 15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Ecological, livelihood, economic and social impacts

CA found: No separate current-affairs linkage; impact pathways are taught as structural relationships
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: one damaged asset, many transmitted costs

```text
LAND FUNCTION DECLINES
        |
        +--> farm yield / forage / fuelwood variability
        +--> runoff, sedimentation and water-treatment costs
        +--> biodiversity and pollination loss
        +--> soil and vegetation carbon loss
        +--> drought and flood sensitivity
        +--> household debt, nutrition and labour burdens
        +--> distress mobility or conflict over access
        `--> public spending on relief and asset repair
```

### 1. Productivity loss is wider than crop yield

Productivity includes food, fibre, fodder, fuel, timber and ecosystem services. A rangeland
can lose nutritional quality before biomass collapses. A farm can maintain yield temporarily
through more fertilizer, pumping or labour while natural capital deteriorates. Economic
accounts that observe output but ignore rising input costs may miss degradation.

### 2. Water costs travel beyond the plot

Erosion removes soil from one place and deposits it elsewhere. Sediment shortens reservoir
life, raises desilting and treatment costs and can damage aquatic habitats. Lower
infiltration reduces dry-season base flow or recharge; rapid runoff can worsen flash floods.
Land restoration is therefore partly water infrastructure.

### 3. Biodiversity loss can hide behind uniform greening

Replacing a native grassland mosaic with a tree monoculture may increase canopy while
reducing habitat for open-country species, forage diversity and locally adapted livelihoods.
Open Natural Ecosystems—grasslands, savannas, scrub and rocky systems—must not be presumed
degraded merely because they lack dense tree cover.

### 4. Climate linkage works in both directions

Land degradation can reduce soil and biomass carbon, weaken evaporative cooling and increase
dust. Climate change can intensify heat, rainfall extremes and drought exposure. Restoration
may improve carbon storage and adaptation, but carbon alone cannot represent biodiversity,
water or rights.

### 5. Distribution matters

The same decline affects households differently. A landowner may finance a borewell; a
tenant, landless labourer or pastoral household may lose work or access. Women often absorb
longer journeys for water, fodder or fuel and may lack formal title even when they manage
land. A neutral hectare count can therefore conceal unequal benefits and burdens.

### 6. Migration is an outcome of interacting pressures

Land decline can contribute to mobility by reducing income and increasing risk. It is rarely
a single-cause explanation. Employment opportunities, conflict, services, social networks,
credit and policy also shape movement. Use "contributes to" rather than claiming automatic
mass migration from one degradation statistic.

### 7. Economic valuation helps, but is incomplete

Valuation can compare avoided erosion, yield gains, carbon, water regulation and restoration
costs. It helps reveal why prevention is often cheaper than repair. Yet cultural identity,
customary access, biodiversity and irreversible threshold loss are not always safely
reduced to one monetary number.

```text
project return
= private production gains
+ avoided downstream/public costs
+ resilience and ecosystem-service gains
- maintenance and opportunity costs
- distributional and ecological risks
```

### 8. Criticism and reply

**Objection:** Restoration is a luxury where immediate livelihood needs dominate.

**Reply:** Well-designed restoration protects productive capacity and can create work,
fodder, water security and diversified income. The objection remains valid against projects
that exclude users, delay benefits or replace functioning commons with restricted
plantations.

### UPSC integration

- Move from on-site productivity to off-site, social and fiscal effects.
- Name unequal incidence across owners, tenants, labourers, pastoralists and women.
- Avoid deterministic migration claims.

### Revision notes

1. Land productivity is broader than crop yield.
2. Rising inputs can mask declining natural capital.
3. Sediment creates downstream reservoir and treatment costs.
4. Lower infiltration can worsen both scarcity and flash runoff.
5. Canopy gain may coexist with biodiversity and livelihood loss.
6. Open Natural Ecosystems are not ecological blanks.
7. Land degradation and climate change reinforce one another.
8. Hectare totals do not reveal distribution.
9. Women may bear labour burdens without secure land rights.
10. Land decline contributes to migration but rarely acts alone.
11. Prevention often avoids larger future costs.
12. Valuation should not erase culture, rights or irreversibility.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why may a restoration project with higher tree cover and positive carbon estimates still impose a net social-ecological loss?

**Model answer:** It may replace a native open ecosystem, reduce forage or species habitat, consume scarce water, restrict customary access or shift benefits away from local users. Carbon and canopy are partial metrics, not a complete welfare test.

**Misconception to avoid:** A project is not successful merely because one biophysical indicator improves.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Land degradation is simultaneously an ecological, economic and justice problem. Discuss.

#### Model response (208 words)

Land is productive infrastructure composed of soil, water, vegetation, biota and
hydrological processes. Its degradation therefore transmits losses across sectors.

Ecologically, erosion removes topsoil and organic carbon, salinity impairs root function,
vegetation simplification reduces habitat, and lower infiltration weakens drought recovery.
Climate change amplifies these stresses, while degraded land stores less carbon and may
produce more dust and runoff.

Economically, households face unstable crops, forage and fuel supplies, often maintaining
output only through higher fertilizer, pumping or labour. Off-site costs include reservoir
sedimentation, water treatment, flood damage and public drought relief. Prevention can
therefore yield returns beyond the treated plot.

The justice dimension concerns who controls land and who bears adjustment. Tenants and
landless labourers may not capture restoration gains. Pastoralists can lose mobility when
commons are fenced. Women may supply restoration labour or collect water and fodder while
lacking formal tenure and decision power. Even a carbon-positive plantation can damage a
native grassland and exclude its users.

Policy should apply the avoid–reduce–reverse hierarchy, secure legitimate tenure, use
participatory landscape planning and monitor soil, water, biodiversity, livelihoods and
distribution. Restoration is thus not a plantation target or a hectare count; it is recovery
of ecological function through institutions that preserve productive options and allocate
benefits fairly.

```text
HOW MUCH?                     BY WHICH PROCESS?                 WHERE?
```

The **Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas of India, 2021**, prepared by
Space Applications Centre, ISRO, maps the **2018–19 timeframe**. It is not a measurement
made in 2021 or 2026.

### 1. National extent and trend

| Atlas timeframe | Land under desertification/land degradation | Share of total geographical area |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2003–05 | 94.53 million hectares | 28.76% |
| 2011–13 | 96.40 million hectares | 29.32% |
| 2018–19 | 97.85 million hectares | 29.77% |

Between 2011–13 and 2018–19, the mapped area increased by **1.45 million hectares**, or
**0.44 percentage points of total geographical area**. The atlas also observes that the
rate of cumulative increase was lower than in the preceding comparison period. Both facts
can be true: extent rose, while the rate of increase slowed.

### 2. Desertification is a subset within the mapped total

The atlas reports **83.69 million hectares** undergoing desertification in dryland regions
in 2018–19, compared with 82.64 million hectares in 2011–13 and 81.48 million hectares in
2003–05. The national land-degradation total is larger because the atlas maps degrading
processes across land-use and climatic contexts, while desertification is the dryland
subset.

### 3. Dominant processes are spatially different

For 2018–19, the atlas reports:

- **water erosion: 11.01%** of India's total geographical area;
- **vegetation degradation: 9.15%**;
- **wind erosion: 5.46%**.

Wind erosion is the leading desertification process in the arid region. Water erosion and
vegetation degradation are prominent in semi-arid and dry sub-humid regions. This defeats
the stereotype that India's desertification map is simply a map of moving sand.

### 4. State evidence requires two denominators

The atlas lists Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Ladakh, Jharkhand, Odisha,
Madhya Pradesh and Telangana, in descending order, as the major contributors to the
national total; together their affected area equals **23.79% of India's total geographical
area** in the 2018–19 mapping.

That is different from asking what share of each State is affected. On the State's own
denominator, Jharkhand, Rajasthan, Delhi, Gujarat and Goa exceed 50% affected area in the
atlas. A small territory can have a high internal percentage while contributing less area
to the national total than a large State.

### 5. Classification combines land use, process and severity

The mapping system combines:

1. land-use class, such as irrigated agriculture, forest/plantation, grassland or scrub;
2. process, such as vegetation degradation, water erosion, wind erosion, salinity,
   waterlogging, mass movement or human-made change;
3. severity.

Its minimum mapping unit and satellite interpretation are suited to regional planning, not
to deciding the condition of every individual field.

### 6. What remote sensing can and cannot establish

Remote sensing offers repeatability, national coverage and spatial targeting. It can reveal
cover, surface condition and productivity patterns that field surveys alone cannot map at
scale. But interpretation needs:

- co-registration and consistent classification;
- trend comparison rather than one image;
- rainfall and land-use context;
- soil and groundwater sampling;
- local knowledge about grazing, fire and management;
- separation of natural open ecosystems from "wasteland."

The atlas itself notes that visible salinity may be detected in imagery, while
alkalinization often needs ground truth and soil analysis.

### 7. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A national atlas provides objective truth and removes the need for local
judgement.

**Reply:** It provides indispensable, standardised evidence, but every map has scale,
classification and temporal limits. Local validation explains cause and social meaning.
The residual policy risk is that administrative categories can convert a mapping label into
an inappropriate intervention.

- Use the three leading process shares to show India's spatial diversity.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the statement "Rajasthan is India's most degraded State" analytically incomplete even if Rajasthan contributes the largest affected area?

**Model answer:** It does not specify the denominator or metric. Largest absolute contribution to India's affected area differs from the highest affected share within a State, and neither alone describes severity, process or ecological condition.

**Misconception to avoid:** A ranking is meaningless unless the unit, denominator, timeframe and variable are stated.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the uses and limits of remote sensing in governing land degradation in India.

#### Model response (197 words)

Remote sensing makes land degradation governable at national and regional scales. The
ISRO-SAC atlas provides repeatable spatial coverage, process classes and comparable
timeframes. For 2018–19 it mapped 97.85 million hectares, or 29.77% of India's geographical
area, under desertification/land degradation. Such evidence identifies erosion, vegetation
decline, salinity and other hotspots, supports State prioritisation and enables change
detection.

Its limits are equally important. A satellite records surface signals, not complete causal
or social explanations. Temporary drought may reduce greenness without persistent
degradation. A greener pixel may be irrigated monoculture or invasive vegetation rather
than ecological recovery. Visible salinity may be mapped, whereas alkalinization and soil
structure require field analysis. National mapping units cannot establish every plot's
condition, tenure conflict or distribution of benefits.

Governance should therefore combine consistent satellite classification with rainfall
normalisation, multi-year productivity trends, soil and groundwater sampling, biodiversity
surveys and local land-user knowledge. Open grasslands must not be labelled empty land
solely because canopy is low. Remote sensing should trigger diagnosis and adaptive
management, not automatically prescribe plantations. Its greatest value lies in transparent
targeting and repeated monitoring; its greatest danger lies in mistaking a scalable proxy
for the whole land system.

## Lesson 7 — The UNCCD's constitutional design

### Visual: a treaty that links global support to local action

```text

      |
      `--> 1994 UNCCD adopted --> entered into force 1996
```

### 1. Why a separate land convention emerged

Climate, biodiversity and land are connected, but the politics and implementation problems
are not identical. Land degradation is experienced locally through soils, water, farming,
grazing and tenure. The UNCCD therefore links international cooperation with nationally and
locally designed action.

The Convention is one of the three Rio Conventions alongside the UNFCCC and CBD. It was
adopted in 1994 and entered into force in 1996. Its title gives particular emphasis to
countries experiencing serious drought and/or desertification, especially in Africa.

### 2. Its objective is developmental as well as environmental

Combating desertification includes:

- prevention and/or reduction of land degradation;
- rehabilitation of partly degraded land;
- reclamation of desertified land.

The purpose is sustainable development in affected areas, not a war against naturally dry
ecosystems. Long-term strategies should improve land productivity and rehabilitation,
conservation and sustainable management of land and water resources.

### 3. Four governing principles

| Principle | Operational meaning |
|---|---|
| participation | populations and local communities help design and implement programmes |
| decentralisation and enabling environment | decisions should be made at appropriate levels while higher levels support action |
| international partnership | affected and developed-country Parties coordinate support and obligations |
| attention to affected countries | priorities reflect needs and circumstances, especially in Africa |

Participation is not a meeting added after a project is designed. Land users possess local
knowledge and bear opportunity costs; their role changes intervention quality and
legitimacy.

### 4. Why Africa is explicit

The Convention's regional annex system addresses Africa, Asia, Latin America and the
Caribbean, the Northern Mediterranean, and Central and Eastern Europe. Africa's special
priority reflects severe vulnerability, poverty, recurrent drought and limited capacity,
not a claim that desertification exists only there.

The UNCCD is the only Rio Convention organised through this specific set of regional
implementation annexes. The distinction is institutional: the UNFCCC and CBD differentiate
implementation in other ways but do not reproduce the UNCCD's five-annex regional structure.

### 5. Binding treaty, nationally differentiated implementation

The UNCCD is legally binding as a treaty. That does not mean the COP imposes identical
land-use commands or one compulsory hectare target on every country. Parties develop
strategies and report implementation within a common framework, while LDN targets are
nationally set.

### 6. Land is cross-sectoral by design

Effective implementation touches agriculture, water, forests, rural development, disaster
risk, local government, science and finance. This creates a coordination advantage—many
goals can converge—but also a responsibility problem when no agency controls the whole
landscape.

### 7. Strongest criticism and reply

**Criticism:** The Convention is weaker than climate governance because land degradation is
diffuse, nationally managed and less visible.

**Reply:** Its participatory, land-and-livelihood architecture addresses problems that a
single global emissions metric cannot. Yet weak finance, fragmented mandates and uneven
reporting remain genuine constraints. The answer is stronger implementation and
accountability, not replacing the land lens with climate carbon accounting.

### UPSC integration

- Do not describe it as a global afforestation convention.
- Explain Africa priority through the regional annex structure.

2. It is one of the three Rio Conventions.
3. Its objective combines combating desertification and mitigating drought effects.
4. Sustainable development in affected areas is central.
5. Prevention, rehabilitation and reclamation appear in Article 1.
6. National and local participation is a foundational principle.
7. International cooperation supports country action.
8. Africa receives particular priority but does not monopolise the problem.
9. Five regional implementation annexes structure differentiated action.
10. A binding treaty does not imply identical compulsory national land targets.
11. UNCCD implementation is inherently cross-sectoral.
12. Carbon benefits are co-benefits, not the Convention's sole purpose.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the UNCCD be legally binding while national LDN targets remain nationally determined?

**Model answer:** Treaty obligations govern cooperation, planning, reporting and implementation processes. Countries retain sovereignty over the ambition and design of their national LDN targets within the common Convention framework.

**Misconception to avoid:** Legally binding treaty status does not mean one uniform land-restoration quota is imposed on every Party.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why does the UNCCD combine environmental restoration with participatory rural development?

#### Model response (147 words)

Desertification is not a detached soil problem. It emerges from interactions among water,
vegetation, farming, grazing, tenure and climatic variation, while its costs fall directly
on land-dependent livelihoods. The UNCCD therefore defines combating desertification
through integrated development of drylands: preventing or reducing degradation,
rehabilitating partly degraded land and reclaiming desertified land.

Participation improves both knowledge and legitimacy. Farmers, pastoralists and local
communities understand seasonal variability, common-property rules and livelihood trade-offs
that remote plans may miss. They also bear the opportunity cost of grazing restrictions,
land-use change or maintenance. National Action Programmes consequently link local action
with enabling policy, science, finance and international cooperation.

The approach is not romantic decentralisation: governments must still protect rights,
coordinate basins and landscapes, fund monitoring and correct harmful incentives. The
Convention combines ecology with rural development because land recovery cannot endure
when the people managing the land are excluded or economically undermined.

### 1. Conference of the Parties

The COP is the supreme decision-making body. Parties negotiate and adopt decisions,
strategies, budgets and institutional mandates. Ordinary sessions are generally biennial.
The COP does not directly build a watershed or manage a village common; it creates the
collective rules and direction under which national implementation is reviewed.

### 2. Committee on Science and Technology

The **CST** advises on scientific and technological matters relevant to desertification,
land degradation and drought. Its task is not political compliance review. It helps connect
evidence, methods and policy, including through the Science-Policy Interface.

Close-option trap:

```text
CST  = what does science say and how can knowledge improve action?
CRIC = what has been implemented, reported and learned?
```

### 3. Committee for the Review of the Implementation of the Convention

The **CRIC** assists the COP by examining implementation and national reporting, identifying
barriers and recommending improvements. The Performance Review and Assessment of
Implementation System, **PRAIS**, supports reporting.

CRIC is not an international environmental court. It reviews collective and national
implementation evidence; it does not issue criminal penalties for a degraded field.

### 4. Secretariat and Global Mechanism

The Secretariat administers and services the Convention process. The **Global Mechanism**
supports resource mobilisation, investment strategies and partnerships. It is a financial
facilitator, not a global fund that automatically pays every restoration project.

### 5. National Action Programmes are the implementation bridge

Article 10 places National Action Programmes at the centre of affected-country
implementation. A credible NAP should:

- analyse causes and affected areas;
- identify long-term strategies and priorities;
- integrate prevention, drought preparedness and restoration;
- clarify responsibilities;
- use participation and local knowledge;
- link measures to budgets, sectors and monitoring;
- remain capable of revision as conditions change.

### 6. Bottom-up does not mean fragmented

Local design is necessary, but degradation crosses holdings and administrative boundaries.
Watersheds, grazing routes, aquifers and dust sources require coordination at landscape,
State and sometimes international scales. The principle is subsidiarity: decide locally
where possible, coordinate higher where necessary.

### 7. Reporting is useful only if it changes decisions

Reporting can reveal trends, finance gaps and implementation barriers. It becomes ritualistic
when countries list projects without connecting them to outcomes. Good reporting separates:

- money allocated, released and spent;
- area enrolled, treated and maintained;
- outputs such as structures or seedlings;
- outcomes such as infiltration, soil carbon, native cover and stable livelihoods;
- distribution across communities and genders.

### 8. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Multiple bodies create bureaucracy without field impact.

**Reply:** Scientific advice, implementation review and finance require different expertise.
The real test is whether their outputs converge in national and local decisions. Duplication,
weak data and project-list reporting remain valid concerns.

### UPSC integration

- COP decides; CST advises scientifically; CRIC reviews implementation.
- Global Mechanism mobilises resources; it is not the Convention Secretariat.
- NAPs translate treaty direction into country strategy.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess whether the UNCCD's institutional architecture is capable of converting global commitments into local land recovery.

The UNCCD divides functions sensibly. The COP supplies political direction and adopts

implementation and reporting. The Secretariat services the process, and the Global

architecture into country priorities.

This design can connect scales. Common indicators and reporting permit comparison; science
can improve diagnosis; finance can support implementation; and participatory NAPs can adapt
measures to watersheds, rangelands and livelihoods. The regional annexes also recognise
different vulnerability and capacity.

Capability does not guarantee delivery. National mandates are often divided among
environment, agriculture, water, forests and rural development departments. Project
reporting may count hectares or structures without measuring ecological function.
Voluntary targets and uneven finance can weaken accountability, while local users may be
consulted after land-use decisions are effectively settled.

The architecture becomes effective when national budgets and land-use planning use the
same outcome framework: avoided degradation, soil and water recovery, secure tenure,
livelihood resilience and repeated monitoring. CRIC review should expose implementation
gaps; CST guidance should improve methods; and the Global Mechanism should help convert
bankable projects into locally legitimate action. The institutional design is adequate,
but convergence and outcome accountability determine whether it reaches the land.

Progress: 9 / 15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: LDN definition, baseline, target and response hierarchy

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: neutrality starts before restoration

```text
BASELINE LAND-BASED NATURAL CAPITAL
                |
                | future land-use decisions
                v
     +---------------------------+
     | 1. AVOID new degradation  |  highest priority
     | 2. REDUCE ongoing decline |
     | 3. REVERSE past decline   |
     +---------------------------+
                |
                v
anticipated losses <---- counterbalance ----> planned gains
                |
                v
amount and quality needed for ecosystem functions/services
remain stable or increase at the chosen scale and timeframe
```

### 1. The operational definition

UNCCD defines **Land Degradation Neutrality** as a state in which the amount and quality of
land resources necessary to support ecosystem functions and services and enhance food
security remain stable or increase within specified temporal and spatial scales and
ecosystems.

Neutrality is therefore not "no activity" and not "every plot returns to pristine nature."
It is a planning and accounting objective for land-based natural capital.

LDN is also the organising concept in **Sustainable Development Goal target 15.3**, which
calls for combating desertification, restoring degraded land and soil, and striving to
achieve a land-degradation-neutral world by 2030. SDG linkage does not turn each country's
voluntary LDN target into one uniform treaty-imposed hectare quota.

### 2. The baseline becomes the minimum target

The LDN principle states that the target equals the baseline. If implementation begins with
a given condition of land-based natural capital, the minimum objective is not to end below
that condition at the target date. Countries may adopt a more ambitious net-gain target.

The baseline is not a moral declaration that existing degradation is acceptable. It is the
reference against which subsequent losses and gains are evaluated.

### 3. Avoid–reduce–reverse is a hierarchy, not a menu

Avoiding degradation usually protects intact function more cheaply and reliably than
recreating it. Reducing ongoing decline comes next. Reversing past degradation is essential
but often uncertain, slow and incomplete.

```text
intact native rangeland threatened by conversion
        |
        +--> preferred: avoid conversion
        +--> weaker: reduce damage after conversion begins
        `--> riskiest: destroy, then promise restoration elsewhere
```

This hierarchy prevents LDN from becoming an unrestricted offset licence.

### 4. Counterbalancing is prospective planning

LDN asks planners to anticipate likely losses from land-use decisions and plan gains within
the same timeframe. Counterbalancing should occur, as far as possible, **like for like**
within the same ecosystem-based land type. It should operate at the scale at which land-use
decisions are made.

A wetland gain cannot casually compensate a grassland loss; a plantation cannot
automatically replace a native rangeland. Crossing land types risks altering ecosystem
composition and hiding irreplaceable loss.

### 5. Neutrality does not erase site-level damage

National or subnational balance can coexist with severe harm at a particular site. This is
why LDN principles require human-rights protection, participation, tenure safeguards,
multi-variable assessment and attention to endangered ecosystems.

### 6. Voluntary national target, common discipline

Countries set national targets according to circumstances and sovereignty. Common
principles, indicators and reporting provide comparability. This resembles nationally
determined architecture, but LDN is not an NDC and the UNCCD is not the Paris Agreement.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** "No net loss" legitimises destructive development by allowing restoration
elsewhere.

**Reply:** The official hierarchy prioritises avoidance, and like-for-like,
rights-sensitive counterbalancing constrains substitution. The objection remains powerful
where governments treat gains as certain, ignore time lags or accept non-equivalent
ecosystems.

- Put avoid before reduce and reverse.
- State baseline, timeframe, scale and land type.

3. Temporal and spatial scales must be specified.

5. Countries may pursue net gain beyond neutrality.
6. Avoid has priority over reduce and reverse.
7. Prevention is generally cheaper and more reliable than repair.
8. Anticipated losses may be counterbalanced by planned gains.
9. Counterbalancing should normally be like for like.
10. Balance at national scale does not erase local harm.
11. LDN targets are nationally set within a common framework.

### Concept check

**Question:** A plan converts intact native grassland and promises an equal area of tree plantation elsewhere. Why is equal area insufficient for LDN?

**Model answer:** LDN concerns quality and function, not hectares alone. The land types are not like for like; grassland biodiversity, soil processes, water use and pastoral access may not be replaced. Avoidance and site-specific safeguards must come first.

**Misconception to avoid:** One restored hectare does not automatically compensate one degraded hectare.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically evaluate the neutrality and counterbalancing logic of Land Degradation Neutrality.

#### Model response (199 words)

Land Degradation Neutrality aims to keep stable or increase the amount and quality of land
resources needed for ecosystem functions, services and food security within defined scales
and timeframes. Its practical strength is to place future land-use decisions on a
balance sheet: the baseline becomes the minimum target, anticipated losses are identified,
and planned gains are built into land-use planning.

The framework is more than offsetting. Its response hierarchy requires governments first to
avoid new degradation, then reduce ongoing decline, and only then reverse past damage.
Counterbalancing should occur, as far as possible, within the same ecosystem-based land
type and planning scale. Common land-cover, productivity and soil-carbon indicators create
a minimum monitoring discipline.

However, neutrality can conceal site-level injustice and ecological non-equivalence.
Restoration gains are uncertain and delayed, while destruction is immediate. A plantation
cannot automatically replace native grassland, wetland or culturally important common land.
National balance can also hide concentrated losses borne by pastoralists, tenants or women.

LDN is therefore defensible only with strict avoidance, conservative accounting,
like-for-like rules, tenure protection, participation and long-term outcome monitoring.
Used this way, neutrality disciplines development planning. Used as hectare-for-hectare
permission to degrade, it reproduces the ecological-commensurability problem familiar from
compensatory afforestation.

## Lesson 10 — How LDN is counted without pretending measurement is perfect

Progress: 10 / 15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Indicators, one-out-all-out rule, gains, losses and safeguards

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: LDN indicator and accounting framework queried
CA search: "official LDN indicators one out all out land cover productivity soil organic carbon"
CA found: No separate current-affairs linkage; accounting rules are the lesson's static core
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the minimum indicator dashboard

```text
LAND UNIT
   |
   +--> LAND COVER CHANGE
   |       conversion and ecosystem structure signal
   |
   +--> LAND PRODUCTIVITY
   |       Net Primary Production / trajectory signal
   |
   `--> SOIL ORGANIC CARBON
           soil-health and carbon-stock signal

INTEGRATION
negative change in any one indicator --> LOSS
positive change in at least one + no negative change --> GAIN
no significant change --> STABLE
```

### 1. Why three indicators

Land cover detects conversion, productivity detects performance over time, and soil organic
carbon represents a crucial stock linked to soil health. No one indicator is sufficient.
A field may remain cropland while productivity and soil carbon decline. A plantation may
increase cover while damaging water or native biodiversity.

### 2. The one-out-all-out rule is precautionary

Under the LDN principles, significant negative change in any one of the three minimum
indicators classifies the unit as a loss. A gain requires at least one significant positive
change and no significant negative change in the others.

This prevents a strong result in one metric from cancelling a warning in another. It does
not mean every tiny measurement fluctuation proves degradation; significance thresholds,
data quality and context remain necessary.

### 3. Minimum indicators are not maximum knowledge

UNCCD principles explicitly allow additional national and subnational quantitative and
qualitative indicators. Useful additions include:

- erosion and sediment;
- salinity, sodicity and compaction;
- groundwater and streamflow;
- species composition and habitat quality;
- yield stability and forage quality;
- tenure security and access;
- gendered labour and benefit distribution.

### 4. Gains and losses must share a timeframe

A planned degradation loss today cannot be balanced honestly by a vague restoration promise
with no completion date. Time lags matter because ecosystem services are unavailable during
the gap, and recovery may fail.

### 5. Leakage and burden shifting

Protecting one area can displace grazing, farming, fuel collection or mining to another.
LDN planning must examine the wider landscape and avoid shifting degradation across
districts, ecosystems, social groups or generations.

### 6. Uncertainty should change decisions

Monitoring contains classification error, cloud and sensor limitations, incomplete soil
data and uncertain baselines. A responsible framework:

1. documents uncertainty;
2. uses conservative assumptions;
3. validates locally;
4. chooses reversible actions where evidence is weak;
5. updates management as monitoring improves.

### 7. A worked accounting example

```text
100,000 ha planning domain

anticipated loss:
2,000 ha cropland with falling productivity

planned gains:
1,200 ha degraded cropland restored
1,000 ha native grassland converted to plantation

Arithmetic area gain = 2,200 ha
LDN conclusion = NOT automatic

Why?
- grassland-to-plantation may be non-equivalent
- soil carbon and productivity trends are unknown
- local access and water impacts are untested
- time lag and restoration failure risk remain
```

### 8. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** One-out-all-out overstates degradation and can be distorted by noisy data.

**Reply:** It is deliberately conservative and should be combined with significance tests,
additional indicators and local interpretation. The residual issue is the trade-off between
global comparability and locally meaningful ecological detail.

- Explain the integration rule accurately.
- Add like-for-like, timeframe, leakage and local validation.

3. Soil organic carbon is the carbon-stock proxy.

5. A gain needs a positive indicator and no negative indicator.

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the indicator and accounting safeguards required for credible Land Degradation Neutrality.

#### Model response (142 words)

Satellite trends require soil, water and biodiversity evidence plus local interpretation.
Tenure security, livelihood access and gendered benefit distribution should supplement the
biophysical dashboard.

Uncertainty must be disclosed and handled conservatively. Thus LDN credibility comes not
from balancing gross hectares but from comparable indicators, ecological equivalence,
rights protection and adaptive verification of lasting function.

### Visual: do not judge four interventions by one tree-count metric

```text
DEGRADED LAND
    |
    +--> RESTORATION
    |    recover ecological function, resilience and services;
    |    reference ecosystem guides direction
    |
    +--> REHABILITATION
    |    recover useful function/productivity;
    |    full original composition may not return
    |
    +--> RECLAMATION
    |    make severely altered land stable and usable again;
    |    common in mined, saline or physically transformed sites
    |
    `--> AFFORESTATION
         establish forest on land not recently forested;
         a land-cover intervention, not a synonym for all recovery
```

The words overlap in practice, but they signal different objectives. Every answer should
state the intended reference condition and the functions being recovered.

### 1. Restoration

FAO describes land restoration as halting degradation or rehabilitating degraded land
through sustainable land management across agricultural, forest, pastoral and natural
ecosystems. In ecological use, restoration aims toward recovery of ecosystem function,
resilience, services and, where feasible, native composition.

Restoration does not require freezing a landscape in one historical year. Climate change,
irreversible soil loss and livelihood needs may make exact replication impossible. A
reference ecosystem supplies direction, not a photocopy.

### 2. Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation recovers function or productivity without necessarily restoring the original
ecosystem in full. A degraded pasture may regain stable cover, forage and infiltration even
if species composition remains altered. It is a legitimate objective when full restoration
is infeasible, provided the reduced ambition is stated honestly.

### 3. Reclamation

Reclamation is commonly used where land has been drastically altered—mines, quarries,
saline land, spoil or unstable surfaces. The first task may be safety, contouring, drainage,
soil reconstruction and return to a viable use. Reclamation can be a step toward ecological
restoration, but usability alone does not prove native ecosystem recovery.

### 4. Afforestation

Afforestation establishes forest on land not recently under forest. It may reduce erosion,
supply products or store carbon where ecologically suitable. It can also damage natural
grasslands and rangelands, increase water demand or simplify habitat. Reforestation concerns
land that was forested more recently; neither term automatically guarantees restoration
quality.

### 5. The intervention ladder

| Condition | First-choice direction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| healthy ecosystem under threat | avoid degradation | protect native rangeland from inappropriate conversion |
| functioning but pressured land | sustainable management | grazing timing, cover retention, water budgeting |
| partly degraded land | rehabilitation/restoration | assisted natural regeneration, erosion control |
| severely transformed land | reclamation then restoration where feasible | mine-spoil reshaping, soil rebuilding |
| suitable non-forest land with a forest objective | afforestation | shelter or production forest after ecological screening |

### 6. Natural regeneration often beats planting

If seed sources, rootstocks and soil processes remain, removing pressure can permit natural
or assisted regeneration at lower cost and with better local adaptation. Planting is useful
where regeneration barriers are real. It should not become the default because it produces
visible targets and contracts.

### 7. Success indicators follow the objective

Tree survival is relevant to afforestation but insufficient for land restoration. A complete
scorecard can include:

- erosion and infiltration;
- soil carbon, salinity and structure;
- native vegetation composition;
- forage or crop productivity and stability;
- water balance;
- biodiversity;
- secure access and livelihood benefits;
- maintenance and persistence through drought.

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Fine distinctions delay urgent action.

**Reply:** Wrongly labelled action can destroy open ecosystems or exaggerate achievement.
Clear objectives speed appropriate design and measurement. The residual difficulty is that
mixed-use landscapes may legitimately pursue several objectives at once.

### UPSC integration

- Define each term by objective, not by activity.
- Afforestation is one possible intervention, not the umbrella category.
- Link success indicators to the stated recovery ambition.

1. Restoration seeks recovery of ecological function, resilience and services.
2. A reference ecosystem gives direction but exact historical replication may be impossible.
3. Rehabilitation restores useful function without necessarily restoring full original composition.
4. Reclamation stabilises and returns severely altered land to viable use.
5. Afforestation establishes forest on land not recently forested.
6. Reforestation and afforestation are not synonyms.
7. Natural grasslands can be harmed by indiscriminate tree planting.
8. Assisted natural regeneration can outperform planting where recovery potential remains.
9. Objective clarity determines the correct indicator.
10. Seedling or tree count is an output, not proof of restored land.
11. Mixed landscapes may combine ecological and livelihood objectives.
12. The avoid–reduce–reverse hierarchy precedes intervention choice.

### Concept check

**Question:** A mined site is reshaped, made physically stable and converted into a managed grass cover, but its former ecosystem is not re-established. Which term is safest?

**Model answer:** Reclamation is the safest primary term because the intervention returns severely altered land to stable use. It may also constitute rehabilitation if useful functions recover, but full ecological restoration has not been demonstrated.

**Misconception to avoid:** Any visible revegetation is not automatically ecological restoration.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish restoration, rehabilitation, reclamation and afforestation in land-degradation policy.

#### Model response (147 words)

reference ecosystem where feasible. Rehabilitation recovers useful function or productivity
without necessarily re-establishing the original species composition. Reclamation is

on land not recently forested and is therefore a specific land-cover intervention, not a

The distinctions affect design and measurement. Mine reclamation may prioritise slope
stability and safe drainage; pasture rehabilitation may prioritise forage, infiltration and
ground cover; ecological restoration adds native composition and resilience. Afforestation
is appropriate only after biome and water screening, because tree planting can damage
natural grasslands or pastoral access. Policy should state the reference condition,
ecological and livelihood objective, timeframe and success indicators rather than report
all interventions as hectares "restored."

### 1. A watershed is a hydrological unit

Water moves downslope regardless of field, village or departmental boundaries. Treating the
ridge reduces runoff before it concentrates. Drainage-line structures then handle remaining
flow. Starting only at the outlet can create storage while leaving erosion and rapid runoff
untreated upstream.

### 2. Structures need biological treatment

Bunds, trenches, check dams and farm ponds can slow or store water. Vegetation cover,
root systems, residue, soil organic matter and land-use rules preserve the gain. The
Department of Land Resources' WDC-PMKSY 2.0 explicitly shifts emphasis from predominantly
mechanical treatment toward biological measures, water productivity, crop diversification,
integrated farming systems and community empowerment.

### 3. Water harvesting must respect the basin balance

Capturing runoff can support recharge and protective irrigation. Too many structures or
uncoordinated pumping can reduce downstream flow or merely transfer extraction in time.
Hydrology, geology, environmental flows and cumulative use must guide design.

### 4. Agroforestry is an arrangement, not simply trees on farms

Agroforestry deliberately combines woody perennials with crops and/or livestock. Possible
benefits include shade, wind reduction, nutrient cycling, fodder, fuel, products and income
diversification. Design choices include species, spacing, rooting pattern, water demand,
tenure and harvest rights.

In drylands, sparse trees integrated with crops or pasture may be appropriate; dense
planting may compete for moisture. Locally adapted systems are safer than a universal
plantation model.

### 5. Crop alignment and soil cover

Dryland resilience improves when crop water demand, duration and sowing decisions match
rainfall and soil storage. Millets, pulses, mixed systems, contingency crops and
integrated livestock can spread risk. Crop labels alone are insufficient: markets, nutrition,
labour and farmer choice determine adoption.

Cover crops, mulch, residue retention and reduced disturbance protect soil, though residue
may also be valued as fodder. Policy must recognise this real trade-off rather than assume
residue is free.

### 6. Pasture and fodder are drought infrastructure

Pasture development, fodder banks and drought reserves can prevent distress grazing of
recovering land. Rotational or planned grazing can support recovery when timing, stocking
and mobility fit variable forage. Exclusion without an alternative access plan can simply
shift pressure elsewhere.

### 7. Maintenance and institutions

Watershed assets often fail after project closure because silt removal, distribution rules
and monitoring are underfunded. User institutions need clear rights, transparent benefit
sharing and resources for maintenance. Landless households require livelihood benefits
beyond gains captured by landowners.

### 8. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Watershed programmes manufacture recharge claims and favour upstream
landowners.

**Reply:** The risk is real when project boundaries ignore aquifers, downstream users and
landlessness. Participatory water budgets, cumulative hydrology, geo-tagged assets and
distributional audits can improve design. The residual problem is long-term outcome data.

3. Drainage-line structures cannot substitute for source-area cover.
4. WDC-PMKSY 2.0 emphasises water productivity and biological measures.
5. Cumulative water harvesting can affect downstream flow.
6. Agroforestry integrates trees with crops and/or livestock.
7. Species, density and water demand decide agroforestry suitability.
8. Crop alignment matches demand with local water and climate.
9. Residue retention competes with fodder needs in many drylands.
10. Pasture and fodder systems reduce drought-driven pressure.
11. Exclusion can cause leakage if access alternatives are absent.
12. Maintenance, water rules and benefit sharing determine persistence.

**Misconception to avoid:** A completed water structure is not the same as a functioning watershed.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain how integrated watershed management can advance both land restoration and drought resilience.

#### Model response (192 words)

Integrated watershed management treats rainfall, runoff, soil, vegetation and livelihoods
within a common hydrological unit. Its physical sequence begins at the ridge: soil cover,
contour barriers and controlled disturbance reduce runoff and erosion. Slope treatments and
vegetative strips improve infiltration, while drainage-line works stabilise gullies and
store or recharge water where geology permits.

Land restoration requires more than structures. Mulch, crop cover, organic inputs and
managed grazing rebuild soil function. Crop alignment and diversified farming reduce water
demand and income risk. Context-specific agroforestry can supply fodder, reduce wind and
diversify products, but dense or unsuitable trees may compete for scarce moisture.

Drought resilience follows because better infiltration and soil storage delay crop stress,
fodder systems reduce distress grazing and diversified livelihoods lower dependence on one
rainfall-sensitive activity. Forecasts and contingency plans can then trigger action before
assets are lost.

The approach has limits. Excessive harvesting can reduce downstream flow; pumping can erase
recharge; upstream landowners may capture benefits; and neglected structures silt up.
Participatory water budgets, aquifer-scale coordination, landless-household benefits,
transparent user rules and long-term ecological monitoring are therefore essential.
Watershed management succeeds when ridge-to-valley hydrology is matched by institution-to-
household equity.

### 1. Mobility can be sustainable management

Pastoral mobility tracks variable water and forage. It can prevent continuous pressure on
one patch and allow seasonal recovery. Sedentarisation is not automatically ecological
progress; where rainfall is highly variable, forced settlement can concentrate animals
around wells and villages.

Mobility still requires governance. Stocking, access, disease control, crop boundaries and
reserve areas need negotiated rules. The analytical choice is not "free grazing versus
fencing" but which institution fits ecological variability.

### 2. Commons are infrastructure

Common rangelands provide forage, migration corridors, drought reserves and social safety
nets. Their value may not appear in individual land titles or crop statistics. Fragmentation
by infrastructure, plantations or conversion can destroy connectivity even when total
hectares appear unchanged.

### 3. Secure tenure changes incentives

Tenure includes ownership, lease, customary use, seasonal passage and collective rights.
FAO identifies responsible tenure governance as an enabling condition for LDN because it
shapes access, management and willingness to invest.

Security does not require converting every common into individual private property.
Recognition and safeguarding of legitimate customary and communal rights may better fit
rangelands.

### 4. Participation must occur before allocation

Consulting users after a site has been assigned for plantation, mining or energy
infrastructure is not meaningful participation. Land users should help define baseline
condition, objectives, access rules, indicators and grievance mechanisms.

### 5. Gender is not an add-on

Women may manage small livestock, fodder, water, seeds and household nutrition while having
weaker title, credit, extension access or representation. A household-level benefit claim
can conceal unequal control.

The FAO-UNCCD tenure guide highlights:

- a gender-responsive approach;
- recognition, recording and safeguarding of legitimate tenure rights;
- participation and consultation, especially for vulnerable groups.

These are design requirements, not merely social safeguards after biophysical planning.

### 6. Community institutions can fail too

Local institutions may reproduce caste, class, gender or insider-outsider exclusion. Elite
capture and weak enforcement are possible. Participation should therefore include
representation rules, transparent records, appeal and external rights protection.

### 7. Restoration with livelihoods

Rangeland restoration can combine temporary resting, reseeding where justified, water-point
management, invasive-species control, fire planning, forage reserves and livestock-value-
chain support. What grows matters: a uniform green cover can still be poor forage or weak
habitat.

### 8. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Grazing is the principal cause of dryland degradation, so exclusion is safest.

**Reply:** Chronic pressure can degrade land, but well-governed mobility and timing can
match variable forage and maintain open ecosystems. Blanket exclusion may shift pressure
and eliminate livelihoods. Site-specific carrying capacity is dynamic, not one fixed number
for all seasons.

### UPSC integration

- Present mobility as an adaptation institution, not a backward practice.
- Treat commons and corridors as functional landscape elements.
- Connect tenure and gender directly to ecological outcomes.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can fencing a degraded common produce improvement inside the fence but worsen landscape degradation?

**Model answer:** Exclusion may shift livestock to neighbouring areas, block seasonal mobility and remove access to drought reserves. Without negotiated alternatives, local improvement can create leakage and livelihood stress elsewhere.

**Misconception to avoid:** Site-level recovery automatically proves landscape-level and social success.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the role of tenure security, pastoral mobility and gender-responsive institutions in dryland restoration.

#### Model response (197 words)

Dryland restoration is governed as much by access as by rainfall. Pastoral mobility allows

When routes and commons are fenced or converted, grazing can concentrate around settlements

assuming household equality.

transparent records, representation and appeal. Ecologically, success should be measured
through forage quality, soil condition, mobility and resilience—not fencing or greenness
alone. Restoration endures when variable landscapes retain negotiated movement, secure
rights and equitable authority.

- participatory landscape visioning;

WDC-PMKSY seeks to improve the productive potential of rainfed and degraded land through

### 1. The economics of prevention

Restoration cost rises as topsoil, seed sources, hydrology and institutions are lost.
Avoidance preserves existing natural capital and avoids time lags, failure risk and
off-site damage. Economic appraisal should therefore compare restoration not only with
"doing nothing" but with earlier prevention.

### 2. Cost-effectiveness is process-specific

Cost per hectare is a weak comparison when interventions deliver different functions.
Useful measures include:

- cost per tonne of soil retained or carbon durably added;
- cost per unit of additional reliable water or reduced sediment;
- change in yield or forage stability, not only average output;
- biodiversity and connectivity gains;
- livelihood benefits and distribution;
- maintenance and reversal-adjusted cost.

Cheap planting can be expensive if mortality is high or a grassland is damaged.

### 3. Finance must include stewardship

Capital budgets favour visible works; ecological recovery needs maintenance, monitoring and
adaptive management over years. Payments or incentives should reward verified function and
community stewardship without commodifying every relationship or dispossessing users.

Private finance can help, but revenue claims based on carbon or biodiversity need additional
safeguards against weak baselines, reversal, double claims and unequal contracts.

⚠️ **Comparative finance assessment:** the UNCCD has historically received less political
visibility and dedicated international finance than the UNFCCC and, to a lesser extent, the
CBD. Slow-onset, spatially diffuse land decline is often misframed as a local agricultural
problem even though it affects food, water, biodiversity, carbon storage and displacement.
This is an implementation constraint, not a legal ranking among the Rio Conventions.

### 4. Monitoring architecture

```text
satellite: coverage, trend, conversion, spatial targeting
field: soil carbon, salinity, infiltration, species and erosion
hydrology: groundwater, streamflow, storage and water quality
social: tenure, access, labour, income stability and grievances
audit: de-duplication, counterfactual, permanence and distribution
```

No layer substitutes for the others. Open data enable scrutiny, but privacy and community
consent matter for household and tenure information.

### 5. Exactly one current-affairs linkage — UNCCD COP17

UNCCD COP17 met in **Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia, from 17 to 28 August 2026**, bringing together the
Convention's **197 Parties** against the background of UNCCD's estimate that **up to 40% of
the world's land is degraded**. The drought negotiation inherited unfinished work from COP16
in Riyadh, which had advanced the global drought discussion and launched the Riyadh Global
Drought Resilience Partnership without creating a binding drought treaty. Two COP17 outcomes
sharpen this topic.

The official UNCCD Finance Day background restated the Financial Needs Assessment launched at
COP16: approximately **USD 355 billion is required every year from 2025 to 2030** to meet
global land-restoration targets and address land degradation, desertification and drought.
Estimated annual investment is about **USD 77 billion**, leaving an annual gap of about
**USD 278 billion**. These are rounded global annual estimates, not cumulative six-year
totals, COP17 pledges or verified disbursements.

First, the drought decision continued discussions to COP18 with a view to a decision on
proactive drought management. It made drought a stand-alone agenda item at COP18 and each
ordinary COP thereafter, and requested continued support and consultations by the
Secretariat and Global Mechanism. Therefore COP17 did **not** create a completed binding
global drought protocol.

Second, the rangelands decision recognised pastoralists as stewards, pastoral mobility as a
resilience strategy, the value of collectively managed pastoral commons and the importance
of women, youth, Indigenous Peoples and local communities. The Rangelands Flagship
Initiative organised work around knowledge, investment and institutions. At its launch, FAO
Deputy Director-General Beth Bechdol stated in FAO's official COP17 report: "Restoration
cannot simply mean making degraded land green again. What grows matters." She added that the
diversity and quality of grasses, forage and other vegetation matter for livestock,
biodiversity and long-term ecosystem productivity and health.

The decision coincided with the **2026 International Year of Rangelands and Pastoralists**.
FAO records that rangelands cover about half of Earth's land surface, pastoralism supports
about **500 million livelihoods**, and rangelands and pastoralists contribute to the nutrition
of up to **one-sixth of humanity**. These figures explain the issue's scale; they do not prove
that every grazing system is sustainable.

**Exam use:** COP17 confirms a shift from plantation-centred restoration toward proactive
drought governance, rangeland quality, mobility and institutional rights. It does not remove
the need to verify national implementation.

### 6. A reform package for India

1. **Prevention screen:** require an avoid-degradation analysis before approving offsets or
   restoration compensation.
2. **Biome-fit classification:** recognise grasslands, scrub and rangelands as ecosystems,
   not default "wasteland."
3. **Unified spatial ledger:** map target, activity, finance, overlap, baseline and outcome.
4. **Outcome contracts:** fund soil, water, biodiversity and livelihood results plus
   maintenance, not only first-year works.
5. **Tenure-first due diligence:** record customary, communal and women's legitimate rights.
6. **Watershed and aquifer budgets:** evaluate cumulative upstream-downstream effects.
7. **Independent sampling:** audit reported gains through field and social evidence.
8. **Adaptive release:** continue finance only when monitoring supports correction.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A multidimensional system is too expensive and slow for national scale.

**Reply:** Tiered monitoring can use satellite screening for all areas and intensive field
audits for samples and high-risk sites. The cost should be compared with failed projects,
double counting and irreversible conversion. The residual trade-off is that perfect
measurement is impossible; decisions must remain conservative and revisable.

### Original Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Design an accountable land-restoration framework for India using prevention economics, remote sensing and tenure safeguards.

#### Model response (220 words)

An accountable framework should begin with the LDN hierarchy: avoid new degradation,
reduce ongoing decline and reverse past damage. Project appraisal must compare restoration
with the cheaper option of preserving intact soil, hydrology and native ecosystems.

A national spatial ledger should assign every claimed hectare a land type, dated baseline,
intervention, funding source and outcome record. ISRO-SAC mapping and platforms such as
VEDAS can screen conversion and productivity trends. Field samples should verify soil
carbon, salinity, infiltration, erosion and species composition; hydrological monitoring
should test groundwater and downstream effects. Social audits should record customary and
communal access, women's rights, labour, benefits and grievances.

Intervention standards must be biome-specific. Native grasslands and rangelands should not
be treated as afforestation blanks. Watershed projects need cumulative water budgets;
agroforestry needs species and moisture screening; reclamation of mines requires stability
before deeper restoration. Funding should cover maintenance and be released against
verified function rather than asset creation alone.

Remote sensing can monitor all sites, while independent intensive audits target ecological
risk and statistical samples. Public dashboards should disclose overlap, uncertainty and
reversal. Grievance and adaptive-management mechanisms should permit correction.

Such a system is more demanding than hectare reporting, but it reduces double counting,
failed planting and dispossession. India's restoration ambition becomes credible when
prevention, ecological evidence and legitimate tenure govern the same land-use decision.

**Question:** When can a land unit count as a gain under the minimum indicator rule?

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why is the avoid–reduce–reverse hierarchy superior to a restoration-first
approach to land degradation?

**Model answer:** The avoid–reduce–reverse hierarchy recognises that intact ecological
function is easier and cheaper to conserve than to recreate. Avoidance protects soil depth,
hydrology, native biodiversity, carbon and existing livelihood relations without a
restoration time lag. Reduction then limits ongoing pressures through sustainable land,
water and grazing management. Reversal addresses damage that has already occurred through
rehabilitation, reclamation or ecological restoration.

A restoration-first approach can legitimise preventable loss and assume that ecosystems
are interchangeable. A plantation elsewhere may not replace native grassland, soil
structure, groundwater function or customary access lost at the original site. Restoration
also carries failure and reversal risks.

The hierarchy does not reject restoration; it places it after prevention and minimisation.
Applied through land-use planning, like-for-like safeguards, tenure protection and outcome
monitoring, it reduces ecological cost while preserving restoration for genuinely degraded
land.

**Quantified rubric (10):** prevention rationale 3; reduce/reverse sequence 2;
non-equivalence and risk 3; safeguards and verdict 2.

## 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** "India's land-degradation challenge is spatially diverse but institutionally
fragmented." Analyse.

**Model answer:** India's degradation is not one expanding desert. The ISRO-SAC atlas for
2018–19 mapped 97.85 million hectares, or 29.77% of national area, under
desertification/land degradation. Water erosion affected 11.01% of total geographical area,
vegetation degradation 9.15% and wind erosion 5.46%. Wind dominates the arid region, while
water erosion and vegetation decline are prominent in semi-arid and dry sub-humid zones.
Salinity, waterlogging, mining and direct conversion add local pathways.

Institutions divide the same landscape among environment, forests, agriculture, rural
development, water, mining and local government. WDC-PMKSY, Green India Mission, CAMPA,
agroforestry and soil programmes therefore use different boundaries, budgets and metrics.
Programme hectares can overlap, while asset creation or planting may be reported without
soil, water, biodiversity or livelihood outcomes.

Reform requires convergence without arithmetic aggregation. A unified spatial ledger should
link each intervention to land type, baseline, process, funding and repeated outcomes.
Watershed and aquifer planning should cross administrative boundaries. Open Natural
Ecosystems need biome-fit standards, while legitimate community, pastoral and women's
tenure must shape design. ISRO-SAC screening should be combined with field and social
audits. India's challenge is spatially differentiated; governance must become equally
place-specific while remaining nationally accountable.

**Quantified rubric (15):** spatial evidence 4; process diversity 3; institutional
fragmentation 3; integrated reforms 4; synthesis 1.

## 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Critically examine Land Degradation Neutrality as a framework for reconciling
development, ecosystem recovery and livelihood justice.

**Model answer:** LDN seeks to keep stable or increase the amount and quality of land
resources needed for ecosystem functions, services and food security within defined scales
and timeframes. It reconciles development and conservation by making the baseline a minimum
target and requiring anticipated losses to be counterbalanced by planned gains.

Its strongest safeguard is the response hierarchy: avoid new degradation, reduce ongoing
decline and reverse past damage. The three minimum indicators—land cover, productivity and
soil organic carbon—create a common monitoring base, while the one-out-all-out rule prevents
one improvement from masking another significant loss.

Yet neutrality can become an offset fiction. Restoration is delayed and uncertain; equal
area is not equal function; and national balance can hide destruction of irreplaceable
grasslands, wetlands or cultural commons. Hectare accounting can also obscure displacement
of pastoralists, insecure tenant rights and women's unequal control of benefits.

LDN is credible only when counterbalancing is like for like, ecosystem conversion is first
tested against avoidance, and additional indicators cover water, biodiversity and social
outcomes. Participatory land-use planning, legitimate tenure protection, conservative
baselines, leakage checks and long-term audits are essential. Properly governed, LDN
disciplines development decisions; reduced to gross area offsets, it can legitimise the
degradation it was designed to prevent.

**Quantified rubric (20):** concept and mechanism 5; indicators and hierarchy 4;
ecological critique 4; livelihood-justice critique 3; safeguards and verdict 4.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Definition and boundary card

- **Land:** soil + vegetation + biota + ecological and hydrological processes.
- **Drylands:** arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas; P/PET ratio 0.05–0.65;
  polar and sub-polar regions excluded.
- **Desertification:** land degradation in drylands from climatic variations and human
  activities.
- **Drought:** below-normal precipitation causing serious hydrological imbalance.
- **Trap:** natural desert ≠ degraded land; drought episode ≠ proven desertification.
- **2020 PYQ qualification:** degrading processes cross popular climatic stereotypes, but
  the strict UNCCD definition remains dryland-specific.

## Driver–process–indicator–impact spine

```text
pressure
climate variability / cover removal / grazing concentration / poor irrigation drainage
    ↓
process
erosion / compaction / salinity / waterlogging / vegetation and SOC decline
    ↓
indicator
cover / productivity / SOC / runoff / salts / groundwater / species
    ↓
impact
yield-forage instability / sediment / biodiversity loss / livelihood stress
```

- Diagnose from process upward to land use, incentive, tenure and climate.
- Multi-year trends and ground truth are essential.
- Thresholds can make recovery path-dependent.

## Soil, water and vegetation recall

- Water erosion: splash → sheet → rill → gully.
- Wind transport: suspension, saltation, surface creep.
- Salinity: soluble salts create osmotic/ion stress.
- Sodicity: exchangeable sodium disperses clay and reduces infiltration.
- Waterlogging: saturated root zone creates oxygen stress.
- Organic matter supports aggregation, water retention, biology and nutrient cycling.
- Vegetation protects through canopy, roots, litter and surface roughness.
- Composition and forage quality matter; greenness alone is weak proof.

## Drought-resilience card

- Meteorological, agricultural, hydrological and ecological drought are distinct.
- Relief protects life and assets after impact; resilience lowers prior vulnerability.
- Core tools: soil cover, infiltration, water budgets, crop/livelihood diversification,
  fodder reserves, contingency plans, social protection and pastoral mobility.
- Restoration cannot eliminate climate hazard or create unlimited water.

## UNCCD institutional card

- Adopted **1994**; entered into force **1996**; one of three Rio Conventions.
- Objective: combat desertification and mitigate drought effects through long-term,
  integrated action and cooperation.
- Regional annexes: Africa, Asia, Latin America and Caribbean, Northern Mediterranean,
  Central and Eastern Europe.
- **COP:** decisions and strategic direction.
- **CST:** science and technology advice.
- **CRIC:** implementation and reporting review.
- **Secretariat:** services Convention process.
- **Global Mechanism:** finance and investment mobilisation.
- **NAP:** national bridge from treaty to implementation.

## LDN one-page recall

- Definition: land-resource quantity and quality needed for functions, services and food
  security remain stable or increase within specified scales and ecosystems.
- SDG target **15.3** calls for restoration and striving for an LDN world by 2030.
- Baseline = minimum target; net gain may be more ambitious.
- Hierarchy: **AVOID → REDUCE → REVERSE**.
- Counterbalance anticipated losses with gains over the same timeframe.
- Prefer **like for like** within the same ecosystem-based land type.
- Minimum indicators: land cover, productivity, soil organic carbon.
- One-out-all-out: any significant negative indicator = loss.
- Gain: at least one positive and none negative.
- Add water, erosion, salinity, biodiversity, tenure, gender and livelihood indicators.
- National balance does not erase site-level harm.

## Recovery vocabulary

- **Restoration:** recover ecological function, resilience and services.
- **Rehabilitation:** recover useful function without necessarily restoring full original
  composition.
- **Reclamation:** stabilise and return severely altered land to viable use.
- **Afforestation:** establish forest on land not recently forested.
- Natural regeneration may be preferable where recovery capacity persists.
- Never afforest native open ecosystems merely because they lack trees.

## Watershed and agroforestry card

- Ridge → slope → drainage line → farm/pasture → institution.
- Pair structures with cover, roots, organic matter and maintenance.
- Use cumulative watershed/aquifer water budgets.
- Agroforestry = planned tree–crop and/or tree–livestock integration.
- Species, density, roots, water demand and tenure decide suitability.
- Residue has both soil-cover and fodder value.
- Asset creation ≠ functioning watershed.

## Pastoral, tenure and gender card

- Mobility can spread pressure and track variable forage.
- Commons provide corridors, reserves and insurance.
- Tenure includes customary, communal and seasonal rights.
- Security need not mean universal individual privatisation.
- Participation must precede allocation and objective setting.
- Gender-responsive planning asks who owns, decides, labours and controls benefits.
- Local institutions need transparency, representation and appeal.

- COP14, 2019: ambition raised to **26 Mha by 2030**.
- 2023 NAP: convergence, landscape and micro-ecosystem planning, prevention and remedial
  models, forestry interventions, capacity and monitoring.
- WDC-PMKSY 2.0: rainfed/degraded land, water productivity, biological treatment, crop

- Do not add overlapping programme figures.
- "Under restoration efforts" ≠ independently verified ecological recovery.

## COP17 single-linkage card

- Ulaanbaatar, 17–28 August 2026.
- 197 Parties; UNCCD frames up to 40% of the world's land as degraded.
- COP16 Riyadh advanced drought negotiations and launched the Riyadh Global Drought
  Resilience Partnership but did not complete a binding drought treaty.
- Drought talks continued to COP18; drought becomes a stand-alone item at COP18 and later
  ordinary COPs; no completed binding global drought protocol.
- Rangelands decision recognised pastoral stewardship, mobility, collective commons and
  participation of women, youth, Indigenous Peoples and local communities.
- 2026 IYRP context: rangelands cover about half of global land; pastoralism supports around
  500 million livelihoods; rangelands and pastoralists contribute to nutrition for up to
  one-sixth of humanity.
- Rangelands Flagship Initiative: knowledge + investment + institutions.
- Key line: restoration quality and forage diversity matter, not cosmetic greening.
- UNCCD finance assessment: about **USD 355 billion needed annually in 2025–2030**,

- Treat all three as rounded global annual estimates, not cumulative totals, COP17 pledges or
  verified disbursements.

## Qualified conclusion

Desertification is not a moving line of sand but a decline in the capacity of dryland
systems to sustain ecological functions and human life. The UNCCD and LDN provide a useful
architecture only when prevention outranks compensation, indicators are interpreted rather
than worshipped, and restoration protects the rights and knowledge of the people who manage
variable landscapes. India's scientific and programme capacity is substantial; credibility
now depends on converting overlapping activity figures into durable, biome-fit and publicly
verified land outcomes.
