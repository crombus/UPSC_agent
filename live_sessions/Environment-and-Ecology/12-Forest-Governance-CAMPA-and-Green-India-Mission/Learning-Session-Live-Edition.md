### Visual first: three different questions

```text
DEVELOPMENT PROPOSAL
        |
        v
Will forest land be used for a non-forest purpose?
        |
        +---- YES ----> DIVERSION APPROVAL
                          |
                          v
                   COMPENSATORY LEVIES
                   (CA cost + NPV + other sums)

                    CAF / CAMPA system

What must be planted because land was diverted? ---> COMPENSATORY AFFORESTATION

Still different question:
Has the damaged ecosystem recovered its native
composition, structure, soil, water and functions? ---> ECOLOGICAL RESTORATION
```

The first asks for legal permission. The second asks for a compensatory activity. The
third asks whether ecological recovery actually occurred. Treating them as one event is
the source of most weak answers.

### 1. Begin with the practical problem

A road, mine, transmission line or other project may seek forest land. If diversion is
approved under the forest-conservation framework, the project proponent - the **user
agency** - bears specified compensatory costs. Those payments do not mean that the
diverted forest has been recreated. They finance measures intended to mitigate part of
the loss.

**Compensatory afforestation (CA)** is plantation raised because forest land has been
diverted. **Forest restoration** is the wider ecological process of recovering a
degraded ecosystem's functions, composition and resilience. A plantation can be an
input into restoration, but plantation and restoration are not identical.

### 2. Diversion versus afforestation

| Dimension | Forest diversion | Compensatory afforestation |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate act | Forest land is approved for non-forest use | Plantation is raised on identified land |
| Principal actor | User agency seeks approval | State forest machinery usually implements works |
| Financial consequence | User agency pays prescribed levies | Approved funds finance plantation and associated works |
| Ecological direction | Existing forest is lost or fragmented | New vegetation is established elsewhere or on degraded land |
| Basic metric | Area and conditions of approved diversion | Area treated, seedlings planted and survival |

The location matters. A mature forest lost at one site may regulate a particular
watershed, connect wildlife habitat and sustain local livelihoods. Planting the same
number of hectares elsewhere does not automatically replace those place-specific
functions.

### 3. Compensation is mitigation, not a licence to ignore avoidance

Sound environmental decision-making follows a sequence:

```text
AVOID unnecessary forest loss
        ->
MINIMISE unavoidable impact
        ->
RESTORE the affected site where feasible
        ->
COMPENSATE residual loss
```

CAMPA mainly operates at the compensation end. Therefore, a large fund corpus cannot
prove that the prior questions - whether diversion should occur and whether impact was
minimised - were answered well.

### 4. The core distinction for UPSC

The exam-ready line is:

> CAMPA is a diversion-linked statutory financing and implementation architecture;
> compensatory afforestation is an activity financed through that architecture; and
> ecological restoration is the outcome standard by which the activity should be judged.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** If ecological equivalence is impossible, compensation is meaningless.

**Reply:** Restoration finance, soil treatment, wildlife work and regeneration can still
reduce residual damage and create public ecological assets.

**Residual:** The mechanism remains defensible only when it does not disguise avoidable
diversion and when outputs are tested against long-term ecological outcomes.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims trap:** CAMPA and Green India Mission are not the same programme.
- **Mains use:** open with the avoidance-minimisation-restoration-compensation hierarchy.
- **Answer discipline:** never use “fund collected” as evidence of “forest restored”.

### Revision notes

1. Diversion is a legal land-use decision.
2. Compensatory afforestation is a diversion-linked plantation obligation.
3. Ecological restoration concerns recovery of ecosystem functions and resilience.
4. A user agency pays compensatory levies after diversion conditions are imposed.
5. CAMPA manages and plans the compensatory fund architecture.
6. Plantation is an output; restoration is an outcome.
7. Location-specific forest services cannot be assumed transferable.
8. Compensation follows, and cannot replace, avoidance and minimisation.
9. Fund accumulation does not prove ecological success.
10. CAMPA and GIM are related but legally and operationally distinct.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a compensatory plantation satisfy an administrative condition without proving ecological restoration?

**Model answer:** The condition may be satisfied when the required land, expenditure and plantation work are recorded. Ecological restoration requires additional evidence of native composition, survival, soil and hydrological recovery, habitat value and sustained community-compatible management. Administrative completion is therefore an output, not proof of outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that equal hectares mean equal ecosystems; area equivalence does not establish functional or locational equivalence.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 1

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish forest diversion, compensatory
afforestation and ecological restoration. Why does the distinction matter for forest
governance?

**Model response:** Forest diversion is the legally approved conversion of forest land to a
non-forest use. Compensatory afforestation is the plantation obligation created to
mitigate such diversion, financed by the user agency through the compensatory-fund
system. Ecological restoration is a broader outcome: recovery of native species,
structure, soil, hydrology, habitat and ecosystem services.

The distinction prevents three errors. First, approval does not establish ecological
acceptability; avoidance and minimisation must precede compensation. Second, plantation
area or expenditure is only an output and cannot prove ecosystem recovery. Third,
off-site planting cannot automatically replace place-specific watershed, biodiversity
and livelihood functions of a mature forest. CAMPA is therefore best viewed as a
necessary mitigation-finance mechanism whose legitimacy depends on ecological quality,
rights due diligence and long-term outcome monitoring, not as a licence for diversion.

**Unique rubric (10):** definitions 3; governance sequence 2; output-outcome distinction
2; ecological/place qualification 2; concise verdict 1.

### 1. What creates the liability?

The liability arises because forest land is permitted to be used for a non-forest
purpose. The **Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016** does not itself grant every
diversion. It creates the fund-management architecture for money collected under the
forest-clearance and judicial-policy framework.

The user agency commonly bears:

- the cost of raising and maintaining compensatory afforestation;
- **Net Present Value (NPV)** for ecosystem services foregone through diversion;
- other project-specific sums, where applicable, such as money connected with catchment
  treatment, wildlife management or additional compensatory measures.

### 2. What NPV means - and does not mean

NPV converts part of the anticipated loss of forest ecosystem services into a present
monetary liability. It makes the user agency internalise costs that a project balance
sheet might otherwise ignore.

```text
FOREST SERVICES
biodiversity + water regulation + soil protection + carbon + local provisioning
                              |
                              v
                monetary valuation for liability
                              |
                              v
                             NPV
```

NPV is an economic valuation tool. It is not a declaration that biodiversity, sacred
value or irreversible habitat loss can be perfectly purchased. Money can finance
mitigation; it cannot make every ecological loss commensurable.

### 3. Where should compensatory afforestation occur?

The usual core principle is plantation on equivalent **non-forest land** transferred
and notified for forest purposes. Where suitable non-forest land is unavailable under
the applicable conditions, compensatory afforestation may be taken on **degraded forest
land**, commonly at twice the diverted area under the standard formulation.

This creates two distinct checks:

1. **Legal-administrative suitability:** Is the land identified, transferred, free from
   incompatible encumbrance and available for the required work?
2. **Ecological-social suitability:** Is plantation appropriate to the native biome,
   water regime, existing use and recognised or pending community rights?

### 4. Diversion area and afforestation area are not one map

A forest may be diverted in one district while compensatory land is identified
elsewhere. This spatial separation creates risks:

- the affected community may lose benefits while another location receives plantation;
- wildlife connectivity lost at the diversion site may remain unrepaired;
- degraded-forest land may already support open natural ecosystems or community use;
- monitoring may count hectares without testing whether the selected site can sustain
  the intended vegetation.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** NPV monetises nature and normalises destruction.

**Reply:** Requiring payment is preferable to externalising all ecological costs and can
fund necessary regeneration and protection.

**Residual:** Valuation must operate inside a prior decision about ecological limits.
Certain losses may require avoidance, rejection or much stronger safeguards rather than
a larger cheque.

### UPSC application

- Define NPV as a compensatory liability, not a market price proving replaceability.
- Trace the chain from diversion condition to user-agency payment.
- Distinguish non-forest CA land from degraded-forest fallback.
- Add site suitability and rights due diligence to a Mains answer.

### Revision notes

1. Diversion approval creates compensatory conditions.
2. The user agency, not the general public, is the source of compensatory levies.
3. CA cost and NPV are different components.
4. NPV values foregone ecosystem services for liability purposes.
5. NPV does not prove complete ecological commensurability.
6. Equivalent non-forest land is the ordinary CA site principle.
7. Degraded forest land may be used under applicable fallback conditions.
8. Administrative availability and ecological suitability are separate tests.
9. Off-site compensation can leave local ecological and livelihood loss unresolved.
10. The 2016 Act manages funds; it is not the whole diversion-approval law.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should NPV be described as a liability instrument rather than the “price of a forest”?

**Model answer:** NPV assigns a monetary burden for selected ecosystem services lost through diversion so that the user agency internalises part of the cost. It cannot fully represent irreversible biodiversity, cultural relationships, unique habitat or location-specific ecological functions; therefore it finances mitigation but does not establish complete substitutability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say that payment of NPV transfers ownership of every ecological value to the user agency.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 2

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain the compensatory-payment chain generated by
forest diversion and evaluate the limits of Net Present Value as an environmental
governance tool.

**Model response:** Diversion begins with a user agency seeking prior approval for using
forest land for a non-forest purpose. Approval conditions generate liabilities,
principally the cost of compensatory afforestation and Net Present Value for foregone
ecosystem services, along with project-specific mitigation sums where required. These
receipts enter the statutory compensatory-fund architecture and are spent through
approved annual plans on afforestation, regeneration, wildlife and related activities.

NPV improves governance by applying a polluter-pays logic: it brings water regulation,
soil protection, carbon and other forest services into project cost. It also provides a
dedicated restoration-finance stream.

Its limits are substantial. Monetary valuation cannot establish like-for-like
replacement of mature biodiversity, sacred landscapes or wildlife corridors. Off-site
planting may not benefit the people and watershed that bear diversion. Further, the
availability of money may create false confidence that every loss is compensable.

Hence NPV should sit within the mitigation hierarchy: avoid and minimise first, restore
where possible, and compensate only residual damage. Its success must be tested through
site suitability, rights safeguards and long-term ecological outcomes.

**Unique rubric (15):** payment chain 4; NPV purpose 3; three limits 4; mitigation
hierarchy 2; balanced conclusion 2.

The architecture must be read in layers. A statutory account holds money; an authority

Likewise, participation may be required by a rights law, mission design, project

### Post-attempt resolution

The Act establishes authority architecture at the national and State levels. It does
not, by itself, state a universal people's-participation mandate for every programme
carried out under it; participation duties must be traced to the applicable legal and
programme context. This supports the first proposition but not the second.

### 1. Two funds

The 2016 Act establishes:

- the **National Compensatory Afforestation Fund** under the Public Account of India;
- a **State Compensatory Afforestation Fund** under the Public Account of each State.

The statutory transfer architecture directs **90 per cent** of the money credited for a
State to that State Fund and **10 per cent** to the National Fund. The ratio tests fiscal
flow, not ownership of forests.

### 2. Two levels of authority

The Act constitutes the **National Compensatory Afforestation Fund Management and
Planning Authority**, commonly called the National Authority or National CAMPA, and
requires State-level authorities.

| Level | Internal organs | Core role |
|---|---|---|
| National Authority | Governing Body, Executive Committee, Monitoring Group | Manage National Fund, approve/support national functions, coordinate, monitor and evaluate |
| State Authority | Governing Body, Steering Committee, Executive Committee | Prepare and execute State plans, administer State Fund and oversee implementing agencies |

The structure separates high-level policy, plan scrutiny, execution and monitoring. In
practice, State forest departments remain central to on-ground works, but statutory
committees create accountability beyond a single field office.

### 3. Fund is not authority

```text
CAF = the account / pool of money
CAMPA authority = the institution that plans, administers and monitors
CA = the afforestation activity
```

This distinction is a frequent objective trap. “CAMPA fund” is common shorthand, but
the Act's precise architecture contains funds and authorities with separate legal roles.

### 4. Why the federal design makes sense

Most works occur within States, so the larger share follows implementation
responsibility. A national share supports coordination, monitoring and activities whose
value crosses State boundaries. The design therefore combines decentralised spending
with national standards.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** A 90 per cent State share guarantees locally responsive restoration.

**Reply:** Proximity can improve planning and execution.

**Residual:** Fiscal location alone does not guarantee ecological or democratic
responsiveness. Plan quality, Gram Sabha engagement, technical capacity, monitoring and
public disclosure determine the result.

### UPSC application

- Memorise **90 State : 10 National**.
- Separate fund, authority and activity.
- Name the National Monitoring Group and State Steering Committee.
- Do not convert a general participation objective into an unsupported universal legal
  statement; identify the applicable statute, rule, guideline and land-right context.

### Revision notes

1. The 2016 Act creates National and State CAFs.
2. The funds sit in the respective Public Accounts.
3. The transfer structure is 90 per cent State and 10 per cent National.
4. National CAMPA is a statutory authority, not merely a scheme.
5. State Authorities administer most on-ground spending.
6. National organs include Governing Body, Executive Committee and Monitoring Group.
7. State organs include Governing Body, Steering Committee and Executive Committee.
8. CAF means fund; CAMPA means management and planning authority.
9. Federal devolution does not itself prove local participation.
10. Monitoring and plan quality remain essential after transfer.

### Concept check

**Question:** What is lost when a candidate uses CAF, CAMPA and compensatory afforestation interchangeably?

**Model answer:** CAF is the statutory fund, CAMPA is the authority architecture that plans and manages it, and compensatory afforestation is one financed activity. Collapsing them obscures who holds money, who decides, what is implemented and how accountability should be assessed.

**Misconception to avoid:** The 90:10 split is between State and National Funds; it is not a rule that 90 per cent of every plantation must be physically located in one administrative category.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 3

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Describe the federal fund-and-authority architecture
under the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016.

**Model response:** The Act establishes a National Compensatory Afforestation Fund in
India's Public Account and State Compensatory Afforestation Funds in the respective
State Public Accounts. The statutory transfer architecture places 90 per cent of the
relevant receipts in the State Fund and 10 per cent in the National Fund.

Institutionally, the National Authority has a Governing Body, Executive Committee and
Monitoring Group. It manages the national share, coordinates standards and supports
monitoring. Each State Authority has a Governing Body, Steering Committee and Executive
Committee and prepares and executes State-level plans through implementing agencies.

The design appropriately sends most money to the level responsible for field works
while retaining national coordination. Yet devolution is not equal to accountability:
ecological planning, rights safeguards, public participation, third-party monitoring and
transparent outcome reporting remain necessary.

**Unique rubric (10):** funds 2; ratio 2; national organs/role 2; State organs/role 2;
accountability qualification 2.

## Lesson 4 - From Annual Plan of Operation to ecological outcome

Progress: 4 / 12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Planning, sanction, spending and monitoring

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first: the accountability chain

```text
FIELD NEED / LEGAL OBLIGATION
          |
          v
ANNUAL PLAN OF OPERATION (APO)
activities + location + physical targets + cost
          |
          v
SCRUTINY / APPROVAL / SANCTION
          |
          v
RELEASE AND EXPENDITURE
          |
          v
OUTPUT VERIFICATION
hectares treated + works completed + seedlings surviving
          |
          v
OUTCOME EVALUATION
native recovery + soil/water + habitat + livelihoods + durability
```

### 1. Why an Annual Plan of Operation matters

The **Annual Plan of Operation (APO)** converts a large fund balance into specific work.
It identifies activities, locations, physical targets and financial requirements for a
financial year. It should connect a legal or ecological need to a measurable task rather
than merely present a spending list.

Typical permitted fields include compensatory afforestation, assisted natural
regeneration, forest protection, wildlife and habitat work, catchment treatment and
specified supporting infrastructure, subject to the Act and Rules.

### 2. Four numbers that must not be confused

| Measurement | Question answered | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Proposal | What does an agency seek to do? | Restore 1,000 ha |
| Allocation/sanction | What money or work has been approved? | Rs X approved |
| Output | What immediate activity was completed? | 800 ha planted |
| Outcome | What changed ecologically or socially? | Native regeneration, stable soil, improved water retention |

Expenditure is neither output nor outcome. An amount can be sanctioned but unreleased,
released but unspent, spent on incomplete work, or spent on work that survives poorly.

### 3. Monitoring must be time-layered

```text
Year 0: site baseline
Year 1: planting / treatment completion
Years 2-5: survival, replacement, protection
Years 5-10: composition, structure, soil and water response
Long term: resilient ecosystem functions and livelihood compatibility
```

Geo-tagging and remote sensing help verify location and canopy. Field ecology is still
needed to test species composition, invasive spread, soil, hydrology and habitat use.
Social monitoring is needed where community access and rights may be affected.

### 4. The utilisation-gap problem

Audit and parliamentary scrutiny have repeatedly treated slow or uneven utilisation as
a governance concern. Yet “spend faster” is incomplete advice. Rushed expenditure can
reward convenient sites, monocultures and construction-heavy works. The better goal is
**timely, plan-led and outcome-accountable utilisation**.

### 5. Digital controls and their limits

Digital systems can make the accountability chain more traceable:

| Control | Governance contribution | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Online APO workflow | proposal creation, scrutiny, approval and expenditure linkage | ecological recovery |
| Geospatial monitoring | location, area, canopy and repeated spatial observation | native composition, rights compliance or durable function |
| Planting-material traceability | seed-source, nursery and provenance records | site suitability, survival or ecosystem quality |

These are planning and verification controls. They must remain linked to baseline ecology,
field evidence, rights safeguards and multi-year outcomes.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** Outcome monitoring is too slow for annual budgeting.

**Reply:** Annual budgets can still finance multi-year cohorts and publish staged
indicators.

**Residual:** Governments need continuity in plot identity and datasets. Without a
baseline and repeated measurement, later canopy cannot be attributed confidently to
the funded intervention.

### UPSC application

- Use the ladder: proposal -> sanction -> release -> expenditure -> output -> outcome.
- Critique both under-utilisation and low-quality expenditure.
- Distinguish digital workflow, geospatial monitoring and planting-material provenance,
  then identify the ecological and social evidence they do not replace.
- Separate remote-sensing verification from field-based ecological verification.

### Revision notes

1. The APO translates fund availability into annual activities.
2. Proposal is not sanction.
3. Sanction is not release.
4. Release is not expenditure.
5. Expenditure is not physical output.
6. Plantation output is not ecological outcome.
7. Monitoring requires a pre-intervention baseline.
8. Survival should be tracked beyond the planting season.
9. Canopy data cannot reveal native composition by itself.
10. Faster spending must not displace ecological quality.
11. Financial and ecological identifiers should be linked plot by plot.
12. Digital planning can improve proposal, approval and expenditure traceability.
13. Geospatial and planting-material records do not prove restoration by themselves.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State reports that it spent its entire annual CAMPA allocation and planted the planned area. What additional evidence is needed before calling the programme successful?

**Model answer:** It needs digital planning and geospatial records linked to a verified baseline, multi-year survival, native-species composition, regeneration, soil and hydrological response, habitat value, rights compliance and evidence that benefits persist after maintenance expenditure ends. Digital approval, expenditure and mapped planting remain controls or outputs, not ecological outcomes.

**Misconception to avoid:** One hundred per cent financial utilisation establishes administrative absorption, not necessarily restoration quality.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 4

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “The central CAMPA challenge is not only
under-utilisation but weak conversion of expenditure into ecological outcomes.”
Analyse.

**Model response:** Under-utilisation delays compensatory works despite a dedicated
statutory corpus. However, expenditure alone can also mislead. The governance chain
runs from proposal and APO to sanction, release, spending, output and outcome. Leakage
or delay can occur at each stage.

Physical outputs such as hectares planted, pits dug and seedlings surviving for one
season are necessary but insufficient. Mature ecological outcomes require native
composition, natural regeneration, soil stability, hydrological recovery, habitat
connectivity and compatibility with community rights. Remote sensing can confirm
location and canopy but cannot alone distinguish monoculture from native restoration.

Reform should therefore link every sanctioned plot to a geo-tagged
baseline, species plan, rights record, expenditure trail and multi-year ecological
indicators. Geospatial monitoring and planting-material provenance controls can strengthen
location and input verification, while
independent field and social audits test ecological quality and rights. Multi-year
maintenance must be built into APOs.

Thus, the objective is neither to preserve an idle corpus nor to maximise year-end
spending. It is timely, transparent expenditure that converts legal compensation into
durable ecological and social value.

**Unique rubric (15):** utilisation diagnosis 3; measurement chain 3; outcome indicators
4; monitoring reforms 3; qualified verdict 2.

## Lesson 5 - Why a plantation cannot automatically replace a mature forest

### Visual first: two hectares, unequal ecology

multi-layer canopy                   even-aged seedlings
old trees and deadwood               little structural diversity
deep soil relationships              disturbed or developing soil
complex food webs                    simplified early-stage web

### 1. Succession sets a time limit on compensation

Ecological succession shows why “plant now, replace forest now” is false. A new
plantation begins an early developmental stage. A mature forest contains accumulated
soil carbon, fungi, cavities, deadwood, vertical layers and specialised species that
cannot be manufactured instantly.

This is the **ecological-commensurability critique**: the accounting units may be
hectares and rupees, while the lost and created ecological objects remain qualitatively
different.

### 2. Native biome before native tree

Even native-tree planting can be wrong where the native ecosystem is grassland,
savanna, scrub, wetland or another open natural ecosystem. Administrative labels such
as “wasteland” or “degraded land” do not establish that the ecological destination
should be forest.

```text
SITE DIAGNOSIS
   |
   +-- native forest? -> regeneration / forest restoration may fit
   |
   +-- natural grassland or savanna? -> conserve open ecosystem
   |
   +-- wetland? -> restore hydrology, not blanket tree cover
   |
   +-- severely altered land? -> staged recovery based on soil and water
```

### 3. Species choice and structure

Fast-growing monocultures may quickly produce canopy or biomass but can perform poorly
on biodiversity, water use and resilience. A stronger plan uses site-suitable indigenous
species, mixed age and structure, assisted natural regeneration and landscape
connectivity where appropriate.

### 4. A fair defence of compensatory afforestation

It is unfair to demand that every young plot immediately match an old forest. The proper
test is whether the intervention follows a credible trajectory:

- suitable site and target ecosystem;
- soil and moisture preparation where needed;
- native diversity and natural regeneration;
- protection and adaptive maintenance;
- repeated measurement over ecologically meaningful time.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** Because equivalence is impossible, all diversion must cease.

**Reply:** Society faces genuine infrastructure and livelihood needs; some impacts may
be unavoidable after rigorous appraisal.

**Residual:** Ecological irreplaceability should raise the threshold for diversion,
strengthen avoidance and prevent compensatory accounting from claiming “no net loss”
where evidence supports only partial mitigation.

### UPSC application

- Link CAMPA to succession theory.
- Distinguish forest cover, plantation and native forest.
- Mention open natural ecosystems in site selection.
- Use “credible restoration trajectory” rather than demanding instant climax.

### Revision notes

1. Equal area does not establish ecological equivalence.
2. Mature forests embody long successional history.
3. A plantation is commonly an early-stage ecosystem.
4. Canopy can rise while biodiversity quality falls.
5. Monoculture may simplify habitat and increase vulnerability.
6. Native species must also fit the native biome.
7. Grasslands and savannas are not necessarily degraded forests.
8. Wetland restoration usually begins with hydrology, not tree planting.
9. Assisted natural regeneration can outperform blanket planting at suitable sites.
10. Compensation should claim partial mitigation, not automatic replacement.
11. Irreplaceability strengthens the case for avoidance.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “plant indigenous species” an incomplete rule for compensatory afforestation?

**Model answer:** The target ecosystem must first be identified. Indigenous trees can still damage a natural grassland, savanna or wetland. Species also need suitable soil, moisture, provenance, mixture and successional staging. Biome fidelity precedes species choice.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Non-forest-looking” or administratively labelled wasteland does not necessarily mean ecologically degraded land awaiting trees.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 5

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically examine the assumption that a new
compensatory plantation can offset the diversion of a mature natural forest.

**Model response:** Compensatory afforestation creates finance and a restoration
obligation, but equal area does not create equal ecology. A mature forest contains
multi-layered vegetation, old trees, deadwood, complex food webs, developed soil,
hydrological regulation and location-specific connectivity accumulated over decades or
centuries. A new plantation begins at an early successional stage.

Equivalence is further weakened when planting is off-site, monocultural or imposed on a
natural grassland, savanna or wetland mislabelled as wasteland. Canopy and survival
metrics may then rise without native habitat recovery.

Yet the mechanism is not valueless. Carefully chosen sites, indigenous mixed species,
assisted natural regeneration, soil-water treatment and long-term protection can create
real ecological benefits. The claim should therefore be proportionate: compensatory
afforestation can mitigate residual loss but rarely replaces a mature forest like for
like.

Policy should apply avoidance first, identify the native biome, protect rights and
monitor composition, soil, hydrology and habitat over time. Ecological irreplaceability
must constrain diversion rather than be hidden by hectare accounting.

2; quality criteria 4; calibrated conclusion 2.

### 1. Why “available land” can be misleading

Land may look empty in a file while supporting grazing, minor forest produce, seasonal
use, sacred sites or customary management. Under the Forest Rights Act, 2006, recognised
and pending rights cannot be treated as an administrative inconvenience.

**Community Forest Resource (CFR) rights** empower a community to protect, regenerate,
conserve and manage a traditionally used forest resource. That role can complement
restoration when plans are co-produced rather than imposed.

### 2. Gram Sabha and institutional participation

GIM's decentralised design emphasises Gram Sabha-linked planning and community
institutions such as Joint Forest Management Committees. CAMPA works also operate within
a broader legal landscape that includes FRA rights, PESA where applicable and project
conditions. The precise participation requirement must therefore be stated with its
legal source and context, not converted into a vague universal slogan.

### 3. Diversion loss and compensation benefit may fall on different people

```text
Community A loses access at diversion site
                  |
                  v
Compensatory plantation occurs at Site B
                  |
                  v
Community B may face altered grazing/access
```

Without distributional analysis, one project can impose two social costs: loss at the
diversion site and enclosure at the compensation site.

### 4. Community participation can improve outcomes

Local institutions can contribute:

- historical knowledge of native species and fire or grazing regimes;
- protection against fire, grazing pressure and illegal felling;
- monitoring of survival and replacement;
- livelihood-compatible use of non-timber forest produce;
- early detection of conflict, invasive species and water stress.

Participation is not automatically conservation. Elite capture, unequal voice and weak
technical support can distort decisions. Representation of women, pastoral users and
other dependent groups matters.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** Rights procedures delay urgent restoration.

**Reply:** Ignoring tenure creates conflict, litigation, enclosure and plantation
failure; due process is a risk-reduction mechanism.

**Residual:** Rights recognition must be matched by technical and financial support.
Neither a title nor a consultation meeting alone guarantees sustainable management.

### UPSC application

- Identify people as rights-holders where law so provides.
- Apply due diligence at both diversion and afforestation sites.
- Distinguish Gram Sabha from JFMC; the latter should not displace the former's legal role.
- Add distributional justice to ecological evaluation.

### Revision notes

1. Administrative vacancy does not prove social vacancy.
2. CFR rights include protect-regenerate-conserve-manage functions.
3. Pending claims require due diligence.
4. Diversion and compensation may affect different communities.

11. Social outcomes belong in restoration monitoring.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should community-rights verification occur even when compensatory afforestation is proposed on “degraded forest land”?

**Model answer:** “Degraded” describes an administrative or ecological condition, not the absence of tenure and use. The land may carry recognised or pending FRA claims, grazing, minor-forest-produce or cultural use. Ignoring these can create enclosure, conflict and failure.

**Misconception to avoid:** Community dependence is not proved or disproved by low canopy density.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 6

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Examine how community forest rights can improve
CAMPA and Green India Mission implementation while also creating institutional
coordination challenges.

**Model response:** Community Forest Resource rights recognise a community's authority to
protect, regenerate, conserve and manage traditionally used forest resources. This can
strengthen CAMPA and GIM through local knowledge of species, fire, grazing and water;
continuous protection; livelihood-compatible management; and social monitoring of
survival and expenditure.

However, afforestation sites may overlap recognised or pending claims. A plot recorded
as degraded forest may sustain grazing, minor forest produce or sacred use.
Department-led plantations can therefore become enclosures. Institutional complexity
also arises because Gram Sabhas, JFMCs, forest departments, State CAMPA committees and
district rights authorities have different legal bases and incentives.

Reform requires pre-site tenure mapping, settlement or recorded treatment of claims,
Gram Sabha-led micro-plans, representative participation, transparent benefit-sharing
and technical support for CFR institutions. Social and ecological audits should track
access, livelihoods, native regeneration and conflict, not merely plantation area.

Community rights are neither an automatic veto nor a decorative consultation. They are
a governance layer that can convert restoration from a departmental work into durable
landscape stewardship when legal authority, ecological knowledge and finance are
aligned.

### 1. Place in climate policy

The **National Mission for a Green India**, or Green India Mission (GIM), is one of the
**eight original missions** launched under the National Action Plan on Climate Change
(NAPCC). The original structure is:

| Original NAPCC mission | Principal focus |
|---|---|
| Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission | solar energy |
| National Mission for Enhanced Energy Efficiency | energy-efficiency improvement |
| National Mission on Sustainable Habitat | buildings, urban systems, waste and transport |

The Government's current description, including the official

(NMCCHH)**. Do not confuse this mission name with the separate **National Programme on
Climate Change and Human Health (NPCCHH)** used in health-sector implementation material.
Therefore write **“eight original; nine in the current Government description, with the
health mission added”**, rather than rewriting the 2008 list as if all nine had been
launched together.

GIM's mission document frames forests as ecological infrastructure, not simply land carrying
trees.

### 2. The historical target architecture

The original mission architecture set out to:

- increase forest and tree cover on **5 million hectares**;
- improve the quality of forest cover on another **5 million hectares**;
- enhance ecosystem services across the treated landscapes;
- improve forest-based livelihoods for about **3 million households**;
- enhance annual carbon sequestration by **50-60 million tonnes** by the original target
  horizon.

These are **historical mission targets**, not claims that every target was achieved and
not the current 2021-2030 implementation framework. Always separate a policy target from
a dated progress report.

### 3. The revised 2021-2030 architecture

MoEFCC's revised **National Mission for a Green India, Mission Document** sets out a ten-year
framework for **2021-2030**. It distinguishes:

- **24 million hectares** of afforestation/tree planting and eco-restoration through
  convergence with ongoing Central and State schemes and CAMPA-linked funds; and
- **1 million hectares** of mission-specific interventions within that wider effort, used
  to establish regionally suitable, innovative and demonstrable restoration models.

The 24 mha figure is therefore a **convergence target**, not an assertion that GIM's own
budget independently treats 24 mha. The 1 mha figure identifies the mission-specific
intervention scale for 2021-2030; it is not “one million hectares every year.”

### 4. What the revision changes - and what it retains

The revision retains the original concern with cover, quality, ecosystem services and
livelihoods, but reorganises implementation around vulnerable landscapes,
micro-ecosystems, convergence and NDC-linked reporting. The historical 5+5 mha
architecture remains useful for understanding GIM's origin; answers on current design
must add the revised 24 mha/1 mha distinction.

### 5. Quantity and quality

| Quantity question | Quality question |
|---|---|
| How much area gained tree or forest cover? | Did native composition, density and resilience improve? |
| How many hectares were treated? | Did soil, water and habitat function recover? |
| How many households were included? | Did livelihood security and decision-making power improve? |
| How much carbon was estimated? | Was the sink durable and biodiversity-compatible? |

GIM's distinctive value is this quality-and-services orientation. It can cover degraded
forests, agroforestry, urban and peri-urban areas, wetlands and other landscapes under
its components, but the ecological prescription must remain site-specific.

### 6. Ecosystem services connect climate and development

Carbon is only one service. Forest landscapes also regulate water, reduce erosion,
support pollination and biodiversity, and provide fuel, fodder and non-timber forest
produce. A carbon-only plantation could therefore meet one narrow indicator while
weakening water or biodiversity.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** Multiple objectives make GIM impossible to measure.

**Reply:** Multi-function landscapes require multiple indicators; a single hectare or
carbon number would be misleading.

**Residual:** The revised framework provides for a National Afforestation Dashboard and
multi-level monitoring, but a dashboard still needs a stable hierarchy of indicators;
otherwise broad objectives can permit selective reporting of whichever output looks
favourable.

**Question:** What makes GIM more than a national tree-planting target?

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the ecosystem-service orientation of the

### 1. Why components differ

Improving a moderately degraded forest is not the same task as creating an urban green
space or restoring a wetland. The revised framework therefore uses a
**micro-ecosystem approach**: interventions are selected for particular vulnerable
sub-landscapes and ecological conditions rather than imposed as one plantation recipe.

- **Quality improvement:** assisted natural regeneration, protection and enrichment.
- **Ecosystem restoration and cover expansion:** site-specific treatment of degraded
  landscapes.
- **Urban/peri-urban greening:** shade, connectivity and urban ecological services.
- **Agroforestry/social forestry:** trees within productive and community landscapes.
- **Wetlands:** hydrological and vegetative restoration.
- **Livelihood/energy support:** reduce unsustainable pressure and increase local gains.

### 2. From micro-ecosystem to State plan

The revised framework uses a bottom-up institutional chain:

1. the **JFMC** identifies landscapes, treatment areas and suitable models and prepares
   site-specific micro-plans;
2. the **Gram Sabha** approves village micro-plans and conducts social audit;
3. each JFMC prepares a **five-year perspective plan**, including benefit-sharing with
   the Forest Department;
4. the **Forest Development Agency (FDA)** coordinates implementation at district or
   divisional level;
5. the **State Forest Development Agency (SFDA)** approves perspective plans and APOs
   and prepares the comprehensive **State Afforestation Perspective Plan** through
   stakeholder consultation and alignment with the State climate plan and convergent
   schemes.

This is an implementation design in the revised Mission Document. It does not displace
statutory Gram Sabha or forest-rights authority.

### 3. Convergence is not mere pooling of money

Convergence means that different programmes perform complementary tasks within one
landscape plan.

```text
CAMPA: diversion-linked statutory finance
MGNREGS: labour-intensive soil and water works
GIM: ecosystem-service and landscape framework
FRA institutions: rights and community governance
State schemes: locally specific support
                         |
                         v
one sequenced micro-plan, not five unrelated work lists
```

Using one scheme to fill another scheme's accounting gap is not genuine convergence.
The shared ecological objective, plot identity, division of responsibility and outcome
indicator must be explicit.

### 4. Monitoring architecture

The revised framework provides for a web-based **National Afforestation Dashboard and
MIS**, GIS and satellite analysis, implementing-agency self-monitoring, Gram Sabha social
audit, Forest Survey of India or specialised remote-sensing review, third-party checks
and selected long-term ecosystem-service monitoring. The dashboard consolidates
reported afforestation and regeneration activity across schemes; it does not convert a
reported hectare into a verified outcome automatically.

### 5. Community institutions

The revised design calls the JFMC the key grassroots implementing unit and places its
site-specific micro-plan before the Gram Sabha for approval. The democratic safeguard is
strongest when the JFMC remains accountable to the Gram Sabha, responsibilities and
benefits are transparent, women and forest-dependent groups participate substantively,
and statutory rights are respected.

### 6. Risks of convergence

- duplicated hectare claims across schemes;
- the same activity charged to multiple budgets;
- blurred accountability when survival is poor;
- incompatible calendars and funding rules;
- labour works completed without follow-up ecological maintenance;
- community participation reduced to signatures.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** Convergence creates too many actors and slows delivery.

**Reply:** Forest landscapes already contain interdependent water, livelihood, rights
and biodiversity problems; isolated schemes can create contradictory works.

**Residual:** Convergence needs one accountable lead, common plot identifiers and
publicly stated responsibility for maintenance and outcomes.

### UPSC application

- Define convergence as coordinated functions, not budget aggregation.
- Trace micro-plan -> JFMC/Gram Sabha -> FDA -> SFDA State Perspective Plan.
- Give one example: MGNREGS soil-water work + GIM ecological plan + CAMPA planting finance.
- Identify duplication and accountability risks.
- Preserve Gram Sabha and FRA roles.

### Revision notes

1. GIM uses differentiated components.
2. The revised framework uses a micro-ecosystem approach.
3. JFMCs prepare site micro-plans for Gram Sabha approval.
4. Five-year JFMC perspective plans include benefit-sharing.
5. FDAs coordinate district/divisional implementation.
6. SFDAs prepare State Afforestation Perspective Plans and approve APOs.
7. CAMPA-GIM convergence is officially envisaged.
8. The National Afforestation Dashboard consolidates reported activity across schemes.
9. GIS, social audit and third-party review perform different monitoring functions.
10. Convergence needs a common landscape plan.
11. Double counting is a major risk.
12. Community approval must be substantive, not ceremonial.
13. A dashboard output is not by itself an ecological outcome.

### Concept check

**Question:** What distinguishes genuine programme convergence from using several scheme names for the same plantation?

**Model answer:** Genuine convergence begins with a shared micro-ecosystem plan, assigns complementary tasks and finance, and carries one traceable result through JFMC/Gram Sabha, FDA and SFDA planning into the dashboard and outcome framework. Merely attaching several scheme names to one plantation invites double counting.

**Misconception to avoid:** More funding sources do not automatically produce a more integrated ecological intervention.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 8

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate convergence as an implementation strategy
for the Green India Mission.

**Model response:** Revised GIM addresses carbon, biodiversity, water and livelihoods
through vulnerable-landscape and micro-ecosystem planning. No single scheme controls
all labour, finance, tenure and technical capacities needed. Convergence can combine
GIM's landscape framework, CAMPA finance, MGNREGS soil-water works, State schemes and
community institutions.

Its value lies in sequencing. JFMC micro-plans approved by the Gram Sabha can feed FDA
implementation and an SFDA State Afforestation Perspective Plan; watershed treatment can
prepare a site, assisted natural regeneration can follow, and maintenance and livelihood
measures can reduce renewed pressure.

However, convergence can obscure responsibility. The same hectare may be reported by
several schemes, expenditure may be duplicated, and no agency may own long-term
survival. Differing financial calendars can produce planting without prior soil work or
later maintenance.

Effective convergence needs one geo-tagged landscape plan, clear division of functions,
single-count reporting through the National Afforestation Dashboard, Gram Sabha social
audit, ecological baselines and named responsibility for multi-year outcomes. Thus
convergence is valuable not as pooled money but as coordinated action with traceable
accountability.

### 2. How to read “area brought under restoration”

The phrase may aggregate activities with different ecological depth: plantation,
assisted natural regeneration, soil-water treatment or other eco-restoration works.

1. What was the baseline condition?
2. Which intervention occurred?
3. What is the measurement date?
4. How much survived?
5. Did native composition and ecosystem function improve?
6. Were livelihood and rights outcomes measured?

### 3. Output is still valuable

Rejecting overclaim does not mean dismissing output data. Area treated and money
sanctioned are necessary for implementation accountability. They become misleading only
when treated as sufficient proof of outcome.

### 4. A compact forest-governance scorecard

| Level | Indicator |
|---|---|

| Delivery | area treated, work type, maintenance completed |

| Society | rights compliance, participation, livelihood/access effect |
| Durability | condition after 3, 5 and 10 years |

**Objection:** Demanding long-term outcomes unfairly delays recognition of current work.

### Visual first: overlap without identity

### 1. The strongest synthesis

CAMPA supplies a comparatively clear statutory finance stream. Revised GIM supplies a
broader micro-ecosystem, ecosystem-service and landscape-quality framework. Its 24 mha
target depends on convergence, while 1 mha is assigned to mission-specific
interventions. Convergence can be valuable when CAMPA-funded works adopt GIM standards
for native biodiversity, water, livelihoods and community institutions.

Convergence does not change the origin of the money or erase the legal obligation for
which it was collected.

### 2. Distinguish neighbouring instruments

| Instrument | Trigger/source | Primary logic |
|---|---|---|
| CAMPA/CAF | Compensatory levies linked to forest diversion | Finance statutory compensation and related works |
| GIM | NAPCC mission; revised 2021-2030 framework combines mission intervention with convergence | Improve cover, quality, services and livelihoods across vulnerable landscapes |
| National Afforestation Programme | Older afforestation programme | Regeneration of degraded forests through programme institutions |
| Green Credit Programme | Rules-based environmental incentive with activity-specific methodologies | Generate credits for specified actions; current tree-plantation credits are non-tradable and transferable only within the stated holding-company/subsidiary exception |
| **“Afforestation and Green Credit Programme”** — Economic Survey 2025-26 formulation | Ecosystem-based restoration incentive framing | Encourage public and private participation in compensatory afforestation and degraded-forest restoration |
| Carbon credit | Verified/remunerated emissions reduction or removal under applicable system | Climate-accounting unit, commonly tCO2e |

A green credit for plantation is not automatically a carbon credit, a CAMPA obligation
or proof of ecological restoration.

### 3. Green Credit Rules and the current plantation methodology

The **Green Credit Rules, 2023** were notified as **S.O. 4458(E), 12 October 2023**
under sections **3, 6 and 25 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**. They define a
green credit as a singular incentive unit for a specified activity that delivers a positive
environmental impact.

Official Gazette text: <https://egazette.gov.in/WriteReadData/2023/249377.pdf>.

The Rules create a programme-wide Registry and trading-platform architecture. That general
architecture must now be read with the activity-specific methodology in force. For tree
plantation, **G.S.R. 592(E), 29 August 2025** superseded the February 2024 calculation
methodology. It makes a plantation applicant eligible to claim only after at least five years
of restoration activity and achievement of at least **40 per cent canopy density**; calculation
then considers vegetation status, canopy-density change and surviving trees.

Official revised Gazette text: <https://egazette.gov.in/WriteReadData/2025/265796.pdf>.

The administrative sequence remains:

```text
register eligible activity
        ↓
verification by designated agency
        ↓
Administrator issues green-credit certificate
        ↓
entry in Green Credit Registry
        ↓
use only as permitted by the applicable activity methodology
```

The **Indian Council of Forestry Research and Education (ICFRE)** is the Administrator.
Rule 10 provides for the electronic Green Credit Registry, and rule 11 requires the
Administrator to establish and maintain a trading platform with Central Government approval.

For **tree-plantation Green Credits**, however, the later methodology is decisive:

- they are **non-tradable**;
- they are **non-transferable**, except for transfer **between a holding company and its
  subsidiary companies**;
- once exchanged for a permitted purpose, the credit is extinguished to that extent and
  cannot be used again.

The exception is therefore limited to transfer between a holding company and its subsidiary
companies; it does not authorise an open-market sale or transfer to unrelated entities. The
Rules also state that the programme is
independent of the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme; the same activity may separately qualify for
carbon credits only under that scheme's own requirements. Neither registration nor issuance
proves native composition, additionality, biodiversity recovery or durable restoration.

### 4. What the Economic Survey formulation does—and does not—mean

Economic Survey 2025-26, Chapter 10, paragraph 10.91 (official Chapter 10 extract,
PDF p. 37; printed p. 413) states that ecosystem-based approaches and restoration are being incentivised
through the **“Afforestation and Green Credit Programme”**, which encourages **public and
private participation in compensatory afforestation and degraded-forest restoration**.

The scope has three parts that should remain together:

1. an ecosystem-based and restoration objective;
2. participation by both public and private actors; and
3. application to compensatory afforestation and degraded-forest restoration.

This Survey formulation should not be converted into a claim that CAMPA, GIM, the National
Afforestation Programme and the Green Credit Programme have become one legal instrument.
Nor does a registered plantation or issued credit prove survival, native composition,
hydrological recovery or durable restoration. It describes an incentive pathway; statutory
trigger, finance source and ecological outcome still require separate evidence.

### 5. The integration design

```text
CAMPA obligation identifies plot and finance
                  |
                  v
GIM micro-ecosystem diagnosis and State Perspective Plan set quality standard
                  |
                  v
rights and community plan shape implementation
                  |
                  v
common output + outcome monitoring
```

The approach prevents parallel plans from reporting the same ecological gain twice.

### 6. Limits of convergence

- CAMPA money is tied to statutory purposes and cannot become an unrestricted mission
  budget.
- GIM objectives are broader than compensating a particular diversion.
- A crediting programme may reward a specified action even before long-term ecosystem
  outcomes are known.
- Different instruments require separate financial and performance ledgers.

### Criticism, reply and residual

**Objection:** One unified programme would be simpler.

**Reply:** The instruments answer different legal and policy questions; merger could
weaken traceability of diversion liability.

**Residual:** Operational coordination should be deep while accounting identity remains
separate.

### UPSC application

- Compare trigger, finance, objective and metric.
- Recommend ecological convergence, not legal conflation.
- Mention separate ledgers and single-count outcomes.
- Distinguish green credits from carbon credits.
- State the Green Credit Rules' EPA legal basis and Registry/platform architecture, then add
  the current plantation-specific rule: non-tradable and non-transferable except between a
  holding company and its subsidiary companies.
- Link plantation-credit eligibility to the five-year restoration period and minimum
  40 per cent canopy-density threshold.
- Use the Survey's combined formulation exactly, including public/private participation and
  both compensatory afforestation and degraded-forest restoration.

### Revision notes

1. CAMPA is diversion-triggered.
2. GIM is a broader NAPCC restoration mission with a revised 2021-2030 framework.
3. CAMPA has dedicated statutory funds.
4. Revised GIM separates a 24 mha convergence envelope from 1 mha of mission-specific work.
5. GIM quality standards can improve CAMPA works.
6. Convergence must preserve legal purpose.
7. NAP is distinct from both CAMPA and GIM.
8. Green Credit Rules, 2023 derive from sections 3, 6 and 25 of the EPA, 1986.
9. Verified activities can generate certificates recorded in the Green Credit Registry.
10. Rule 11 provides a general approved domestic trading-platform architecture.
11. G.S.R. 592(E) of 29 August 2025 supersedes the earlier plantation methodology and
    requires at least five years of restoration activity and 40 per cent canopy density.
12. Plantation credits are non-tradable and non-transferable except between a holding
    company and its subsidiary companies.
13. A plantation credit exchanged for a permitted purpose is extinguished to that extent;

### Concept check

**Question:** What should be integrated between CAMPA and GIM, and what should remain separate?

### 1. Why scope matters to CAMPA

If land falls within the prior-approval regime and is diverted, compensatory conditions
and levies can be triggered. The legal definition and coverage of forest therefore sit
upstream of the fund.

### 2. The 2023 statutory changes

The amendment renamed the 1980 law and specified applicability by reference to land
notified as forest under the Indian Forest Act or another law and land recorded as
forest in government records on or after 25 October 1980, subject to the Act's detailed
provisions.

Official Gazette text: <https://egazette.gov.in/WriteReadData/2023/247866.pdf>.

The proviso to the recorded-forest clause is exact and narrow:

> "Provided that the provisions of this clause shall not apply to such land, which has
> been changed from forest use to use for non-forest purpose on or before the
> **12th December, 1996** in pursuance of an order, issued by any authority authorised
> by a State Government or an Union territory Administration in that behalf."

The date is tied to an authorised land-use change. It is not a general exemption for every
unrecorded, cleared or altered parcel.

It also created exemptions for specified categories. The border provision is exact:
forest land situated within **100 kilometres along an international border, Line of Control
or Line of Actual Control** may fall within the exemption only when it is proposed for a
**strategic linear project of national importance and concerning national security**.
Distance from the border alone is not sufficient.

Two separate size-limited clauses should not be merged into that 100-km rule:

- up to **10 hectares** proposed for security-related infrastructure; and
- up to **5 hectares** for a defence-related project, paramilitary camp or Central
  Government-specified public-utility project in a notified Left Wing Extremism-affected area.

The subsection makes these exemptions subject to Central Government-specified terms and
conditions, including tree planting to compensate for felling. Therefore the provision is a
conditional statutory exemption, not a blanket environmental clearance, land grant or waiver
of every other applicable law. Its environmental significance is spatial because strategic
linear projects can cross biodiversity-rich border landscapes.

### 3. The judicial counterweight

In February 2024 proceedings associated with **Ashok Kumar Sharma v. Union of India**,
the Supreme Court directed that the broad dictionary meaning of forest associated with
**T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad (1996)** continue pending compilation of State
expert-committee records of forest-like land.

The safe formulation is:

> The statute specified and narrowed its recorded/notified coverage, while the Court
> preserved the broader ecological meaning in the interim pending State records.

Do not say either that the Godavarman approach has disappeared or that the interim
direction permanently settles every future boundary.

### 4. What changed procedurally in 2026—and what remained pending

The dated court materials in this section are **legal-status evidence** needed to state the
current procedural position accurately. They are not a second substantive current-affairs
anchor; the session's single standalone CA anchor remains the 10 July 2026 National CAMPA
review.

Court's **16 March 2026** order initiated categorisation of the very large body of pending
interlocutory applications. Its **13 April 2026** order recorded more than 800 pending
applications, directed an online questionnaire and Registrar-level triage, and
contemplated separate contemporary proceedings for distinct issue groups after
categorisation.

After an extension on **19 June 2026**, the Court's **4 August 2026** order recorded that
only 75 pro-forma responses had been received. It granted Advocates-on-Record and
parties-in-person a **final additional three weeks from 4 August** to submit completed
pro formas to the Amicus Curiae, stated that no further extension would be granted, and
directed that, subject to the order's stated exceptions, non-compliant interlocutory
applications would stand dismissed for non-prosecution. The matters were listed for
**31 August 2026**. A Supreme Court Registry circular dated **11 August 2026** communicated
that final extension and its consequence; it did not create a later deadline.

the Court's [19 June extension order](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/1995/2997/2997_1995_1_802_71835_Order_19-Jun-2026.pdf),
the [4 August daily order](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/143865129/) and the official
[11 August Registry circular](https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s3ec0490f1f4972d133619a60c30f3559e/uploads/2026/08/2026081156.pdf).

This remains procedural restructuring of a continuing-mandamus docket. The April and
August steps did **not** state that the broad Godavarman position or the February 2024
interim protection had been overruled. Therefore, the safe status is limited: the Court
was categorising and filtering pending applications, while no substantive forest-scope
reversal should be inferred from those procedural directions.

A separate but connected constitutional challenge remained pending in **Ashok Kumar Sharma,

distinguished that constitutional question from disputes over the status of particular land
parcels, which were to be pursued before the jurisdictional High Courts.

A contemporaneous [16 September 2026 court report](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/bharat/sc-to-test-forest-definition-under-conservation-act-leaves-land-disputes-to-hcs-enn26091605494)
records this distinction and the direction concerning incomplete State and Union Territory
expert-panel exercises.

This was a procedural definition of the case's scope, not a judgment upholding or striking
down section 1A. The cited 16 September report records no final merits disposition.
Accordingly, an answer should state that the Court was examining constitutional validity at
that hearing, while the February 2024 interim direction and the Godavarman framework had not
been displaced by a final merits ruling reported in that source.

| Climate value | Full recognition of tenure and cultural values |

The same discipline applies to CAMPA and GIM: a single metric cannot represent the
whole socio-ecological system.

### 5. International forest declarations: the distinctions to learn first

International forest cooperation may take the form of a treaty, political declaration,
pledge, platform or certification standard. These forms differ in legal force,
participation and accountability. A candidate should establish each attribute from the
instrument's own record rather than infer it from an ambitious title, a target or the
presence of prominent participants.

**UPSC Prelims 2021, GS-I, Booklet B, Question 44 - exact scan wording**

> With reference to the 'New York Declaration on Forests', which of the following
> statements are correct?
>
> 1. It was first endorsed at the United Nations Climate Summit in 2014.
> 2. It endorses a global timeline to end the loss of forests.
> 3. It is a legally binding international declaration.
> 4. It is endorsed by governments, big companies and indigenous communities.
> 5. India was one of the signatories at its inception.
>
> Select the correct answer using the code given below.
>
> (a) 1, 2 and 4
> (b) 1, 3 and 5
> (c) 3 and 4
> (d) 2 and 5

Attempt it before continuing.

#### Post-attempt resolution

The **New York Declaration on Forests (NYDF)** was launched at the United Nations Climate
Summit in **2014**. It is a **voluntary, non-legally binding political declaration**, not a
treaty. Its forest-loss architecture included a shared timeline to halve natural-forest loss
by 2020 and strive to end it by 2030, alongside restoration and finance goals.

Its endorsement base crossed legal categories: national and subnational governments,
companies, Indigenous Peoples' organisations and civil-society organisations participated.
That breadth does not make the declaration legally binding. The Government of India was
**not among the national-government endorsers at inception**; an Indian organisation's
endorsement cannot be substituted for India's national signature.

Accordingly, the launch, timeline and diverse-endorser propositions are supported; the
legally binding and India-at-inception propositions are not.

### 6. Recognition programmes: institution first, reason second

For an assertion-reason style recognition question, first identify the awarding
institutions from the programme's own record. Then verify the stated reason for the
recognition independently. Only after both truth values are established should the
explanatory link be tested.

#### Post-attempt resolution

**Tree Cities of the World** is a programme associated with the **Food and Agriculture

statutory forest category, proof of native-ecosystem restoration or a transfer of land

The first statement is not supported because it substitutes UNCDF for FAO; the second

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a forest-carbon project report credible carbon performance yet remain deficient as forest governance?

**Model answer:** Carbon verification may establish a baseline-related emissions or stock result, while tenure, biodiversity, water, cultural use, representation and benefit-sharing remain weak. Forest governance is broader than a carbon ledger and requires these social and ecological safeguards.

**Misconception to avoid:** Certification for one environmental attribute is not certification of every legal, ecological and distributive outcome.

### Original Mains practice - Lesson 12

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Assess the promise and limits of community-based
forest-carbon initiatives within India's wider forest-governance architecture.

**Model response:** Community forest-carbon initiatives can align climate finance with
local stewardship. REDD+ rewards reduced deforestation and degradation, conservation,
sustainable management and enhanced stocks. The Khasi Hills community project
illustrates the potential role of customary institutions, monitoring and benefit-sharing.

Their promise lies in continuous local protection, livelihood incentives and finance
for conservation beyond short project budgets. They can complement GIM's ecosystem-
service approach and community forest rights.

Limits arise from baseline uncertainty, additionality, permanence, leakage and unequal
benefit distribution. Carbon stock does not prove biodiversity, hydrological recovery
or tenure justice. Certification also cannot substitute for FRA compliance, Gram Sabha
authority or transparent contracts. Project claims must be tied to a defined monitoring
period; credit volume and income should never be inferred without evidence.

India should integrate carbon projects with recognised rights, native-biome management,
public carbon and social audits, grievance mechanisms and diversified livelihood
benefits. Community carbon can strengthen forest governance when carbon remains one
measured service within a rights-based ecosystem, not the sole value of the forest.

**Unique rubric (20):** REDD+ mechanism 4; community promise 4; carbon-integrity limits
4; rights/ecology limits 4; integrated way forward and verdict 4.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the National and State Compensatory
Afforestation Fund architecture and its basic accountability logic.

**Model answer (129 words):** The Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016 establishes the
National CAF in India's Public Account and State CAFs in State Public Accounts. User
agencies pay CA costs, NPV and related sums when diversion conditions require them.
Ninety per cent of receipts goes to the relevant State Fund and 10 per cent remains
national.

National and State Authorities manage funds through Governing Bodies and executive
structures. The national tier also has a Monitoring Group; States have Steering
Committees. This places finance near field implementation while retaining national
coordination.

Accountability extends beyond transfer and expenditure. APO disclosure, rights due
diligence, geo-tagged plots and multi-year monitoring must connect money to survival,
native composition, soil-water recovery and durable livelihoods. Digital workflows,
geospatial evidence and planting-material provenance strengthen control, but none alone
proves ecological restoration.

**Why this earns marks:** It combines the ratio and institutions with the causal source
of funds and a governance qualification.

**Quantified rubric (10):** legal funds 2; payment source 2; 90:10 2; authorities 2;
accountability 2.

## Final 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “Forest-restoration policy fails when it measures
money and hectares but not ecosystem function.” Discuss with reference to CAMPA and
Green India Mission.

**Model answer:** CAMPA and GIM solve different problems. CAMPA receives
diversion-linked levies and finances afforestation and related works. Revised GIM is a
2021-2030 NAPCC framework: 1 million hectares of mission-specific demonstrative work
sits within a 24-million-hectare convergence effort directed at cover, quality,
ecosystem services and livelihoods.

Money and hectares remain necessary administrative indicators: they reveal sanction,
absorption and delivery. They are insufficient because a new plantation cannot
automatically replace a mature forest's species, soil, hydrology, habitat connectivity
or local livelihood functions. Canopy can increase through a monoculture, and tree
planting can damage a natural grassland or wetland. Off-site compensation may also
shift costs between communities.

Evaluation should follow a results chain: proposal -> sanction/release -> expenditure ->
area and work output -> survival/regeneration -> native composition, soil-water and
habitat outcomes -> livelihood and rights effects -> durability. Geo-tagging and remote
sensing should be combined with field ecology and social audits.

CAMPA-GIM convergence can help by applying GIM's micro-ecosystem, State Perspective Plan
and ecosystem-service standards to statutory works. Digital APO, GIS and the National
Afforestation Dashboard can connect finance and reported activity, but field ecology and
social audit remain necessary. Success is timely spending that produces a
rights-compatible, native-biome restoration trajectory, not a large corpus or count.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer compares instruments, identifies measurement
failure, supplies examples and proposes an executable indicator chain.

**Quantified rubric (15):** instrument distinction 3; ecological critique 4; social/biome
qualification 2; results chain 4; conclusion 2.

## Final 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Critically evaluate India's compensatory
afforestation governance and propose a framework that aligns development, ecological
restoration and community rights.

**Model answer:** India's framework internalises part of forest diversion's cost through
user-agency payments, statutory National and State funds, a 90:10 transfer architecture
and planned afforestation, regeneration and wildlife works. It creates dedicated finance
and federal accountability rather than leaving mitigation discretionary.

Three limits remain. First, ecological commensurability is weak: mature native forest
cannot be replaced quickly by an off-site, even-aged plantation. Second, implementation
can privilege sanctions, spending and hectares over native composition, soil, water and
durability. Third, “available” degraded land may support grassland biodiversity,
grazing or recognised and pending forest rights.

A balanced framework should:

1. enforce avoidance and minimisation before compensation;
2. read current forest scope through both amended law and judicial protection;
3. conduct native-biome and tenure mapping before site approval;
4. use Gram Sabha-linked plans and fair benefit arrangements;
5. align CAMPA works with revised GIM micro-ecosystem and ecosystem-service standards;
6. use operational Digital APO, GIS evidence and GIM monitoring while preserving separate
   finance and attribution ledgers;
7. publish three-, five- and ten-year ecological and social audits.

Development need not be denied, but compensation claims must remain proportionate.
The test is whether residual damage is transparently mitigated through durable,
rights-based ecological recovery.

**Why this earns marks:** It credits the statutory design, identifies three distinct
failure layers and links each to a feasible reform.

**Quantified rubric (20):** design strengths 4; ecological critique 4; measurement and
rights critique 4; seven-part reform framework 6; qualified verdict 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## The legal-financial spine

- Forest diversion for non-forest use is upstream of the compensatory-fund chain.
- User agencies bear CA cost, NPV and other applicable compensatory sums.
- The CAF Act, 2016 establishes National and State Funds and statutory authorities.
- Fund transfer: **90 per cent State Fund; 10 per cent National Fund**.
- National organs: Governing Body, Executive Committee, Monitoring Group.
- State organs: Governing Body, Steering Committee, Executive Committee.
- CAF is money; CAMPA is authority; CA is activity.
- APO converts fund availability into annual, location-specific work.
- Digital APO is operational for FY 2026-27 submission, verification and approval.
- National CAMPA reported the GIS Lab as established and presented progress on
  Harit-SANKALP; only Digital APO was explicitly described as operationalised.
- GIS evidence and the planting-material traceability functions described for
  Harit-SANKALP do not substitute for ecological outcome evidence.

## Diversion, afforestation and restoration

- Diversion = approved land-use change.
- CA = plantation obligation linked to diversion.
- Restoration = recovery of native composition, structure, soil, water, habitat and
  resilience.
- Use the mitigation hierarchy: avoid -> minimise -> restore -> compensate.
- Equal area does not establish equal ecosystem.
- A mature forest and a young plantation occupy different successional stages.
- Off-site compensation may not repair local watershed, corridor or livelihood loss.

## Site and biome discipline

- Equivalent non-forest land is the ordinary CA-site principle; degraded forest land is
  a conditional fallback under the applicable framework.
- “Wasteland” and “degraded land” are not self-proving ecological diagnoses.
- Natural grasslands, savannas, scrub and wetlands must not receive blanket tree planting.
- Native biome comes before native species.
- Prefer assisted natural regeneration where ecological conditions support it.
- Monitor diversity, regeneration, soil, water and habitat - not canopy alone.

## Rights and community governance

- Verify recognised and pending FRA rights before committing land.
- CFR rights include protect-regenerate-conserve-manage functions.
- Gram Sabha and JFMC have different legal and operational positions.
- Participation must be informed, representative and linked to real decisions.
- Diversion and compensation can impose costs on different communities.
- Track access, livelihoods, benefit-sharing and conflict as outcomes.

## Green India Mission

- GIM is one of the eight original NAPCC missions.
- Original eight: Solar; Enhanced Energy Efficiency; Sustainable Habitat; Water; Sustaining
  the Himalayan Ecosystem; Green India; Sustainable Agriculture; Strategic Knowledge for
  Climate Change.
- The current Government description counts nine after adding the National Mission on
  Climate Change and Human Health.
- Preserve the distinction: eight original missions; nine currently described, with
  NMCCHH as the ninth mission. NPCCHH is a separate programme label used in implementation
  material and is not the mission's official name.
- Historical architecture: increase cover on 5 mha and improve quality on another 5 mha.
- Revised Mission Document: ten-year implementation framework for 2021-2030.
- Revised scale: 24 mha through convergence, including 1 mha of mission-specific,
  regionally suitable demonstrative interventions.
- Do not describe 24 mha as GIM-budget-only work or 1 mha as an annual target.
- Services: biodiversity, water, carbon, soil and provisioning benefits.
- Components include forest-quality improvement, restoration/cover, urban greening,
  agroforestry/social forestry, wetlands and livelihood/energy support.
- Micro-ecosystem planning adapts treatment to vulnerable sub-landscapes.
- JFMC micro-plans require Gram Sabha approval; FDA coordinates implementation and SFDA
  prepares the State Afforestation Perspective Plan and approves APOs.
- The National Afforestation Dashboard/MIS consolidates reported activity; GIS, social
  audit, third-party review and long-term field evidence serve different verification roles.
- GIM is broader than diversion-linked compensation.
- Convergence should coordinate CAMPA, MGNREGS, State schemes and community institutions.

## The measurement ladder

- Proposal = intended work.
- Allocation/sanction = approved resource.
- Release = money transferred.
- Expenditure = money used.
- Output = activity completed.
- Intermediate result = survival/regeneration/canopy response.
- Outcome = durable ecological and social improvement.
- Impact = wider, persistent landscape resilience.
- Standing CAMPA implementation evidence separates Digital APO operationalisation,
  GIS Lab establishment and presented Harit-SANKALP progress from GIM financial and
  treatment reports.
- The 10 July 2026 National CAMPA review is the session's single dated implementation
  snapshot; classify its digital-workflow, GIS-Lab and portal-progress claims separately
  from field output and ecological outcome.

## Legal status and category traps

- Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 was renamed the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan)
  Adhiniyam, 1980.
- The 2023 amendment specified notified/recorded scope and stated exemptions.
- Its recorded-forest clause does not apply where an authorised order changed the land from
  forest to non-forest use on or before **12 December 1996**.
- The 100-km exemption applies along an international border, LoC or LAC only to a
  national-importance strategic linear project concerning national security.
- Separate limits are up to 10 ha for security infrastructure and up to 5 ha for specified
  defence, paramilitary-camp or public-utility projects in a notified LWE-affected area.
- Exemptions remain subject to Central terms and conditions, including compensatory tree planting.
- The Supreme Court's February 2024 interim direction retained the broader Godavarman
  dictionary meaning pending State records.
- Orders dated 16 March and 13 April 2026 initiated categorisation and questionnaire-based
  triage of 800-plus pending applications.
- The 4 August order recorded 75 responses, granted a final three weeks from that date,
  warned of dismissal for non-prosecution subject to stated exceptions, and listed the
  matters for 31 August 2026; the 11 August Registry circular communicated that extension.
- As a legal/procedural status update rather than a second CA anchor, the 16 September 2026
  hearing in W.P.(C) 1164/2023 confined the case to the pending constitutional
  validity of the 2023 statutory definition; parcel-specific disputes were left to High Courts.
- The cited 16 September hearing report records no final merits ruling displacing the
  February 2024 interim position.
- These procedural steps did not state that the earlier substantive forest-scope position
  was reversed.
- State the sequence: statutory change -> interim judicial protection -> procedural
  reorganisation while records and issue-specific adjudication continue.
- Legal forest, recorded forest area and FSI forest cover are different categories.

## Instrument distinctions

- CAMPA: statutory, diversion-linked, user-agency funded.
- GIM: revised 2021-2030 NAPCC landscape mission, micro-ecosystems, quality and services.
- NAP: distinct older afforestation programme.
- Green Credit Rules, 2023: S.O. 4458(E), under sections 3, 6 and 25 of the EPA, 1986.
- A verified activity can generate a certificate recorded in the Green Credit Registry;
  rule 11 provides the programme's general approved domestic trading-platform architecture.
- G.S.R. 592(E), 29 August 2025 superseded the earlier tree-plantation methodology:
  eligibility follows at least five years of restoration activity and at least 40 per cent
  canopy density.
- Tree-plantation Green Credits are non-tradable and non-transferable except between a
  holding company and its subsidiary companies.
- A plantation credit exchanged for a permitted purpose is extinguished to that extent and
  cannot be used again.
- Green credits are legally distinct from carbon credits; dual eligibility requires compliance
  with each framework independently.
- Economic Survey 2025-26 uses the combined formulation **“Afforestation and Green Credit
  Programme”** for encouraging public and private participation in compensatory afforestation
  and degraded-forest restoration.
- The Survey formulation is an incentive pathway, not proof that the neighbouring instruments
  have merged or that ecological restoration has been achieved.
- Carbon credit: climate-accounting unit under its applicable system.
- REDD+: deforestation/degradation reduction plus conservation, sustainable management
  and stock enhancement.
- Carbon performance cannot replace biodiversity, rights and livelihood evaluation.
