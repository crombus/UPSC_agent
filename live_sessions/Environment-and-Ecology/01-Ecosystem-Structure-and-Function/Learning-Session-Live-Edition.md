# Environment and Ecology 01 — Ecosystem Structure and Function

An ecosystem is not a list of species. It is a set of living relationships sustained by water, nutrients and usable energy. Follow what enters, what moves, what returns and what is lost; then ask which connections a disturbance would sever.

## Lesson 1 — A living system needs a physical setting

Progress: 1/9 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Biotic and abiotic structure

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
Sunlight + rainfall + temperature + soil minerals + water
                         ↓
                   PRODUCERS
                         ↓ food
                   CONSUMERS
                         ↓ dead remains
                   DECOMPOSERS ──→ mineral pool ──→ producers
                         ↘ heat escapes at each energy transfer
```

*The nutrient arrow returns to producers; the heat arrow does not.*

Imagine a pond after a dry season. Fish cannot persist simply because fish are present: water depth, dissolved substances, plants, prey and decomposers must also support them. An **ecosystem** is the interacting community plus its physical and chemical surroundings functioning together. A. G. Tansley introduced the term in **1935**. “Self-regulating” means feedbacks can maintain processes within limits, not that an ecosystem is closed, perfectly balanced or invulnerable to disturbance.

**Ecological organisation — enlarge the frame one level at a time**

```text
ORGANISM
one individual
    ↓ same species, same area and time
POPULATION
    ↓ interacting populations of different species
COMMUNITY
    ↓ community + abiotic setting + exchanges
ECOSYSTEM
    ↓ broad climatic formation containing many ecosystems
BIOME
    ↓ all ecosystems and life-supporting zones on Earth
BIOSPHERE
```

| Level | Decisive question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | Which individual is being studied? | One fish |
| Population | How does one species persist in this place? | All individuals of that fish species in the pond |
| Community | How do different populations interact? | Fish, algae, insects and microbes |
| Ecosystem | How do the community and abiotic setting function together? | Pond organisms plus water, sediment, light and nutrients |
| Biome | Which broad climate-linked formation contains many systems? | Tropical grassland or tropical rainforest |
| Biosphere | Where does life interact across the planet? | The global life-supporting zone |

The sequence is a change of analytical scale, not a claim that every boundary is sharp. A community excludes abiotic conditions; an ecosystem includes them. A biome is broader than one ecosystem and is characterised mainly by climate and dominant life forms.

**Biotic** means living: autotrophic producers, feeding consumers and organisms processing dead material. **Abiotic** means non-living conditions and material: light, temperature, rainfall, water, rock, soil, minerals and topography. Soil conditions are *edaphic*; temperature and rainfall are *climatic*. At the Earth-system scale the lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere provide the physical setting for the biosphere, the zone of life. A pond exchanges organisms, water and materials with its surroundings; defining a system is an analytical choice, not a claim that its boundary is sealed.

Why separate structure from function? **Structure** records which components exist and how they are arranged. **Function** asks what they do: fix energy, transfer food, break down remains and regenerate nutrients. Removing shoreline plants changes structure; eroded banks and reduced shelter show altered function. Conversely, apparent tree cover alone does not prove soil regeneration. A wetland cannot be assessed by inventorying birds while ignoring inflow and water chemistry.

**Objection:** “If the fish and plants are counted, the ecosystem has been described.” **Reply:** A count misses their relations with water and with each other. Yet relations cannot be inferred from one snapshot either; monitor conditions and processes over time. In a GS-III answer, open with a local system boundary, classify its biotic and abiotic components and trace one causal link to an outcome.

**Revision notes**

1. An organism is one individual; a population comprises members of the same species in a defined area and time.
2. A community contains interacting populations of different species.
3. An ecosystem adds the abiotic setting and exchanges of energy and matter to the community.
4. A biome is a broad climate-linked formation containing many ecosystems; it is not one local habitat.
5. The biosphere is the planetary zone of life, not a synonym for a single ecosystem.
6. Tansley introduced the term **ecosystem** in 1935.
7. Biotic components include producers, consumers and organisms processing dead matter.
8. Climatic factors include temperature and rainfall; edaphic factors concern soil and related ground conditions.
9. Structure means components and arrangement; function means processes and outcomes.
10. Open exchange can coexist with limited self-regulation; neither implies invulnerability.
11. Nutrients can return to producer-accessible pools, whereas transferred energy ultimately dissipates as heat.

What exactly do different organisms do *within* the same system? We next examine place and role.

### Concept check

**Question:** A village pond still contains the same fish species but has lost submerged vegetation and receives nutrient-rich runoff. How would you test whether its ecological functioning has changed?

**Attempt first:** Write the missing structural component, abiotic change and one process to monitor; only then uncover the reveal.

**Worked reveal**

**Model answer:** Compare the pond's vegetation and fish with its runoff inputs, food links, dissolved oxygen and decomposition over time. Lost vegetation changes shelter and primary production; runoff changes nutrient and oxygen conditions. An unchanged fish list is a snapshot of some structure, not a functional assessment.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Self-regulating” does not mean an ecosystem will neutralise unlimited polluted inflow.

**Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words.** Explain how biotic and abiotic components jointly determine the functioning of a village pond.

**Model (about 111 words):** A pond is a functional unit, not a collection of fish. Water depth, light and dissolved minerals constrain aquatic producers; their production supports consumers, while microbes return nutrients from dead material. For example, a village pond receiving silt-rich runoff may lose light penetration and submerged vegetation even if fish are still observed. The structural change reduces shelter and alters the route of energy into the food web. Assess both the living assemblage and water quality, seasonal depth and decomposition rather than assuming the visible fish population proves ecological health. “Self-regulation” operates only within limits; excessive nutrient input can overwhelm the system. Local management should protect catchment inflow as well as organisms.

**Why this earns marks (10):** Definition and system boundary 2; causal chain 4; named Indian setting 2; qualification and management 2. Merely listing abiotic factors would miss the directive “how”.

## Lesson 2 — An address, a job, and the spaces between

Progress: 2/9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Habitat, niche, ecotone and stratification

Book context: Queried — ecosystem terminology, niche dimensions, ecotones and altitudinal life zones.
CA search: "India April September 2026 ecotone habitat niche forest stratification official site:pib.gov.in"
CA found: No verified dated release specifically documenting an ecotone, niche or measured forest stratification in that window.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
           WESTERN GHATS FOREST (schematic)
           canopy:        light / branches / nesting
           understory:    filtered light / foliage
           shrubs:        cover / flowering plants
           forest floor:  litter / fungi / soil fauna
                           ↕ organisms use several layers
 forest ── transition zone (ecotone) ── grassland
```

*Height creates different opportunities within a forest; a transition is a zone, not a pencil line.*

Two birds can inhabit the same forest but feed at different heights or at different times. **Habitat** answers “where?”; a **niche** describes the multidimensional “how does it live and interact?” A practical decomposition asks about habitat niche (place), trophic niche (food acquisition) and reproductive niche (breeding conditions). This is a teaching decomposition, not three separate anatomical parts. Identical resource use in the same setting tends to provoke competition; competitive exclusion is a limiting principle under specific conditions, not proof that two species never overlap. Temporal or spatial **resource partitioning** allows coexistence despite partial overlap.

A **life zone** is an altitudinal band whose temperature and precipitation permit characteristic communities. It is not the same as a vertical layer *inside* one forest. **Stratification** names those layers—canopy, understory, shrub and floor. More layers can offer more niche opportunities, but layering alone neither establishes a numerical species count nor guarantees endemism. In the Western Ghats, use this as a plausible mechanism for diverse microhabitats, not as an unmeasured claim about every site.

At a forest–grassland junction or mangrove fringe, conditions and communities change across an **ecotone**. Its width varies; organisms from adjoining communities can coexist or compete there. The **edge effect** is a *possible tendency* for locally higher richness or density from overlap and additional resources. An exposed, polluted or human-created edge may instead lose specialist interior species. More visible edge activity is not automatically a conservation gain. In the Sundarbans, tidal and salinity gradients complicate any simple “two communities meet” picture.

**Objection:** “More edge always means more biodiversity.” **Reply:** Richness near some edges can rise, but fragmentation increases edge while shrinking intact interiors and connectivity; what happens depends on species, scale and disturbance. A GS-III response should distinguish pattern from unconditional rule. In Prelims, separate address from function and a transition *zone* from a boundary line.

**Revision notes**

1. Habitat answers **where an organism lives**; niche explains how it uses resources and interacts.
2. A practical niche analysis separates habitat, trophic and reproductive dimensions.
3. Species may share a habitat without occupying identical niches.
4. Competitive exclusion applies when resource use is sufficiently identical under the relevant conditions.
5. Resource partitioning can separate feeding time, height, prey or breeding space.
6. Stratification is vertical layering within one ecosystem, such as canopy, understory, shrub and forest floor.
7. A life zone is a broader altitude-related community shaped by temperature and precipitation.
8. An ecotone is a variable-width transition zone, not a cartographic line.
9. An edge effect is conditional; some edges support overlap, while exposed or fragmented edges can harm interior specialists.
10. More layers or more visible edge organisms do not alone prove endemism, connectivity or resilience.

These niches require a supply of usable food, which leads to production.

### Concept check

**Question:** A road divides a forest, creating more exposed border while the canopy layers inside surviving patches remain intact. What competing ecological effects should an assessment investigate?

**Attempt first:** Distinguish the new transition from the fate of interior specialists before reading below.

**Worked reveal**

**Model answer:** Assess both the new edge habitat, which may favour some organisms, and the reduced interior area and connectivity, which can harm interior specialists. Compare microclimate and movement across the road with undivided forest; vertical layering inside surviving patches does not automatically compensate for isolation.

**Misconception to avoid:** A conditional edge effect does not imply that fragmenting a continuous habitat improves conservation.

**Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words.** Differentiate habitat, niche and ecotone, and assess how forest fragmentation alters ecological opportunities.

**Model (about 147 words):** Habitat is the physical setting; niche is how an organism uses resources and interacts there. A forest bird may nest in canopy but forage below, so a shared habitat need not mean an identical niche. An ecotone is a transition zone between communities, not their cartographic border. A road through a Western Ghats forest creates exposed edges and may increase resources for some edge-tolerant species. Yet it also reduces continuous interior habitat and alters light, humidity and movement routes; local edge richness therefore cannot stand in for whole-landscape integrity. Stratification within each remaining patch offers niche variety, but patch isolation can interrupt reproduction and dispersal. The strongest case for development is access and connectivity for people; the ecological reply is to assess road alignment, crossing opportunities and cumulative fragmentation rather than declaring all roads impermissible. Monitor specialist occupancy and movement, not just species observed near the edge.

**Why this earns marks (15):** Three defined distinctions 4; mechanism and Indian case 5; objection and qualified reply 4; measurable verdict 2.

## Lesson 3 — The entrance and attrition of energy

Progress: 3/9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Autotrophs, productivity, biomass and trophic transfer

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — producers, trophic levels, dry biomass, productivity and energy transfer.
CA search: "India April September 2026 primary productivity phytoplankton oxygen official site:pib.gov.in"
CA found: No reliable dated last-six-month primary release establishing a new primary-productivity or oxygen-share estimate; no global percentage is asserted.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
LIGHT / chemical substrate
          ↓ captured by autotrophs
      GROSS FIXATION (GPP)
          ├── producer respiration → heat
          └── NET PRODUCTION (NPP) → growth → consumers / detritus
                                                ↓ respiration → heat
```

*Net production is what remains after producers meet their own respiratory costs.*

In a field the grass is not manufacturing sunlight; it captures light and stores chemical energy in organic matter. **Producers** are autotrophs that make organic food from inorganic carbon using light (most familiar plants, algae and phytoplankton) or chemical energy (certain chemosynthetic microorganisms). Do not claim *every* producer is green or that all ecosystems have sunlight at their immediate base. Energy ultimately needs an input: a deep-sea chemosynthetic system uses chemical substrates, not direct photosynthesis. Chemosynthesis is not a claim that energy appears from nothing.

**Gross primary productivity (GPP)** is the *rate* of total organic energy or carbon fixed by producers. They respire too. **Net primary productivity (NPP) = GPP − producer respiration**; it is the rate of new producer material potentially available to consumers *and* decomposers, not a guarantee that consumers eat it all. **Biomass** is a standing stock of organic material at a given time, commonly reported as dry mass. A fast-turnover plankton population can have modest standing biomass but substantial productivity. A standing stock is not a rate. This distinction matters when interpreting aquatic ecosystems.

**Three ecological pyramids answer three different questions**

```text
NUMBERS                         BIOMASS                         ENERGY
individual count               standing dry mass              transfer rate

grassland: upright             land: usually upright          always upright
      hawks                           hawks                         hawks
     rabbits                         rabbits                      rabbits
many grasses                    large grass mass             producer energy

single tree: inverted          water: often inverted         never inverted
many insects/parasites         consumers may outweigh        heat loss at every
above one producer             plankton standing crop        trophic transfer
```

| Pyramid | What is measured | Upright case | Inverted or irregular case | Precise qualification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Numbers | Individuals at each trophic level | Grassland: many grasses, fewer herbivores, still fewer predators | One tree may support many herbivorous insects; parasite chains can add still more organisms above a few hosts | Size is ignored, so one tree and one insect each count as one; spindle or diamond shapes are possible |
| Biomass | Standing dry organic mass at a stated time | Most terrestrial producer communities hold more biomass than their herbivores | Many aquatic systems have low standing phytoplankton biomass but greater consumer biomass at a snapshot | Rapid producer turnover supports consumers; inversion does not mean energy increases upward |
| Energy | Energy flow or production per unit area per unit time | Producers contain the widest energy base and successive levels narrow | None | Respiration and other losses make the energy pyramid always upright |

Conventional pyramids simplify a web into levels. Decomposers act on material from several levels and are often not fitted neatly into one bar; omnivores can occupy different positions in different feeding chains. Thus pyramid shape is parameter-specific and ecosystem-specific. A single pond can have an inverted biomass pyramid and an upright energy pyramid at the same time.

Energy enters a feeding level and is used for metabolism, movement, maintenance and growth; some passes to predators and some enters detritus. At successive transfers the useful fraction usually shrinks, and heat escapes. A roughly **10% transfer** is a classroom approximation, *not a fixed law for every species, site or trophic step*. Therefore food chains tend to be short and energy does **not** cycle back through decomposers to be reused as sunlight. Nutrients can return; energy cannot. Do not confuse the later treatment of energy pyramids with the specific task here: explain the mechanism, not an invented measured efficiency for an Indian site.

**Objection:** “If a decomposer feeds on a dead plant, energy has recycled to a living organism.” **Reply:** The decomposer uses *remaining chemical energy* in the detritus, dissipating more as heat. New producer growth still needs fresh incoming energy. An ocean-food-chain PYQ can be approached by identifying the self-feeding base before asking who consumes whom; never infer an individual organism's diet just from its habitat.

**Post-attempt PYQ review:** For 2021 Prelims GS-I Q22 on ocean-food-chain producers, revisit the choice made before Lesson 1. Classify each organism named in the official paper by energy acquisition rather than habitat; the procedure is not an answer key.

**Revision notes**

1. Producers use light or chemical substrates to build organic matter from inorganic carbon.
2. GPP is the total fixation rate; NPP equals GPP minus producer respiration.
3. NPP can enter grazing and detrital pathways; it is not identical to material actually eaten.
4. Biomass is a standing dry-mass stock, whereas productivity is a rate.
5. A pyramid of numbers counts individuals and ignores their body size.
6. Number pyramids may be upright, inverted or spindle-shaped; a single tree supporting many insects is a classic inversion.
7. A pyramid of biomass is usually upright on land but may be inverted in aquatic systems.
8. Aquatic biomass inversion reflects low standing crop and rapid phytoplankton turnover, not upward creation of energy.
9. A pyramid of energy measures flow through time and is always upright.
10. Omnivores and decomposers expose the limits of forcing a food web into fixed bars.
11. The “10% rule” is an approximation; transfer efficiency varies by organism and ecosystem.
12. Nutrients can cycle, but respiratory heat cannot be recycled into producer energy.

The next problem: most organisms do not feed in a single straight line.

### Concept check

**Question:** A coastal plankton community increases its gross fixation and producer respiration by equal amounts. How would you determine the change in net production available to downstream pathways?

**Attempt first:** Compare changes in GPP and respiration algebraically without presuming a change in NPP.

**Worked reveal**

**Model answer:** Subtract producer respiration from gross fixation before and after the change. Equal increases cancel, leaving NPP unchanged in this example. Even that net production is not all eaten by grazers: some becomes detritus.

**Misconception to avoid:** A rise in gross fixation is not automatically a rise in net energy reaching grazers.

**Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words.** Explain why energy diminishes along a food chain although decomposers return nutrients to producers.

**Model (about 111 words):** Producers capture incoming light or chemical energy and retain part as net primary production after respiration. A grazer assimilates only some consumed material and expends energy in movement and maintenance; later consumers repeat these losses. Thus energy transfer is directional and progressively dissipative. In an Indian mangrove creek, fallen leaves also feed a detrital pathway: microbes use remaining chemical energy while releasing mineral nutrients that mangroves can absorb again. This return of matter does not recover the heat dissipated during respiration. The familiar ten-per-cent transfer figure is an approximate teaching rule rather than a measurement for every mangrove trophic link. Protecting both primary production and detrital processing helps maintain the network.

**Why this earns marks (10):** Mechanism 5; explicit energy–nutrient contrast 2; bounded Indian example 2; qualification 1.

## Lesson 4 — A web of eating and cooperation

Progress: 4/9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Trophic roles, food webs and biotic interactions

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — producers, consumers, food chains, food webs and ecological interactions.
CA search: "India April September 2026 food web pollinators ecosystem official site:pib.gov.in"
CA found: No verified doctrine-specific official release in the last six months from that search.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
        grasses ──→ grazers ──→ predator
           │          │            ↑
           └──→ insects ──→ insect-eating bird
 flowering plant ←→ pollinating insect  (separate reproductive interaction)
 dead remains of every branch ──→ detrital system
```

*Feeding links and reproductive partnerships coexist; the diagram does not claim all named organisms share one field site.*

A **food chain** is a selected sequence of feeding transfers; a **food web** overlays many such sequences. Grass feeds more than one consumer, and a predator may use more than one prey. **Trophic level** describes feeding position, not a permanent badge on an animal: an omnivore may feed at different levels. **Herbivores** consume producers; **carnivores** consume animals; **omnivores** use both. **Filter feeding** describes straining suspended particles or organisms from water, not a trophic level: inspect what is filtered. A species' diet must be checked rather than guessed from its name.

Other interactions alter the web without being a predation arrow. **Mutualism** benefits both partners, as in some specialised plant–pollinator relationships; the interacting partners and degree of specificity have to be established for each plant rather than generalised to all trees. **Symbiosis** describes close interspecific association and does not by itself guarantee both benefit. **Parasitism** benefits one organism at the host's expense; a **parasitoid** develops in or on a host and ultimately kills it, unlike a conventional parasite that typically does not need to kill its host to complete its life cycle. Do not treat an entire insect order as sharing one feeding mode. Some insects cultivate fungi; this is not a license to assign fungus farming to a taxon without the question's actual evidence.

Alternative food links *can* buffer loss of one prey, but a web does not guarantee recovery when a keystone resource, pollinator or common abiotic support fails. A predator loss in an Indian grassland could release grazing pressure, yet species-specific responses, hunting, rainfall and land use must be investigated before alleging an observed trophic cascade. A flagship predator may also protect surrounding habitat if conservation secures corridors and prey, not simply because its numbers increase.

**Objection:** “A more connected web is always more stable.” **Reply:** Redundant links can cushion some shocks, whereas shared dependencies can transmit others; investigate interaction strengths and disturbance type. In Prelims, separate method of feeding, trophic position and interspecific relationship rather than memorising organism names.

**Revision notes**

1. A food chain is one selected feeding path; a food web connects many paths.
2. Trophic level describes a feeding position within a particular chain, not a permanent species label.
3. Herbivores consume producers; carnivores consume animals; omnivores use both.
4. An omnivore may occupy different trophic positions in different chains.
5. Filter feeding is a method of obtaining suspended material, not a trophic level.
6. Mutualism benefits both partners; symbiosis only specifies a close association and does not itself state the outcome.
7. A parasitoid develops in or on a host and ultimately kills it; this differs from ordinary predation and typical parasitism.
8. Specialised plant–pollinator relationships must be established species by species.
9. Alternative prey links can buffer some losses, but a shared drought or producer failure can affect the entire web.
10. A flagship animal protects wider functioning only when habitat, prey, movement and other interactions are maintained.

What happens to the remains when all these links die? Follow the detrital pathway.

*Fragmenting litter and chemically decomposing it are connected but not identical jobs.*

Why does a forest not simply accumulate an ever-thickening layer of unaltered leaves? In ecology “decomposer” may be used broadly for the whole detrital community; for precise Prelims reasoning distinguish fragmenters from microbial mineralisers. Fungi are not plants making food by photosynthesis; they obtain organic nutrients from other material. Decay also depends on oxygen, moisture and temperature. Waterlogging can slow oxygen-dependent pathways, so one cannot infer a uniform rate from the amount of litter alone.

Decomposer respiration releases energy as heat, not reusable energy back into a plant. Therefore the loop in the picture is the path of *elements*, not a perpetual-motion food chain. Wetlands can trap sediments and retain or transform some nutrients, but they are not unlimited waste-disposal plants: high load or altered hydrology can impair their functions.

**Objection:** “All leaf loss from a forest means successful recycling.” **Reply:** Removal by burning, runoff or export is not equivalent to local nutrient return. A relevant Prelims question may list candidate organisms; answer by their actual feeding behaviour, not by a convenient textbook archetype.

*A wetland is shaped as much by its catchment and water regime as by the organisms seen at the water surface.*

Surface water is their medium, not an oxygen-producing organism: photosynthesis makes oxygen, while oxygen can also dissolve from the atmosphere into water. Dissolved oxygen is usually reported as mass per volume of water; atmospheric oxygen is part of a gas mixture. To compare them, use the same basis (for example, oxygen mass per equal volume of air and water), rather than comparing an air percentage directly with a water concentration. At ordinary environmental conditions, even well-oxygenated surface water holds **much less oxygen per equal volume** than air; water temperature, salinity and pressure affect its dissolved concentration. This physical comparison is separate from photosynthetic output or *net atmospheric* oxygen gain after respiration and decay. Do not attach an unverified global oxygen-production share to rainforests or oceans. Some marine animals filter particles; filter feeding tells us how particles are collected, not which species is necessarily a primary producer.

An aquatic system may be marine (ocean), freshwater (lake, river) or transitional (estuary). The terrestrial category includes forests and grasslands; a **biome** is a large characteristic ecological formation, not a synonym for every local patch. A wetland's hydroperiod (when and how long it holds water), vegetation and sediment dynamics jointly govern food supply, refuge and floodwater storage. In the Sundarbans, mangrove roots, tidal exchange and salinity-dependent vegetation offer nursery habitat and help buffer some coastal impacts; protective performance depends on width, condition, local geomorphology and the event. Never promise total cyclone protection.

Heavy metals, however, may persist, accumulate or be remobilised. “Wetlands absorb all heavy metals safely” is both scientifically unsafe and a poor policy inference. Protect inflows and measure loads before attributing purification.

Combine canopy extent with species composition, recruitment, water and soil condition, food-web evidence and recovery after a known shock. WII habitat studies and BSI/ZSI surveys can help, but site-specific impact assessment and affected communities' observations remain important. Resist claiming that every diverse site is resilient or that every plantation is barren. Define the risk, then select indicators of the functions and recovery paths exposed to that risk.

**Why this earns marks (20):** Both proposals fairly stated 3; distinct limitations 6; named Indian evidence and concrete case 4; strongest objection and feasible reply 4; qualified assessment 3.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

**Attempt A — question (unworked):** A restored wetland shows more canopy cover and more harvested fish, but inflow sewage has increased. Which of those observations, if any, settles whether assimilative capacity improved?

**Stop; decide what additional measurements are needed before opening the reveal.**

**Worked reveal A:** Neither alone. Cover is structural extent and fish harvest is one provisioning outcome. Check load, oxygen, hydrology, contaminant retention and downstream effects across seasons before asserting improved assimilation.

**Attempt B — question (unworked):** Can a producer gain nutrients from dead matter while the useful energy once stored in that matter is already dissipating?

**Stop; trace an element and a joule along different paths.**

**Worked reveal B:** Yes. Mineral elements can return to soil/water and be taken up. Respiration by consumers and decomposers dissipates chemical energy as heat; fresh energy must enter for new production.

**Attempt C — question (unworked):** A food web offers alternate prey but all prey rely on the same drying lake. Does alternation alone guarantee recovery?

**Stop; identify the shared dependency.**

**Worked reveal C:** No. Alternate trophic links may buffer a lost prey species but cannot guarantee recovery after loss of the common water/production base. Observe the disturbance and regeneration.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These are **new practice questions**, not past-paper quotations. Write first; only then read each separate model.

**10 marks / 150 words — Question:** Explain why an increase in primary productivity is not proof of an increase in biodiversity in a forest landscape.

**Attempt before reveal:** Separate a rate of production from composition and resilience.

**Worked model (about 110 words):** Primary productivity is the rate at which producers build new organic material; biodiversity describes variation among organisms and their relationships. A rapidly growing single-species plantation in India can accumulate material quickly while offering fewer habitat and food options than a mixed native forest. Greater gross fixation may also be offset by greater producer respiration, so distinguish GPP from NPP before claiming more food reaches consumers. Yet low diversity cannot be deduced from productivity alone either: survey composition, regeneration, interactions and response to disturbance. The useful policy comparison is not “growth versus nature” in the abstract; it is whether the specific arrangement preserves the functions required by local communities and species.

**Scoring note (10):** Definitions 3; causal counterexample 4; evidence/qualification 3.

**15 marks / 250 words — Question:** Analyse the relationship between ecotones, habitat fragmentation and the interpretation of biodiversity indicators in India.

**Attempt before reveal:** Explain the edge effect, its non-universality and consequences for interiors.

**Worked model (about 154 words):** An ecotone is a transition between ecological communities; some transitions contain species from both and show a local edge effect. This does not mean that every new forest edge is beneficial. A road across a Western Ghats forest can increase exposed border while reducing interior area, shade and organism movement. A roadside species count may consequently rise even if interior specialists decline and reproduction is disrupted. The opposing argument is that edge habitats can legitimately support useful species; they should not be dismissed as devoid of value. Compare interior and edge species, connectedness and the disturbance regime rather than treating either count as a whole-landscape verdict. Stratification within forest patches can maintain niche opportunities, but it cannot automatically repair severed corridors. Road design and cumulative impact assessment should test alignments and movement routes; manage the habitat mosaic according to observed function and seasonal use, not the single indicator “more species at the new edge”.

**Scoring note (15):** Defined concepts 3; cause and Indian example 5; objection/reply 3; measurable planning 4.

**20 marks / 250 words — Question:** Evaluate ecosystem-based adaptation for a coastal district facing flood risk, livelihoods pressure and uncertain ecological data.

**Attempt before reveal:** Link one structural element to several functions; address alternatives, monitoring and distributional costs.

**Worked model (about 166 words):** Ecosystem-based adaptation protects processes that lower vulnerability while supporting livelihoods. In a Sundarbans-facing coastal district, connected mangroves and tidal creeks can provide fish nursery habitat, retain carbon and moderate some coastal impacts. Shoreline performance varies with site conditions and event strength; mangroves cannot replace evacuation or safe buildings. A fish-harvest measure reveals only provisioning benefit; flood buffering, nutrient processes and community ties also matter. MISHTI is a named restoration initiative, but its stated area ambition must not be presented as a verified protection outcome. A port or settlement may create substantial jobs; compare siting and alternatives, maintain tidal exchange and disclose who bears livelihood losses during restoration. Assess flood exposure, creek connectivity, native vegetation establishment, water quality and fish recruitment through repeated monitoring, using community knowledge alongside formal surveys. If data are scarce, report uncertainty and adopt reversible measures rather than inventing carbon or surge-reduction figures. Ecological function is one part of an adaptation portfolio, not a slogan or a substitute for engineering and social protection.

**Scoring note (20):** Functional mechanism 5; competing development case 4; named policy with target caveat 3; monitoring and distribution 5; qualified judgement 3.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### System boundary and material–energy logic

- Ecosystem: interacting living community plus abiotic physical/chemical setting; Tansley introduced the term in 1935. Open exchange and conditional self-regulation coexist.
- Organisation scale: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere. A community includes interacting populations; an ecosystem adds abiotic conditions and exchanges; a biome is a broad climate-linked formation.
- Lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere provide the biosphere's physical frame; climatic and edaphic influences shape local systems. Structure (components/arrangement) must be traced to function (process/outcome).
- Producers fix inorganic carbon using light or chemical energy; gross primary productivity is total fixation rate and NPP = GPP − producer respiration. NPP can enter grazing *or* detrital pathways.
- Biomass is standing organic material, conventionally reported as dry mass; it is not the same as a production rate. Approximate trophic transfer does not fix an efficiency for every system.
- Number pyramids may be upright, inverted or spindle-shaped; one tree can support many insects and parasite chains can add organisms above a few hosts.
- Biomass pyramids are usually upright on land but may invert in aquatic systems because rapidly replaced phytoplankton maintain low standing biomass while supporting larger consumer biomass.
- Energy pyramids are always upright because energy dissipates at every transfer; a biomass inversion never implies an energy inversion.
- Feeding, metabolism and decomposition dissipate energy as heat; mineral elements can return to producer-accessible pools. Never draw an energy-recycling arrow.

### Living connections and places

- Habitat = where; niche = role, resource use and relations, usefully analysed by place, feeding and reproduction. Competitive exclusion depends on conditions; resource partitioning allows partial overlap.
- Vertical stratification (canopy through litter) differs from altitudinal life zones. More layers can increase opportunities but do not alone prove endemism or resilience.
- Ecotone is a variable-width transition, often with competition and sometimes an edge effect; fragmented edges can harm interior specialists and movement even if local richness rises.
- Food chain = selected path; food web = connected paths. Herbivore, carnivore and omnivore are feeding categories; filter feeding is an acquisition method, not a level. An omnivore can enter more than one trophic position.
- Mutualism (both benefit), symbiosis (close association, outcome not specified) and parasitoid development (eventual host death) are distinct. Plant–pollinator partnerships vary; never extrapolate an exact species answer without the printed question.
- Detritivores ingest/fragment litter; bacteria and fungi perform key chemical decomposition. Moisture, oxygen, temperature and nutrient export affect actual cycling.

### Indian water, limits and adaptation

- Oceans, freshwater and estuaries have different conditions but retain producer–consumer–decomposer and abiotic dependencies. Phytoplankton produce by photosynthesis; water contains dissolved oxygen, not biological production by water itself. Compare the oxygen *concentration* of water and air on the same volume basis; ordinary well-oxygenated water holds much less oxygen than air, with solubility varying by temperature, salinity and pressure.
- Wetland vegetation, hydrology and microbes can retain sediments, process nutrients and support food webs; they do not make an unlimited heavy-metal sink. The June 2026 Ramsar designation records recognition, not a measured restoration.
- Sundarbans mangrove structure can support fish nurseries, carbon storage and limited coastal buffering; quantify only with site- and event-specific evidence.
- Carrying capacity is conditional on regenerative supply, assimilation of wastes, consumption and management over time. The 2019 GS-III Q17 demand has **Define + Explain regional planning use**: map seasonal supplies and pollution loads, evaluate location and extraction choices, monitor indicators and revise limits; a concept definition alone misses its second directive.
- MoEFCC policy, WII habitat ecology and BSI/ZSI surveys inform protected-area, forest and EIA decisions. Species inventory, designation and clearance are not proofs of function.

### Valuation, stability and response

- MEA services: provisioning, regulating, supporting, cultural. Avoid double-counting supporting processes already reflected in outputs; IPBES Nature's Contributions to People gives room to material, regulating, non-material and relational values.
- MISHTI's cited restoration extent is a target, not an observed carbon or shoreline result; NPCA concerns aquatic conservation. Test hydrology, establishment and affected livelihoods.
- Resistance is limited change during a shock; resilience is recovery afterward. A diverse food web may buffer some losses but not a shared drought or disrupted foundational resource.
- ISFR 2023 forest-and-tree-cover figure is dated extent, **8,27,357 sq km / 25.17%**, not native forest composition or measured health. A productive plantation is not automatically diverse or resilient.
- Keystone influence, flagship appeal and umbrella-habitat benefits differ. Protect corridors and ecosystem processes rather than treating an isolated animal count as proof of recovery.
- Examiner chain: disturbance → structural link lost → functional loss → affected people → appropriate indicator → governance option → uncertainty and residual risk.
