# IPCC Assessment Reports — Live Session Edition

The IPCC is best understood as a **global assessment system**. It does not negotiate treaties, set national targets or run a climate laboratory. It asks expert teams to assess the published evidence, makes uncertainty visible, and gives governments a common scientific reference for climate decisions.

### 1. The mandate

The World Meteorological Organization and the United Nations Environment Programme established the IPCC in 1988. It is an intergovernmental body with 195 member states. Its central task is to assess published scientific, technical and socio-economic knowledge concerning climate change, its impacts and future risks, and options for adaptation and mitigation.

Three negatives are crucial:

1. It does **not** conduct its own primary research.
2. It does **not** negotiate climate treaties; that is the UNFCCC process.
3. It does **not** prescribe a single policy to governments.

Its reports are therefore **policy-relevant but not policy-prescriptive**. They may assess costs, feasibility, risks and trade-offs, but the political choice among options remains with governments and societies.

### 2. The Panel and the Bureau are not the same

| Institution | Composition | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| Panel | Representatives of member governments meeting in plenary | Elects leadership, agrees the work programme and outlines, and formally endorses reports |
| IPCC Bureau | Chair, Vice-Chairs, Working Group Co-Chairs and Vice-Chairs, and Task Force Co-Chairs | Scientific and technical guidance, management advice and specified decisions such as expert selection |
| Task Force Bureau | Co-Chairs and regional members supporting the TFI | Guides greenhouse-gas-inventory methodology work |
| Secretariat | Permanent coordinating office hosted at WMO in Geneva | Organises sessions, supports governance and coordinates report production |
| Technical Support Units | Teams supporting each Working Group and the TFI | Day-to-day scientific, technical and organisational support |

The Bureau is elected for an assessment cycle and is designed to reflect geographic balance as well as scientific and technical requirements. Bureau members guide the process; they do not personally write every report.

### 3. The three Working Groups

**Working Group I — The Physical Science Basis**

It assesses observations, climate processes, attribution, models, future projections, carbon budgets, oceans, cryosphere, extremes and sea level.

**Working Group II — Impacts, Adaptation and Vulnerability**

It assesses observed and projected impacts on ecosystems and societies, exposure, vulnerability, risk, adaptation options, adaptation limits and climate-resilient development.

**Working Group III — Mitigation of Climate Change**

It assesses emissions trends, sectoral mitigation, technologies, behaviour and demand, policy instruments, finance, feasibility, equity and pathways. It assesses options without advocating one specific option.

### 4. The Task Force on National Greenhouse Gas Inventories

The TFI develops and refines methods for estimating national greenhouse-gas emissions and removals. Its products support comparable national inventories used in climate reporting. It also supports inventory software and an Emission Factor Database.

> **UPSC trap:** The TFI is not a fourth Working Group. It is a separate Task Force alongside the three Working Groups.

### 5. Why this design matters

The Panel gives reports intergovernmental standing; the author teams provide scientific assessment; the review system tests balance and traceability; and the Secretariat and Technical Support Units keep a very large international process operational. No single component can substitute for the others.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** WMO + UNEP; three Working Groups; TFI; assessment rather than research.
- **Mains:** Explain why an intergovernmental assessment body can possess both scientific authority and political usability without becoming a treaty negotiator.
- **Trap:** Do not call the IPCC a UNFCCC subsidiary body. Its reports inform UNFCCC negotiations, but the institutions are distinct.

### Revision notes

1. The IPCC was established in 1988 by WMO and UNEP.
2. It has 195 member states.
3. The Panel is the plenary body of member governments.
4. The Bureau supplies scientific, technical and strategic guidance.
5. Working Group I assesses physical climate science.
6. Working Group II assesses impacts, vulnerability and adaptation.
7. Working Group III assesses mitigation.
8. The TFI develops national inventory methodologies.
9. The Secretariat is hosted at WMO in Geneva.
10. IPCC reports are policy-relevant, not policy-prescriptive.
11. The IPCC assesses existing knowledge; it does not conduct primary research.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it incorrect to describe the TFI as Working Group IV?

**Model answer:** The IPCC has three Working Groups. The Task Force on National Greenhouse Gas Inventories is a separate body that develops and refines inventory methodologies; it is not numbered as a Working Group.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every scientific arm under the Panel as a Working Group obscures the TFI's distinct methodology function.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain the institutional architecture through which the IPCC converts dispersed climate knowledge into a usable global assessment.

**Illustrative model:** The IPCC combines intergovernmental legitimacy with expert assessment. Its 195 member governments constitute the Panel, which elects leadership, agrees work programmes and report outlines, and formally endorses reports. Three Working Groups divide the substantive task into physical science, impacts/adaptation/vulnerability, and mitigation. The Task Force on National Greenhouse Gas Inventories separately develops methods for comparable national emissions and removals estimates. Expert authors assess published literature, while Review Editors protect the integrity of responses to comments. The Bureau provides scientific and strategic guidance; the Secretariat and Technical Support Units coordinate production. This structure allows the IPCC to be policy-relevant without negotiating treaties or prescribing a national policy. Its authority therefore rests on a combination of broad governmental participation, diverse expertise, transparent review and calibrated communication rather than on original laboratory research.

**Quantified rubric (10):** mandate and boundary 2; Panel/Bureau distinction 2; Working Groups and TFI 3; support and review architecture 1; analytical conclusion 2.

### Visual: choose the report by the question being asked

| If the question is... | Product | Example |
|---|---|---|
| What is the state of knowledge across the whole field? | Assessment Report | AR6 Working Group reports |
| What urgent, bounded theme needs focused assessment? | Special Report | Global Warming of 1.5°C; Ocean and Cryosphere; Climate Change and Land |
| How should countries measure and report inventories? | Methodology Report | 2006 Guidelines; 2019 Refinement |
| What integrated message emerges from the cycle? | Synthesis Report | AR6 Synthesis Report, 2023 |
| What can be summarised from already assessed material for a specific request? | Technical Paper | Based on existing IPCC reports |

The products differ by purpose, scope and evidentiary function—not simply by length.

### 1. Assessment Reports

A numbered assessment cycle produces broad Working Group contributions and a concluding Synthesis Report. The Working Group reports are not three volumes of the same subject:

```text
WG I: What is happening physically, and why?
WG II: What risks and impacts follow, who is vulnerable, and what can adaptation do?
WG III: How can emissions be reduced or removals enhanced, and under what conditions?
```

AR6 was completed through WGI in 2021, WGII and WGIII in 2022, and the Synthesis Report in 2023.

### 2. Special Reports

Special Reports assess a specific policy-relevant theme within or between assessment cycles. In the sixth cycle:

- **SR1.5 (2018):** impacts of 1.5°C warming and related pathways;
- **Climate Change and Land (2019):** land degradation, food security, land management and greenhouse-gas fluxes;
- **Ocean and Cryosphere in a Changing Climate (2019):** oceans, ice, sea level and associated risks.

A Special Report is not merely a shorter Assessment Report. Its defining feature is a focused question approved by the Panel.

### 3. Methodology Reports

Methodology Reports provide practical methods and guidance, especially for national greenhouse-gas inventories. Their central question is not “What will the climate be?” but “How should emissions and removals be estimated and reported consistently?”

This is why methodology can exercise quiet power: a country's claim depends partly on definitions, system boundaries, categories, emission factors and treatment of removals.

### 4. Synthesis Reports

The Synthesis Report integrates the Working Group contributions and relevant Special Reports. It does not commission a fresh body of primary research. Its value is integration:

```text
physical change
    + exposure and vulnerability
    + response options
    + equity, finance and feasibility
    = integrated risk-and-action picture
```

The AR6 Synthesis Report contains a Summary for Policymakers and a longer report. Its SPM is approved line by line, while the longer report is adopted section by section.

### 5. Technical Papers and supporting material

Technical Papers are based on material already contained in IPCC reports and respond to a defined need; they are not another full assessment. Workshop material, expert-meeting material, databases and software can support IPCC work but should not be confused with an Assessment Report.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims trap:** “Special” means theme-specific, not unofficial.
- **Prelims trap:** A Methodology Report can be highly consequential without projecting future temperature.
- **Mains use:** Match evidence to the report: WGI for physical findings, WGII for risk and adaptation, WGIII for mitigation, Synthesis for integration.

### Revision notes

1. Assessment Reports provide broad periodic assessments.
2. Each Working Group has a distinct substantive domain.
3. Special Reports answer focused, Panel-approved questions.
4. SR1.5 was released in 2018.
5. The land and ocean/cryosphere Special Reports were released in 2019.
6. Methodology Reports standardise estimation and reporting methods.
7. The TFI leads inventory-methodology work.
8. A Synthesis Report integrates cycle-wide findings.
9. Technical Papers rely on material already assessed.
10. Report type determines what claim it can support.

### Concept check

**Question:** Which report type should be cited for rules used to estimate national emissions, and why?

**Model answer:** A Methodology Report should be cited because it develops the technical methods, categories and guidance used to estimate and report national greenhouse-gas emissions and removals consistently.

**Misconception to avoid:** Citing a Working Group projection as if it were the inventory-accounting rulebook.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish IPCC Assessment, Special, Methodology and Synthesis Reports.

**Illustrative model:** Assessment Reports periodically review the full knowledge base through three Working Group contributions: physical science; impacts, adaptation and vulnerability; and mitigation. Special Reports examine a bounded, urgent theme, as SR1.5 did for the difference between 1.5°C and higher warming. Methodology Reports perform a different task: they establish methods for estimating and reporting national greenhouse-gas emissions and removals, supporting comparability among inventories. The Synthesis Report then integrates the Working Group and relevant Special Report findings into an overarching account suitable for policymakers. Thus, the categories are functional rather than merely differences in size. An Assessment Report supplies breadth, a Special Report supplies thematic focus, a Methodology Report supplies an accounting method, and a Synthesis Report supplies integration. Correct report identification prevents candidates from attributing a scientific projection to an inventory manual or treating an outline as an assessed finding.

**Quantified rubric (10):** assessment 2; special 2; methodology 2; synthesis 2; distinction and example quality 2.

The strength of the process lies in iteration and traceability, not in a single act of peer review.

### 1. Scoping and outline

The Panel first decides to prepare a product. A scoping process identifies its central questions and chapter structure. The Panel then agrees the outline. An agreed outline defines the assessment's scope; it does **not** establish any scientific conclusion.

### 2. Author roles

| Role | Function |
|---|---|
| Coordinating Lead Author | Coordinates a chapter and integrates its assessment |
| Lead Author | Drafts assigned sections and evaluates the evidence |
| Contributing Author | Supplies specific expertise, text, data or figures |
| Review Editor | Ensures substantive review comments receive appropriate consideration |
| Chapter Scientist | Provides technical and organisational support where appointed |

Authors are selected after nominations by governments, observer organisations and the Bureau. Selection considers expertise, range of scientific and socio-economic views, geography, gender, and a mix of experienced and first-time IPCC participants. Industry and non-governmental experts may participate where their expertise is relevant.

### 3. Evidence base

Peer-reviewed literature receives priority, but relevant government, industry and other non-peer-reviewed material may be used. Such material carries an additional responsibility to establish quality and validity. The IPCC assesses evidence; it does not treat publication status alone as proof.

### 4. Review stages

The First Order Draft undergoes expert review. Authors then prepare a Second Order Draft and a first SPM draft. These undergo simultaneous expert and government review. Review Editors monitor whether substantive comments are addressed. After finalisation, review comments and author responses are made public.

This structure corrects a common simplification:

```text
not: governments write the science
not: scientists publish without government scrutiny
but: experts assess; reviewers challenge; governments review;
     scientists retain responsibility for the assessment;
     the SPM is agreed with governments in plenary
```

### 5. Approval, adoption and acceptance

| Term | Meaning in IPCC procedure | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Approval | Detailed line-by-line discussion and agreement | Summary for Policymakers |
| Adoption | Section-by-section endorsement | Synthesis longer report; overview parts of Methodology Reports |
| Acceptance | Recognition that material is a comprehensive, objective and balanced assessment without line-by-line endorsement | Underlying Working Group reports |

These words are procedural terms. They are not interchangeable synonyms.

### 6. Why government participation does not erase scientific responsibility

Government delegates can seek clarity, consistency and policy relevance in the SPM. Scientist-authors must ensure the approved wording remains supported by the underlying report. If wording cannot be supported, it cannot legitimately stand merely because delegates prefer it.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** author selection follows nominations; authors volunteer expertise; reports undergo multiple review rounds.
- **Mains:** The IPCC's legitimacy comes from an unusual combination of expert responsibility, open review and intergovernmental endorsement.
- **Trap:** “Approved line by line” applies to the SPM, not every sentence of every chapter.

### Revision notes

1. The Panel first agrees the product and outline.
2. An outline is a scope document, not a scientific finding.
3. Experts are nominated by governments, observers and the Bureau.
4. The relevant Bureau selects author teams.
5. Diversity criteria accompany expertise.
6. First Order Draft review is by experts.
7. Second Order Draft and first SPM draft receive expert and government review.
8. Review Editors monitor treatment of substantive comments.
9. Review comments and responses are published after completion.
10. SPMs are approved line by line.
11. Underlying reports are accepted.
12. The Synthesis longer report is adopted section by section.

### Concept check

**Question:** Does government approval of an SPM mean that governments conduct or own the underlying research?

**Model answer:** No. Expert authors assess the literature and remain responsible for scientific consistency. Governments participate in review and line-by-line SPM approval, while the underlying report is accepted rather than rewritten line by line by governments.

**Misconception to avoid:** Conflating intergovernmental endorsement of a summary with political authorship of the assessed science.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse how the IPCC report-preparation process attempts to combine scientific rigour, diversity and political legitimacy.

**Illustrative model:** The IPCC begins with Panel-approved scope and chapter outlines, preventing an author team from defining the entire mandate unilaterally. Governments, observer organisations and the Bureau nominate experts; the relevant Bureau selects Coordinating Lead Authors, Lead Authors and Review Editors with attention to expertise, disciplinary range, geography, gender and prior IPCC experience. Authors assess peer-reviewed literature while using other relevant material only with additional quality checks.

Rigour is reinforced through iteration. The First Order Draft receives expert review. A revised Second Order Draft and first Summary for Policymakers are reviewed by experts and governments. Review Editors ensure substantive comments receive consideration, and comments and responses are later published. At plenary, governments and authors approve the SPM line by line, while the underlying Working Group report is accepted.

This creates dual legitimacy: technical claims remain anchored in the assessed report, while governments gain ownership of the policy-relevant summary. The same design can encourage cautious consensus language, but government preference cannot legitimately create a finding unsupported by the underlying assessment. The process is therefore neither ordinary journal peer review nor treaty negotiation; it is a transparent, iterative science-policy assessment.

**Quantified rubric (15):** scope and selection 3; evidence treatment 2; review sequence 4; endorsement distinctions 2; tension and qualification 3; conclusion 1.

## Lesson 4 — Confidence, Likelihood and the Discipline of Uncertainty

Progress: 4 / 12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Calibrated language

Book context: IPCC uncertainty guidance and the AR6 Synthesis SPM provide the controlling definitions.
CA Search: "IPCC calibrated uncertainty confidence likelihood official"
CA Found: None used in this lesson.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: two different questions

```text
CONFIDENCE asks:
"How strong is the finding, considering evidence and agreement?"
        evidence: limited / medium / robust
        agreement: low / medium / high
        output: very low → low → medium → high → very high confidence

LIKELIHOOD asks:
"What probability is assigned to this outcome or range?"
        output: calibrated probability language
```

Confidence is not a hidden probability; likelihood is not a synonym for evidentiary strength.

### 1. Confidence

Confidence synthesises expert judgement about the **type, amount, quality and consistency of evidence** and the **degree of agreement**. It uses five levels:

```text
very low | low | medium | high | very high
```

“High confidence” does not mean “90% probability.” It means the assessment team judges the finding to have strong evidentiary and agreement support.

### 2. Likelihood

| IPCC term | Assessed probability |
|---|---:|
| Virtually certain | 99–100% |
| Very likely | 90–100% |
| Likely | 66–100% |
| More likely than not | >50–100% |
| About as likely as not | 33–66% |
| Unlikely | 0–33% |
| Very unlikely | 0–10% |
| Exceptionally unlikely | 0–1% |

The additional expressions **extremely likely** (95–100%) and **extremely unlikely** (0–5%) may also be used where appropriate.

### 3. Ranges, best estimates and tails

A projection can contain several different statements:

- a **best estimate**;
- a **likely range**;
- a confidence level in the underlying assessment;
- a low-likelihood, high-impact outcome outside the likely range.

These must not be collapsed. For example, WGI gives likely sea-level ranges under scenarios while also stating that higher outcomes linked to deeply uncertain ice-sheet processes cannot be ruled out. The tail is relevant to risk management without becoming the central estimate.

### 4. Why “low confidence” does not mean “false”

Low confidence may reflect limited evidence, disagreement or both. It signals that the statement should be used cautiously and with its conditions visible. Conversely, “high confidence” does not make a regional figure universal.

### 5. Risk language

WGII often assesses risk through interaction:

```text
CLIMATE HAZARD × EXPOSURE × VULNERABILITY
                  ↓
                RISK
```

A hazard can intensify while risk changes differently across places because exposure, poverty, infrastructure and adaptive capacity differ. This is why global climate information cannot be mechanically converted into an India-specific damage number.

### UPSC application

- Quote the confidence or likelihood attached to a finding when it matters.
- Distinguish a central range from a low-likelihood tail.
- In a Mains answer, use uncertainty to qualify action—not to imply ignorance.
- In Prelims, reject any statement equating “high confidence” with a fixed probability.

### Revision notes

1. Confidence combines evidence and agreement.
2. Confidence uses five qualitative levels.
3. Likelihood expresses quantified probability.
4. “Likely” means 66–100%.
5. “Very likely” means 90–100%.
6. “Virtually certain” means 99–100%.
7. “About as likely as not” means 33–66%.
8. Confidence is not statistical probability.
9. A likely range is not the full set of physically possible outcomes.
10. Low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes remain relevant to risk.
11. Risk joins hazard, exposure and vulnerability.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a finding be “high confidence” without being described as “very likely”?

**Model answer:** High confidence summarises the strength and agreement of evidence, whereas very likely assigns a 90–100% probability to an outcome. The finding may support a strong qualitative judgement without a defensible numerical probability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Translating every confidence label into a probability percentage.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Why is calibrated uncertainty language a strength rather than a weakness of IPCC assessments?

**Illustrative model:** IPCC uncertainty language separates two questions. Confidence expresses how evidence quality, quantity, consistency and expert agreement support a finding. Likelihood assigns a calibrated probability to an outcome, such as “likely” for 66–100% or “virtually certain” for 99–100%. This prevents false precision and allows policymakers to distinguish a robust conclusion, a probable range and a low-likelihood high-impact tail. It also makes disagreement and regional limitations visible instead of hiding them behind a single headline. For risk management, uncertainty does not imply inaction: severe consequences may justify preparation even when probability is low. The system therefore improves traceability and guards against both exaggeration and sceptical misuse. Its limitation is communicative—readers may wrongly interpret “medium confidence” as weak probability—so answers must preserve the exact qualifier and explain what it measures.

**Quantified rubric (10):** confidence 2; likelihood 2; risk value 2; misuse prevention 2; limitation and conclusion 2.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: scenario architecture changed

```text
EARLIER SRES
socio-economic and emissions storylines
        ↓
AR5 / CMIP5: RCPs
concentration pathways labelled by approximate 2100 radiative forcing
        ↓
AR6 / CMIP6: SSP–forcing combinations
socio-economic pathway + forcing level
        ↓
climate projection, impacts and response analysis
```

Scenarios are conditional explorations of possible futures, not forecasts of what will happen.

### 1. RCP: a concentration pathway

**Representative Concentration Pathways** describe trajectories of atmospheric concentrations and their approximate radiative forcing by 2100. The familiar labels—RCP2.6, RCP4.5, RCP6.0 and RCP8.5—refer approximately to watts per square metre of forcing, not to degrees Celsius.

An RCP label does not itself specify one complete economy, population path or policy story. Different socio-economic developments could, in principle, lead to a similar forcing pathway.

### 2. SSP: a socio-economic pathway

**Shared Socioeconomic Pathways** describe alternative societal development conditions:

| SSP family | Broad narrative |
|---|---|
| SSP1 | Sustainability |
| SSP2 | Middle of the road |
| SSP3 | Regional rivalry |
| SSP4 | Inequality |
| SSP5 | Fossil-fuelled development |

These narratives describe challenges to mitigation and adaptation. They are not moral rankings or predictions.

### 3. Combined AR6 labels

In **SSP2-4.5**:

- `SSP2` identifies the socio-economic pathway;
- `4.5` identifies the approximate radiative forcing level in 2100.

WGI prominently assessed five illustrative scenarios:

```text
SSP1-1.9  very low emissions
SSP1-2.6  low emissions
SSP2-4.5  intermediate emissions
SSP3-7.0  high emissions
SSP5-8.5  very high emissions
```

### 4. What a scenario is not

- not a prediction;
- not an assigned probability;
- not an IPCC recommendation;
- not automatically a policy target;
- not a temperature label;
- not proof that the socio-economic story and forcing outcome are inseparable in every model exercise.

The IPCC explicitly treats scenarios as possible sets of human choices and conditions, without declaring one set more probable than another.

### 5. The RCP8.5 trap

RCP8.5 and SSP5-8.5 are high or very high emissions pathways. It is unsafe to call either simply “business as usual” without defining assumptions and context. They remain useful for stress testing and risk analysis, but usefulness does not establish probability.

### 6. Scenario, projection and forecast

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scenario | A conditional set of assumptions |
| Projection | Modelled outcome conditional on those assumptions |
| Forecast/prediction | Claim about the future expected to occur |

The correct grammar is: **“Under SSP5-8.5, the modelled projection is…”** rather than **“The IPCC predicts SSP5-8.5 will happen.”**

### UPSC application

- Decode both halves of an SSP label.
- State the baseline and time period attached to a projection.
- Never convert forcing directly into temperature by reading the label.
- Use low and high scenarios to show how human choices change outcomes.

### Revision notes

1. SRES preceded RCPs.
2. RCPs were central to AR5/CMIP5.
3. RCP numbers refer approximately to 2100 radiative forcing.
4. RCP numbers are not warming levels.
5. SSPs are socio-economic pathways.
6. AR6 combines SSP narratives with forcing levels.
7. SSP2-4.5 joins a middle-of-the-road narrative with about 4.5 W/m² forcing.
8. Scenarios are conditional, not predictions.

### Concept check

**Question:** What do the two parts of “SSP1-2.6” represent?

**Model answer:** SSP1 represents a sustainability-oriented socio-economic pathway, while 2.6 represents the approximate radiative forcing level in 2100. The label defines a conditional scenario, not a forecast or a 2.6°C temperature rise.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading the number after the hyphen as projected warming in degrees Celsius.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain the evolution from RCPs to SSP-based scenarios and identify the main interpretive errors candidates should avoid.

**Illustrative model:** Representative Concentration Pathways, used prominently in AR5, describe concentration trajectories labelled by approximate radiative forcing in 2100. Thus, RCP4.5 refers to forcing, not 4.5°C of warming. An RCP also does not contain one necessary population, technology or policy history.

Shared Socioeconomic Pathways add structured societal narratives. SSP1 emphasises sustainability, SSP2 a middle-of-the-road development path, SSP3 regional rivalry, SSP4 inequality and SSP5 fossil-fuelled development. AR6 combines a socio-economic pathway with a forcing level: in SSP2-4.5, the first element is the societal pathway and the second is approximate 2100 forcing.

Three cautions follow. First, scenarios are conditional explorations, not predictions or assigned probabilities. Second, a scenario label is neither an IPCC recommendation nor a Paris target. Third, a high-emissions pathway such as SSP5-8.5 may be useful for stress testing without being described loosely as the inevitable “business-as-usual” future.

The analytical gain from SSP–forcing combinations is that climate outcomes can be connected to social choices while retaining multiple possible futures. Accurate answers therefore use the formula: assumptions → emissions and concentrations → forcing → projected climate response.

### Visual: risk can be reduced, transferred or left residual

```text
HAZARD × EXPOSURE × VULNERABILITY
              ↓
            RISK
              ↓
     ┌────────┴─────────┐
     ▼                  ▼
ADAPTATION          MITIGATION
reduces present     limits future

     └─ hard limit

      LOSSES AND DAMAGES
```

Mitigation, adaptation and loss-and-damage responses answer different parts of the risk chain.

### 1. Unequal vulnerability

The AR6 Synthesis Report assessed that approximately **3.3–3.6 billion people** live in contexts highly vulnerable to climate change. Vulnerable communities that contributed least to current climate change are disproportionately affected.

This does not mean every person in that range faces the same hazard. Vulnerability is shaped by poverty, marginalisation, infrastructure, governance, health, ecosystem condition and access to finance.

Between 2010 and 2020, mortality from floods, droughts and storms was **15 times higher in highly vulnerable regions than in regions with very low vulnerability** (**high confidence**). The number measures unequal outcomes across vulnerability contexts; it is not a claim that hazard frequency alone was fifteen times greater.

WGII also found that climate change has caused substantial damages and increasingly irreversible losses in terrestrial, freshwater, coastal and open-ocean ecosystems (**high confidence**). Some first climate-driven species extinctions are already irreversible (**medium confidence**), while glacier retreat and permafrost thaw are pushing other changes toward irreversibility.

### 2. Adaptation gaps

WGII found adaptation planning and implementation across all regions, but progress is uneven. Much observed adaptation is fragmented, small-scale, incremental, sector-specific and focused on planning or current risks. At current rates, the adaptation gap will continue to grow.

### 3. Soft and hard limits

| Limit | Meaning | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Soft adaptation limit | Adaptation is currently constrained but the constraint may be overcome | Finance, institutions, knowledge or technology limit coastal protection |
| Hard adaptation limit | No feasible adaptive action can avoid intolerable risk | Some ecosystems lose their capacity to adapt beyond critical warming |

WGII assessed that some warm-water coral reefs, coastal wetlands, rainforests, polar systems and mountain systems are already reaching or surpassing hard limits. Human systems may face soft limits because of finance, governance or technical constraints.

### 4. Maladaptation

Maladaptation increases or shifts vulnerability, raises emissions, worsens inequity or reduces future options. A seawall may protect assets in the short term yet encourage more exposure behind it or damage coastal ecosystems if it is isolated from a long-term adaptive plan.

The test is not “Was an adaptation project built?” but “Did it reduce long-term, distribution-sensitive risk without locking in new harm?”

### 5. Losses and damages

The IPCC uses “losses and damages” descriptively for adverse observed or projected impacts, including economic and non-economic harm. It is related to, but not identical with, the negotiated UNFCCC category and its finance arrangements.

```text
mitigation reduces future warming
adaptation reduces manageable risk
limits + residual risk leave harm
that harm appears as losses and damages
```

Avoid saying all climate damage is “beyond adaptation.” Some is avoidable; some can be reduced; some remains residual; and some systems reach limits.

### 6. Climate-resilient development

WGII links adaptation with mitigation and sustainable development. Inclusive governance, ecosystem protection, finance, local and Indigenous knowledge, climate services and social protection can reduce vulnerability while avoiding maladaptation.

- Separate hazard from exposure and vulnerability.
- Define soft and hard limits before giving examples.
- Keep “losses and damages” as an assessed impact concept distinct from a particular fund.
- Evaluate adaptation by equity, flexibility and long-term risk.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is inadequate finance usually a soft rather than a hard adaptation limit?

**Model answer:** Finance can block implementation under present conditions, but it can potentially be increased or redirected. A hard limit exists when no feasible adaptation can avoid intolerable risk, even if resources are available.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling every expensive or politically difficult adaptation problem physically impossible.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish adaptation gaps, maladaptation, adaptation limits and losses and damages.

**Illustrative model:** An adaptation gap is the difference between implemented adaptation and the level needed to meet an accepted risk-reduction goal. AR6 finds that many measures remain fragmented, incremental and unequally distributed, so the gap is growing.

Maladaptation is different: it is action that increases or shifts vulnerability, raises emissions, entrenches inequality or closes future options. A protective structure can become maladaptive if it encourages risky development or damages natural buffers.

An adaptation limit is reached when objectives cannot be secured through adaptation. A soft limit reflects constraints such as finance, knowledge, governance or technology that may be overcome. A hard limit exists when no feasible action can avoid intolerable risk; some coral reefs and other ecosystems are already approaching or surpassing such limits.

Losses and damages are adverse economic or non-economic impacts that occur despite, in the absence of, or beyond adaptation. The assessment concept should not be reduced to one international fund.

The four ideas therefore form a progression: insufficient action creates gaps; poorly designed action creates maladaptation; escalating warming creates limits; and residual impacts create losses and damages. Mitigation remains essential because adaptation cannot indefinitely offset rising hazards.

Carbon dioxide removal complements deep cuts; it does not erase the need to reduce gross emissions.

### 1. The 2019 emissions baseline

WGIII assessed global net anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions at **59 ± 6.6 GtCO₂-eq in 2019**. This was about **12% higher than 2010** and **54% higher than 1990**. Average annual emissions in 2010–2019 were higher than in any previous decade, although their growth rate slowed.

### 2. The policy and NDC gap

WGIII assessed that global 2030 emissions associated with NDCs announced before COP26 would make it **likely** that warming exceeds 1.5°C during the twenty-first century and would require rapid post-2030 acceleration to keep warming below 2°C. Policies implemented by the end of 2020 were projected to produce still higher emissions.

The AR6 Synthesis Report translates the implemented-policy literature into a temperature outcome: **3.2°C warming by 2100, with a 2.2–3.5°C range** (**medium confidence**). This is conditional on the assessed policy set and literature cut-off, not a forecast immune to later policy change.

### 3. Pathways consistent with temperature limits

In modelled pathways with immediate action:

| Pathway | Global net GHG change from 2019 by 2030 | By 2050 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Limit warming to 2°C (>67%) | −27% [13–45%] | −63% [52–76%] |
| Limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%), no/limited overshoot | −43% [34–60%] | −84% [73–98%] |

Global net-zero CO₂ is reached in the **early 2050s** in pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C with no or limited overshoot, and around the **early 2070s** in pathways limiting warming to 2°C.

These are global modelled pathways, not identical national quotas.

### 4. Feasible mitigation potential

WGIII estimated that options costing **USD 100 per tCO₂-eq or less** could reduce global emissions by at least half of the 2019 level by 2030. More than half of this assessed potential came from options below USD 20 per tCO₂-eq. Large contributions include solar and wind, efficiency, reduced conversion of natural ecosystems and methane reductions.

Cost estimates vary by region and context. A global marginal-cost estimate is not a project tender price for India.

### 5. Demand-side change

Mitigation is not limited to power plants. Urban form, public transport, building design, material efficiency, circularity, diet, reduced waste and service-oriented consumption can reduce demand while protecting well-being.

### 6. Carbon dioxide removal

CDR removes CO₂ from the atmosphere through biological or technological methods. Modelled net-zero pathways use CDR to counterbalance residual emissions and, in some pathways, to achieve net-negative emissions.

Three qualifications are essential:

1. removal durability and measurement matter;
2. land-based removal can compete with food, water and biodiversity;
3. speculative future removals cannot justify delayed gross-emission cuts.

### 7. Equity, finance and feasibility

Feasibility depends on finance, institutions, technology, public acceptance, land, materials and distributional effects. A pathway can be technically possible yet politically or socially difficult. A just transition asks who pays, who gains, who loses and how capabilities differ.

Average annual modelled mitigation investment requirements for 2020–2030 in pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C or 2°C are **three to six times current levels** (**medium confidence**). AR6 also assessed that sufficient global capital and liquidity exist, but barriers and misallocation prevent finance from reaching many regions and sectors.

The Synthesis Report joins causal science, lived risk and response feasibility into one decision framework.

### 1. The integrated headline

The Synthesis Report states that human activities have unequivocally caused global warming, with global surface temperature reaching **1.1°C above 1850–1900 in 2011–2020**. It links this warming to widespread adverse impacts and unequal vulnerability.

Its best estimate is that 1.5°C is reached in the near term, defined as 2021–2040, across the considered scenarios and modelled pathways. Under SSP1-1.9 it is more likely than not to be reached; under higher-emissions scenarios it is likely or very likely to be exceeded. Near-term reach does not predetermine end-century warming, which remains strongly dependent on emissions.

### 2. Near-term action changes long-term feasibility

Deep, rapid and sustained mitigation, together with accelerated adaptation in this decade, reduces projected losses and damages and brings co-benefits such as cleaner air and health gains. Delay:

- locks in high-emissions infrastructure;
- raises stranded-asset and cost risks;
- narrows feasible pathways;
- increases reliance on removals;
- increases losses and damages.

The implemented-policy assessment points to **3.2°C [2.2–3.5°C] by 2100** (**medium confidence**). This gap explains why distant net-zero claims cannot substitute for policies that alter the near-term emissions trajectory.

### 3. Existing infrastructure and the carbon budget

The Synthesis Report assessed with **high confidence** that projected CO₂ emissions from existing fossil-fuel infrastructure without additional abatement would exceed the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget at 50% likelihood. This is a lock-in argument: assets built today shape cumulative emissions for decades.

### 4. Adaptation and mitigation are complements

Mitigation limits the growth of hazards. Adaptation manages risks that are already present or unavoidable. Development choices shape exposure and vulnerability. The integrated concept is **climate-resilient development**.

```text
mitigation without adaptation → avoidable present harm
adaptation without mitigation → limits overwhelmed by rising hazard
development without risk screening → new exposure and lock-in
```

### 5. The Synthesis Report and policy

The report informs:

- UNFCCC Global Stocktake deliberations;
- national risk assessments;
- NDC design and long-term strategies;
- infrastructure appraisal;
- adaptation planning;
- climate-finance priorities.

It does not automatically determine any of these decisions. Scientific assessment can identify consequences and options; it cannot allocate burdens or compel implementation.

Finance is part of the feasibility judgement. Average annual modelled mitigation investment requirements for 2020–2030 in 1.5°C or 2°C pathways are **three to six times current levels**, even though the Synthesis Report assesses sufficient global capital and liquidity to close investment gaps if barriers are reduced.

### 6. A disciplined answer formula

```text
finding + confidence
    → mechanism
    → differentiated risk
    → response option
    → limit / trade-off
    → policy implication
```

- Do not quote “1.1°C” without its baseline and period.
- Do not claim the IPCC ordered a fossil-fuel policy; it assessed pathways and consequences.

**Illustrative model:** AR6 establishes the physical premise: human activities caused global surface temperature to reach 1.1°C above 1850–1900 in 2011–2020. Its deeper synthesis is that each increment of warming changes the available choice set.

WGII shows why. Risks arise through hazard, exposure and vulnerability; adaptation gaps persist; some ecosystems already face hard limits; and losses and damages rise with warming. WGIII shows that pathways limiting warming require deep near-term cuts—global net greenhouse gases fall 43% from 2019 by 2030 in 1.5°C pathways with no or limited overshoot. Delay locks in high-emissions infrastructure, increases costs and dependence on carbon removal, and makes transitions more disruptive.

Choices also narrow through development. Poorly planned urbanisation creates exposure; maladaptation locks in vulnerability; ecosystem degradation removes natural buffers. Conversely, inclusive governance, resilient infrastructure, demand reduction, clean energy and ecosystem protection can combine mitigation, adaptation and development benefits.

The IPCC does not prescribe one national policy or distribute the global carbon budget among countries. It clarifies consequences, feasibility and trade-offs. AR6 therefore describes a closing window for climate-resilient development: not an instant physical cliff, but a progressive loss of safer, cheaper and more equitable options as emissions and vulnerability accumulate.

**Quantified rubric (20):** physical premise 3; WGII integration 4; WGIII pathway 4; lock-in and development 4; mandate qualification 2; reasoned conclusion 3.

The IPCC's major tensions are design trade-offs, not simple evidence that the institution succeeds or fails.

### 1. Consensus and caution

The Panel seeks agreement through discussion. Line-by-line SPM approval can produce language acceptable to governments with very different interests. Critics argue that this may favour conservative wording. The reply is that author teams must preserve consistency with the underlying assessment, and agreement gives the final summary unusual diplomatic usability.

The safe formulation is:

> The process may encourage cautious consensus language, but it also creates shared ownership of a scientifically bounded summary.

### 2. Assessment lag

Assessment depends on published literature and cut-off dates. A completed report can therefore be authoritative yet unable to include the newest paper or event. The answer is not to discard it, but to cite the report date and supplement it with later authoritative evidence where necessary.

### 3. Regional limits

Global averages are often more robust than local projections. Regional model resolution, internal variability, data gaps and local vulnerability make place-specific conclusions more conditional. This is why the IPCC provides regional information and confidence levels rather than pretending every location is equally known.

### 4. Models and scenarios

Models are structured representations, not crystal balls. Their value lies in testing physical understanding and conditional futures. Uncertainty comes from:

- future human choices;
- model structure and parameters;
- internal climate variability;
- incomplete observations;
- high-impact processes such as ice-sheet instability.

Agreement among models can strengthen confidence, but shared assumptions can also create correlated error. Observations, process knowledge and multiple lines of evidence therefore matter.

### 5. What the IPCC cannot do

The IPCC cannot:

- enforce emissions reductions;
- allocate a fair national carbon budget by itself;
- guarantee finance;
- settle ethical disputes through modelling;
- predict the date and location of every disaster;
- turn a scenario into a probability;
- replace national and local risk assessment.

### 6. What it can do exceptionally well

It can establish the assessed state of knowledge, make uncertainty explicit, compare pathways, identify robust findings and disagreements, and provide a common evidentiary platform for governments that disagree about policy.

- Do not use assessment lag to dismiss robust long-term findings.

**Question:** How can line-by-line government approval be both a strength and a limitation?

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Critically evaluate the proposition that the IPCC's institutional authority arises from the same features that constrain it.

**Illustrative model:** The IPCC's authority rests on breadth, transparency and intergovernmental participation. Yet authority should not be exaggerated. An IPCC scenario is not a forecast, an assessed pathway is not a national quota, and a global finding is not a local impact estimate.

The IPCC is strongest as a common evidentiary platform: scientifically bounded, politically usable and transparent about uncertainty. Its legitimacy is therefore generated by managed tension rather than institutional purity.

## Final 10-mark question — 150 words

**Question:** Why must the IPCC be described as policy-relevant but not policy-prescriptive?

**Model answer:** The IPCC assesses scientific, technical and socio-economic evidence concerning climate change, risks, adaptation and mitigation. It may compare pathways, costs, feasibility, co-benefits and trade-offs, enabling governments to understand consequences. ## Final 15-mark question — 250 words

**Question:** Discuss how scenarios and calibrated uncertainty make IPCC projections useful without turning them into predictions.

**Model answer:** IPCC scenarios are conditional futures. RCPs describe concentration pathways labelled by approximate radiative forcing in 2100. SSPs describe socio-economic conditions, and AR6 combines them: SSP2-4.5 joins a middle-of-the-road societal pathway with about 4.5 W/m² forcing. The IPCC does not declare one scenario certain or assign each a probability.

Models then estimate outcomes conditional on those assumptions. Calibrated uncertainty prevents the result from being presented as false certainty. Confidence expresses the strength of evidence and agreement, while likelihood assigns probability: “likely” means 66–100% and “very likely” 90–100%. Best estimates, likely ranges and low-likelihood high-impact tails can therefore coexist.

This makes projections useful in three ways. First, comparing low- and high-emissions scenarios reveals the consequences of human choices. Second, ranges support robust planning instead of one-number dependence. Third, tail risks support precaution where consequences are severe.

Misuse occurs when RCP8.5 is called inevitable business as usual, forcing numbers are read as temperature, or a global likely range is converted into a local forecast. The correct statement is conditional: under a specified scenario, baseline and horizon, the assessed outcome lies within a stated range and confidence. Thus, scenarios explore possibility while calibrated language communicates how strongly each conclusion is supported.

**Quantified rubric (15):** scenario architecture 4; confidence/likelihood 4; utility 3; misuse 2; conclusion 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Identity and mandate

- Established in **1988** by **WMO and UNEP**; **195 member states**.
- Assesses published scientific, technical and socio-economic knowledge.
- Does not conduct primary research, negotiate treaties, impose national targets or prescribe one policy.
- Core formula: **policy-relevant, not policy-prescriptive**.

## Institutional map

- **Panel:** member governments in plenary; elects leadership, agrees work and outlines, endorses reports.
- **Bureau:** Chair, Vice-Chairs, Working Group leadership and TFI Co-Chairs; scientific and strategic guidance.
- **WG I:** physical science, attribution, projections, carbon budgets, extremes, ocean and sea level.
- **WG II:** impacts, exposure, vulnerability, risk, adaptation, limits and climate-resilient development.
- **WG III:** mitigation trends, sectors, pathways, policy, finance, feasibility and equity.
- **TFI:** national greenhouse-gas inventory methods, software and emission-factor support.
- **Secretariat:** hosted at WMO, Geneva; **TSUs:** support each assessment arm.

## Product distinctions

- **Assessment Report:** broad periodic state of knowledge.
- **Special Report:** focused, urgent theme—SR1.5 (2018), land (2019), ocean/cryosphere (2019).
- **Methodology Report:** estimation and reporting methods.
- **Synthesis Report:** integrates Working Groups and relevant Special Reports.
- **Technical Paper:** based on material already assessed.

## Report-production chain

```text
decision → scoping → outline → nominations → author selection
→ First Order Draft/expert review
→ Second Order Draft + SPM/expert-government review
→ final drafts → plenary endorsement → public comments/responses
```

- **Approval:** line by line, especially SPM.
- **Adoption:** section by section, especially Synthesis longer report.
- **Acceptance:** underlying report recognised as comprehensive, objective and balanced.
- Author teams include CLAs, LAs and CAs; Review Editors supervise treatment of substantive comments.
- Peer-reviewed literature has priority; other relevant literature requires added quality scrutiny.

## Calibrated uncertainty

- **Confidence:** evidence + agreement; very low to very high.
- **Likelihood:** probability.
- Virtually certain 99–100%; very likely 90–100%; likely 66–100%; about as likely as not 33–66%; unlikely 0–33%; very unlikely 0–10%; exceptionally unlikely 0–1%.
- Confidence is not probability.
- Preserve best estimate, likely range and low-likelihood high-impact tail separately.

## Scenario evolution

- **SRES:** earlier integrated storylines.
- **RCPs:** concentration/forcing pathways; numbers approximate W/m² in 2100.
- **SSPs:** socio-economic pathways.
- **SSP2-4.5:** SSP2 narrative + about 4.5 W/m² forcing.
- Scenarios are not predictions, probabilities, recommendations or temperature labels.
- Avoid treating RCP8.5/SSP5-8.5 as an inevitable future.

## AR6 WGI essentials

- Human influence unequivocally warmed atmosphere, ocean and land.
- 2011–2020 temperature: **1.09°C [0.95–1.20] above 1850–1900**.
- Best-estimate human-caused warming to 2010–2019: **1.07°C**.
- Long-term best estimates for 2081–2100: **1.4°C SSP1-1.9; 1.8°C SSP1-2.6; 2.7°C SSP2-4.5; 3.6°C SSP3-7.0; 4.4°C SSP5-8.5**.
- Near-term 1.5°C reach or exceedance is assessed across all five scenarios with different likelihoods.
- Global mean sea-level rise, 1901–2018: **0.20 m [0.15–0.25]**.
- Likely 2100 rise relative to 1995–2014: **0.28–0.55 m SSP1-1.9**; **0.63–1.01 m SSP5-8.5**.
- Remaining budget from start-2020: **500 GtCO₂ for 50% likelihood of 1.5°C**; **1,150 GtCO₂ for 67% likelihood of 2°C**.
- Do not treat dated budget as the current remainder.

## AR6 WGII essentials

- **3.3–3.6 billion** people live in highly vulnerable contexts.
- Mortality from floods, droughts and storms was **15 times higher** in highly vulnerable regions than in very-low-vulnerability regions during 2010–2020.
- Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability.
- Climate change has caused substantial damages and increasingly irreversible ecosystem losses.
- Adaptation is increasing but gaps remain uneven and are growing.
- **Soft limit:** potentially overcome; **hard limit:** no feasible adaptation avoids intolerable risk.
- Maladaptation increases or shifts vulnerability, inequity or future lock-in.
- Losses and damages are economic and non-economic harms; not identical to one negotiated fund.
- Adaptation and mitigation are complements.

## AR6 WGIII essentials

- 2019 global net anthropogenic GHG emissions: **59 ± 6.6 GtCO₂-eq**.
- 12% above 2010; 54% above 1990.
- Pre-COP26 NDC emissions make twenty-first-century exceedance of 1.5°C likely.
- Implemented policies assessed in AR6 lead to **3.2°C [2.2–3.5°C] by 2100**.
- 1.5°C no/limited-overshoot pathways: **−43% [34–60%] by 2030**, **−84% [73–98%] by 2050**, relative to 2019.
- 2°C pathways: −27% by 2030; −63% by 2050.
- Net-zero CO₂: early 2050s for 1.5°C pathways; early 2070s for 2°C pathways.
- Options costing ≤USD 100/tCO₂-eq could reduce at least half of 2019 emissions by 2030.
- Average annual modelled mitigation investment needs for 2020–2030 are **three to six times current levels** in 1.5°C/2°C pathways.
- CDR supports residual balancing; it does not excuse delayed gross cuts.

## AR6 synthesis and policy use

- Completed in March 2023; latest completed assessment on 3 October 2026.
- Best-estimate 1.5°C reach lies in the 2021–2040 near term across considered scenarios and pathways.
- Implemented policies imply 3.2°C [2.2–3.5°C] by 2100 in the assessed literature.
- Near-term mitigation and adaptation reduce losses, damages and future costs.
- Delay locks in emissions, vulnerability, stranded assets and higher removal dependence.
- Existing unabated fossil infrastructure threatens the 1.5°C carbon budget.
- Finance exists globally, but modelled mitigation investment needs remain three to six times current levels and access barriers are unequal.
- IPCC evidence informs the Global Stocktake, NDCs, long-term strategies and adaptation plans but does not determine political burdens.

## India and South Asia

- Regional pattern: heatwaves, drought in arid/semi-arid South Asia, monsoon-region floods and Hindu Kush Himalayan glacier melt.
- Floods, drought and heat stress threaten food availability and prices in South and Southeast Asia.
- Indus and Ganges basins may face severe mid-century water-scarcity challenges as climate acts as a stress multiplier.
- Combine global IPCC evidence with Indian observations and local exposure data.
- Equity frame: historical responsibility + capability + development space + finance, alongside domestic adaptation and mitigation responsibility.
- Global mean sea level is not local relative sea level.

## Rapid traps

1. Three Working Groups, not four.
2. TFI is separate and methodology-focused.
3. SPM approval does not mean governments conducted the science.
4. An agreed outline is not a finding.
5. High confidence is not 90% probability.
6. RCP/SSP forcing numbers are not °C.
7. Scenarios are not predictions.
8. A global pathway is not India's assigned quota.
9. Carbon budgets require date and probability.
10. Losses and damages are not synonymous with a fund.
11. Adaptation has limits; CDR has limits.
12. AR6 remains the completed findings baseline until AR7 products are formally published.
