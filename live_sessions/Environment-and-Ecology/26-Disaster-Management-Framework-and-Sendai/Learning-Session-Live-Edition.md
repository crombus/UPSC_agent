## Lesson 1 — Risk is produced, not merely received

### Visual: the risk equation as a thinking tool

```text
HAZARD                dangerous event/process
   x
EXPOSURE              people/assets/ecosystems in harm's way
   x
VULNERABILITY         susceptibility to damage
   ÷
CAPACITY              ability to anticipate, cope, adapt and recover
   =
DISASTER RISK
```

This is a conceptual relation, not a universal arithmetic formula. It disciplines the answer by asking which component policy can change.

### 1. Hazard is not disaster

A cyclone over an uninhabited ocean is a hazard but not yet a community disaster. A moderate rainfall event can become disastrous where drains are blocked, wetlands are filled, settlements occupy low land and warning cannot trigger action. The event supplies the physical trigger; development and social conditions shape loss.

**Exposure** answers “who or what is located there?” **Vulnerability** answers “why is the exposed unit susceptible?” **Capacity** answers “what enables anticipation, coping and recovery?”

### 2. Vulnerability has several dimensions

| Dimension | Mechanism | India-centric example |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | unsafe site or weak construction | unreinforced building in a seismic zone |
| Social | age, disability, exclusion or care burden | warning not accessible to a deaf person |
| Economic | low savings, insecure work, no fallback | daily-wage household cannot evacuate early |
| Environmental | degraded protective ecosystem | wetland loss raises urban flood peaks |
| Institutional | weak plans, staff or coordination | outdated district plan and no drill |
| Attitudinal | distrust, fatalism or exclusion from decisions | warning ignored because past alerts lacked credibility |

Vulnerability is relational. A wheelchair user is not inherently vulnerable; an inaccessible warning, vehicle and shelter create vulnerability.

### 3. Capacity and resilience

Capacity includes knowledge, institutions, money, social networks, safe infrastructure, redundancy and the ability to learn. **Resilience** is the capacity to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt, transform and recover while preserving essential functions. “Bounce back” is incomplete if the pre-disaster condition was unsafe.

Useful resilience properties:

```text
robustness    withstand stress
redundancy    alternative routes/systems
resourcefulness mobilise people, information and finance
rapidity      restore essential functions quickly
adaptability  learn and change future risk
```

### 4. Risk is dynamic

Urban growth, climate change, migration, ageing infrastructure and ecosystem loss can create or redistribute risk. New construction can lock in exposure for decades. Therefore risk assessment must be repeated, not treated as a one-time map.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The hazard's intensity ultimately determines disaster loss.
**Reply:** Intensity matters, but comparable hazards produce very different losses because exposure, vulnerability and capacity differ.
**Residual:** Social analysis cannot erase physical thresholds; extreme events can exceed even strong systems, so residual risk and contingency capacity remain.

### UPSC use

Begin any disaster answer by naming the hazard, exposed system, dominant vulnerabilities and capacities. This prevents a generic list of measures.

### Revision notes

1. A hazard is a potentially damaging event or process; it is not automatically a disaster.
2. Exposure identifies people, assets, services and ecosystems located in harm's way.
3. Vulnerability explains susceptibility; capacity explains ability to manage impact.
4. Risk is dynamic because development choices alter exposure and vulnerability.
5. Physical, social, economic, environmental, institutional and attitudinal vulnerabilities can compound.
6. A person is not inherently vulnerable; inaccessible systems create vulnerability.
7. Resilience is broader than rapid restoration.
8. Robustness, redundancy, resourcefulness, rapidity and adaptability are useful resilience elements.
9. Residual risk remains after prevention and mitigation.
10. Risk maps require periodic updating and local validation.
11. The same hazard can create unequal loss across groups and places.
12. A good answer identifies the risk component each recommendation changes.

### Concept check

**Question:** A flood embankment reduces water entry but encourages dense settlement immediately behind it. Which risk components changed?

**Model answer:** The embankment initially reduces hazard impact or exposure, but perceived safety can attract more people and assets, increasing exposure. If maintenance, evacuation and residual-risk awareness remain weak, vulnerability can also rise. Structural protection can therefore redistribute or accumulate risk.

**Misconception to avoid:** A protective structure does not eliminate residual risk or justify uncontrolled exposure behind it.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity interact to determine disaster risk.

**Model (131 words):** A hazard becomes a disaster only when it interacts with exposed and vulnerable people, assets or ecosystems whose capacity is insufficient. Hazard describes the potentially damaging event; exposure identifies what lies in its path. Vulnerability arises from unsafe buildings, poverty, exclusion, environmental degradation or weak institutions. Capacity includes knowledge, finance, warning, social networks and resilient services that enable anticipation, coping and recovery.

Thus identical rainfall can cause limited loss in a city with protected drains, wetlands, warnings and safe housing, but catastrophic loss where floodplains are encroached and vulnerable settlements lack evacuation support. Risk reduction can avoid exposure through land-use planning, lower vulnerability through codes and social protection, and strengthen capacity through drills, redundant infrastructure and local organisations. The hazard matters, but disaster loss is co-produced by development and governance choices.

## Lesson 2 — The disaster-management cycle: act before, during and after impact

Progress: 2 / 17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Prevention, mitigation, preparedness, response, recovery and Build Back Better

### Visual: a cycle, not a sequence that ends

```text
PREVENTION -> MITIGATION -> PREPAREDNESS
      ^                         |
      |                         v
RECOVERY <- REHABILITATION <- RESPONSE
      |
      +--> Build Back Better feeds safer land, codes, services and livelihoods
```

### 1. Prevention and mitigation

**Prevention** avoids creation or occurrence of avoidable risk: keeping construction out of a high-risk channel, shutting an unsafe industrial process, vaccinating against a biological threat. Not every natural hazard can be prevented.

**Mitigation** reduces severity or consequences. Structural measures include retrofitting, drainage, embankments and shelters. Non-structural measures include zoning, codes, ecosystem protection, insurance incentives and public education.

### 2. Preparedness

Preparedness builds the capability to act when threat is imminent or impact occurs:

- contingency and evacuation plans;
- emergency operation centres and incident command;
- stockpiles, shelters and medical surge;
- trained volunteers and responders;
- interoperable communications;
- mock exercises and continuity plans;
- warning messages linked to specific actions.

A plan without trained people, transport, accessible shelters and exercises is a document, not preparedness.

### 3. Response

Response protects life and meets immediate needs: warning confirmation, evacuation, search and rescue, triage, food, water, temporary shelter, protection, restoration of essential communications and rapid needs assessment. Speed matters, but accountability and “do no harm” matter too.

### 4. Recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction

**Relief** meets urgent needs. **Rehabilitation** restores basic services, livelihoods and functional capability. **Reconstruction** rebuilds damaged assets. **Recovery** is the wider process connecting all three to social, economic and institutional renewal.

**Build Back Better (BBB)** means recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction reduce future risk. It may require safer location, stronger standards, redundancy, restored ecosystems, diversified livelihoods and maintenance systems—not merely a stronger replica in the same unsafe site.

### 5. Why the cycle loops

Post-disaster assessment reveals failed buildings, warning gaps and exclusion. That evidence must update land-use plans, codes, training and finance. Otherwise recovery reproduces the conditions of the next disaster.

### Trade-off

Response readiness cannot be sacrificed on the assumption that prevention will work perfectly. Yet politically visible relief should not crowd out low-visibility mitigation. Risk-based budgeting must sustain both.

### UPSC use

Locate every proposed measure in the cycle. If an answer lists only rescue and compensation, it is reactive. If it lists only prevention, it ignores residual risk.

### Revision notes

1. Prevention avoids new or avoidable risk; mitigation reduces likely consequences.
2. Structural and non-structural measures are complementary.
3. Preparedness converts knowledge into executable capability.
4. Mock drills test assumptions, roles, communication and evacuation time.
5. Response prioritises life safety and immediate essential needs.
6. Relief, rehabilitation, reconstruction and recovery are not synonyms.
7. Build Back Better reduces future risk during recovery.
8. BBB can require relocation, livelihood change and management reform, not only stronger concrete.
9. Post-event learning must feed the next prevention and preparedness cycle.
10. Residual risk requires response capacity even after strong mitigation.
11. Relief visibility can crowd out prevention unless budgets protect mitigation.
12. Every recommendation should be assigned to a cycle phase and responsible actor.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is rebuilding a destroyed settlement exactly as before inconsistent with Build Back Better?

**Model answer:** If the previous location, design, services or livelihood pattern created vulnerability, identical reconstruction restores the same risk. BBB uses recovery to improve siting, standards, service redundancy, accessibility, livelihoods and maintenance while protecting rights and social networks.

**Misconception to avoid:** BBB does not mean only faster construction or stronger buildings.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Differentiate prevention, mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery. Why must they be treated as a continuous cycle?

**Model (164 words):** Prevention avoids creation of risk, for example by prohibiting settlement in an active flood channel. Mitigation reduces expected impact through retrofitting, drainage, shelters, ecosystem buffers or enforceable codes. Preparedness develops plans, warning-action protocols, trained personnel, stockpiles, evacuation transport and drills. Response covers evacuation, rescue, medical care, relief and restoration of essential services. Recovery includes rehabilitation of services and livelihoods and reconstruction of assets.

They form a cycle because each phase changes the next. Risk assessment guides prevention; mitigation reduces response burden; preparedness determines whether warnings become action; response data reveals vulnerable groups and failed systems; and recovery can either reproduce risk or Build Back Better. For instance, reconstructing a flood-damaged hospital at the same elevation without backup power restores exposure, whereas safer siting, elevated utilities and continuity planning reduce future loss.

No phase is sufficient alone. Prevention cannot remove residual risk, while response without mitigation repeats avoidable loss. Budgets, plans and accountability should therefore link ex-ante risk reduction with response readiness and risk-informed recovery.

### Visual: evolution of the global framework

```text
YOKOHAMA STRATEGY 1994
ten prevention principles
        |
        v
HYOGO FRAMEWORK 2005-2015
five priorities; DRR as multi-sector work
        |
        v
SENDAI FRAMEWORK 2015-2030
four priorities + seven measurable global targets
```

Sendai was adopted on **18 March 2015** at the Third UN World Conference on Disaster Risk Reduction in Sendai, Japan. It is voluntary and non-binding.

### 1. The four priorities for action

1. **Understanding disaster risk.**
2. **Strengthening disaster risk governance to manage disaster risk.**
3. **Investing in disaster risk reduction for resilience.**
4. **Enhancing disaster preparedness for effective response and to “Build Back Better” in recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction.**

The order is analytical: know risk, govern it, invest in reduction, then prepare and recover better. Response remains essential but is not the starting point.

### 2. The seven global targets

| Target | By the stated deadline |
|---|---|
| A | substantially reduce global disaster mortality by 2030, comparing 2020-2030 with 2005-2015 average per 100,000 |
| B | substantially reduce affected people globally by 2030 on the same decadal per-100,000 comparison |
| C | reduce direct disaster economic loss relative to global GDP by 2030 |
| D | substantially reduce critical-infrastructure damage and disruption of basic services, including health and education, by 2030 |
| E | substantially increase countries with national and local DRR strategies by 2020 |
| F | substantially enhance international cooperation to developing countries by 2030 |
| G | substantially increase availability of and access to multi-hazard early warning and disaster-risk information and assessments by 2030 |

> **Memory logic:** A-D reduce losses; E-G increase means. E alone had a 2020 deadline.

### 3. Priorities are not targets

Priorities tell governments what to do. Targets specify global results or means to measure. “Understanding risk” is Priority 1, not Target A. “Mortality” is Target A, not a priority.

### 4. Monitoring

The Sendai Framework Monitor uses **38 internationally agreed indicators** for the seven targets. Monitoring requires comparable mortality, affected-person, economic-loss, infrastructure, strategy, cooperation and warning-access data. Missing baselines or changing definitions can create false trends.

### 5. Sendai versus Hyogo

Hyogo had five priorities and strengthened institutional DRR. Sendai sharpened the focus on preventing new risk, reducing existing risk, exposure, health, critical infrastructure, recovery and measurable targets. Do not claim Hyogo ignored prevention or that Sendai is legally binding.

### 6. India's policy bridge: the Prime Minister's Ten-Point Agenda

At the **Asian Ministerial Conference on Disaster Risk Reduction (AMCDRR), New Delhi, November 2016**, the Prime Minister set out a Ten-Point Agenda that translates Sendai into a practical Indian and international policy programme:

| # | Agenda point |
|---:|---|
| 1 | make disaster-risk management a principle of every development sector |
| 2 | extend risk coverage from poor households and small enterprises to corporations and nation States |
| 3 | place women's leadership and participation at the centre of DRR |
| 4 | invest in risk mapping and shared understanding of hazards and exposure |
| 5 | use technology to improve the efficiency of risk management |
| 6 | build a network of universities working on disaster issues |
| 7 | use social media and mobile technology for risk communication |
| 8 | build on local capacity and initiative |
| 9 | study every disaster and institutionalise lessons learned |
| 10 | strengthen cohesion in international disaster response |

The agenda is not another statutory tier or a replacement for Sendai. It is a policy bridge: mainstreaming addresses risk creation; coverage and women's leadership address inclusion; mapping and technology strengthen risk knowledge; universities and post-event studies improve learning; local capacity and international cohesion improve implementation.

### Limitation and reply

**Criticism:** Sendai's voluntary character and global targets do not compel a country to meet a quantified national outcome.
**Reply:** Shared definitions, indicators and reporting create comparability and political accountability.
**Residual:** Monitoring is only as good as national loss data and local implementation; reported strategy existence may not prove reduced risk.

### UPSC use

When asked for “global targets,” name A-G. When asked for “priorities,” list the four exact action priorities. A high-scoring answer then diagnoses which priority is weakest in the case at hand.

### Revision notes

1. Sendai covers 2015-2030 and was adopted on 18 March 2015.
2. It succeeded the Hyogo Framework for Action 2005-2015.
3. Sendai is voluntary and non-binding.
4. Priority 1 is understanding disaster risk.
5. Priority 2 is strengthening risk governance.
6. Priority 3 is investing in DRR for resilience.
7. Priority 4 joins preparedness, effective response and Build Back Better.
8. Sendai has seven global targets A-G.
9. Targets A-D reduce losses; E-G increase means of implementation.
10. Target E alone carried a 2020 deadline.
11. The Sendai Framework Monitor uses 38 agreed indicators.
12. A strategy on paper is a means indicator, not proof of lower mortality or loss.
13. Hyogo's five priorities and Sendai's four priorities plus seven targets must not be conflated.
14. The Prime Minister announced the Ten-Point Agenda at AMCDRR, New Delhi, in November 2016.
15. Its themes connect development, universal risk coverage, women's leadership, risk mapping, technology, universities, communication, local capacity, learning and international cooperation.

### Concept check

**Question:** A country reports that every district has a DRR strategy. Which Sendai target does this support, and what does it not prove?

**Model answer:** It supports Target E, which concerns national and local DRR strategies. It does not by itself prove progress on mortality, affected people, economic loss or critical-infrastructure disruption under Targets A-D. Strategy quality, finance and implementation require separate evidence.

**Misconception to avoid:** The existence of a plan is not equivalent to a resilience outcome.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Describe the four priorities and seven global targets of the Sendai Framework. Assess its principal accountability limitation.

**Model (157 words):** The Sendai Framework 2015-2030 organises action through four priorities: understanding disaster risk; strengthening risk governance; investing in DRR for resilience; and enhancing preparedness for effective response while Building Back Better in recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction.

Its seven targets are: A, lower disaster mortality; B, fewer affected people; C, lower direct economic loss relative to GDP; D, less damage to critical infrastructure and disruption of basic services; E, more countries with national and local DRR strategies; F, stronger international cooperation for developing countries; and G, wider access to multi-hazard warning and risk information. A-D reduce losses, while E-G strengthen means; Target E alone had a 2020 deadline.

Sendai's strength is a common measurable vocabulary, supported by 38 agreed indicators. Its limitation is that it is voluntary and sets global rather than automatically enforceable national obligations. Data gaps and strategy-counting can also mask weak implementation. Its value therefore depends on financed local plans, comparable loss data and public accountability.

### Visual: legal-status ladder

```text
BILL / proposal
      -> passed by Parliament
      -> Presidential assent and Gazette publication
      -> commencement notification where required
      -> provision in force
      -> rules/regulations/State notification
      -> institution constituted
      -> operational capacity and outcomes
```

### 1. The base statute

The **Disaster Management Act, 2005** created a national-State-district architecture and statutory planning, response, relief, mitigation and capacity-building functions. The Act treats disaster management as a continuing governmental responsibility, not temporary relief administration.

The Constitution contains no separate entry titled “disaster management.” The legal basis is commonly traced, among other sectoral powers, to **Concurrent List Entry 23** on social security and social insurance and **Concurrent List Entry 29** on preventing the inter-State spread of infectious or contagious diseases affecting humans, animals or plants. Entry 29 is especially relevant to biological emergencies; neither entry is a free-standing constitutional entry called disaster management. Responsibilities therefore operate through Union, State and local competencies, the central statute and cooperative federal practice. Public order or ordinary law-and-order situations are not transformed into disasters merely by administrative convenience.

### 2. The current amendment is enacted law

The **Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025, Act 10 of 2025** received assent and was gazetted on **29 March 2025**. Notification **S.O. 1648(E), 8 April 2025** appointed **9 April 2025** as the commencement date. It must not be described as a pending Bill or a provision-wise proposal.

### 3. Key operative additions

| Provision | Legal effect |
|---|---|
| s. 2(da) | defines a disaster database covering assessment, fund allocation/expenditure, preparedness/mitigation plans and risk registers |
| s. 2(ea) | defines disaster risk through hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity |
| s. 8A | gives statutory footing to the National Crisis Management Committee |
| s. 8B | gives statutory footing to the High Level Committee |
| s. 41A | enables a State to constitute Urban Disaster Management Authorities |
| s. 44A | enables a State to constitute a specialist State Disaster Response Force |
| s. 60A | permits hazard-related government action and provides a contravention penalty |
| s. 76A | gives NDMA regulation-making power with prior Central approval |

The amendment also shifts preparation of national and State plans to NDMA and SDMAs, prescribes review/update cycles, provides for hazard-wise nodal departments and strengthens database duties.

### 4. Enacted does not mean universally constituted

Sections 41A and 44A use **“may”**. They create enabling powers. A UDMA or State force exists only after the relevant State Gazette notification. The Act's commencement is settled; rollout is an operational question.

A UDMA may be constituted for a State capital and each Municipal Corporation city, except the NCT of Delhi and the UT of Chandigarh. It is chaired by the Municipal Commissioner, with the District Collector as Vice-Chairperson, and prepares an Urban Plan for SDMA approval.

### 5. Database status discipline

The Act mandates creation of a national disaster database and State inputs. The statute does not itself prove that every field is populated, validated, public or interoperable. Current operational status needs separate official evidence.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A central statute over-centralises a State-facing function.
**Reply:** Cross-border hazards, national standards, specialised forces and fiscal support require Union coordination, while implementation remains heavily State, district and local.
**Residual:** Ambiguous accountability and uneven local capacity can persist unless plans, finance and data specify responsibility.

### UPSC use

Always distinguish law, commencement, enabling provision, notification, constitution, operation and outcome. That single discipline prevents most current-status errors.

### Revision notes

1. The Disaster Management Act, 2005 remains the principal statute but must be read as amended.
2. Act 10 of 2025 was assented and gazetted on 29 March 2025.
3. All provisions commenced on 9 April 2025 under S.O. 1648(E).
4. The amendment is no longer a Bill or draft.
5. Sections 8A and 8B give statutory footing to NCMC and HLC.
6. Section 41A concerns Urban Disaster Management Authorities.
7. Section 44A concerns a State Disaster Response Force, not the SDRF fund.
8. Sections 41A and 44A are enabling and require State action.
9. Section 2(da) defines the disaster database.
10. Section 2(ea) defines disaster risk using hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity.
11. Section 76A concerns NDMA regulations.
12. Statutory mandate, notified body, operating system and measured outcome are separate statuses.
13. The Constitution has no dedicated entry named disaster management.
14. The constitutional footing draws, among other powers, on Concurrent List Entries 23 and 29; these must not be misdescribed as a dedicated disaster-management entry.

### Concept check

**Question:** Section 41A is in force. Does that prove every Municipal Corporation has an Urban Disaster Management Authority?

**Model answer:** No. Section 41A empowers a State Government to constitute a UDMA by Gazette notification. The legal power is operative, but each authority's existence requires a State notification. Operational staffing, plan quality and performance require still further evidence.

**Misconception to avoid:** An enabling provision does not automatically constitute every permitted body.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain the major changes introduced by the Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025. Why must legal and operational status be distinguished?

**Model (167 words):** The Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025 received assent on 29 March 2025 and came into force on 9 April 2025. It defines disaster risk and a disaster database; gives statutory status to the National Crisis Management Committee and High Level Committee; enables Urban Disaster Management Authorities and State Disaster Response Forces; authorises hazard-related directions; and gives NDMA regulation-making power. It also strengthens national and State database duties, hazard-wise nodal arrangements and plan preparation and review.

Legal status must be read precisely. The Act and its commencement are settled facts. By contrast, sections 41A and 44A use enabling language: a State must issue its own Gazette notification before a particular urban authority or force exists. Similarly, a statutory database duty does not prove full data population, interoperability or publication.

This distinction matters for accountability. Calling a permission an operational achievement exaggerates implementation, while calling an enacted amendment a Bill understates current law. Evaluation should therefore follow enactment, commencement, subordinate rules, constitution, staffing, functioning and outcomes as separate stages.

## Lesson 5 — Union architecture: policy, coordination, crisis management, capacity and response

### Visual: do not collapse distinct national roles

```text
POLICY/GUIDANCE       COORDINATION         MAJOR CRISIS       FINANCE DECISION
NDMA (PM)       ->    NEC (Home Secy)      NCMC (Cab Secy)    HLC
     |                    |                       |               |
NIDM capacity       ministries/States       national-scale      assistance under
NDRF superintendence monitoring/directions  coordination        response/mitigation funds
```

### 1. Ministry of Home Affairs

MHA has overall national coordination for disaster management and works with affected States, line ministries, NDMA, NDRF, NIDM, civil defence, armed forces and technical agencies. Coordination does not erase the State's primary operational responsibility.

### 2. NDMA

The **National Disaster Management Authority**, chaired by the Prime Minister, lays down national policies, plans and guidelines, approves relevant plans and exercises general superintendence, direction and control over the National Disaster Response Force. It is an apex policy and oversight body, not the field commander for every local incident.

### 3. NEC

The **National Executive Committee**, chaired by the Union Home Secretary, coordinates and monitors implementation across Union ministries and States. It translates policy into inter-ministerial executive coordination and may issue directions within the Act.

### 4. NCMC and HLC

The **National Crisis Management Committee**, chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, is the nodal body for major disasters with serious or national ramifications. It is an escalation/coordination mechanism, not a substitute for the NDMA-SDMA-DDMA cascade.

The **High Level Committee** handles specified financial-assistance decisions under response and mitigation funds. The 2025 amendment gave both bodies statutory footing; it did not create their administrative existence from nothing.

### 5. NIDM

The **National Institute of Disaster Management** is the nodal capacity-development institution for human-resource development, training, research, documentation and policy advocacy. Training output should be judged through retained competence, exercises and institutional adoption—not only course counts.

### 6. NDRF: the force

The **National Disaster Response Force** is a specialised response force under the Act. MHA reported **16 operational battalions** with sanctioned strength **18,581** on 22 July 2025. This is an input-capacity fact, not proof of national response sufficiency.

### 7. NPDRR

The **National Platform for Disaster Risk Reduction**, chaired by the Union Home Minister, is a multi-stakeholder review and advisory platform involving Union, State, parliamentary and urban-local representation. It reviews, appraises and advises; it is not a parallel command authority.

### 8. Technical agencies remain hazard-specific

IMD, CWC, INCOIS, GSI, health agencies and others generate specialised warning or risk information. NDMA does not replace scientific mandates; it helps integrate them into action.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Multiple national bodies duplicate responsibility.
**Reply:** Policy, executive coordination, major-crisis escalation, finance, training and field response are genuinely different functions.
**Residual:** Overlap becomes harmful when triggers, information ownership and command relationships are unclear or unexercised.

The **State Disaster Management Authority**, chaired by the Chief Minister, lays down State policy and approves the State plan. The **State Executive Committee**, chaired by the Chief Secretary, coordinates and monitors State implementation and response.

The **District Disaster Management Authority** is chaired by the District Magistrate/Collector, with an elected local-authority representative as Co-Chairperson, subject to statutory provisions. It prepares and implements the district plan, coordinates line departments, ensures guideline compliance and links warning, evacuation, relief and recovery to actual settlements.

Districts hold local knowledge but often face thin specialist staff, multiple Collector responsibilities, weak risk data, irregular plan updating and inadequate exercises. This is the architecture's recurrent implementation gap.

### 3. Local authorities

Panchayats, municipalities, cantonment boards, town-planning authorities and service utilities control land use, drainage, water, sanitation, roads, local health and community facilities. They must:

- enforce building and land-use rules;
- maintain drainage, shelters and lifelines;
- prepare continuity and evacuation arrangements;
- identify vulnerable residents;
- support relief, rehabilitation and reconstruction.

Residents, SHGs, CBOs, ward committees and volunteers know mobility constraints, informal routes, local languages, isolated households and social conflict. Their role is co-production, not unpaid substitution for State duty.

### 6. Subsidiarity and escalation

Act at the lowest capable level; escalate when scale exceeds capacity. This avoids both over-centralisation and abandonment of weak local institutions.

## Lesson 8 — Disaster finance beyond relief: reduction, transfer and social protection

Progress: 8 / 17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Risk financing, insurance, risk layering and accountability

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: finance matched to the layer of risk

```text
frequent / smaller loss   -> contingency budget, maintenance, social protection
medium loss               -> reserve funds, insurance, contingent credit
rare / catastrophic loss  -> pooled sovereign support, reinsurance, catastrophe instruments

physical risk reduction must run across every layer
```

### 1. Four ways of treating risk

- **accept:** knowingly retain manageable residual loss;
- **avoid:** do not create the exposure;
- **reduce:** lower probability or consequence;
- **transfer:** shift contractual financial loss, for example through insurance.

Transfer does not reduce physical hazard. An insured unsafe house remains unsafe.

### 2. Public finance

Annual budgets should fund maintenance, mapping, staff, drills and small mitigation rather than wait for emergency grants. Dedicated response funds address immediacy; mitigation funds finance ex-ante projects; recovery requires multi-year reconstruction and livelihood support.

### 3. Insurance and risk pooling

Insurance can provide quicker liquidity and protect households, farms, firms and public assets. But premiums can be unaffordable, basis risk can leave losses uncovered, claims may be delayed, and repeated high-risk rebuilding can create moral hazard. Public subsidy should target protection gaps without masking unsafe siting.

**Parametric insurance** pays when a measured trigger crosses a threshold rather than after item-by-item loss assessment. It can be fast but creates basis risk: the trigger may be met without matching loss, or loss may occur without trigger.

### 4. Contingent credit and catastrophe instruments

Pre-arranged credit provides liquidity after defined events. Catastrophe bonds shift specified risk to investors, but require strong data, modelling and transaction capacity. They should be discussed as possible tools, not claimed as widespread Indian deployment without dated evidence.

### 5. Social protection

Cash transfers, employment guarantees, food security, health protection and portable entitlements can prevent a hazard shock from becoming long-term poverty. They are risk-financing tools when designed for rapid scale-up and inclusion.

### 6. Accountability

Track:

```text
allocation -> release -> expenditure -> asset/service delivered
-> maintenance -> beneficiary reach -> reduced loss or faster recovery
```

CAG and legislative audit matter because announcement and utilisation are not the same. Loss accounting should separate emergency relief, reconstruction and mitigation outcomes.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Insurance privatises a public responsibility.
**Reply:** Risk transfer can complement universal public relief and mitigation.
**Residual:** It becomes unjust if premiums, exclusions or data gaps leave high-risk poor households unprotected or if public policy substitutes insurance for prevention.

### UPSC use

Use a layered portfolio. Do not present one fund or insurance product as a complete finance strategy.

### Revision notes

1. Risk acceptance, avoidance, reduction and transfer are distinct strategies.
2. Insurance transfers financial loss; it does not make the asset physically safer.
3. Frequent losses are usually better managed through budgets, maintenance and social protection.
4. Catastrophic layers may require pooling, reinsurance or sovereign support.
5. Parametric insurance pays against a trigger and can suffer basis risk.
6. Contingent credit provides pre-arranged liquidity.
7. Catastrophe instruments require reliable risk and loss data.
8. Public subsidy should close protection gaps without rewarding unsafe exposure.
9. Scalable social protection prevents transient shocks from becoming chronic poverty.
10. Recovery finance must extend beyond immediate relief.
11. Allocation, release, expenditure, delivery and outcome are separate statuses.
12. Audit should examine maintenance and avoided or reduced loss, not expenditure alone.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a fully insured building still represent poor disaster policy?

**Model answer:** Insurance can reimburse financial loss but does not correct unsafe siting, weak design, service interruption or danger to life. If coverage encourages reconstruction in the same unsafe form, it can preserve or increase risk. Risk transfer must accompany avoidance, reduction and enforceable standards.

**Misconception to avoid:** Financial compensation is not equivalent to physical resilience.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Discuss the role and limitations of risk-transfer instruments in disaster management.

**Model (147 words):** Risk transfer shifts part of disaster-related financial loss to another party through insurance, reinsurance, contingent credit or catastrophe instruments. It can provide rapid liquidity, protect household and government balance sheets and support recovery without unpredictable post-event borrowing. Parametric products can pay quickly when a pre-agreed hazard trigger is crossed.

However, transfer does not reduce physical hazard or exposure. Premiums may exclude poor or high-risk households; claims can be delayed; parametric products create basis risk; and subsidised coverage may encourage rebuilding in unsafe locations. Catastrophe instruments also require reliable models, long loss records and administrative capacity.

India should therefore use risk layering: budgets and social protection for frequent losses, insurance and contingent finance for medium shocks, and sovereign pooled support for catastrophic events. Every layer must accompany land-use control, resilient construction and mitigation investment. Risk transfer is a financing complement, not a substitute for prevention or public responsibility.

```text
land-use choice + building standard + infrastructure design + budget priority
```

### 1. Components of risk assessment

A defensible assessment specifies hazard probability/intensity, exposed population/assets/services, vulnerability functions, capacities, cascading dependencies and uncertainty. Historical maps must be updated for urbanisation, ecosystem change and climate trends.

### 2. Land-use planning

Land-use regulation prevents new exposure through floodplain zoning, coastal setbacks, slope controls, industrial buffers and relocation from intolerable risk. Yet zoning can displace low-income households unless safe serviced alternatives and tenure protection exist.

### 3. Building codes

Codes translate hazard knowledge into design rules. Resilience requires:

- hazard-appropriate code;
- local bye-law adoption;
- qualified design and construction;
- inspection and certification;
- retrofit of existing vulnerable stock;
- enforcement against informal and public construction;
- maintenance over asset life.

A code book without compliance is not mitigation.

### 4. Structural and non-structural measures

| Structural | Non-structural |
|---|---|
| retrofitting, shelters, drainage, slope stabilisation, redundancy | zoning, codes, pricing, warning, insurance, ecosystem protection, continuity planning |

Structural projects can transfer risk downstream or encourage exposure. Non-structural rules can be inequitable or ignored. Use both and test system effects.

### 5. Critical infrastructure

Hospitals, power, water, transport, telecom, data systems and emergency facilities are lifelines. Resilience requires criticality mapping, design standards, backup power, redundant routes, spare capacity, cyber/physical security, continuity plans and rapid repair contracts.

**Functional continuity** matters more than structural survival alone. A hospital building standing without water, oxygen, access or power is not resilient.

### 6. CDRI: resilience across the infrastructure life cycle

India launched the **Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI)** at the UN Climate Action Summit on **23 September 2019**. It is an international coalition, not India's domestic disaster-command authority. Its value lies in linking four infrastructure questions:

| Life-cycle question | CDRI emphasis |
|---|---|
| What can fail? | disaster-risk assessment and probabilistic risk information |
| What standard should apply? | resilient design, implementation and evolving codes |
| How will resilience be financed? | investment standards and risk-financing mechanisms |
| How should damaged systems return? | Build Back Better in physical assets and management systems |

CDRI therefore broadens “strong infrastructure” into risk-informed siting, standards, finance, maintenance, continuity and recovery. Membership or guidance does not prove that a particular asset is resilient; project-level design, compliance and performance still require evidence.

CDRI's official membership listing reported **58 member countries and 12 member organisations as of June 2026**. This demonstrates convening reach, not completed infrastructure retrofits or measured loss reduction.

### 7. Ecologically sensitive areas: manage development without manufacturing risk

Rapid infrastructure can improve access, energy and emergency response while simultaneously increasing slope instability, flood peaks, habitat fragmentation or exposure if it ignores ecological limits. The contradiction is managed through a decision sequence, not through a blanket “development versus environment” slogan:

```text
strategic need
   -> cumulative and project risk assessment
   -> avoid the highest-risk site
   -> minimise footprint and protect natural buffers
   -> hazard-specific design and construction controls
   -> independent monitoring + maintenance + stop-work thresholds
   -> local participation, disclosure and accountable recovery planning
```

In the Himalaya, road cutting, tunnelling, hydropower and hill-town construction require slope, drainage, seismic and carrying-capacity analysis rather than isolated project clearance. In the Western Ghats, quarrying and transport corridors require cumulative catchment and slope assessment. On coasts and floodplains, ports, roads and urban expansion must retain mangroves, wetlands, dunes and drainage paths. The principle is **risk-sensitive development**: avoid intolerable exposure, redesign reducible risk and reject activity whose residual risk cannot be governed.

### 8. Maintenance and retrofit

New flagship infrastructure attracts attention, but existing schools, bridges, drains, dams and hospitals often contain the largest exposure. Risk-informed asset management prioritises inspection, retrofit and maintenance based on consequence of failure.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Strict codes and zoning slow housing and infrastructure.
**Reply:** Unpriced risk creates larger future fiscal and human costs.
**Residual:** Compliance must be affordable, transparent and paired with technical support and safe alternatives, or it can drive construction into informality.

### Concept check

A complete assessment covers access, utilities, communications, staff, supplies, backup systems and rapid restoration.

**Misconception to avoid:** Critical-infrastructure resilience is not identical to structural strength.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** How can risk assessment and development regulation prevent the creation of new disaster risk?

**Model (170 words):** Risk assessment identifies hazard probability and intensity, exposed people and assets, vulnerability, coping capacity, interdependencies and uncertainty. Its purpose is not merely to produce maps; it must alter land use, investment and design.

Floodplain zoning, coastal setbacks, slope controls and industrial buffers avoid new exposure. Building codes translate hazard knowledge into engineering rules, while inspection, certification and penalties create compliance. Existing vulnerable schools, hospitals, bridges and housing require prioritised retrofit and maintenance. Critical infrastructure planning must protect functional continuity through backup power, redundant routes, spare capacity, continuity plans and rapid-repair contracts.

Structural measures such as embankments or drains should be tested for transferred risk and induced development. Non-structural measures—ecosystem protection, warning, insurance incentives and public disclosure—address behaviour and governance. Regulation must also provide safe serviced alternatives; eviction without affordable relocation merely redistributes vulnerability.

Thus a risk assessment is successful when it changes a development decision and is updated as exposure and climate conditions change. Prevention requires enforceable, inclusive and maintained systems, not static hazard atlases or code books.

### Visual: warning is a chain

```text
RISK KNOWLEDGE
who/where/what action is possible?
        |
DETECTION + MONITORING + FORECAST
        |
AUTHORITATIVE DECISION AND MESSAGE
        |
MULTI-CHANNEL DISSEMINATION
        |
RECEIPT -> COMPREHENSION -> TRUST -> ACTION
        |
FEEDBACK: false alarms, missed groups, outcome
```

If any link fails, a technically accurate forecast may save no one.

### 1. Multi-hazard means integrated, not identical

Different hazards permit different lead times and agencies:

| Hazard family | Principal Indian technical role | Warning reality |
|---|---|---|
| weather/cyclone/heat | IMD | forecasts and impact-based warnings |
| river floods | CWC with basin/state systems | gauge/model-based forecasts |
| tsunami | INCOIS/Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre | earthquake and sea-level detection |
| landslide | GSI and State/local systems | susceptibility/forecast services where available |
| earthquake | NCS rapid event information | occurrence cannot be deterministically predicted |

An earthquake sensor detects an event; it does not predict the exact time, place and magnitude in advance. A tsunami warning follows rapid earthquake and ocean assessment.

### 2. Impact-based warning

“200 mm rain” is a parameter. “Low-lying roads may become impassable; move vehicles and avoid underpasses” is impact and action information. Impact-based warning combines forecast, exposure, vulnerability and consequence.

### 3. Dissemination

NDMA's **SACHET** uses Common Alerting Protocol architecture to support interoperable alerts across channels. Cell broadcast, SMS, television, radio, sirens, apps, social media, local volunteers and public-address systems provide redundancy.

Platform existence does not prove last-mile reach. Track:

```text
alert issued -> device/channel reached -> person understood
-> action was feasible -> action occurred -> loss changed
```

### 4. Accessibility and trust

Warnings require local languages, audio/visual/tactile alternatives, simple actions, accessible shelters, transport and support for children, older persons, migrants and persons with disabilities. Repeated false alarms or inconsistent official messages reduce compliance.

### 5. Early action

Forecast-based action releases resources or triggers steps before impact: pre-position rescue teams, open shelters, protect hospital supplies, move livestock, drain reservoirs under protocol, suspend transport or activate cooling centres.

### 6. Global benchmark

Early Warnings for All targets universal protection by **2027** through four pillars: UNDRR risk knowledge, WMO observations/forecasting, ITU dissemination and IFRC preparedness/response. It operationalises Sendai Target G.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** More sensors and faster alerts solve warning gaps.
**Reply:** They reduce information delay.
**Residual:** People still need trusted authority, accessible messages, transport, shelter and the power to act; otherwise technology can widen inequality.

### UPSC use

Name the complete chain and one hazard-specific institution. Avoid generic “use technology” answers.

### Revision notes

1. Early warning is an end-to-end social and technical chain.
2. Risk knowledge identifies exposed groups and feasible actions.
3. Detection and forecast methods are hazard-specific.
4. Forecast, prediction, detection and warning are different terms.
5. Earthquakes cannot be deterministically predicted by current operational systems.
6. IMD, CWC, INCOIS and GSI have different technical mandates.
7. Impact-based forecasts translate parameters into expected consequences and actions.
8. SACHET uses Common Alerting Protocol architecture for interoperable dissemination.
9. Multi-channel redundancy reduces single-point communication failure.
10. Alert issuance is not proof of receipt, understanding or action.
11. Accessible warnings require language and disability-sensitive formats.
12. Early action links thresholds to pre-agreed finance and operations.
13. EW4All targets universal protection by 2027 and supports Sendai Target G.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a 100% technically accurate forecast not sufficient evidence of an effective early-warning system?

**Model answer:** The warning may be delayed, unauthorised, inaccessible, mistrusted or unactionable. People may lack transport, shelter or income to comply. Effectiveness requires risk knowledge, authoritative communication, receipt, comprehension, feasible early action and outcome feedback, not forecast skill alone.

**Misconception to avoid:** Warning accuracy and warning effectiveness are related but not identical.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** What constitutes an effective multi-hazard early-warning system? Discuss India's institutional requirements.

**Model (169 words):** An effective early-warning system links four functions: risk knowledge; hazard detection, monitoring and forecasting; authoritative dissemination; and preparedness for early action. It must identify who is exposed, predict or detect the specific hazard, communicate likely impacts and enable a feasible response.

India requires hazard-specific science within an integrated chain. IMD leads weather and cyclone warnings, CWC supports river-flood forecasting, INCOIS operates tsunami warning, and other agencies address earthquakes and landslides. NDMA's CAP-based SACHET architecture can distribute consistent alerts across multiple channels. Local administrations, utilities, transport providers, health systems and volunteers must then convert alerts into evacuation, shelter, service protection or cooling action.

The system must be multilingual and disability-inclusive. Redundant channels matter where power or telecom fails. Drills should test receipt, comprehension and action, while feedback should examine false alarms and missed groups.

Technology alone is insufficient: a precise forecast fails if residents lack trusted information, transport or safe shelter. Performance should therefore be measured by timely protective action and reduced loss, not only sensors installed or messages sent.

### Visual: technology must support a decision

```text
OBSERVE       satellite, radar, gauges, sensors, crowdsourced reports
   |
LOCATE        GIS layers, exposure inventories, network dependencies
   |
MODEL         forecast, scenario, damage or evacuation analysis
   |

   |
```

### 1. GIS and remote sensing

GIS combines spatial layers for hazard, population, buildings, shelters, hospitals, roads and utilities. Remote sensing provides repeated observations where ground access is difficult.

Applications across the cycle:

- pre-disaster susceptibility and evacuation mapping;
- during-disaster route, inundation and damage assessment;
- post-disaster change detection and reconstruction siting.

A visually precise map can still be wrong if data are stale, resolution is inappropriate or informal settlements are missing.

### 2. Satellites, radar, gauges and drones

Satellites cover wide areas; radar provides high-frequency local weather information; river and ocean gauges validate models; drones provide rapid local imagery. Each has cloud, terrain, battery, airspace, calibration, privacy or interpretation limits.

### 3. AI and machine learning

AI can detect patterns, fuse data, downscale forecasts, identify damage and prioritise resources. An MHA reply on **11 February 2026** stated that IMD had integrated AI/ML models into forecasting and described seven-day advance flood and cyclone applications under Mission Mausam. This is dated programme-status evidence, not proof of uniform skill for every location or hazard.

AI limitations:

- biased or incomplete training data;
- rare extremes outside historical patterns;
- false precision and weak explainability;
- model drift as climate and cities change;
- dependence on sensors, power and connectivity;
- automation bias in command decisions;
- privacy and exclusion in person-level data.

### 4. National disaster database

The amended Act's database definition includes risk assessment, fund allocation and expenditure, preparedness and mitigation plans, and risk registers. NDMA must create the national database; SDMAs feed State data.

Good governance requires metadata, common definitions, validation, update cycles, interoperability, access controls and public transparency consistent with privacy and security. “Database created” is not the same as “complete, current and decision-useful.”

### 5. Crowdsourcing and social media

Public reports can reveal blocked roads or trapped persons faster than official surveys. They can also spread rumours, duplicate requests, expose personal data or be manipulated. Verification, geolocation confidence and authoritative correction are essential.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Human judgement introduces bias, so automated allocation is more objective.
**Reply:** Models can improve consistency and speed.
**Residual:** Training data and objectives embed choices; accountable human review, appeal and field verification remain necessary.

### UPSC use

For every technology, write function -> decision -> limitation -> fallback.

### Revision notes

1. GIS stores, analyses and presents spatial information.
2. Remote sensing provides repeated observations but still needs ground validation.
3. Technology should be linked to a specific decision in the disaster cycle.
4. Stale or missing exposure data can make precise-looking maps misleading.
5. Satellites, radar, gauges and drones have different spatial and temporal strengths.
6. AI can fuse data, forecast patterns and classify damage.
7. MHA's 11 February 2026 reply is dated evidence of AI/ML use, not universal forecast skill.
8. Rare extremes and changing climate can weaken historical models.
9. Explainability, false precision and automation bias are governance risks.
10. The amended Act mandates a national disaster database and State inputs.
11. A database needs common definitions, validation, updates and interoperability.
12. Data access must balance transparency, privacy and security.
13. Crowdsourced information requires verification and rumour control.
14. Every digital system needs offline and redundant fallback.

### Concept check

**Question:** A damage-classification AI identifies one neighbourhood as low priority, but informal houses were absent from its training and base map. What failed?

**Model answer:** The model's data and representation failed. Missing informal settlements produced biased exposure and damage estimates. Human review, community reports, ground verification and uncertainty flags are needed before allocation. Technical consistency cannot correct systematic invisibility.

**Misconception to avoid:** Algorithmic output is not objective when source data omit vulnerable groups.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess the role and limitations of geospatial and artificial-intelligence tools in disaster management.

**Model (175 words):** Geospatial tools improve disaster decisions by integrating hazard, exposure, vulnerability and infrastructure data. GIS supports land-use planning, shelter siting and evacuation routes; satellites and radar monitor wide areas; gauges validate flood and ocean models; drones provide rapid local imagery. During response, these tools can identify blocked routes and likely damage, while post-event imagery guides assessment and reconstruction.

Artificial intelligence can fuse observations, detect patterns, downscale forecasts and classify damage. MHA reported AI/ML integration in weather forecasting in February 2026. Yet programme use does not establish uniform accuracy. Models may inherit missing informal settlements, biased loss records or changing climate relationships. Rare extremes, weak explainability, connectivity failure, privacy concerns and automation bias can distort decisions.

Technology should therefore be governed through common data standards, update cycles, uncertainty communication, independent validation, ground truth and accountable human review. The national disaster database mandated by the amended Act can improve planning and finance only if data are complete, interoperable and decision-useful.

Digital tools reduce information uncertainty; they do not replace institutions, local knowledge, accessible warning or offline fallback.

### 1. Community as first responder

Neighbours act before external teams arrive. Community-based DRR uses local knowledge for hazard mapping, warning relay, evacuation, first aid, search support, shelter management, damage validation and recovery monitoring.

Participation requires roles, training, equipment, insurance/protection for volunteers, refresher drills and integration with DDMA/ULB/PRI command. It must not become withdrawal of public responsibility.

### 2. Aapda Mitra and volunteer capacity

Aapda Mitra illustrates organised community response training. Pilot and expanded phases must not be conflated, and trained headcount does not equal current readiness. Retention, refresher training, equipment and activation protocols matter.

### 3. Participation must be organised, representative and protected

Participation works because residents hold route, household, livelihood and trust information that an external team may not possess. Two Indian applications show different functions: **Aapda Mitra** institutionalises trained local volunteers, while SHGs, NGOs and community-based organisations have supported warning, search-and-rescue, damage assessment and relief in coastal and tsunami preparedness.

| Participation advantage | Recurring challenge | Strengthening measure |
|---|---|---|
| local hazard and household knowledge | elite capture or exclusion of women, caste groups, migrants or persons with disabilities | representative committees and public participant lists |
| trusted warning relay | rumours, language barriers and low trust | authorised multilingual communication and repeated drills |
| rapid first response | weak equipment, insurance and command linkage | training, protective equipment, volunteer cover and DDMA/ULB protocols |
| local relief verification | favouritism and opaque beneficiary selection | transparent criteria, grievance channels and social audit |
| recovery ownership | consultation without budget or decision power | devolved roles, costed plans and community monitoring |

Community action is therefore neither spontaneous romanticism nor unpaid substitution for government. It becomes effective when law and administration give local actors defined roles, resources, safeguards and an accountable connection to formal command.

### 4. Gender and power

Women may carry care burdens, lose documentation or land access, face unsafe sanitation and gender-based violence, yet are often excluded from planning. “Women are vulnerable” is incomplete; identify the mechanism and include women in authority, assessment and recovery decisions.

### 5. Disability inclusion

Universal warning and shelter design should be normal preparedness, not special charity. Disability-disaggregated registers must protect privacy and remain updated. Evacuation plans should be co-designed with persons with disabilities and their organisations.

### 6. Displacement and protection

Displaced people may face aid discrimination, violence, trafficking, recruitment, lost documents and livelihood rupture. Disaster displacement does not automatically create refugee status under the 1951 Convention, whose test centres on persecution. Domestic law, human rights and specific relief systems still apply.

### 7. Animals and livelihoods

Livestock and companion animals influence whether households evacuate. Inclusive planning should consider safe animal shelter and veterinary support where relevant, without compromising human safety.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Disaggregated planning is too complex during emergency response.
**Reply:** Pre-disaster design simplifies response by identifying needs, responsibilities and accessible options in advance.
**Residual:** Registers can stigmatise or expose personal data; consent, minimisation and security are required.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is distributing the same SMS warning to everyone not necessarily inclusive?

**Model answer:** People differ in language, literacy, sensory access, phone ownership, trust and ability to act. Inclusive warning uses multiple formats and local relay, then provides accessible transport, shelters, care and livelihood protection. Equal messages can produce unequal safety.

**Misconception to avoid:** Formal equality of communication does not guarantee substantive equality of protection.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Community participation and social inclusion are central, not peripheral, to disaster resilience. Discuss.

**Model (171 words):** Communities are first responders and repositories of local knowledge. They can map hazards, relay warnings, assist evacuation and first aid, verify damage and monitor recovery. Programmes such as Aapda Mitra provide a route for trained volunteer capacity, but headcount without refresher training, equipment and command integration is insufficient.

Inclusion determines whether this capacity protects everyone. Persons with disabilities need accessible warnings, assisted transport and usable shelters. Women and girls may face care burdens, unsafe sanitation, violence and exclusion from compensation or land decisions. Children need reunification and education continuity; older persons need mobility and chronic-care support; migrants need multilingual communication and portable entitlements.

Participation must therefore be co-designed through DDMAs, local bodies, SHGs, CBOs and organisations of affected groups. Vulnerability registers require consent and data security. Communities should receive authority, resources and protection rather than being used as unpaid substitutes for the State.

Resilience is not achieved when the average population receives a warning; it is achieved when the last, least mobile and least powerful person can understand and act safely.

## Lesson 13 — Climate adaptation and DRR: convergence without conceptual collapse

Progress: 13 / 17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Climate-sensitive hazards, slow-onset risk and ecosystem-based DRR

### Visual: overlapping, not identical agendas

```text
DISASTER RISK REDUCTION                 CLIMATE ADAPTATION
all hazards                             climate variability/change
earthquake, chemical accident,          heat, drought, flood, cyclone,
tsunami, flood, heat...                 sea-level and slow-onset change
             \                         /
              \---- SHARED ZONE ------/
risk assessment, resilient infrastructure, warning,
ecosystem buffers, social protection, local planning
```

### 1. DRR is broader by hazard

DRR covers natural, socio-natural, biological and technological hazards, including earthquakes and industrial accidents that climate adaptation does not explain. Climate adaptation addresses current and future climate-related impacts, including slow-onset change.

### 2. Adaptation is broader by future climate horizon

Adaptation may transform agriculture, water systems, settlements and ecosystems over decades even without a discrete disaster event. DRR often uses event/loss and emergency-management institutions. Planning should connect them while preserving mandates and evidence.

### 3. Attribution discipline

Climate change can alter probability or intensity of some extremes, but no individual event should be attributed casually. Risk can increase even without hazard change because exposure and vulnerability rise. Write “climate-sensitive” unless event-attribution evidence supports a stronger claim.

### 4. Slow-onset risk

Drought, desertification, sea-level rise, salinisation and ecosystem degradation unfold over months or years. Longer lead time does not guarantee action. Indicators, livelihood thresholds, anticipatory finance and social protection are needed before crisis becomes visible.

### 5. Ecosystem-based DRR

Mangroves can reduce surge and wave energy; wetlands store floodwater; forests and vegetated slopes reduce erosion; dunes and reefs can buffer coasts. These are living infrastructure with biodiversity and livelihood co-benefits.

Eco-DRR is not a universal substitute for engineering. Protection varies by width, condition, hazard magnitude and site. Combine ecosystems with warnings, codes and engineered measures.

The Economic Survey 2025-26 describes mangrove restoration and protected wetlands as climate-resilience and flood-moderation measures. Programme ambition or finance does not prove site-level avoided loss.

### 6. Avoid, adapt, address

Mitigation avoids future climate forcing; adaptation reduces vulnerability to unavoidable impacts; residual loss and damage concerns impacts not avoided or adapted to. Disaster relief, adaptation finance and loss-and-damage mechanisms are not synonyms.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Integrating DRR and adaptation creates one coherent system.
**Reply:** Shared data, finance and local plans reduce duplication.
**Residual:** Full merger can obscure non-climatic hazards, differing time horizons, legal mandates and attribution standards.

### UPSC use

### Revision notes

1. DRR covers climate and non-climate hazards.
2. Climate adaptation addresses climate variability and long-term change.
3. The agendas overlap but are not synonyms.
4. Earthquake and industrial-accident risk demonstrate DRR's wider hazard scope.
5. Long-term water and crop transformation demonstrate adaptation's future horizon.
6. Event attribution requires specific evidence.
7. Exposure and vulnerability can raise losses without a change in hazard.
8. Slow-onset risks need thresholds and anticipatory action.
9. Mangroves, wetlands, forests, dunes and reefs can provide Eco-DRR services.
10. Ecosystem buffers are site- and condition-dependent.
11. Eco-DRR complements rather than automatically replaces engineering.
12. Mitigation, adaptation, disaster relief and loss-and-damage response are distinct.
13. Convergence should occur through shared risk information, plans and finance.

### Concept check

**Question:** A city restores wetlands to reduce flooding. Is this climate adaptation, DRR or both?

**Model answer:** It is DRR because it reduces flood risk and can be climate adaptation where it addresses current or projected climate-driven rainfall and water-level changes. The measure also has ecological benefits. Its classification depends on objective and evidence; the physical action can serve both agendas.

**Misconception to avoid:** Overlap does not erase the different scope and accountability of DRR and adaptation.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the convergence and distinctions between climate-change adaptation and disaster risk reduction.

**Model (147 words):** Climate adaptation and disaster risk reduction overlap where climate-sensitive hazards affect exposed and vulnerable communities. Both use risk assessment, resilient infrastructure, early warning, ecosystem restoration, social protection and local planning. Wetland restoration, for example, can moderate floods while helping a city adapt to changing rainfall.

They are not identical. DRR covers non-climatic hazards such as earthquakes, tsunamis and industrial accidents. Adaptation also addresses gradual climate change through long-term shifts in water, agriculture, health and settlement systems even without a discrete emergency. Their time horizons, institutions and finance may therefore differ.

Integration should use shared climate-risk data, compatible local plans, anticipatory finance and ecosystem-based approaches. Yet answers must avoid attributing every disaster to climate change; exposure and vulnerability can raise loss independently. Convergence is strongest when it reduces present risk while preparing for future climate conditions, without neglecting non-climatic hazards or confusing adaptation with relief and loss-and-damage response.

## Lesson 14 — Urban, cascading and compound disasters: protect the system, not one asset

Progress: 14 / 17 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Urban risk and critical-infrastructure interdependence

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: one failure becomes many

```text
EXTREME RAIN
   |
drainage failure -> road/metro closure -> staff cannot reach hospital
   |                                      |
substation flood -> power loss -----------+
   |                                      |
telecom outage -> warning/payment failure -> water and emergency coordination fail
```

### 1. Cascading, compound and systemic risk

**Cascading risk** occurs when failure in one system triggers another. **Compound risk** combines multiple hazards or drivers simultaneously or sequentially—for example cyclone wind, storm surge and extreme rainfall. **Systemic risk** spreads through interdependence beyond the original site.

Natural-hazard-triggered technological accidents—often called Natech events—include flood damage to a chemical facility or earthquake rupture of hazardous pipelines.

### 2. Why cities amplify risk

Impervious surfaces accelerate runoff; filled wetlands remove storage; dense settlement raises exposure; basements and underground systems create traps; informal housing may lack tenure and services; utilities share corridors; governance is fragmented among agencies.

Urban flood causation should therefore combine:

```text
extreme rainfall or river/tide condition
+ altered catchment and drainage
+ exposure in low land
+ maintenance and coordination failure
= disaster
```

### 3. Two urban-flood cases: shared risk, different urban mechanisms

| Case | Causal features | Governance and infrastructure lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Mumbai, 2005** | Exceptionally intense rainfall struck a coastal city with low-lying and reclaimed land, constrained drainage and waste-blockage vulnerabilities. Runoff, outfall and transport constraints interacted rather than operating as isolated failures. | Protect natural drainage corridors and outfalls; maintain drains and solid-waste systems; update drainage design for extreme rainfall and coastal backflow; map low-lying exposure; and provide redundancy for transport, power, telecom and emergency access. |
| **Chennai, 2015** | Northeast-monsoon extreme rainfall met altered wetlands and waterways, urban expansion into natural storage or conveyance spaces, and stressed drainage and land-use systems. | Plan at catchment scale across wetlands, tanks, waterways and storm drains; prevent further loss of storage; coordinate reservoir, drainage and emergency decisions; protect lifelines; and align land-use permission with flood pathways. |

The comparison is analytically useful:

```text
Mumbai 2005
-> low-lying/reclaimed exposure + drainage/outfall and waste-management stress

Chennai 2015
-> altered wetland/waterway storage + catchment and land-use stress

Shared conclusion
-> extreme rain becomes an urban disaster when hydrology, exposure,
   infrastructure dependence and fragmented governance reinforce one another
```

The cases should not be reduced to city names. Mumbai foregrounds drainage capacity, outfalls, maintenance and coastal lowlands; Chennai foregrounds wetland and waterway protection, catchment-scale planning and land-use control. Both show why emergency pumping and relief cannot substitute for prevention, maintained infrastructure and coordinated metropolitan governance.

### 4. Critical infrastructure

Identify essential services and dependencies:

- health and emergency care;
- water and sanitation;
- power and fuel;
- transport and logistics;
- telecom and digital payments;
- food and medicine supply;
- dams, industrial facilities and data systems.

Continuity needs redundancy, backup sites, protected utilities, manual fallback, mutual-aid contracts, spare components, cybersecurity and restoration priorities.

### 5. Informality and distributive justice

Informal settlements may occupy exposed land because safer housing is unaffordable. Eviction-only risk reduction transfers cost to the poor. Upgrading, tenure-sensitive relocation, rental support and livelihood access are part of resilient planning.

### 6. Urban governance after the amendment

UDMA can provide a city-scale platform where notified. It must coordinate with DDMA, ULB departments, utilities, police, health and neighbouring districts. Metropolitan hazards routinely cross municipal boundaries, so the institution's geography must match the risk system.

Do not collapse four different urban-resilience instruments:

| Instrument | Character and function |
|---|---|
| **CDRI** | international coalition promoting risk assessment, resilient standards, finance and Build Back Better for infrastructure; not city command |
| **Urban Flooding Cell** | coordination mechanism recommended by NDMA's 2010 urban-flood guidelines; a recommendation is not proof of current universal constitution |
| **Urban Flood Risk Management Programme (UFRMP)** | sanctioned mitigation programme financed through the National Disaster Mitigation Fund; sanction is not completed works or reduced loss |
| **UDMA under s. 41A** | statutory enabling route for a State-notified urban authority; existence and operation require State-specific evidence |

These distinctions prevent institutional confusion: a guideline recommendation, a financed programme, an international standards coalition and a statutory authority perform different jobs.

The Urban Flooding Cell was recommended in NDMA's **2010 Guidelines on Management of Urban Flooding**; the recommendation does not prove that a universal functioning Cell was constituted. UFRMP has moved beyond recommendation into sanctioned mitigation finance: Chennai was approved in December 2023, six further cities in July 2024, and **Phase II for 11 cities was approved on 1 October 2025 with an outlay of ₹2,444.42 crore on a 90:10 basis**. Approval and release remain distinct from completed drainage, maintained assets or reduced flood loss.

### 7. Crowd disasters

Crowd density can itself generate hazard. Capacity-linked entry, holding areas, one-way circulation, accessible exits, real-time density monitoring, medical access and authoritative communication are prevention measures. Technology does not compensate for exceeding safe capacity.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Redundancy is inefficient duplication.
**Reply:** Spare routes, backup power and distributed capacity carry an insurance value when failure consequences are large.
**Residual:** Unlimited redundancy is unaffordable; criticality and consequence analysis should prioritise it.

### UPSC use

Write one three-link cascade. It demonstrates systems thinking better than a long list of damaged assets.

### Revision notes

1. Cascading risk is failure propagation from one system to another.
2. Compound risk combines multiple hazards or drivers.
3. Systemic risk spreads through interdependent networks.
4. Natech events are technological accidents triggered by natural hazards.
5. Urban risk reflects hazard, altered catchments, dense exposure and fragmented governance.
6. Critical infrastructure is defined by essential function and consequence of failure.
7. Functional continuity requires backup, redundancy, rapid repair and manual or offline fallback.
8. Informal settlement is often an affordability and governance outcome; eviction can transfer rather than reduce vulnerability.
9. UDMA needs metropolitan and inter-district coordination.
10. Crowd disasters require flow and capacity design.
11. Redundancy should be prioritised by criticality, not applied without limit.
12. The Urban Flooding Cell is a 2010 guideline recommendation; UFRMP is an NDMF-financed mitigation programme.
13. CDRI, UFC, UFRMP and UDMA differ respectively as coalition, recommended cell, financed programme and State-notified authority.
14. Mumbai 2005 combined exceptionally intense rainfall with low-lying/reclaimed exposure, drainage constraints and waste blockage.
15. Chennai 2015 combined northeast-monsoon extreme rainfall with altered wetlands/waterways and drainage and land-use stress.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can strengthening a hospital building fail to protect health services during an urban flood?

**Model answer:** Health service depends on power, water, oxygen, telecom, access roads, staff, medicines and waste systems. If those networks fail, a structurally intact hospital may stop functioning. Resilience therefore maps and protects dependencies, backup and restoration priorities.

**Misconception to avoid:** Asset hardening alone is not system resilience.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Urban disasters are increasingly cascading failures of interdependent systems. Discuss.

**Model (179 words):** Urban disasters become systemic because dense populations and infrastructure depend on shared networks. Extreme rain can overwhelm altered drainage, flood substations, close roads and disrupt telecom. Power loss then disables water pumping, hospitals and digital communication, while blocked transport prevents staff and supplies from reaching critical facilities. A single hazard thus creates cascading service failure.

Compound events deepen the problem: cyclone wind, storm surge and rainfall may occur together, while a flood can trigger a chemical accident. Informal settlements often bear disproportionate loss because affordable housing, tenure and services are weak.

Risk reduction must map critical infrastructure and dependencies, protect utilities, create redundant routes and backup power, maintain manual fallback and pre-contract rapid repair. Land-use and drainage planning should restore wetlands and catchment storage. City institutions must coordinate ULB departments, utilities, DDMA, police, health and neighbouring jurisdictions; a notified UDMA can support this role.

Redundancy has cost, so consequence analysis should prioritise hospitals, water, power, telecom and emergency access. Urban resilience should be measured by continuity and equitable restoration of essential services, not the survival of isolated flagship assets.

### Visual: recovery choices create the next risk profile

```text

        |
temporary shelter + essential services + protection
        |

        |
RECONSTRUCTION DECISION
   +--> restore same site/design -> risk recreated
   +--> safer site/design/system -> Build Back Better
```

### 1. Recovery is not the last relief truck

Recovery begins during response but extends through housing, infrastructure, public services, livelihoods, ecosystems, mental health, documentation, compensation and institutional learning. Different households recover at different speeds.

### 2. Damage, loss and needs

- **damage:** physical destruction;
- **economic loss:** flow of foregone production, income or services;
- **needs:** resources and institutional action required for recovery.

Assessment must include unpaid care, informal work, tenancy, ecosystem services and service disruption—not only registered assets.

### 3. Build Back Better

BBB under Sendai Priority 4 requires:

- safer siting and resilient design;
- code compliance and quality control;
- restored and redundant lifelines;
- inclusive housing and public space;
- livelihood diversification and social protection;
- ecosystem restoration;
- improved institutions, data and maintenance.

Relocation may reduce exposure but can destroy livelihoods, access and social networks. It needs consent, services, tenure and income restoration.

### 4. Speed versus participation

Fast reconstruction can lock in poor design or exclude renters and informal workers. Excessively slow consultation prolongs displacement. Use pre-disaster recovery frameworks, standard designs with local choice, transparent beneficiary criteria and grievance redress.

### 5. Recovery finance and procurement

Emergency procurement needs speed and integrity. Publish contracts, unit costs and progress; verify construction quality; separate immediate relief from long-term capital work. Insurance payout and fund release are inputs, not proof of recovery.

### 6. Psychological and social recovery

Mental-health support, family reunification, protection from violence, cultural continuity and community institutions affect long-term resilience. Rebuilding houses without restoring agency is incomplete.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** BBB delays urgent reconstruction through higher standards and consultation.
**Reply:** Pre-approved standards, risk maps and recovery plans can combine speed with safety.
**Residual:** Higher standards require finance, skills and maintenance; otherwise safer design remains unaffordable or unenforced.

### UPSC use

Use claim -> safer change -> named implementation condition -> qualification.

### Revision notes

1. Recovery includes services, livelihoods, housing, ecosystems and institutions.
2. Damage, economic loss and recovery needs are different categories.
3. Informal work and unpaid care can be omitted from asset-based assessments.
4. Build Back Better belongs to Sendai Priority 4.
5. BBB reduces future risk rather than restoring identical pre-disaster conditions.
6. Safer siting and code-compliant design are only two BBB dimensions.
7. Lifeline redundancy, livelihoods, ecosystems and governance also matter.
8. Relocation requires consent, services, tenure and income restoration.
9. Speed and participation are a genuine recovery trade-off.
10. Pre-disaster recovery planning improves both speed and quality.
11. Emergency procurement needs transparency and quality control.
12. Fund release or insurance payout is an input, not a recovery outcome.
13. Psychosocial and cultural recovery affect resilience.

### Concept check

**Question:** A government rebuilds all houses to a stronger design but moves families far from jobs and schools without consultation. Is this Build Back Better?

**Model answer:** Only partially. Structural safety improved, but livelihood access, services, consent and social networks may have worsened vulnerability. BBB is multidimensional and rights-sensitive; it must improve safety and the capacity to sustain life, not only the building shell.

**Misconception to avoid:** A stronger structure can coexist with weaker social and economic resilience.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the meaning of Build Back Better and the challenges of applying it in post-disaster recovery.

**Model (139 words):** Build Back Better means using recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction to reduce future disaster risk rather than reproducing pre-disaster vulnerability. It includes safer siting, code-compliant construction, redundant lifelines, accessible housing, restored ecosystems, livelihood diversification, social protection and stronger maintenance and institutions.

Application faces trade-offs. Rapid reconstruction can lock in weak design, opaque procurement or exclusionary beneficiary lists, while prolonged consultation extends displacement. Relocation may lower physical exposure but destroy livelihoods, schools and social networks. Higher standards also need finance, skilled labour, inspection and long-term maintenance.

Governments should prepare recovery frameworks before disasters, use transparent damage-loss-needs assessments, protect renters and informal workers, provide grievance redress and publish contracts and quality audits. Communities need genuine design choices and livelihood support. Success should be measured through restored essential services, reduced exposure and vulnerability, and equitable recovery—not houses completed or money released alone.

### Visual: accountability loop

```text
law -> policy -> plan -> budget -> assigned officer -> implementation
```

### 1. National planning

India's first National Disaster Management Plan was issued in 2016; as of **3 October 2026**, the **revised NDMP 2019** remains the latest published national plan. It aligns disaster planning with Sendai, the SDGs and climate commitments and assigns responsibilities across phases and hazards.

The 2025 amendment changes the statutory plan-preparation architecture and provides review and update cycles. The Act's change does not itself prove a new public plan edition has been issued.

### 2. The NDMP's five themes and accountability limitation

The 2016 NDMP organised action through five thematic areas:

1. understanding disaster risk;
2. inter-agency coordination;
3. investing in DRR through structural measures;
4. investing in DRR through non-structural measures; and
5. capacity development.

This creates a comprehensive action map, but a documented limitation of the 2016 plan is that it did not set its own goals, targets, timeframes or funding projections comparable to Sendai or the SDGs. The criticism should not be repeated mechanically as if every provision of the revised 2019 plan were identical. The operational test remains whether the current plan translates themes into costed tasks, deadlines, accountable officers and measurable outcomes.

### 3. Federal coordination

States hold primary responsibility for rescue, relief and rehabilitation, while the Union supplements severe needs with technical, logistical, force and financial support. Cooperative federalism needs:

- common minimum standards with State adaptation;
- interoperable data and warnings;
- transparent assistance assessment;
- mutual aid and pre-positioning;
- respect for local context and accountability;
- clear command when hazards cross borders.

Centralisation can speed coordination in a major event but can weaken local ownership if plans become compliance documents. Complete decentralisation can produce incompatible standards and unequal capability.

### 4. Plan quality

Audit a plan through:

1. current risk and exposure data;
2. named responsible officer and substitute;
3. time-bound actions and budget source;
4. resource and contact inventory;
5. accessible warning and evacuation SOP;
6. exercise record and corrective actions;
7. recovery framework;
8. public version and grievance route.

### 5. CAG, legislative and social accountability

CAG performance audits examine legality, economy, efficiency and effectiveness. The national 2013 preparedness audit and State reports, including Karnataka Report No. 8 of 2025, show why institutional existence, plan production, preparedness and expenditure require independent testing. Audit findings are evidence of sampled implementation, not automatic nationwide generalisation.

Legislatures scrutinise policy and funds; courts review legality and rights; media and civil society reveal gaps; communities validate whether warning and relief reached them.

### 6. Outcome measurement

Count outcomes as well as inputs:

| Input/output | Better result indicator |
|---|---|
| volunteers trained | retained skill and drill performance |
| alerts sent | timely receipt and protective action |
| funds released | eligible reach, asset quality and reduced recovery time |
| plans approved | exercises, updates and completed mitigation |
| shelters built | accessibility, maintenance and safe occupancy |

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Outcome attribution is impossible because disasters differ.
**Reply:** Perfect causal isolation is rare, but comparable indicators, counterfactual analysis, exercises and repeated trends improve judgement.
**Residual:** Avoid simplistic cross-event casualty comparisons without controlling for hazard intensity and exposure.

### UPSC use

Conclude with a testable reform: funded action, responsible tier, deadline, public indicator and audit route.

3. A changed Act does not prove publication of a new plan edition.
4. States have primary response responsibility; the Union supplements severe needs.

6. A plan requires named responsibility, time, budget and resources.
7. Exercises should generate corrective-action records.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the number of district plans an inadequate national preparedness indicator?

**Model answer:** It measures document existence, not risk currency, staffing, budgets, warning reach, resource availability, exercise performance or corrective action. A plan can be copied, outdated or untested. Preparedness indicators must examine executable capability and outcomes.

**Misconception to avoid:** Counting plans can reward compliance paperwork rather than risk reduction.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** India's disaster-management challenge is increasingly one of accountable implementation rather than institutional absence. Examine.

**Model (174 words):** India has a dense framework: the amended Disaster Management Act, NDMA-SDMA-DDMA institutions, specialised response forces, statutory funds, the NDMP, technical warning agencies and community programmes. The central challenge is converting these instruments into local capability.

Accountability should link statutory duties to responsible officers, deadlines, public indicators, exercise corrections and audit. CAG, legislatures, courts, civil society and affected communities provide different checks. India does not primarily need another organisational chart; it needs funded district and urban capacity, interoperable data and measurable implementation across the risk-management cycle.

### 1. The deepest paradigm shift

The advanced question is not whether India can respond better; it is whether development stops manufacturing risk. Floodplain encroachment, unsafe construction, infrastructure interdependence and degraded ecosystems can raise loss even if forecasts improve.

### 2. The DDMA paradox

The tier with the most local knowledge and immediate responsibility often has the least dedicated technical capacity. Collector leadership provides authority but also creates competing priorities. Reform options include permanent multidisciplinary district cells, shared State technical pools, accredited local risk professionals and predictable mitigation finance.

### 3. Sendai's accountability asymmetry

Targets A-D measure losses that depend on hazard exposure as well as policy. Targets E-G measure means that governments control more directly. A country can improve strategy and warning indicators without clearly lowering loss. The 38-indicator framework enables monitoring but cannot substitute for causal evaluation.

### 4. The 2025 amendment's asymmetry

The amendment strengthens apex accountability by giving statutory status to NCMC and HLC and creating database duties. Yet UDMA and State-force provisions are enabling. This can produce a stronger top while local rollout remains uneven.

### 5. Finance must purchase risk reduction

The FC16 architecture separates response and mitigation, links grants to validated NDMIS data and introduces stronger allocation discipline. Advanced evaluation asks whether projects are selected by expected loss reduction, maintained and independently assessed. Expenditure absorption is not the same as resilience.

### 6. Urban governance mismatch

Risk systems cross ward, municipal and district boundaries, while authority and budgets remain fragmented. Metropolitan risk authorities need utility participation, common data, continuity standards and elected/community accountability—not merely another meeting body.

### 7. Technology's political economy

High-resolution data can improve targeting but can also privilege visible, formally mapped assets. AI procurement can create vendor lock-in and opaque models. Open standards, public-interest access, local ground truth and appeal mechanisms are resilience safeguards.

### 8. Uneven preparedness maturity across hazards

India's preparedness is not uniformly mature. The difference is institutional, not a claim that any hazard is “solved”:

| Hazard family | Relative strength | Continuing limitation |
|---|---|---|
| cyclones | long-evolved observation, forecasting, warning, evacuation protocols and shelter practice | exposure and hazard intensity differ across events, so casualty comparisons require controlled evidence |
| heatwaves | expanding forecasts and Heat Action Plans | newer local institutions, uneven ward/rural vulnerability analysis, occupational protection, cooling access and finance |
| urban floods | weather forecasting and growing mitigation investment | loss depends heavily on drainage, catchment change, wetland loss, utility interdependence and fragmented city authority |

Cyclone experience shows the value of repeated investment, standard operating procedures, last-mile evacuation and learning after events. Those institutional lessons can be transferred, but the same technical model cannot simply be copied to heat or urban floods. Heat action depends on health, labour, housing, water and local thresholds; urban-flood preparedness depends on land use, drainage, catchments and lifeline governance.

### 9. Reform package

1. risk-screen public and private investment before approval;
2. fund district/urban technical capacity;
3. convert plans into costed, time-bound actions;
4. maintain interoperable loss, risk and finance data;
5. audit warning reach and exercise performance;
6. protect inclusion and privacy;
7. integrate ecosystems and critical-infrastructure continuity;
8. publish recovery and mitigation outcomes.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** More monitoring and accountability slow emergency action.
**Reply:** Pre-agreed standards, delegated thresholds and transparent data can speed action.
**Residual:** Emergency discretion remains necessary; retrospective audit must distinguish good-faith urgency from avoidable opacity or neglect.

### UPSC use

Use this depth to qualify a Core answer, not to replace the clear institutional and Sendai foundation.

### Revision notes

1. Disaster risk can be created by ordinary development decisions.
2. Better response does not compensate for continuous creation of new exposure.
3. DDMA combines local responsibility with recurrent specialist-capacity constraints.
4. Permanent district cells and State technical pools can reduce the capacity gap.
5. Sendai loss targets and means targets have different accountability properties.
6. Thirty-eight indicators improve comparability but not automatic causal attribution.
7. The 2025 amendment strengthens apex bodies more automatically than local rollout.
8. Enabling urban and State-force provisions require notifications and resources.
9. Mitigation finance should be prioritised by expected risk reduction and maintenance.
10. Urban risk crosses administrative boundaries and utility silos.
11. AI and high-resolution data can reproduce formal-sector visibility bias.
12. Open standards, ground truth and appeal protect public-interest technology.
13. Emergency discretion should coexist with retrospective audit.
14. Advanced critique should end in specific, testable reforms.
15. Cyclone preparedness is more institutionally mature than heatwave and urban-flood preparedness, but cross-event outcome claims still require comparable evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a country improve Sendai Target E and Target G indicators without clearly reducing Targets A-D?

**Model answer:** It can adopt more DRR strategies and expand warning availability while exposure, inequality, unsafe construction or hazard intensity continue rising. Strategies and warnings are means; mortality, affected people, economic loss and infrastructure disruption are outcomes shaped by implementation and wider development.

**Misconception to avoid:** Means-of-implementation progress is valuable but cannot be reported as loss reduction.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Critically examine the proposition that India's disaster-management deficit lies less in institutional design than in local capability, accountability and risk-producing development.

**Model (221 words):** India's institutional design is extensive: an amended national law, NDMA-SDMA-DDMA tiers, crisis and finance committees, specialised forces, response and mitigation funds, technical warning agencies and a Sendai-aligned plan. The deficit increasingly lies in converting this architecture into reduced risk.

Development continues to create exposure through unsafe construction, floodplain occupation, degraded ecosystems and tightly coupled urban lifelines. DDMAs hold local responsibility but often depend on Collectors with competing mandates, thin technical staff and irregularly tested plans. The 2025 amendment strengthens apex bodies and databases, while urban authorities and State forces still require discretionary State notification and resourcing.

Accountability also favours visible inputs—plans, alerts, training and expenditure—over warning comprehension, exercise performance, service continuity and avoided loss. Sendai's means targets can improve while mortality or economic loss remains uncertain. FC16's mitigation windows and NDMIS condition create opportunities, but money must be linked to risk-based selection, maintenance and independent evaluation.

Preparedness is also uneven: cyclone warning and evacuation have a longer institutional history than heat-action or urban-flood systems, which require newer cross-sector local arrangements.

Reform should screen development for risk, create permanent district and metropolitan technical capacity, cost plans, publish interoperable loss and finance data, audit drills and warning reach, protect vulnerable groups and combine ecosystems with resilient infrastructure. Institutions remain necessary; their credibility depends on locally exercised, measurable and publicly accountable capability.

## Substantive teaching for 2026 GS-III Question 7

The apparent contradiction is not “infrastructure versus no infrastructure.” It is **risk-blind speed versus risk-informed development**. Ecologically sensitive areas—Himalayan slopes, Western Ghats, coasts, floodplains, islands and forest corridors—often combine fragile ecosystems, high hazard exposure and infrastructure deficits.

```text
strategic/service need
 -> multi-hazard and cumulative-impact assessment
 -> avoid the highest-risk site or alignment
 -> minimise footprint and induced development
 -> resilient design + ecosystem buffers
 -> monitor thresholds and maintain assets
 -> disclose residual risk and emergency plan
```

Management requires carrying-capacity and basin/landscape assessment rather than isolated project clearance; slope, drainage, sediment, seismic, fire and cascading-risk analysis; alternatives and no-project options; enforceable building and road standards; nature-based solutions; community and local-government participation; transparent data; and independent monitoring. A mountain road, hydropower cascade or coastal corridor should be judged by service benefits, induced exposure, evacuation and maintenance—not construction completion alone. The mitigation hierarchy begins with avoidance; compensation cannot make every high-risk site acceptable.

## Substantive teaching for 2026 GS-III Question 18

Community participation is foundational because residents provide local risk knowledge, warning trust, first response, identification of vulnerable households, relief verification and recovery ownership. Indian illustrations include trained **Aapda Mitra** volunteers, SHG/CBO roles in evacuation and relief, and community-linked cyclone-shelter and warning arrangements.

Participation can fail through token consultation, elite capture, caste/gender exclusion, inaccessible communication, volunteer attrition, weak equipment and insurance, unclear command, misinformation, political interference or opaque beneficiary lists. It must not become unpaid substitution for State duty.

Strengthening measures are phase-specific:

| Before | During | After |
|---|---|---|
| participatory risk maps, representative committees, accessible warnings, refresher drills and equipment | authorised volunteer roles, interoperable command, evacuation/first aid and protection protocols | social audit, grievance redress, damage validation, psychosocial support and livelihood/reconstruction decisions |

DDMAs, ULBs and PRIs should finance and institutionalise these roles, include women and organisations of persons with disabilities in decision-making, protect volunteers, verify skills through exercises and publish corrective actions. Participation is effective when it changes authority and outcomes, not merely attendance.

**Model answer:** The first may mean a State Disaster Response Force under section 44A; the second means the State Disaster Response Fund under section 48. The full term must be written because personnel and finance are legally distinct.

## Check 5 — Sendai

**Question:** Which targets measure losses and which mainly measure implementation means?

**Model answer:** Targets A-D concern mortality, affected people, economic loss and critical-infrastructure/basic-service disruption. Targets E-G concern strategies, international cooperation and multi-hazard warning/risk-information access.

## Check 6 — warning

**Question:** What evidence would show that an alerting platform improved outcomes?

**Model answer:** Timely delivery to at-risk groups, accessible comprehension, feasible protective action, reduced evacuation time or service disruption, and feedback correcting missed groups or false alarms—not message count alone.

## Check 7 — finance

**Question:** Why is a mitigation-project sanction not a DRR outcome?

**Model answer:** Sanction establishes approval and finance. Risk reduction requires construction or institutional delivery, quality, maintenance, beneficiary reach and evidence that exposure, vulnerability or expected loss changed.

## Check 8 — climate boundary

**Question:** Why should an earthquake-resilience programme not be described as climate adaptation?

**Model answer:** Earthquake hazard is geophysical, not caused by climate change. The programme is DRR. Some shared tools, such as resilient infrastructure or social protection, may also serve adaptation elsewhere, but conceptual overlap does not change the hazard's cause.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question — institutional precision

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish NDMA, NEC, NCMC, NIDM and NDRF in India's disaster-management architecture.

**Model (132 words):** NDMA, chaired by the Prime Minister, is the apex policy and guideline authority and exercises general superintendence over the response force. The National Executive Committee, chaired by the Union Home Secretary, coordinates and monitors implementation across ministries and States.

The National Crisis Management Committee, chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, is the nodal body for major disasters with serious or national ramifications. NIDM is the nodal capacity-development institution for training, research, documentation and policy advocacy. NDRF is the specialised operational response force; MHA reported 16 operational battalions in July 2025.

These bodies are complementary rather than hierarchical substitutes. NDMA sets direction, NEC coordinates execution, NCMC escalates major crises, NIDM develops competence and NDRF undertakes specialist field response. Clear triggers and exercised protocols are necessary because an organisational chart alone does not guarantee coordination.

**Unique quantified rubric (10):** NDMA 2 | NEC/NCMC 3 | NIDM/NDRF 3 | distinction 1 | conclusion 1

## Original 15-mark question — resilience architecture

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** A disaster-resilience framework must connect risk knowledge, institutions, infrastructure, inclusion, finance and recovery. Discuss.

**Model (167 words):** Disaster resilience is the capacity to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt and recover without recreating risk. It begins with risk knowledge: hazard, exposure, vulnerability, capacity and infrastructure interdependencies must be mapped and updated.

Institutions convert knowledge into action. NDMA provides policy, States adapt it, and DDMAs, ULBs and communities implement warnings, evacuation and local mitigation. Resilient infrastructure combines codes, retrofit, maintenance, redundancy and continuity plans. Inclusion ensures persons with disabilities, women, children, older people and migrants can receive warnings, evacuate and recover with dignity.

Finance must layer contingency budgets, response and mitigation funds, insurance and social protection. Technology—GIS, remote sensing, interoperable alerts and AI—should support decisions while disclosing uncertainty and retaining human accountability. Recovery must Build Back Better through safer siting, restored livelihoods, ecosystems and stronger services.

Sendai's priorities and targets provide a global benchmark, but strategy or alert counts do not prove outcomes. A credible framework therefore links every input to exercised capability, service continuity, equitable recovery and measurable reduction in mortality, disruption and loss.

**Unique quantified rubric (15):** definition/risk knowledge 3 | institutions 3 | infrastructure/inclusion 3 | finance/technology 3 | BBB/outcomes 2 | conclusion 1

## Original 20-mark question — critical reform

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Critically evaluate India's disaster-risk-governance architecture after the Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025.

**Model (203 words):** India's current architecture combines the Disaster Management Act, NDMA-SDMA-DDMA tiers, executive and crisis committees, specialised forces, statutory funds, technical warning agencies and a Sendai-aligned plan. The 2025 amendment, in force from 9 April 2025, strengthens this structure by defining disaster risk and a disaster database, giving statutory footing to NCMC and HLC, enabling urban authorities and State response forces, and expanding plan and regulation provisions.

Its strength is differentiated responsibility: national policy and standards, State adaptation, district coordination, local service delivery and community action. FC16's response-mitigation architecture and data condition can improve fiscal discipline, while interoperable warning and geospatial tools strengthen preparedness.

However, enacted power is not implementation. UDMA and State-force provisions require State notifications; district technical capacity remains uneven; plans may lack budgets and exercises; databases may be incomplete; and input counts can replace outcome evaluation. Urban and compound risks cross administrative silos, while vulnerable groups may remain invisible in formal data.

Reform should create permanent district and metropolitan technical cells, cost and time plans, risk-screen development, audit warning reach and drills, publish interoperable loss/fund data, protect privacy and link mitigation finance to maintained risk reduction. The amendment improves legal architecture; resilience will depend on locally exercised and publicly accountable capability.

**Unique quantified rubric (20):** current legal changes 5 | institutional strengths 3 | finance/technology 3 | implementation critique 5 | reforms 3 | verdict 1

# REMEDIATION

## Error 1 — hazard equals disaster

**Repair:** Show exposure, vulnerability and capacity. A hazardous event becomes a disaster through serious disruption beyond coping capacity.

## Error 2 — mitigation equals preparedness

**Repair:** Mitigation lowers likely impact; preparedness develops capability to act when threat or impact occurs.

## Error 3 — resilience means returning to normal

**Repair:** The old “normal” may contain unsafe siting and exclusion. Add adaptation, transformation and BBB.

## Error 4 — Sendai priorities equal Sendai targets

**Repair:** Four action priorities; seven targets A-G. Write both lists separately.

## Error 5 — the 2025 amendment is a Bill

**Repair:** It is Act 10 of 2025, gazetted 29 March and in force 9 April 2025.

## Error 6 — section 41A created every UDMA automatically

**Repair:** It enables State constitution by Gazette notification; rollout and operation need separate evidence.

## Error 7 — NDMA, NEC, NCMC and NDRF are interchangeable

**Repair:** policy, executive coordination, national-crisis coordination and specialist response are distinct.

## Error 8 — NDRF/SDRF always mean funds

**Repair:** Write “force” or “fund.” Both acronyms can identify different institutions.

## Error 9 — corpus equals resilience

**Repair:** Trace allocation through release, delivery, maintenance and reduced risk.

## Error 10 — alert sent equals warning success

**Repair:** Test receipt, comprehension, feasible action and outcome.

## Error 11 — technology removes uncertainty

**Repair:** State data gaps, model error, rare extremes, connectivity and human accountability.

## Error 12 — vulnerable groups need identical treatment

**Repair:** Name group-specific warning, evacuation, shelter and recovery barriers.

## Error 13 — every extreme event is climate-caused

**Repair:** Use climate-sensitive language and preserve attribution evidence; many hazards are non-climatic.

## Error 14 — Build Back Better means stronger buildings

**Repair:** Add siting, lifelines, livelihoods, ecosystems, rights, institutions and maintenance.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Risk vocabulary

- Hazard = potentially damaging process/event; disaster = serious disruption beyond coping capacity.
- Exposure = people/assets/services/ecosystems in harm's way.
- Vulnerability = physical, social, economic, environmental, institutional and attitudinal susceptibility.
- Capacity = knowledge, institutions, finance, social networks, infrastructure and ability to act.
- Working logic: hazard x exposure x vulnerability / capacity -> risk.
- Resilience = resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt, transform and recover while preserving essential function.
- Properties: robustness, redundancy, resourcefulness, rapidity, adaptability.
- Risk is dynamic and development can create new risk.

## B. Disaster-management cycle

- Prevention avoids new/avoidable risk.
- Mitigation reduces likely consequences; structural + non-structural.
- Preparedness = plans, warnings, drills, shelters, logistics, continuity and trained people.
- Response = evacuation, rescue, triage, immediate relief and essential-service restoration.
- Rehabilitation restores basic function/livelihoods; reconstruction rebuilds assets; recovery is wider.
- BBB reduces future risk in recovery; not identical restoration.
- Residual risk means response capacity remains necessary.

## C. Sendai exactness

- Adopted 18 March 2015; period 2015-2030; voluntary/non-binding.
- Priority 1 understand risk.
- Priority 2 strengthen risk governance.
- Priority 3 invest in DRR for resilience.
- Priority 4 preparedness/effective response + BBB.
- Targets A-D: mortality, affected people, economic loss/GDP, critical infrastructure/basic services.
- Targets E-G: strategies, cooperation, multi-hazard warning/risk information.
- Target E deadline 2020; others 2030.
- Sendai Framework Monitor: 38 agreed indicators.
- Yokohama 1994 -> Hyogo 2005-15 (five priorities) -> Sendai.
- PM's Ten-Point Agenda: AMCDRR, New Delhi, November 2016.
- Agenda themes: mainstream development; universal risk coverage; women's leadership; mapping; technology; university networks; social/mobile communication; local capacity; lessons from disasters; cohesive international response.

## D. Current legal status

- DM Act 2005 is principal law, read with 2025 amendment.
- Act 10 of 2025: assent/Gazette 29 Mar 2025; in force 9 Apr 2025 via S.O. 1648(E).
- s. 2(da) disaster database; s. 2(ea) disaster risk.
- s. 8A NCMC; s. 8B HLC.
- s. 41A UDMA; s. 44A State Disaster Response Force.
- s. 60A hazard-related action; s. 76A NDMA regulations.
- s. 41A/44A are enabling: State notification and operations require separate proof.
- UDMA chair Municipal Commissioner; vice-chair District Collector; Delhi/Chandigarh excluded.
- No dedicated constitutional entry exists; the legal footing draws, among other powers, on Concurrent List Entry 23 and Entry 29.
- Statute != notification != constitution != operation != outcome.

## E. National institutions

- MHA: overall national coordination.
- NDMA, PM-chaired: policy, plans, guidelines, NDRF superintendence.
- NEC, Home Secretary-chaired: coordination and monitoring.
- NCMC, Cabinet Secretary-chaired: major disasters with national ramifications.
- HLC: specified financial-assistance decisions.
- NIDM: capacity building, training, research, documentation, advocacy.
- NDRF force: 16 operational battalions; sanctioned 18,581 (MHA, 22 Jul 2025).
- NPDRR, Home Minister-chaired: multi-stakeholder review/advice.

## F. State, district and local chain

- SDMA, CM-chaired: State policy and plan.
- SEC, Chief Secretary-chaired: coordination and monitoring.
- DDMA, DM/Collector-chaired with elected local Co-Chair: district plan and implementation.
- ULB/PRI: land use, drainage, local services, shelters, relief and recovery.
- DDMA challenge: thin specialist capacity, competing duties, data and finance gaps.
- MHA reply 11 Feb 2026: Karnataka BBMP was the one reported UDMA then; not national rollout.
- Community co-produces last-mile action; does not replace public duty.

## G. Funds and forces

- NDRF force != NDRF fund; SDRF force != SDRF fund.
- s. 46 national response fund; s. 47 national mitigation fund; s. 48 State/district response and mitigation funds.
- FC16 2026-31: State corpus ₹2,04,401 crore.
- SDRF ₹1,63,521 crore; SDMF ₹40,880 crore; 80:20.
- National NDRF+NDMF allocation ₹79,406 crore.
- Sharing: 75:25 non-NEH; 90:10 NEH.
- Complete validated NDMIS data becomes grant condition from 2027-28.
- **4 Aug 2026 current anchor:** MHA confirmed heatwaves and lightning in the notified list for 2026-31.
- Corpus, release, expenditure, delivery and outcome are separate.

## H. Disaster finance

- Treat risk through acceptance, avoidance, reduction and transfer.
- Insurance transfers financial loss; does not reduce physical exposure.
- Parametric insurance offers speed but creates basis risk.
- Risk layering: budget/social protection -> insurance/credit -> catastrophic sovereign/pool support.
- Social protection prevents temporary shock becoming chronic poverty.
- Catastrophe instruments require strong data; do not claim widespread Indian deployment without evidence.
- Audit allocation -> release -> delivery -> maintenance -> outcome.

## I. Risk-informed development

- Risk assessment = hazard + exposure + vulnerability + capacity + interdependency + uncertainty.
- Map must alter land use, design or budget.
- Zoning prevents new exposure but needs safe affordable alternatives.
- Codes need adoption, inspection, certification, retrofit and maintenance.
- Structural measures can transfer risk or induce development.
- Non-structural: zoning, codes, warning, insurance, ecosystem protection and continuity.
- Critical infrastructure resilience means functional continuity.
- Backup, redundancy, spare capacity and rapid repair are essential.
- CDRI links risk assessment, standards, finance and BBB across the infrastructure life cycle; it is not operational command.
- CDRI reported 58 member countries and 12 member organisations as of June 2026; membership is not an infrastructure-resilience outcome.
- Ecologically sensitive infrastructure needs cumulative assessment, avoidance, low-impact design, monitoring and enforceable residual-risk limits.

## J. Early warning

- Chain: risk knowledge -> detection/forecast -> authoritative message -> dissemination -> comprehension -> action -> feedback.
- IMD weather/cyclone/heat; CWC river floods; INCOIS tsunami; GSI landslide; NCS rapid earthquake information.
- Earthquake prediction != rapid event detection.
- Impact-based forecast translates parameter to consequence/action.
- SACHET uses CAP for interoperable alerts.
- Alert sent != received/understood/acted upon.
- EW4All target 2027; pillars UNDRR, WMO, ITU, IFRC.

## K. Technology and data

- GIS integrates spatial risk layers; remote sensing supplies repeated observation.
- Satellites, radar, gauges and drones have complementary scales.
- AI can fuse data and classify/forecast but inherits bias and uncertainty.
- MHA 11 Feb 2026 reply: dated AI/ML forecasting integration status, not universal skill proof.
- National database needs metadata, definitions, validation, updates, interoperability and privacy.
- Crowdsourcing needs verification and rumour control.
- Digital systems require offline fallback.

## L. Community and inclusion

- Community roles: map/train/drill before; relay/evacuate/aid during; validate/recover after.
- Participation needs authority, equipment and protection.
- Disability: accessible warning, transport, shelter and recovery.
- Gender: safety, care, sanitation, maternal health, property/compensation and decision power.
- Children: reunification, safe space, nutrition, schooling.
- Elderly: mobility, medicines and care continuity.
- Migrants: multilingual alerts and portable entitlements.
- Displaced persons are not automatically 1951 Convention refugees.
- Equal message != equal ability to act.
- Aapda Mitra and organised SHG/CBO roles provide Indian participation examples.
- Main barriers: exclusion, elite capture, weak equipment, command gaps, rumours and opaque relief; remedies require representative authority, training, finance, protection and social audit.

## M. Climate-DRR convergence

- DRR covers climate and non-climate hazards.
- Adaptation addresses climate variability and long-term change.
- Shared tools: risk data, warning, resilient infrastructure, ecosystems, social protection.
- Do not attribute every disaster to climate change.
- Slow-onset risk needs anticipatory thresholds and finance.
- Eco-DRR: mangroves, wetlands, forests, dunes, reefs.
- Ecosystems complement, not automatically replace, engineering.
- Mitigation, adaptation, relief and loss-and-damage response are distinct.

## N. Urban, compound and cascading risk

- Cascading = failure triggers failure; compound = multiple hazards/drivers; systemic = spread through networks.
- Natech = natural hazard triggers technological accident.
- Urban risk: impervious cover + lost wetlands + dense exposure + fragmented utilities/governance.
- Lifelines: power, water, health, transport, telecom, food/medicine, data.
- Informal settlement requires upgrading or rights-sensitive relocation.
- UDMA must coordinate DDMA, utilities, ULB and neighbouring jurisdictions.
- UFC = NDMA 2010 recommended coordination cell; UFRMP = NDMF-financed mitigation programme.
- UFRMP status: Chennai approved Dec 2023; six cities Jul 2024; Phase II approved 1 Oct 2025 for 11 cities, ₹2,444.42 crore, 90:10.
- CDRI = international infrastructure-resilience coalition; UDMA = State-notified statutory authority under s. 41A.
- Crowd safety = capacity, flow, entry/exit, holding, communication and medical access.

## O. Recovery and BBB

- Damage, economic loss and recovery needs differ.
- BBB covers siting, code, lifelines, inclusion, livelihoods, ecosystems and institutions.
- Relocation requires consent, services, tenure and income.
- Pre-disaster recovery frameworks balance speed and participation.
- Procurement needs transparency and quality audits.
- Fund release or house count != recovery outcome.
- Mental health, culture and agency matter.

## P. Accountability and advanced reform

- NDMP 2016 first; revised NDMP 2019 latest published plan located.
- Five NDMP themes: risk understanding, coordination, structural investment, non-structural investment and capacity development.
- Documented 2016-plan gap: no own goals, targets, timeframes or funding projections; test the revised plan through costed actions and measurable responsibility.
- Act change does not prove a new plan edition.
- States lead response; Union supplements severe needs.
- Plan quality: data, officer, deadline, budget, inventory, SOP, drill, recovery, grievance.
- CAG/legislative/judicial/social audit test implementation.
- Inputs need outcome indicators.
- DDMA technical cells, metropolitan coordination and costed plans are priority reforms.
- Sendai means targets can improve without clear loss reduction.
- Cyclone warning/evacuation institutions are more mature than heatwave and urban-flood arrangements; avoid uncontrolled casualty comparisons.
- Emergency discretion needs retrospective accountability.

## Q. Rapid PYQ routes

- 2018: before/after Sendai measures + Hyogo comparison.
- 2019: define vulnerability + types + interaction with hazard/exposure/capacity.
- 2020 GS-II: federalism first; DM Act as one central-coordination example.
- 2020 GS-III: reactive relief -> whole-cycle law, institutions, warning, finance and BBB.

- 2026 Q7: infrastructure-DRR contradiction -> cumulative assessment, avoidance, resilient design, ecosystems, monitoring and local accountability.
- 2026 Q18: community value -> Indian examples -> participation barriers -> representative, trained, resourced and audited local institutions.

## R. Qualified conclusion

India's framework is strongest when the full chain works: risk-informed development prevents new exposure; national and State institutions set standards and finance; districts, cities and communities execute inclusive warning and mitigation; response protects life; and recovery reduces future risk. The decisive test is not institutional presence but measurable, locally exercised capability.

## Status and uncertainty controls

- Review date: **3 October 2026**.
- The 2025 amendment is enacted and wholly in force from 9 April 2025; it is not a Bill, draft or provision-wise pending law.
- Sections 41A and 44A are enabling; named authority/force rollout requires a State notification.
- The 11 February 2026 MHA reply is a dated implementation snapshot, not permanent nationwide status.
- The FC16 amounts are accepted award-period recommendations, not expenditure or outcome figures.
- Heatwave/lightning inclusion creates eligibility under the stated operational guidelines; it is not proof of relief delivery or heat-risk reduction.
- AI/ML integration is reported programme status; no uniform accuracy, seven-day skill or outcome is asserted for every hazard and location.
- CAG findings are audit-scope evidence and are not generalised beyond their audited jurisdiction/sample.
- Hazard examples are used only to establish supported causal, governance and infrastructure lessons; claims beyond the cited evidence are not inferred.
