### Command triage — baseline decisions

Without looking ahead, spend six minutes on these decisions:

1. A comprehension question asks for two causes and one consequence. Write the three answer slots.
2. A précis passage contains 330 space-separated words and asks for about one-third. Calculate the coaching target.
3. An essay prompt contains the word *although*. State what kind of thesis it demands.
4. A usage sentence appears acceptable but unusual. State whether you should rewrite it automatically.
5. Write the status of “120/300” in six words or fewer.

### Decision rules — marks, minutes and risk

The paper rewards compliance before elegance. Local official papers repeatedly instruct candidates to attempt all questions, write answers in English, obey specified word limits and strike off blank portions. The 2025 paper states, “All questions are to be attempted.” It also directs that specified word limits should be adhered to. These are short, exact operational instructions, not permission to assume that every future layout will remain unchanged.

The recurring sampled distribution is essay 100, comprehension 75, précis 75 and two usage/vocabulary groups totalling 50. The safest planning unit is therefore the **mark-minute-risk triangle**:

| Question | Main risk | Planning response |
|---|---|---|
| Essay | drift, repetition, unfinished conclusion | choose, outline, draft, count, revise |
| Comprehension | missed limb or outside knowledge | decode command, locate support, allocate |
| Précis | wrong ratio, copied wording, missing concession | map, calculate, compress, recount, obey title rule |
| Usage/vocabulary | careless overcorrection | answer certain items first; preserve accepted variants |

Format evidence needs an OCR warning. The 2019 PDF text extraction reads “Maximum Marks : 800”, but visual inspection recorded in the permitted source register shows printed **300**, and its section arithmetic is 100 + 75 + 75 + 25 + 25 = 300. Never silently treat an OCR string as visual truth.

Use a two-pass attempt:

```text
PASS 1: secure marks
clear usage items → direct comprehension → essay skeleton → précis idea map

PASS 2: judgement and polish
inference/tone → transformations → full essay → précis count → script audit
```

### Control card — pre-attempt recall

- Read every paper-specific instruction before choosing a question.
- Treat 180 minutes and 300 marks as verified sample-paper facts, not an eternal guarantee.
- Preserve the 2019 “800 OCR / 300 printed” warning.
- Translate every *and*, *or* and semicolon in a question into answer slots.
- Calculate a précis target before drafting.
- Choose an essay only after finding a qualified thesis and four distinct moves.
- Do not rewrite a standard variant merely because another form sounds more formal.
- Reserve a hard final-check window.
- Mark 120/300 only as a non-official coaching safety target.
- Record section scores and error codes after each mock.

### Guided allocation rehearsal

**Task:** “Explain the administrative and social costs of an unclear public notice, and suggest one remedy.”

**Demand map:** administrative costs | social costs | one remedy.

**Worked response plan:** one sentence on repeated staff queries and processing delay; one sentence on unequal harm to citizens with less time or literacy support; one sentence proposing plain-language testing before publication. The plan answers all three limbs before adding detail.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate spends 65 minutes perfecting the essay, then rushes the précis and leaves two usage subparts blank. Diagnose the strategic failure and redesign the remaining workflow.

**Model answer:** The candidate treated one answer as unlimited work and ignored section opportunity cost. A hard boundary should stop the essay after planning, drafting and a short revision window. The remaining time should be reallocated by marks and risk: map and draft the précis with an explicit target, answer all direct comprehension limbs, secure certain usage items, then use the final reserve for count and error checks. The redesign values a complete, compliant script over one polished section.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Qualifying” does not mean that unfinished sections are harmless. **Remediation:** rehearse the six section alarms—8, 50, 84, 127, 165 and 180 minutes—until stopping becomes automatic.

### Examples first — classify before rules

Label the underlined function and correct only what is defective:

1. The **news** from the districts are encouraging.
2. The office bought three **equipments**.
3. Between the chairperson and **I**, the proposal remained confidential.
4. Every reader should test **their** inference against the passage.
5. Walking through the archive, **the missing page was noticed**.

### Coached sentence mapping

**Sentence:** “A series of urgent messages were sent to Rina and myself, who was waiting outside.”

**Step 1—skeleton:** subject *series*; finite verb *were sent*.

**Step 2—agreement:** *series* is singular here, so *was sent*.

**Step 3—case:** after *to*, use *me*, not *myself*.

**Step 4—reference:** *who was waiting* may attach to *myself* awkwardly and excludes Rina. If both waited, write: “A series of urgent messages was sent to Rina and me while we were waiting outside.”

### Rule discovery — function, count and reference

A word class is a job performed in a sentence. *Record* is a noun in “the record” and a verb in “record the result.” Start with the finite verb, then locate its subject. This prevents an intervening plural noun from stealing agreement: “The quality of the replies **is** uneven.”

Nouns require three decisions:

```text
COUNTABLE? ──yes──> singular needs determiner / plural can take many-fewer
    │
    no
    ↓
MASS NOUN ────────> much-little-less / measure expression
    │
    └── meaning shifted? paper → material / a paper → article
```

Common examination traps include *advice, information, equipment, furniture, evidence, research, progress* and *news*. Use *items of equipment* or *pieces of advice*, not invented ordinary plurals. Some nouns vary by meaning, so classification must follow context.

Pronouns need correct case, one clear antecedent and consistent number. Use object forms after a preposition: *between you and me*. Reflexives need a same-clause antecedent or emphatic role; *Please contact myself* should be *Please contact me*. Clear singular *they* is accepted modern formal English: *Every applicant should retain their receipt.* A plural recast remains a useful clarity option.

Modifier errors are meaning errors. An opening phrase normally modifies the grammatical subject immediately after it. “After reviewing the file, the omission became obvious” falsely says the omission reviewed the file. Recast: “After reviewing the file, the clerk noticed the omission.”

### Retrieval grid

- Classify a word by function in its sentence.
- Find the finite verb before deciding agreement.
- Separate the head noun from an interrupting *of*-phrase.
- Test count, mass and shifted meanings.
- Give a singular count noun a determiner.
- Use measure expressions with mass nouns.
- Choose pronoun case by syntactic role.
- Give every pronoun one logical antecedent.
- Accept clear singular *they* or recast in the plural.
- Place a modifier beside its intended target.
- Test transitivity before attempting passive voice.

### Concept check

**Question:** Correct and code this sentence with the smallest justified changes: “Each of the research assistants were asked to submit two evidences which they had collected personally.”

**Model answer:** “Each of the research assistants **was** asked to submit two **pieces of evidence that** they had collected personally.” *Each* controls singular agreement [AGR]. *Evidence* is ordinarily uncountable, so a measure expression is required [CNT]. *That* is a conservative choice for an identifying relative clause, though *which* without a comma is also standard in many varieties [VAR]. Singular *they* correctly refers to *each assistant* and need not be changed.

**Misconception to avoid:** The nearest plural noun does not always control the verb. **Remediation:** box the head subject, cross out interrupting phrases temporarily and re-read the skeleton.

## Lesson 3 — Sentence grammar: completeness, agreement, time and connectors

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Owner context: canonical Basic 02 checked
🔍 Meaning check: tense and conditional choices tied to time relations
📰 Usage discipline: conservative exam choices separated from false “only correct” claims
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Progress: 3 / 10  |  Stage: Skills Course  |  Skill: Building complete and logical sentences

```text
ERROR LADDER
1 fragment or run-on?
2 subject–verb controller?
3 event time and aspect?
4 article/determiner?
5 verb–preposition pattern?
6 conjunction, parallelism or modifier?
```

### Error ladder — governing rules

A sentence needs a finite verb and a complete independent thought. A dependent opener beginning with *because, although, when* or *if* must attach to a main clause. Two independent clauses cannot be joined by a comma alone.

Agreement follows the grammatical controller:

| Pattern | Safe examination form |
|---|---|
| each/every/one of + plural noun | singular verb |
| a number of / the number of | plural / singular |
| along with/as well as | agree with first subject |
| either...or / neither...nor | normally agree with nearer subject; recast awkwardness |
| fraction/percentage + of | agree with noun after *of* |
| there is/are | agree with following real subject |

Tense represents a relation, not a decorative form. Use simple past with a finished past time; present perfect for a past event linked to an unfinished present period or present result; past perfect only when an earlier past point needs marking. In future time clauses, write “When the office **publishes** the form, we **will test** it,” not ordinarily *when it will publish*.

Conditionals encode reality:

```text
real future: if + present → will/may/can
unreal present: if + past → would + base
unreal past: if + past perfect → would have + participle
```

Articles depend on sound and identifiability: *a university, an hour, the file you sent*. General plural and mass nouns often take zero article. Prepositions should be learned as complete patterns: *comply with, depend on, responsible for, discuss the proposal*. Parallel structures must coordinate equivalent units.

### Timed fault scan

Repair these five items and name the governing relation:

1. Because the final bus had already left.
2. The notice was brief, several readers misunderstood it.
3. Neither the volunteers nor the coordinator were available.
4. If the office will publish the form today, we can test it tomorrow.
5. The report aims to identify delays, improving access and that costs should fall.

### Repair demonstration

**Faulty paragraph:** “A number of citizen has complained yesterday. The office has promised that when it will receive the missing records, it would issue a reply.”

**Timeline:** complaints occurred yesterday → simple past. Promise is current, and record receipt is a future condition → present in the time clause plus future in the result.

**Repair:** “A number of **citizens complained** yesterday. The office **has promised** that when it **receives** the missing records, it **will issue** a reply.”

### Sentence-control audit

- Attach every dependent fragment to a main clause.
- Repair comma splices with a full stop, semicolon or conjunction.
- Identify the agreement controller, not the nearest noun.
- Put finished-time events in the simple past.
- Use perfect aspect only when its time relation is needed.
- Keep ordinary future *will* out of *if/when/until* clauses.
- Preserve real, unreal-present and unreal-past conditional meanings.
- Choose *a/an* by sound.
- Use *the* when the reference is identifiable.
- Learn verb and preposition as one construction.
- Keep coordinated items parallel.
- Recast a grammatical but clumsy sentence when clarity improves.

### Concept check

**Question:** Correct this paragraph without changing its intended time sequence: “If the committee would have met last week, it will finish the review now. However the chairperson, along with two advisers, are still waiting for an information.”

**Model answer:** “If the committee **had met** last week, it **would be finishing** the review now. However, the chairperson, along with two advisers, **is** still waiting for **information**.” The first sentence is a mixed conditional: unreal past condition with a present result [COND]. The agreement controller is singular *chairperson* [AGR]. *Information* is a mass noun and does not take *an* [CNT/ART]. A comma follows the introductory conjunctive adverb *However* [PUNC].

**Misconception to avoid:** Every mention of the future needs *will*. **Remediation:** draw two points—condition time and result time—before selecting forms.

## Lesson 4 — Punctuation and capitalisation as a meaning system

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Owner context: canonical Basic 03 checked
🔍 Syntax check: punctuation decisions anchored to clause structure
📰 Variation discipline: serial comma and quotation styles treated as legitimate variants
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Progress: 4 / 10  |  Stage: Skills Course  |  Skill: Making structure visible

```text
PUNCTUATION DECISION TREE
two complete clauses?
├─ coordinator present → comma + coordinator
├─ close relation → semicolon
├─ second explains complete first → colon
└─ separate emphasis → full stop

removable information? → paired commas or dashes
identifying information? → no surrounding commas
```

### Contrast pair — punctuation changes meaning

**Unpunctuated:** “The pilot which began in june had a clear aim reduce delays however its records were incomplete”

**Clause map:** main statement + non-restrictive relative detail + announced explanation + contrasting independent clause.

**Repair:** “The pilot, which began in **June**, had a clear aim: reduce delays; however, **its** records were incomplete.”

An equally sound final split is: “The pilot, which began in June, had a clear aim: reduce delays. However, its records were incomplete.”

### Meaning-test diagnostic

Punctuate and capitalise:

1. after the district review the officers returned to bhopal
2. the proposal had one weakness it assumed that every user had internet access
3. the forms that contained signatures were accepted
4. its unclear whether the teachers lounge has been repaired
5. the director said the rule is simple record reasons

### Clause-to-mark rules

Punctuation follows syntax. A comma can separate an introductory phrase, list items or a non-restrictive clause, but it cannot stand alone between independent clauses. A semicolon joins closely related complete clauses; a colon introduces an explanation, list or restatement after a complete announcing clause. A dash creates sharper interruption and should be used sparingly.

Relative-clause punctuation changes meaning:

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *The forms that contained signatures were accepted.* | only the signed forms |
| *The forms, which contained signatures, were accepted.* | all the forms contained signatures |

Apostrophes mark possession or omission, not an ordinary plural: *the reader's note; the readers' notes; it's clear; its purpose*. Hyphenate a useful compound modifier before a noun—*a well-tested process*—but usually not after the verb—*the process is well tested*. Capitalise proper names, languages, formal titles used with names, named institutions and Acts; do not capitalise a generic role merely to make it important.

Quotation punctuation varies between standard British and American styles. Preserve a consistent system and distinguish direct from indirect speech. A direct question takes a question mark; a reported question does not: “She asked whether the office was open.”

### Punctuation audit card

- Map clauses before choosing marks.
- Never place a comma between subject and verb.
- Never join independent clauses with a comma alone.
- Use a colon only after a complete announcing unit.
- Use semicolons for close clauses or complex list items.
- Punctuate restrictive and supplementary information by meaning.
- Separate contraction *it's* from possessive *its*.
- Do not form ordinary plurals with apostrophes.
- Hyphenate useful compound modifiers before nouns.
- Capitalise proper names, not generic importance.
- Use question marks only for direct questions.
- Accept consistent standard punctuation variants.

### Concept check

**Question:** Edit this sentence and explain two meaning choices: “The officers who completed the course, received certificates however the officers lounge remained closed.”

**Model answer:** If only some officers completed the course: “The officers who completed the course received certificates; however, the officers' lounge remained closed.” The identifying relative clause takes no commas, and no comma belongs between subject and verb. A semicolon separates the independent clauses before *however*. The plural possessive is *officers'*. If all previously identified officers completed the course, commas may surround “who completed the course”; that change alters the meaning and requires context.

**Misconception to avoid:** Punctuation is a single-answer decoration exercise. **Remediation:** state the intended relation—identity, addition, explanation or separation—before inserting a mark.

## Lesson 5 — Vocabulary, spelling, word formation, idioms and proverbs

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Owner context: canonical Basic 04 checked
🔍 Retrieval check: meaning, class, collocation and register tested together
📰 Fairness discipline: defensible synonyms and standard spelling variants accepted
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Progress: 5 / 10  |  Stage: Skills Course  |  Skill: Exact words in usable contexts

| Entry field | Question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| class | noun, verb, adjective or adverb? | *credible* = adjective |
| denotation | what does it plainly mean? | believable |
| collocation | what normally accompanies it? | credible explanation |
| register | formal, neutral or informal? | neutral-formal |
| contrast | what nearby word differs? | credulous = too ready to believe |

### Context-sorting method

Vocabulary is controlled choice, not display. Learn a word with its class, plain meaning, normal partners and one contrast. A synonym must fit denotation, intensity and register. *Reduce* does not always mean *eradicate*; *childlike* and *childish* carry different evaluations.

Confusables need a contrast sentence:

```text
affect (usually verb) → influence an outcome
effect (usually noun) → an outcome or result
effect (verb) → bring about a change
```

Word formation begins with the slot. A determiner often predicts a noun; a linking verb often predicts an adjective; *to* commonly predicts a base verb. Spelling improves through word families and retrieval: *maintain → maintenance; receive → receipt; decide → decision*. British and American standard variants can both be correct if consistently used.

Idioms have conventional non-literal meanings. A creditworthy sentence must force that meaning. “The contractor cut corners” is too thin; “The contractor cut corners by omitting the required safety test” makes the improper economy clear. A proverb expresses a general lesson and should support, not replace, reasoning.

### Retrieval sort

Complete without a dictionary:

1. The new evidence may ___ the earlier claim. (*corroborate/corroboration*)
2. The clerk was ___ enough not to reveal the address. (*discreet/discrete*)
3. Correct: “The maintanance team definately recieved the message.”
4. Give a contextual antonym for *rigid* in “a rigid procedure”.
5. Use *cut corners* naturally in a formal sentence.
6. Explain why “Where there is a will, there is a way” is not a factual guarantee.

### Lexical recall card

- Learn every word with part of speech and collocation.
- Choose a sense licensed by context.
- Match synonym intensity and register.
- Keep antonyms in the same grammatical class where possible.
- Separate confusables through contrast sentences.
- Infer word form from the grammatical slot.
- Retrieve spellings from word families.
- Accept consistent British or American standard spelling.
- Make an idiom's non-literal meaning unmistakable.
- Use a proverb as support, not proof.
- Avoid invented quotations and decorative rarity.
- Re-test missed entries through original sentences.

### Usage demonstration

**Item:** “The explanation was plausible but the committee remained incredulous.”

*Plausible* means apparently reasonable or believable. *Incredulous* describes people who are unwilling or unable to believe, not a claim that is unbelievable. The sentence is correct: a plausible account can still face incredulous listeners. Replacing *incredulous* with *incredible* would change the grammar and meaning.

### Concept check

**Question:** Supply and justify the best forms: “The audit produced a ___ conclusion because the team checked the figures ___. Its care helped ___ public concern.” Use forms related to *defend, independent,* and *alleviation*.

**Model answer:** “The audit produced a **defensible** conclusion because the team checked the figures **independently**. Its care helped **alleviate** public concern.” The noun slot after *a* needs an adjective modifying *conclusion*. The verb *checked* needs the adverb *independently*. After *helped*, the base verb *alleviate* is natural. *Definitive* would overstate finality and is not a form of *defend*.

**Misconception to avoid:** A related word is automatically the correct form. **Remediation:** mark the slot N/V/Adj/Adv, then test meaning and collocation.

## Lesson 6 — Error correction, voice, narration and transformation

📚 Owner context: canonical Basic 05 checked
🔍 Equivalence check: correction and transformation preserve proposition

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Progress: 6 / 10  |  Stage: Skills Course  |  Skill: Minimal repair without meaning loss

```text
MEANING-FIRST EDITING SCAN
finite verb → AGR → TNS/COND → CNT/ART → PRO
→ PREP/PHR → WF/SPL → MOD/PAR → PUNC → VAR
```

### Before-and-after transformation

**Original:** “Only Leela checked the final table.”

Possible meanings:

```text
ONLY Leela checked it → nobody else checked
Leela only CHECKED it → she did not approve or alter it
Leela checked only THE FINAL TABLE → no other table
```

Moving *only* is not stylistic polishing; it changes focus. A transformation must retain the intended scope.

### Constraint rules

Correction begins with intended meaning. Make the smallest change that restores clear standard formal English. Use codes so a miss becomes a retrievable rule:

| Code | Family | Repair question |
|---|---|---|
| AGR/CNT/PRO | agreement, countability, pronoun | what controls number and reference? |
| TNS/COND | time and condition | what is real, unreal, earlier or continuing? |
| ART/PREP/PHR | construction | what complete pattern is required? |
| MOD/PAR/PUNC | structure | what modifies or coordinates what? |
| SPL/WF | spelling or form | what family and slot apply? |
| VAR | accepted variation | is this genuinely wrong or merely less preferred? |

Voice requires a suitable object. Preserve tense, aspect, modal, object roles and necessary agent. With two objects, more than one passive may be possible: “The applicants have been sent revised schedules” or “Revised schedules have been sent to the applicants.”

Narration requires speaker, listener, reporting time and purpose. A request becomes *asked/requested + object + to-infinitive*; a yes/no question uses *whether/if* and statement order. Backshift is contextual, not mechanical. Universal truths normally remain present.

Transformation passes only if tense, polarity, agent, emphasis, scope and proposition survive. *Unless* safely replaces *if...not* only when polarity remains equivalent. *No sooner...than* and *hardly/scarcely...when* require inversion and correct pairing. Passive voice or a degree change is not valid when it alters the comparison set or focus.

### Transformation trial

Correct or transform:

1. “The panel discussed about the complaint.”
2. Passive: “The office has sent the applicants revised schedules.”
3. Report: Mira said to Arun, “Please wait until I return.”
4. Begin with *Unless*: “If the record is not verified, it should not be published.”
5. Correct only if necessary: “Every witness should give their account separately.”
6. Add a tag: “Let us examine the evidence, ___?”

### Equivalence audit

- Establish intended meaning before editing.
- Scan finite verb and agreement first.
- Make the smallest valid correction.
- Label accepted variants as variants.
- Confirm an object before forming passive voice.
- Preserve tense, aspect, modal and object roles.
- Choose a reporting verb by communicative purpose.
- Use statement order in reported questions.
- Backshift only when context requires it.
- Preserve polarity in *unless* transformations.
- Use correct correlative pairs.
- Preserve comparison set, agent, emphasis and scope.
- Derive question tags from auxiliary and polarity.
- Log, rewrite and retry every coded error.

### Concept check

**Question:** Transform and justify: “The supervisor said to the trainees, ‘Do not publish the table unless you have checked every total.’”

**Model answer:** “The supervisor **told the trainees not to publish** the table unless they **had checked** every total.” *Told + object + not to-infinitive* reports the negative command. *They* replaces *you* because the trainees are now referred to in the third person. With a past reporting frame, *have checked* may backshift to *had checked*. Retaining *have checked* can be justified if the instruction is being reported while still immediately operative; the context should decide [VAR].

**Misconception to avoid:** Reported speech is a table of compulsory word substitutions. **Remediation:** write speaker, listener, reporting time and continuing validity above the sentence.

### Passage annotation trial

Read this short original paragraph:

> A public dashboard may publish accurate numbers and still create a false impression. If it counts applications received but not applications completed, growth may indicate either wider access or a growing backlog. The figure becomes useful only when readers know what was counted, over what period and against which outcome.

Answer:

1. Why can an accurate number mislead?
2. What does *the figure* refer to?
3. Is the tone dismissive, celebratory or cautionary? Give one signal.

### Evidence checklist

- Read commands before close reading.
- Mark thesis, contrast, reason, example, concession and conclusion.
- Answer from passage evidence.
- Separate literal statement from inference.
- Use calibrated verbs such as *suggests* or *implies*.
- Name tone with a stance adjective.
- Explain the function of an example.
- Resolve references logically, not by nearest noun alone.
- Fit vocabulary meaning to local context.
- Allocate every multipart limb before drafting.
- Start with the answer, not a ceremonial phrase.
- Quote only a crucial word or phrase.
- Keep responses clear, correct and concise.

### Inference and command rules

Read the questions before the passage so commands become search instructions. Literal questions ask what the passage states. Inference combines clues but must remain minimally extended. Tone is attitude, not subject; *critical, cautious, qualified, approving, sceptical* are more useful than *informative*. Purpose asks what an example or contrast does in the argument.

For every multipart question, draw answer boxes. A 15-mark item asking for “causes, effects and limits” requires all three. Begin with the answer rather than “The author says...”. Use outside knowledge only if the question explicitly permits it; the ordinary comprehension task tests fidelity to the passage.

### Guided evidence-to-answer demonstration

**Worked prompt:** Why does the paragraph distinguish receipt from completion, and what reporting improvement follows?

**Demand:** purpose of distinction | improvement.

**Worked answer:** It shows that a higher receipt count can represent either access or unresolved workload. Reporting should therefore pair intake with completion or outcome data and define the period and unit counted.

The response paraphrases both limbs and adds no policy theory absent from the paragraph.

### Concept check

**Question:** Read the original passage and answer in no more than 90 words: “Rules are often criticised for slowing action, yet a well-designed rule can preserve speed by reducing repeated argument. When roles, evidence standards and escalation paths are known beforehand, teams need not renegotiate authority during every emergency. The danger lies not in rules as such but in rules that are inaccessible, contradictory or never tested.” What qualified claim does the author make, and which two conditions support it?

**Model answer:** The author argues that rules can accelerate rather than obstruct action when they settle recurring coordination questions in advance. This benefit depends first on clarity about roles, evidence and escalation, which prevents authority from being renegotiated during an emergency. It also depends on rules being accessible, internally consistent and tested in practice. The passage therefore criticises defective rules, not rule-based action itself.

**Misconception to avoid:** A plausible opinion is not automatically a passage inference. **Remediation:** underline two textual clues for every inferential sentence and delete anything they cannot support.

### Compression stages

A précis preserves the architecture of thought, not the surface wording. Keep the thesis, major reasons, indispensable concession and final judgement. Remove illustrations, repetition, rhetorical questions and decorative phrasing unless an example carries a unique distinction.

For this course, count space-separated words and treat a hyphenated form as one. This is a transparent coaching convention, not a claimed official counting rule. Record:

```text
SOURCE COUNT → ONE-THIRD TARGET → FINAL COUNT
```

“About” does not publish a tolerance. Aim near the calculated target without sacrificing a major idea; any number or tolerance printed in the paper overrides coaching practice. The sampled official papers direct candidates not to title the précis. The 2025 wording is: “Do not give or suggest a title to it.” Follow that literally: write no title and no title-like label.

Paraphrase structure as well as isolated words. Preserve technical terms where substitution would distort meaning. Keep the author's stance and qualification; do not add a moral, example or personal opinion.

### Guided count audit

**Diagnostic source map:** visible purchase bias | software alone insufficient | process + training + outcome evaluation required.

**Model compression:** “Software improves institutions only when reliable processes, trained users and outcome-based management support the visible investment.”

**Count:** source 59 → target about 20 → model 16. The model is too short for a formal one-third task even though its meaning is sound. A better proportionate version is:

“Software creates value only when institutions pair investment with reliable records, trained users, redesigned processes and outcome-based evaluation in practice.”

**Count:** 20 words.

### One-third trial

Compress this 59-word original passage to one sentence of about 20 words:

> Organisations often buy new software because purchase is visible and training is not. Yet a tool cannot improve work when users do not understand its purpose, records are unreliable, or managers reward speed without accuracy. Technology produces value only when institutions redesign the surrounding process, teach the people who operate it and examine whether the promised outcome actually improves.

### Fidelity-and-ratio audit

- Count the source using one declared method.
- Calculate the instructed proportion before drafting.
- Mark thesis, reasons, concession and verdict.
- Remove examples and repetition first.
- Preserve the author's qualification.
- Paraphrase syntax and wording.
- Retain irreplaceable technical terms.
- Write connected prose, not notes.
- Add no opinion, evidence or moral.
- Obey the no-title instruction literally.
- Count the final draft.
- Repair ratio by cutting detail, not central logic.

### Concept check

**Question:** Write a one-third précis of the original passage below. Give no title.

> Public consultation is sometimes treated as a contest in which the most frequently repeated demand must win. That view confuses listening with counting. A consultation can reveal practical obstacles, missing information and groups that a policy designer failed to notice, but it cannot transfer every decision to whoever submits the largest number of responses. Participants may possess unequal time, organisation and access; a numerical majority among submissions may therefore differ from the affected public. Nor are all preferences mutually compatible. Authorities must still explain the objective, compare evidence, consider rights and bear responsibility for the final choice. This does not make consultation decorative. Its value lies in forcing decision-makers to encounter knowledge outside their own offices and to state why some suggestions were accepted, modified or rejected. A credible process publishes the question clearly, provides accessible ways to participate, identifies whose voices may be missing and reports the reasons for the final decision. Consultation should improve the quality and legitimacy of judgement, not replace judgement with an unexamined count.

**Model answer:** Public consultation should inform, not replace, responsible judgement. It can reveal barriers, missing knowledge and overlooked groups, but unequal participation and conflicting preferences make response counts unreliable. Authorities must compare evidence and rights, broaden access, identify absent voices, and explain why suggestions were accepted, modified or rejected. Accountable reasoning gives consultation its legitimacy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Shortness alone is not précis quality. **Remediation:** compare the draft against four boxes—thesis, reasons, qualification and verdict—then recount. **Count record:** source 169 words → target about 56 words → model 53 words.

### Prompt-choice diagnostic

For the prompt **“Convenience can save time without necessarily improving life,”** write:

1. a one-sentence qualified thesis;
2. four distinct paragraph jobs;
3. one genuine counterargument;
4. one unsafe claim you will avoid.

### Guided outline workshop

**Prompt:** “Efficiency without explanation can weaken trust.”

**Weak thesis:** Efficiency is important but trust is also important.

**Qualified thesis:** Faster decisions can improve public service, but when institutions do not explain criteria, errors and appeal routes, efficiency appears arbitrary and eventually undermines cooperation.

**Body spine:** value of speed → why reasons matter → meaningful objection about emergency action → proportionate response through brief reasons and later review.

### Paragraph-craft rules

Choose the topic for which you can define every key term, state a qualified position, develop four non-repeating moves and use defensible examples. The word *can* invites conditions; *although* or *yet* requires a concession; *and* may require a relationship rather than two separate mini-essays.

Plan for six minutes:

| Minute | Output |
|---:|---|
| 1 | prompt in your own words |
| 2 | thesis with qualification |
| 3–4 | four paragraph jobs |
| 5 | objection or limit |
| 6 | conclusion direction |

Write continuous prose rather than a GS-style list. Each paragraph should have one controlling claim, explanation, a safe illustration where useful and a link to the thesis. Avoid invented statistics, quotations and grand openings. Plain correct language scores more reliably than ornament.

For coaching, count space-separated words and target about 600 because that demand appears in the sampled official papers. Reserve five minutes to check thesis, paragraph progression, counterargument, unsupported claims, agreement, punctuation and count.

### Essay revision card

- Restate every key term before choosing.
- Find the tension or relationship in the wording.
- State a qualified arguable thesis in the introduction.
- Give each body paragraph a distinct job.
- Explain mechanisms, not slogans.
- Use only safe, relevant examples.
- Link examples back to the claim.
- Present a real counterargument or limit.
- Answer or accommodate the counterargument.
- Prefer plain formal English.
- Avoid invented facts and quotations.
- Conclude from the developed argument.
- Count words and remove repetition before substance.

### Concept check

**Question:** Write a responsive essay plan and a model introduction for: “A society learns not only from success but also from how it examines failure.” Keep the introduction between 90 and 120 words.

**Model answer:** **Thesis:** Failure becomes socially useful only when institutions preserve evidence, protect honest reporting, distinguish blame from explanation and convert findings into tested reform. **Plan:** (1) success can conceal fragile processes; (2) fair inquiry identifies causes and preserves trust; (3) fear-driven blame suppresses evidence; (4) learning needs assigned corrective action and re-testing; counterargument—some failures require immediate accountability; response—accountability and systemic learning are compatible. **Model introduction:** Societies celebrate success because it offers confidence and examples to imitate. Yet success may hide weak systems that happened not to break. Failure exposes assumptions, neglected risks and the actual consequences of decisions. It becomes valuable, however, only when examination is honest and followed by change. A society learns from failure when it preserves evidence, allows candid reporting, assigns responsibility fairly and tests whether corrective action works. Without those conditions, inquiry becomes either ritual blame or organised forgetting. This discipline turns an adverse event into knowledge without excusing negligence or denying the need for prompt accountability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Balance means writing equal praise and criticism without a thesis. **Remediation:** underline the claim, qualification and final direction; if any is absent, rewrite before drafting.

**Question:** What distinction does the passage make, and why does explanation matter?

- Find finite verb, subject, object/complement and modifiers.
- Classify words by function.
- Control countability, pronoun case/reference and modifier attachment.
- Repair fragments and comma splices.
- Make agreement follow the grammatical controller.
- Choose tense and conditionals from time and reality.
- Use articles, determiners and prepositions as meaning systems.
- Punctuate clauses and relative information by syntax.
- Learn vocabulary through class, meaning, collocation, register and contrast.
