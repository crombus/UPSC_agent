# Centre-State and Inter-State Relations — Live Session Edition

India’s Constitution does not merely divide power between the Union and the States. It also explains **how the two levels make laws, implement them, share money, settle disputes and preserve a common national market**. This session follows that operating logic: competence first, administration next, finance after that, and finally the institutions that manage conflict and cooperation.

The visual shows why a Centre-State controversy cannot be understood through a single slogan such as “centralisation”. The same policy may be legally within State competence, administratively dependent on Union coordination and financially dependent on Union transfers.

### 1. Begin with an ordinary example

Imagine a nationwide disease outbreak.

- **Legislative question:** Does the governing law rest on public health, a State field, or on another constitutionally available Union/Concurrent field?
- **Administrative question:** May the Union issue directions, and which government possesses the delivery machinery?
- **Financial question:** Who funds hospitals, vaccines and emergency transfers?
- **Inter-State question:** How are movement, quarantine and cross-border coordination managed?

The answer changes at each stage. Identifying the correct channel prevents a common UPSC error: listing every federal grievance without explaining the mechanism involved.

### 2. The four channels

| Channel | Central question | Main constitutional area | Typical dispute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legislative | Who can enact the rule? | Part XI, Articles 245–255 | Union law allegedly enters a State field |
| Administrative | Who must implement, direct or coordinate? | Part XI, Articles 256–263 | Union direction versus State executive autonomy |
| Financial | Who taxes, receives, grants or borrows? | Part XII, especially Articles 265–293 | Divisible pool, grants, cesses or borrowing consent |
| Inter-State/economic | How do governments coexist horizontally? | Articles 261–263; Part XIII | Water, recognition of judgments, councils or trade barriers |

These channels overlap but do not collapse into one another. A State may possess legislative competence but lack fiscal capacity. The Union may finance a programme without acquiring general legislative competence over its subject. A council may coordinate policy but cannot replace a court or tribunal where binding adjudication is required.

### 3. The governing idea: self-rule plus shared rule

Federal relations combine two constitutional needs:

```text
SELF-RULE
States retain meaningful fields, institutions and electoral accountability
                            +
SHARED RULE
Union and States cooperate on national markets, crises and common resources
                            =
WORKABLE FEDERALISM
```

The strongest objection to a powerful Union is that financial and administrative dependence may empty State autonomy of practical meaning. The reply is that a continental country needs national coordination, redistribution and minimum standards. The residual problem is therefore not “Union power or State power”, but **how to make Union capacity operate through legally bounded and consultative institutions**.

### 4. Topic boundary

This topic concentrates on the **machinery of relations**. It uses federal theory when needed, but its main task is operational:

- competence and conflict;
- directions and delegation;
- tax sharing and borrowing;
- dispute settlement and coordination;
- institutional trust.

Full local-government devolution, emergency doctrine, the Governor’s complete office and the complete institutional law of the Finance Commission, GST Council, NITI Aayog and CBI extend beyond this operating map. Their Centre-State consequences are taught here in full; unrelated institutional detail is not.

### 5. UPSC application

A strong answer should begin by identifying the relevant channel:

> **Answer line:** Centre-State relations are a linked constitutional system of legislative competence, administrative execution, fiscal interdependence and inter-governmental coordination.

For a broad 15- or 20-marker, use all four. For a narrow question, resist decorative breadth. A question on repugnancy needs legislative doctrine; a question on fiscal federalism needs transfers, grants, borrowing and bargaining.

### Revision notes

1. Centre-State relations operate through legislative, administrative, financial and inter-State channels.
2. Legislative relations principally occupy Articles 245–255.
3. Administrative relations principally occupy Articles 256–263.
4. Financial relations principally occupy Articles 265–293.
5. Articles 261–263 and 301–307 govern important inter-State and common-market questions.
6. Legal competence does not guarantee administrative or financial capacity.
7. Union funding does not automatically transfer legislative competence.
8. Consultative institutions are not substitutes for binding adjudication.
9. Self-rule and shared rule must coexist.
10. Diagnose the channel before evaluating centralisation or autonomy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a single public-policy dispute involve more than one dimension of Centre-State relations?

**Model answer:** A policy may require a law, executive implementation, public funding and cross-border coordination. Each function rests on a different constitutional channel. A State can possess subject-matter competence while depending on Union finance or inter-State cooperation. The channels interact, but each must be justified separately.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that the government financing a policy automatically acquires legislative or executive control over the entire subject.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** “Centre-State relations are better understood as an operating system than as a static division of powers.” Explain.

**Model answer — 146 words:**
The Constitution divides power, but it also creates mechanisms through which the two levels continuously interact. Legislative relations determine territorial reach, subject competence and conflict under Articles 245–255. Administrative relations govern implementation, directions and delegation under Articles 256–263. Financial provisions distribute tax proceeds, grants and borrowing authority, making both levels fiscally interdependent. Inter-State provisions manage water disputes, policy coordination, recognition of civil judgments and the national market.

These channels often converge. Public health may be a State responsibility, yet an epidemic can require parliamentary legislation within valid entries, Union directions, fiscal assistance and inter-State movement rules. Similarly, GST combines shared legislative power, revenue apportionment and a joint council.

However, interaction must not erase assigned spheres. Each exercise of power requires its own constitutional basis. Thus, Indian federalism is not merely a map of separate compartments; it is a rule-bound system for cooperation, conflict and negotiated implementation.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** operating-system thesis 2; four-channel explanation 4; interaction example 2; autonomy qualification 1; direct conclusion 1.

### Visual: The competence funnel

```text
1. WHERE DOES THE LAW OPERATE?
   Parliament: whole/part of India; extra-territorial operation permitted
   State: State territory, subject to sufficient territorial nexus
                         ↓
2. WHAT IS THE LAW REALLY ABOUT?
   Union List | State List | Concurrent List
                         ↓
3. IS THE MATTER UNENUMERATED?
   Article 248 + Union List Entry 97 → Parliament
                         ↓
4. ONLY THEN ASK:
   overlap? repugnancy? exceptional State-field entry?
```

The funnel prevents premature use of “Union supremacy”. Courts first ask whether the legislature possessed competence at all.

### 1. Territorial reach under Article 245

Parliament may legislate for the whole or any part of India. Article 245(2) adds that a parliamentary law is not invalid merely because it has extra-territorial operation. That does not mean every remote global fact can be regulated without constitutional connection; it means extra-territorial effect alone is not a ground of invalidity.

A State legislature ordinarily legislates for its own territory. The **territorial-nexus doctrine** permits a State law to affect an object or transaction outside the State where:

1. the connection with the State is real rather than illusory; and
2. the liability imposed is pertinent to that connection.

**Example:** A State may tax a transaction with substantial commercial connection to it even if one element occurred elsewhere.
**Limit:** Territorial nexus concerns the law’s geographical reach. It does not answer which List contains the subject.

Parliamentary Acts may also be adapted or excluded in constitutionally authorised territorial settings. Under Article 240 the President may make regulations for the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, Puducherry in the constitutionally specified circumstances, and Ladakh. Fifth- and Sixth-Schedule arrangements also permit specified gubernatorial adaptations or exclusions in scheduled and tribal areas. These are express constitutional arrangements, not a general State veto over parliamentary law.

### 2. Distribution through the Seventh Schedule

| List | Examination-safe conventional count | Core character | Examples |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Union List | 100 | Matters requiring national control | defence, currency, inter-State trade, corporation tax |
| State List | 61 | Regional governance fields | police, public order, public health, agriculture, markets |
| Concurrent List | 52 | Both levels may legislate | criminal law, marriage, education, forests |

Use **100/61/52** as the standard examination convention. A literal count of currently operative entries may produce **98/59/52** because entries have been inserted and omitted without renumbering the Schedules. The distinction explains the alternative figure; it does not replace the conventional UPSC recall line.

Article 246 establishes constitutional priority:

- Union List competence operates notwithstanding the other clauses.
- Concurrent competence is subject to Union List priority.
- State List competence is subject to both.

This priority does not mean Parliament may relabel any State matter as Union. Courts interpret entries broadly, identify the law’s true nature and protect genuine State competence.

### 3. Residuary power

Article 248 read with Entry 97 of the Union List places unenumerated matters, including unenumerated taxes, with Parliament.

```text
Search List II and List III fairly
              ↓
Matter genuinely not found
              ↓
Article 248 + Entry 97
              ↓
Parliamentary competence
```

The strongest Union-side argument is adaptability: new technologies and services should not require constitutional amendment whenever the Lists are silent. The strongest State-side objection is that the Union might use “residue” to absorb matters that fairly belong to a State entry. The reply is judicial sequencing: residue is reached only after the matter is not reasonably located in the State or Concurrent Lists.

### 4. The Forty-second Amendment shift

The Forty-second Amendment, 1976 moved five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List:

1. education;
2. forests;
3. weights and measures;
4. protection of wild animals and birds;
5. administration of justice.

The move enlarged parliamentary competence but did not make these subjects exclusively Union matters. States retain concurrent legislative capacity, subject to Article 254.

### 5. Close distinctions

| Confusion | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Territorial nexus vs pith and substance | Reach of State law vs true subject of law |
| Residuary power vs Union List power | Unenumerated matter vs expressly listed matter |
| Concurrent subject vs Union subject | Both may legislate vs Parliament alone ordinarily legislates |
| List priority vs unlimited Union power | Constitutional tie-breaking vs genuine competence boundary |

### UPSC application

> **Answer line:** Legislative federalism begins with territory and true subject-matter; Union priority resolves a constitutionally recognised overlap but cannot manufacture competence where none exists.

### Revision notes

1. Article 245 governs territorial extent.
2. Parliamentary law is not invalid merely for extra-territorial operation.
3. A State law needs a real and pertinent territorial nexus for extra-State effect.
4. Territorial nexus concerns reach, not subject classification.
5. Use 100/61/52 as the examination convention; 98/59/52 is a literal operative-entry count caused by omissions and insertions.
6. Article 246 gives Union List priority, then Concurrent, then State.
7. List entries are broad fields, not narrow definitions.
8. Article 248 and Entry 97 place genuine residue with Parliament.
9. Residuary power includes unenumerated taxation.
10. The Forty-second Amendment moved five State subjects to the Concurrent List.
11. Transfer to the Concurrent List does not eliminate State competence.

### Concept check

**Question:** How does territorial nexus differ from residuary competence?

**Model answer:** Territorial nexus asks whether a State law has a sufficient geographical connection to an extra-State object. Residuary competence asks which legislature may regulate a subject not enumerated in the State or Concurrent Lists. The first concerns reach; the second concerns subject allocation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not invoke Entry 97 before fairly testing whether the matter falls within an existing State or Concurrent entry.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Examine how territorial reach, the Seventh Schedule and residuary power together reveal the Union-weighted but bounded character of Indian legislative federalism.

**Model answer — 149 words:**
Article 245 gives Parliament nationwide reach and protects its laws from invalidity merely because of extra-territorial operation. State legislation is territorially confined, though a real and pertinent nexus can sustain limited extra-State effect.

Subject competence is then distributed through the Seventh Schedule. Article 246 gives priority to the Union List, followed by the Concurrent and State Lists. Article 248 and Union Entry 97 place genuinely unenumerated matters, including taxes, with Parliament. The Forty-second Amendment’s transfer of five subjects to the Concurrent List further enlarged shared central capacity.

The arrangement is nevertheless bounded. List entries must be interpreted broadly, and residue cannot absorb a matter fairly located in the State List. Territorial nexus cannot cure lack of subject competence. Courts therefore identify the law’s true character before invoking priority.

India’s legislative design is Union-weighted because Parliament possesses priority, but it remains federal because assigned fields, not Union preference, determine competence.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** territorial rule 2; List structure 3; residue 2; Forty-second Amendment 1; limits 1; qualified verdict 1.

### Visual: Five keys to the State List

| Key | Article | Who turns it? | Duration or consequence |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **R**ajya Sabha national-interest resolution | 249 | Rajya Sabha, two-thirds present and voting | State List matter or Article 246A GST; resolution up to one year, renewable; law has six-month tail |
| **N**ational Emergency | 250 | Emergency already in operation | State List matter or Article 246A GST; law has six-month tail after Emergency ends |
| **S**tates’ request | 252 | Two or more State legislatures | Applies to requesting/adopting States; only Parliament amends/repeals |
| **I**nternational obligation | 253 | Parliament | Operates as required to implement treaty/decision |
| **P**resident’s Rule | 356 | Constitutional emergency route | Parliamentary law continues until altered by competent legislature |

> **Mnemonic:** **R-N-S-I-P**.

The table is not a list of interchangeable powers. Each route answers a different coordination problem.

### 1. Article 249: national interest without Emergency

Rajya Sabha may declare by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting that Parliament should legislate on a State subject in the national interest.

After the 101st Amendment, the route also extends to Parliament making GST law of the kind contemplated by Article 246A. Article 249 is therefore no longer confined textually to State List entries.

- The resolution operates for up to one year.
- It may be renewed.
- A parliamentary law made under it becomes inoperative six months after the resolution ceases, except for past acts.

**Federal logic:** The chamber representing States supplies the trigger.
**Objection:** Unequal State representation and party discipline may weaken that safeguard.
**Reply:** It remains a special supermajority route, not ordinary Lok Sabha dominance.

### 2. Article 250: temporary crisis competence

While a National Emergency operates, Parliament may legislate on State matters. The law loses prospective force six months after the Emergency ends.

Like Article 249, Article 250 also expressly includes the relevant Article 246A GST field during the Emergency.

Article 250 differs from Article 249 in source and justification:

```text
Article 249 → national-interest resolution by Rajya Sabha
Article 250 → existing National Emergency
```

### 3. Article 252: consent-based uniformity

When two or more State legislatures pass resolutions requesting parliamentary legislation on a State matter, Parliament may enact a common law for them. Other States may adopt it later.

Examples traditionally include the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 and Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974.

The important cost of uniformity is that a participating State cannot independently amend or repeal the parliamentary enactment. Only Parliament can do so, although the original trigger came from the States.

### 4. Article 253: external obligation

Parliament may legislate to implement treaties, international agreements, conventions or decisions, even where the subject would otherwise lie in the State field.

Standard illustrations include laws implementing international privileges, anti-hijacking obligations and trade-related intellectual-property commitments. Each illustration must remain tied to the relevant external obligation rather than being used as proof of unlimited treaty power.

The strongest argument is national responsibility: India cannot accept an international obligation and then plead internal division of powers. The strongest federal concern is overbreadth—an international instrument should not become a blank cheque to restructure unrelated State regulation. The proper answer is purpose-linked implementation and judicial review of competence and rights.

### 5. Article 356: temporary substitution of legislative power

During President’s Rule, Parliament may exercise the State legislature’s law-making power. Unlike Articles 249 and 250, a law made through this route does not automatically expire with the proclamation. It continues until altered or repealed by a competent legislature.

That persistence does not convert the State List permanently into Union territory. The route remains tied to an exceptional proclamation subject to parliamentary approval and judicial review.

### 6. Conflict and procedural controls: Articles 251 and 255

Article 251 governs inconsistency between a State law and a parliamentary law made under Articles 249 or 250. The State legislature does not lose its competence, but the parliamentary law prevails and the State law remains inoperative to the extent of inconsistency while the parliamentary law continues to have effect.

Article 255 prevents specified recommendation or previous-sanction requirements from becoming purely technical traps after enactment. A law is not invalid solely because the required recommendation or previous sanction was absent if the constitutionally appropriate final assent cures that procedural defect. Article 255 does not cure lack of legislative competence or other substantive unconstitutionality.

### 7. The 2020 centralisation problem

The Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897, the Disaster Management Act, 2005 and the 2020 Farm Acts illustrate why the constitutional entry matters more than the political label.

The Farm Acts relied substantially on Concurrent List Entry 33 concerning trade and commerce in foodstuffs but affected agriculture and markets associated with State List Entries 14 and 28. The Acts were repealed in 2021. The episode shows three different questions:

1. Was there a plausible entry supporting parliamentary competence?
2. Did the law substantially affect State fields?
3. Was consultation adequate even if competence existed?

Legal validity and cooperative legitimacy are related but not identical.

### Verified PYQ linkage

**UPSC GS-II 2020, Q11:** “Indian Constitution exhibits centralising tendencies to maintain unity and integrity of the nation. Elucidate in the perspective of the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897; The Disaster Management Act, 2005 and recently passed Farm Acts.” **15 marks, 250 words.**

**Demand:** Explain how named laws reveal centralising capacity, then qualify that capacity through competence, consultation, necessity and federal safeguards.
**Answer approach:** holding-together design → named statutes and List entries → national-coordination case → State-autonomy cost → *Bommai*, courts and political repeal → cooperative verdict.

1. Parliament enters the State field through five distinct routes.
2. Article 249 does not require an Emergency.
3. Article 249 needs a two-thirds Rajya Sabha vote of members present and voting.
4. Articles 249 and 250 cover State List matters and the specified Article 246A GST field.
5. Articles 249 and 250 laws have a six-month tail.
6. Article 252 begins with resolutions of at least two States.
7. Only Parliament can amend or repeal an Article 252 law for participating States.
8. Article 253 implements international obligations.
9. Article 356 legislation can continue after President’s Rule.
10. Article 251 gives temporary parliamentary-law priority for Articles 249/250 conflicts.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is Article 252 simultaneously federal and centralising?

**Model answer:** It is federal because State legislatures initiate parliamentary action through consent resolutions. It is centralising because, after enactment, participating States cannot unilaterally amend or repeal the law; that power remains with Parliament.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat all five State-field routes as Emergency powers. Articles 249, 252 and 253 have different non-emergency triggers.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Compare the constitutional safeguards and federal costs of Parliament’s five routes into the State List.

**Model answer — 185 words:**
Parliament may enter the State field through five exceptional routes, each carrying a different safeguard.

Article 249 uses a two-thirds Rajya Sabha national-interest resolution, retaining a State-chamber trigger. Article 250 depends on a National Emergency and is temporary, with a six-month tail. Both routes also reach the specified Article 246A GST field. Article 252 is consent-based: at least two States request uniform law, but only Parliament may later amend or repeal it. Article 253 gives Parliament sufficient authority to implement international obligations, reflecting India’s single external personality. Article 356 temporarily substitutes parliamentary law-making during failure of State constitutional machinery; the proclamation remains reviewable, though laws made under it continue until altered.

The routes supply coordination, uniformity and crisis capacity. Their federal cost is potential displacement of regional choice, especially where consultation is weak or the legal entry is stretched. Article 252 may lock consenting States into parliamentary amendment, while Article 253 may centralise broad regulatory fields.

The safeguards therefore differ: chamber consent, emergency duration, State initiation, purpose-linked treaty implementation and judicial/parliamentary control. Exceptional competence is legitimate only when its distinct trigger and limits are respected.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** five triggers 5; duration/effect comparison 3; federal safeguards 3; costs/objections 2; qualified verdict 2.

### Visual: The correct judicial sequence

```text
STEP 1  What is the law really about?
        PITH AND SUBSTANCE
                    ↓
STEP 2  Is the overlap only incidental or ancillary?
        ALLOW WORKABLE ENCROACHMENT
                    ↓
STEP 3  Can both entries/provisions operate?
        HARMONIOUS CONSTRUCTION
                    ↓
STEP 4  Are two laws in the same Concurrent field irreconcilable?
        ARTICLE 254 REPUGNANCY
                    ↓
STEP 5  Does constitutional Union priority remain decisive?
        FEDERAL SUPREMACY AS LAST RESORT
```

The central lesson is sequence. “Union law wins” is not the starting presumption.

### 1. Pith and substance

**Pith and substance** asks for the law’s true nature and dominant purpose. A law validly situated in one List does not fail merely because it incidentally touches another List.

**Example:** A State law genuinely regulating moneylending may incidentally affect promissory instruments.
**Limit:** Incidental overlap cannot save a law whose dominant character lies outside State competence.

This doctrine makes federalism workable. Without it, modern regulation—where health, trade, environment and technology overlap—would become impossible.

### 2. Ancillary and implied power

A grant of legislative competence includes powers reasonably necessary to make it effective. The doctrine prevents a List entry from being reduced to a hollow label.

Its limit is proportionality to the granted field. Ancillary power cannot become an independent route for acquiring another legislature’s core subject.

### 3. Colourable legislation

Colourable legislation applies the maxim that what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly. The court asks whether the legislature has disguised a lack of competence.

```text
BAD POLITICAL MOTIVE alone ≠ colourable legislation
DISGUISED ABSENCE OF LEGISLATIVE POWER = colourable problem
```

The doctrine concerns competence, not judicial assessment of policy wisdom.

### 4. Harmonious construction

Where constitutional entries or provisions appear to overlap, courts try to give meaningful operation to both. This protects the assumption that the Constitution created functioning spheres rather than contradictory commands.

Harmonious construction cannot erase an express priority clause. It reconciles where possible; it does not rewrite the Constitution.

### 5. Article 254 repugnancy

Repugnancy ordinarily concerns:

1. a parliamentary law and a State law;
2. operating in the same Concurrent field;
3. producing a real and irreconcilable conflict.

Article 254(1) makes the Union law prevail and the State law void to the extent of repugnancy. Under Article 254(2), a State law reserved for and receiving Presidential assent may prevail in that State. Parliament may subsequently override it.

Presidential assent does not cure lack of State competence. A State must first have power to legislate on the subject.

### 6. Federal supremacy

Federal supremacy is the constitutional tie-breaker after reconciliation fails. Article 246 priority and Article 254 prevent legal paralysis.

**Strongest objection:** Calling supremacy a tie-breaker understates India’s structural Union bias.
**Reply:** The bias is real, but judicial doctrines preserve State law through broad entries, incidental encroachment and reconciliation before priority operates.
**Residual:** Outcomes still depend on how courts characterise the law’s dominant purpose.

### 7. Key cases

| Case | Use |
|---|---|
| *Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee* | Dominant character and incidental encroachment |
| *K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo* | Colourable legislation concerns competence, not motive |
| *M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India* | Real, direct and irreconcilable conflict for repugnancy |
| *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* | Federalism is part of the Basic Structure |

### Verified PYQ linkage

**UPSC GS-II 2019, Q4:** “From the resolution of contentious issues regarding distribution of legislative powers by the courts, ‘Principle of Federal Supremacy’ and ‘Harmonious Construction’ have emerged. Explain.” **10 marks, 150 words.**

**Demand:** Define both doctrines and explain their sequence.
**Answer approach:** allocation → pith and substance → reconciliation → Article 246/254 priority → State-law assent qualification → supremacy as exceptional tie-breaker.

### Revision notes

1. Begin with the law’s pith and substance.
2. Genuine incidental encroachment may survive.
3. Ancillary power makes an assigned entry effective.
4. Colourable legislation tests disguised incompetence.
5. Bad motive alone does not establish colourability.
6. Harmonious construction preserves both provisions where possible.
7. Article 254 principally addresses Concurrent-field conflict.
8. Repugnancy must be real and irreconcilable.
9. Article 254(2) protects an assented State law only within that State.
10. Parliament may later override the assented State law.
11. Presidential assent cannot create missing competence.
12. Federal supremacy is the last-stage resolution, not the first assumption.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should Article 254 not be the first doctrine applied whenever a Union and State law overlap?

**Model answer:** The overlap may involve different Lists, may be merely incidental, or may be reconcilable. Courts must first identify each law’s dominant character and attempt harmonious operation. Article 254 applies principally when laws in the same Concurrent field create an actual irreconcilable conflict.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate every Union-State overlap with repugnancy.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** “Harmonious construction protects federal autonomy, while federal supremacy prevents constitutional paralysis.” Discuss.

**Model answer — 167 words:**
The Constitution distributes broad legislative fields that inevitably overlap. Courts therefore begin with pith and substance: a law survives if its dominant character lies within competence despite incidental encroachment. Ancillary power similarly permits measures needed to make an assigned field effective.

Harmonious construction then attempts to preserve meaningful operation of both entries or provisions. This protects State autonomy because Union priority is not invoked merely on superficial overlap. Colourable-legislation review separately prevents a legislature from disguising what it lacks power to do.

Supremacy becomes necessary only where reconciliation fails. Article 246 gives Union List priority, while Article 254 ordinarily makes parliamentary law prevail over repugnant State law in a Concurrent field. A President-assented State law may operate within that State, but Parliament may later override it.

The objection is that Union priority structurally disadvantages States. Yet refusing any tie-breaker would produce contradictory legal commands. The defensible sequence is therefore reconciliation first and supremacy last. Indian legislative federalism is accommodative in interpretation but Union-weighted at the irreconcilable residue.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** pith/ancillary foundation 3; harmonious construction 3; Article 246/254 supremacy 4; assent qualification 2; objection/reply 2; verdict 1.

The visual separates three ideas often merged in exam answers: previous sanction, gubernatorial reservation and presidential assent.

### 1. Previous sanction is not reservation

Previous Presidential sanction is a constitutional precondition for specified State legislation. Article 304(b), for example, requires previous sanction before a State Bill imposing a reasonable public-interest restriction on trade is introduced.

Reservation occurs after a Bill has passed the State legislature. The Governor sends it to the President instead of completing the State assent process.

```text
Previous sanction = permission at the legislative threshold
Reservation       = post-passage presidential consideration
```

### 2. Article 200

The controlling judicial position identifies three constitutional choices:

1. assent;
2. reserve the Bill for the President;
3. withhold assent and return a non-Money Bill with comments.

A Bill that would so derogate from the High Court’s powers as to endanger its constitutional position must be reserved.

The Governor’s choice under Article 200 is discretionary rather than bound by State-Cabinet advice according to the Supreme Court’s advisory opinion of 20 November 2025. This does not make the Governor an unrestricted political veto.

If the Governor returns a non-Money Bill under the first proviso and the legislature passes it again, with or without amendment, the Governor cannot withhold assent again. The controlling 20 November 2025 opinion clarifies that the remaining constitutional choices are assent or reservation for the President.

### 3. Article 201

For a reserved Bill, the President may assent or withhold assent. In the case of a non-Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it for reconsideration.

Where the President directs return, the State legislature must reconsider the Bill within six months. Unlike the first proviso to Article 200, Article 201 does not compel presidential assent merely because the State legislature passes the Bill again.

### 4. Ordinances under Article 213

The Governor may promulgate an ordinance when the State legislature is not in session, but Presidential instructions are required in specified cases. Broadly, this control applies where a comparable State Bill would have required previous Presidential sanction, where the Governor would have considered reservation necessary, or where the resulting State Act would have lacked validity without Presidential assent.

The rule prevents the ordinance route from evading Union controls that would have applied to an ordinary State Bill. It does not require prior Union permission for every State ordinance.

### 5. Static legal-status control

The Supreme Court’s opinion in *In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India*, 2025 INSC 1333, supplies the controlling propositions:

- courts cannot create rigid decision deadlines where the Constitution supplies none;
- courts cannot invent “deemed assent”;
- the merits of the Governor’s constitutional choice are not reviewed;
- prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction permits limited judicial scrutiny and a direction to act within a reasonable period;
- Article 361 protects the Governor personally but does not immunise constitutional inaction from that limited review.

This position displaced the general use of the April 2025 case-specific timelines and deemed-assent reasoning.

### 6. Objection, reply and residual risk

**Objection:** A Union-appointed Governor can convert reservation or delay into an indirect Union veto over an elected State legislature.
**Reply:** The Constitution itself creates reservation in specified situations, judicial review reaches prolonged inaction, and political accountability can demand reasons and neutral conventions.
**Residual:** Absence of fixed timelines and merits review leaves substantial space for political friction.

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Critically examine whether Articles 200 and 201 create a necessary federal safeguard or an instrument of Union control.

The safeguard argument is that reservation protects constitutional structure, national consistency and judicial independence. It also permits scrutiny where a State law raises serious competence or constitutional concerns.

Delay or reservation can therefore frustrate an elected State majority, particularly under opposing parties. Article 201 does not compel assent even after reconsideration.

The mechanism is constitutionally legitimate, but its federal acceptability depends on prompt reasoned action, narrow use of reservation and politically neutral gubernatorial conventions.

The decisive word is **may**. Article 365 supplies relevant constitutional material; it does not mechanically impose President’s Rule.

### 1. Article 256: implementation duty

Executive power generally follows the legislative distribution. State executive power ordinarily extends to State List matters and to Concurrent List matters, except where the Constitution or a parliamentary law expressly gives executive power to the Union. Union executive power correspondingly extends to Parliament’s exclusive fields and to constitutionally or statutorily assigned Concurrent responsibilities.

State executive power must ensure compliance with parliamentary laws and existing laws applicable in the State. Union executive power extends to giving directions necessary for that purpose.

This reflects a practical fact: Parliament often legislates, but State officers implement. Without some coordination power, a national law could exist only on paper.

**Limit:** Article 256 is not a general power to administer every State subject. The direction must remain connected to the constitutional implementation duty.

### 2. Article 257: non-impediment and special directions

State executive power must be exercised so as not to impede or prejudice Union executive power. The Union may issue directions regarding:

- construction and maintenance of communications declared nationally or militarily important;
- measures for railway protection.

The Constitution also accounts for additional costs imposed by specified directions. This matters because federal commands can otherwise externalise national expenses onto State budgets.

### 3. Other constitutionally authorised directions

- Article 339(2): Union directions concerning schemes essential for Scheduled Tribe welfare.
- Article 350A: facilities for mother-tongue instruction at the primary stage for children belonging to linguistic minorities.

These provisions are targeted. They do not create one unlimited central direction power.

### 4. Articles 355, 356 and 357

Article 355 imposes a Union duty to protect States against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure that State government is carried on according to the Constitution.

Article 355 is constitutional context, not a free-standing takeover provision. Article 356 contains the exceptional proclamation mechanism where State government cannot be carried on according to the Constitution. It requires parliamentary approval and remains judicially reviewable.

During President’s Rule, Article 357 permits Parliament to confer State legislative power on the President and authorise delegation. The arrangement is temporary; it does not permanently transfer the State List.

Article 360 is a separate financial-emergency route. It permits directions concerning financial propriety and may require specified State Money Bills or other financial Bills to be reserved for presidential consideration. No Financial Emergency has been proclaimed. Article 360 must not be confused with ordinary borrowing consent under Article 293.

During a National Emergency under Article 352, Union executive directions may extend to any matter, although State governments continue. During President’s Rule, the President may assume State executive functions and powers vested in the Governor or another State executive authority, subject to the constitutional proclamation framework.

### 5. Article 365

Where a State fails to comply with Union directions, the President may hold that a situation has arisen in which government of the State cannot be carried on according to the Constitution.

Three safeguards prevent automaticity:

1. the direction must itself be constitutionally valid;
2. non-compliance supplies material, not an irreversible legal result;
3. any Article 356 proclamation must independently satisfy constitutional, parliamentary and judicial controls.

### 6. Judicial discipline and political change

*State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* accepted that Article 356 satisfaction was not wholly beyond review, though the early standard was deferential. *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* transformed the position:

- proclamation is judicially reviewable;
- relevant material is required;
- majority should ordinarily be tested on the floor;
- the Assembly should not be dissolved before parliamentary approval;
- a wrongly dismissed government may be restored;
- federalism is part of the Basic Structure.

The reduced use of Article 356 since the mid-1990s also reflects coalition politics, regional parties and higher political costs. Conflict did not disappear; it shifted toward Governors, assent, finance and agencies.

**Demand:** Provide causal explanation, separating legal and political factors.
**Answer approach:** *Bommai* safeguards → later judicial vigilance → coalition/regional-party bargaining → electoral and reputational costs → conflict migration to other instruments → qualified conclusion.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Direction powers backed by Articles 365 and 356 make State autonomy conditional on Union approval.
**Reply:** Directions are textually limited, Article 365 is non-automatic, and *Bommai* supplies parliamentary and judicial constraints.
**Residual:** A State may still face strong compliance pressure long before an Article 356 case reaches court.

1. Executive power generally follows legislative distribution.
2. States ordinarily execute Concurrent-field law unless Union power is specially assigned.
3. Article 256 links State implementation to parliamentary/applicable laws.
4. Union directions under Article 256 must relate to that duty.
5. Article 257 prevents State action from impeding Union executive power.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Article 365 not make President’s Rule automatic after State non-compliance?

**Model answer:** Article 365 permits the President to draw a constitutional conclusion from non-compliance, but an Article 356 proclamation still requires separate satisfaction based on relevant material, parliamentary approval and judicial review. The direction’s own validity and the seriousness of non-compliance also remain examinable.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not write a mechanical chain in which every refusal of a Union direction necessarily ends the State government.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Analyse whether the Union’s administrative-direction powers are compatible with meaningful State autonomy.

**Model answer — 169 words:**
Articles 256 and 257 recognise that parliamentary laws and Union functions often depend on State machinery. States must implement applicable parliamentary laws and avoid impeding Union executive power; the Union may issue necessary directions, including on nationally important communications and railway protection. Targeted powers also exist for Scheduled Tribe welfare and linguistic-minority instruction.

The centralising concern arises because Article 365 permits non-compliance to support a conclusion relevant to Article 356. Directions can therefore exert political and fiscal pressure even before formal emergency action.

Yet the power is bounded. Directions require a constitutional connection and do not create general Union administration of State subjects. Article 365 is evidentiary, not automatic. Article 356 requires separate satisfaction, parliamentary approval and *S.R. Bommai* review; majority ordinarily belongs on the legislative floor.

Administrative directions are therefore compatible with federalism when used to secure valid national law and protected constitutional purposes. They become coercive when detached from their textual field or used as substitutes for consultation. Regular inter-governmental forums should precede compulsion wherever urgency permits.

The design distinguishes **transfer of work** from **transfer of constitutional competence**.

### 1. Article 258

The President may, with the consent of a State government, entrust Union executive functions to that State or its officers.

Parliament may also confer powers and impose duties on States through legislation even without State consent where constitutionally competent. The Constitution provides for adjustment of extra administrative costs.

### 2. Article 258A

The Governor may, with Union consent, entrust State executive functions to the Union or its officers.

The asymmetry is important:

- voluntary executive entrustment works in both directions;
- parliamentary legislation may impose State duties without consent;
- a State legislature cannot similarly compel Union officers.

### 3. What entrustment does not do

Entrustment is functional. The assigning government does not permanently surrender its constitutional field, and the receiving machinery does not acquire general power over the subject.

**Analogy:** A bank may authorise an agent to perform a transaction without transferring ownership of the account.
**Limit of analogy:** Governments act under public constitutional duties, not private agency alone; statutory responsibility and accountability remain decisive.

### 4. All-India Services

Article 312 permits Parliament to create a new All-India Service after Rajya Sabha declares by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting that this is necessary in the national interest.

The established All-India Services are:

- Indian Administrative Service;
- Indian Police Service;
- Indian Forest Service, created in 1966.

The Indian Foreign Service is a Central Civil Service, not an All-India Service.

The architecture combines:

| National element | State element |
|---|---|
| common recruitment and training | service in State cadres and field administration |
| national standards and mobility | knowledge of regional administration |
| central role in cadre and disciplinary architecture | immediate supervision in State assignments |

Ambedkar defended common services for strategic administrative posts. The strongest State objection is that cadre control, deputation and disciplinary leverage may make officers more responsive to the Union than to the elected State government. The reply is that common standards, national mobility and administrative continuity prevent parochial capture and support constitutional governance. The residual challenge is designing genuinely joint cadre management.

### 5. Integrated administration beyond services

India also uses an integrated judiciary and many shared institutions rather than maintaining wholly separate federal and State systems. Integration improves consistency but adds Union influence. The constitutional test is whether integration supports common standards while leaving elected State governments meaningful policy authority.

### 6. Public Service Commission controls

Public Service Commissions create another Centre-State connection:

- State Public Service Commission members are appointed by the Governor, but removal lies with the President under the constitutional procedure.
- Parliament may establish a Joint State Public Service Commission for two or more States after resolutions from the concerned State legislatures; its Chairperson and members are appointed by the President.
- The Union Public Service Commission may serve a State’s needs when the Governor requests it and the President approves.
- UPSC may also assist two or more States, on request, in designing and operating joint recruitment schemes for services requiring special qualifications.

These rules combine State recruitment needs with nationally protected institutional independence. The objection is that presidential removal and appointment links may enlarge Union influence. The reply is that protected removal and joint arrangements can insulate commissions from immediate State political pressure and permit shared specialist capacity.

Related administrative controls also show the integrated design: State High Court judges are presidentially appointed through the constitutional consultative process, Parliament may establish a common High Court for two or more States, and a State Election Commissioner—though appointed by the Governor—cannot be removed at gubernatorial pleasure because the Constitution gives High-Court-judge-like removal protection.

### UPSC application

> **Answer line:** Delegation and All-India Services make Indian administration collaborative rather than watertight; their legitimacy depends on distinguishing shared execution from central ownership of State policy.

1. Article 258 enables Union-to-State entrustment with State consent.
2. Article 258A enables State-to-Union entrustment with Union consent.
3. Parliament may impose State duties by law without consent where competent.
4. Additional administrative costs are constitutionally adjustable.
5. Entrustment transfers work, not the underlying constitutional field.
6. Article 312 governs creation of new All-India Services.
7. Rajya Sabha needs a two-thirds present-and-voting national-interest resolution.
8. IAS, IPS and Indian Forest Service are All-India Services.
9. Indian Foreign Service is not an All-India Service.
10. Recruitment/training are nationally organised; immediate field control is State-based.
11. Cadre management creates both uniformity and federal tension.
12. SPSC members are gubernatorially appointed but presidentially removable.
13. Parliament may create a Joint State PSC after requests from the State legislatures.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Article 258 entrustment not alter the constitutional distribution of legislative power?

**Model answer:** Article 258 reallocates performance of executive functions by consent; it does not amend the Seventh Schedule or transfer legislative competence. The entrusting government retains constitutional responsibility, while the receiving machinery performs specified tasks under the arrangement.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat administrative delegation as permanent surrender of the subject itself.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** “All-India Services are simultaneously instruments of national integration and sources of federal tension.” Examine.

**Model answer — 141 words:**
Article 312 permits nationally recruited services to operate across Union and State administrations. The IAS, IPS and Indian Forest Service provide common professional standards, institutional memory, inter-State mobility and experienced personnel for strategic posts. Their State cadres also connect national policy with local administration.

The same design creates tension. Recruitment, deputation and significant cadre control involve the Union, while officers serve elected State governments in immediate field assignments. States may therefore fear divided loyalty or central leverage, especially during political disagreement.

The answer is neither complete central control nor isolated State services. Rajya Sabha’s special-majority trigger for new services recognises the federal interest. Joint cadre management, transparent deputation rules, fixed professional norms and protection from partisan transfers can align accountability.

All-India Services strengthen unity when they supply neutral capacity; they weaken federal trust when cadre powers become instruments of political control.

The consent rule and judicial exception protect different constitutional values.

### 1. Why State consent exists

Police and public order are State List fields. The CBI does not possess a dedicated constitutional article or comprehensive CBI Act. It uses police powers under the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946.

- Section 2: the establishment’s foundational territorial jurisdiction.
- Section 5: Union power to extend DSPE powers to a State area.
- Section 6: such extension ordinarily requires State government consent.

The CBI is therefore not an automatically competent national police force throughout India.

### 2. General and case-specific consent

**General consent** is standing permission enabling routine operation in specified classes of cases. A State may withdraw it, after which fresh investigations ordinarily need case-specific consent.

Withdrawal does not automatically erase:

- every lawfully commenced investigation;
- jurisdiction founded on facts outside the withdrawing State;
- a valid constitutional-court direction.

Because State positions change, no undated list or count of States withdrawing consent should be memorised.

### 3. Constitutional-court exception

In *State of West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights* (2010), a Constitution Bench held that High Courts under Article 226 and the Supreme Court under Article 32 may direct a CBI investigation within a State without its consent.

This does not abolish federalism:

- Section 6 continues to bind the ordinary executive route.
- Courts exercise independent constitutional jurisdiction to protect rights and fair investigation.
- The Union executive cannot invoke the judgment as a general power to ignore consent.

### 4. The autonomy-versus-accountability debate

| State-autonomy case | National-accountability case |
|---|---|
| Police is constitutionally a State field | Corruption and organised crime cross borders |
| Consent checks centrally directed investigation | State refusal may delay serious cases |
| Withdrawal may protect against partisan use | Withdrawal may shield politically connected actors |

The deepest problem is institutional neutrality. Consent can be either a federal safeguard or a political shield depending on who controls each government. A dedicated statute, transparent case-allocation standards, independent appointment and oversight, and clear consent consequences would reduce this ambiguity.

### 5. Static case-status control

In *State of West Bengal v. Union of India* (2024), the Supreme Court rejected a preliminary objection and allowed the State’s Article 131 suit concerning CBI action to proceed. The decision addressed maintainability; it did not finally decide every substantive allegation. It should not be cited as a final invalidation of the CBI’s actions.

General consent facilitates routine work; its withdrawal makes fresh cases dependent on specific permission.

This protects federalism by preventing an unrestricted central police force and checking politically directed investigation. Yet withdrawal may delay inter-State corruption cases, create investigative gaps or itself serve partisan interests.

The constitutional balance lies in *State of West Bengal v. CPDR* (2010). High Courts and the Supreme Court may direct a CBI probe without State consent to protect rights and fair investigation. That judicial exception does not authorise the Union executive to ignore Section 6. Nor does withdrawal automatically erase every validly commenced case.

The regime is therefore defensible but incomplete. A dedicated CBI statute, transparent jurisdictional rules, independent appointments and parliamentary oversight would preserve State autonomy while reducing safe havens and partisan allegations.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** constitutional foundation 3; DSPE mechanism 4; federal benefit 2; investigation cost 2; judicial exception 2; reform and verdict 2.

## Lesson 9 — The Constitutional Architecture of Public Finance

Progress: 9 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Tax authority, revenue assignment, grants, immunities and borrowing

### Visual: From tax authority to usable State resources

The flow shows why “States receive 41 per cent” never describes the whole fiscal relationship. The tax base, excluded levies, grants and borrowing conditions also matter.

### 1. No tax without law

Article 265 requires authority of law for both levy and collection. Taxing competence must be expressly supported; it is not implied from a general regulatory entry.

The Concurrent List contains no general tax entry. GST’s shared legislative power arises from the special Article 246A, not from placing GST in the Concurrent List.

Article 248 and Entry 97 give Parliament genuine residuary taxing power.

The standard textbook classification counts 13 tax entries in the Union List and 18 in the State List. The number is less important than the constitutional rule: taxing authority requires a specific tax entry or special provision and cannot be inferred from a general subject entry.

### 2. Revenue distribution

| Article | Mechanism | Key distinction |
|---:|---|---|
| 268 | Union levy; States collect and appropriate specified stamp duties | Not ordinary divisible-pool sharing |
| 269 | Union levies/collects specified inter-State taxes and assigns them to States | Assignment, not Finance Commission devolution |
| 269A | Union levies/collects inter-State GST and apportions it | Special GST architecture |
| 270 | Shareable Union taxes distributed between Union and States | Divisible pool |
| 271 | Parliament may impose Union-purpose surcharge | Proceeds are not shared |

Article 276 permits State or local taxation of professions, trades, callings and employments subject to the constitutional cap of ₹2,500 per person per year.

### 3. Article 286 and territorial limits on State supply taxes

Article 286 prevents a State from imposing tax on a supply of goods or services where the supply takes place outside the State or in the course of import into, or export out of, India. Parliament may formulate principles for determining when a supply falls within these categories.

The provision protects the national and external market from overlapping State claims. It does not remove ordinary State taxing competence within the constitutionally assigned field; it fixes territorial and cross-border limits.

Article 287 limits State electricity taxation where electricity is consumed by, or sold for consumption to, the Union, and where it is used for the construction, maintenance or operation of railways in the constitutionally protected circumstances.

Article 288 addresses taxation of water or electricity stored, generated, consumed, distributed or sold by an authority established by Parliament for regulating or developing an inter-State river or river valley. A State law imposing such tax must be reserved for the President and receive assent before it becomes effective.

Together, Articles 286–288 show that State taxing power is substantial but cannot fragment external trade, burden protected Union/railway consumption or unilaterally tax specified inter-State river authorities.

### 4. Article 274 protection of State financial interests

Specified Bills affecting State financial interests may be introduced in Parliament only on the President’s recommendation. The protection covers Bills that:

- impose or vary a tax or duty in which States are interested;
- vary the income-tax meaning of agricultural income;
- affect principles on which money is distributable to States; or
- impose a Union-purpose surcharge on a specified tax or duty.

“Net proceeds” means collections after the cost of collection. The Comptroller and Auditor-General certifies those proceeds, and the certificate is final. The Presidential-recommendation requirement protects State interests procedurally, but it does not give each State a veto.

### 5. Non-tax revenues

Tax sharing is only part of public finance. Major Union non-tax sources include railways, communications, banking, broadcasting, coinage/currency-related receipts, central public enterprises and escheat or lapse. Important State non-tax sources include irrigation, forests, fisheries, State public enterprises and escheat or lapse.

These revenues ordinarily remain with the government whose functions or property produce them. They therefore affect real fiscal capacity even though they do not enter the Article 270 divisible pool.

### 6. Grants

Article 275 grants are constitutionally structured grants-in-aid, including need-based purposes and Finance Commission recommendations.

It also supports specific grants for promoting Scheduled Tribe welfare and raising the level of administration in Scheduled Areas, including the constitutionally specified Assam-related purpose. These are distinct constitutional purposes, not merely examples of general discretionary assistance.

Article 282 permits the Union or a State to make grants for any public purpose even outside its legislative field. This flexibility supports national development programmes.

**Strongest objection:** Article 282 allows the Union to influence State priorities through conditional schemes and bypass the normal Finance Commission channel.
**Reply:** National redistribution and common service goals sometimes need targeted funding that a general tax formula cannot provide.
**Residual:** Conditionality may narrow State choice even where the underlying subject is regional.

### 7. Tax immunities

Article 285 generally protects Union property from State taxation, subject to parliamentary provision and transitional rules.

Article 289 generally protects State property and income from Union taxation, subject to constitutionally recognised trade or business exceptions that Parliament may regulate.

These immunities prevent one level from casually weakening the other through taxation. They are qualified rather than absolute:

- a government-owned corporation or company is a separate legal entity and does not automatically inherit the government’s immunity;
- local-authority property and income do not receive the State’s Article 289 immunity from Union taxation;
- State immunity does not extend to customs and excise duties merely because the State owns or handles the goods;
- Parliament may tax State commercial operations while protecting a trade or business declared incidental to ordinary governmental functions.

### 8. Borrowing

Article 292 governs Union borrowing. The Union may borrow within or outside India subject to parliamentary limits.

Article 293 governs State borrowing:

- a State borrows within India;
- if a Union loan or Union-guaranteed loan remains outstanding, Union consent is required for further borrowing;
- consent may carry conditions.

The Union-side case is macroeconomic stability and creditor protection. The State-side objection is that consent can become fiscal policy leverage. A fair arrangement therefore needs transparent debt rules rather than ad hoc bargaining.

### 9. Emergency effects on financial relations

During a National Emergency, the President may modify the constitutional distribution of specified revenues between the Union and the States for the constitutionally permitted period, extending to the end of the financial year in which the Emergency ceases. During a Financial Emergency, Union directions may require financial propriety, reduction of salaries and reservation of State Money Bills or other financial Bills for presidential consideration.

These are exceptional emergency consequences. They do not describe normal Finance Commission devolution or ordinary Article 293 borrowing consent.

### 10. Close-option traps

- GST competence comes from Article 246A, not a Concurrent List tax entry.
- Article 271 surcharge is not shareable merely because Article 270 taxes are shared.
- Article 275 and Article 282 grants differ in constitutional structure and discretion.
- The Finance Commission does not directly control all public transfers.
- States cannot borrow abroad under Article 293.

### Revision notes

1. Article 265 requires express authority of law for levy and collection.
2. The Concurrent List has no general tax entry; GST uses Article 246A.
3. Articles 268, 269 and 269A respectively concern State appropriation, assignment and inter-State GST.
4. Article 270 creates the divisible pool; Article 271 surcharge proceeds are not shared.
5. Articles 286–288 limit State taxes on cross-border supplies, protected electricity consumption and specified river-valley authorities.
6. Article 274 requires Presidential recommendation for specified State-interest tax Bills.
7. Article 275 includes need-based grants plus specific ST-welfare and Scheduled-Area administration grants; Article 282 is discretionary.
8. Non-tax revenue ordinarily remains outside Article 270.
9. Article 276 caps profession tax; Articles 285/289 immunities exclude separate corporations and important local/commercial/customs-excise cases.
10. Articles 292–293 divide Union and State borrowing powers.
11. Outstanding Union loans may trigger a State consent requirement.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a rise in the States’ devolution percentage coexist with continued fiscal centralisation?

**Model answer:** The percentage applies only to the divisible pool. Union cesses and surcharges outside that pool, conditional Article 282 schemes, expenditure responsibilities and Article 293 borrowing conditions can preserve or increase Union leverage despite a higher headline share.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the devolution percentage as a percentage of every Union receipt.

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Explain how the Constitution combines fiscal sharing with Union leverage over the States.

**Model answer — 160 words:**

Article 275 supplies constitutionally structured grants.

Article 282 permits discretionary grants for any public purpose, enabling centrally sponsored schemes and conditions in State fields. Article 293 requires Union consent for further State borrowing where Union loans or guarantees remain outstanding. Parliament also holds residuary taxing power, while GST depends on a shared but nationally harmonised system.

These mechanisms may support redistribution, national minimum standards and macroeconomic discipline. However, excluded levies, tied grants and borrowing conditions can reduce practical State autonomy.

The constitutional design is therefore not fiscal equality but managed interdependence. Its legitimacy depends on a broad divisible pool, transparent grant conditions, predictable devolution and rules-based borrowing consent.

The evolution is from parallel plan and non-plan bargaining toward a more formula-based arrangement, not from dependence to complete State autonomy.

### 1. The Finance Commission mechanism

Article 280 requires the President to constitute a Finance Commission every fifth year or earlier. It consists of a Chairperson and four other members, with qualifications prescribed by Parliament.

Its principal tasks include:

- vertical distribution of shareable Union taxes between Union and States;
- horizontal distribution of the States’ share among States;
- principles governing Article 275 grants;
- measures to augment State Consolidated Funds for panchayat and municipality resources on State Finance Commission recommendations;
- other matters referred in the interests of sound finance.

Article 281 requires the report and an explanatory memorandum on action taken to be laid before Parliament. Recommendations are advisory, not judicially enforceable commands, though departures carry political and justificatory costs.

### 2. Vertical and horizontal devolution

```text
DIVISIBLE POOL
      ↓
VERTICAL DEVOLUTION
Union share ↔ collective States' share
      ↓
HORIZONTAL DEVOLUTION
States' collective share distributed among States
```

Vertical imbalance arises because the Union controls broad revenue sources while States undertake major development and welfare expenditure. Horizontal imbalance arises because States differ in income, population, geography, ecology and revenue capacity.

No formula can maximise every value:

- income distance and need favour poorer States;
- population recognises expenditure scale;
- demographic performance rewards slower population growth;
- forest and area recognise cost disabilities;
- efficiency criteria reward contribution or effort.

### 3. The Planning Commission era

The Eightieth Amendment, 2000 implemented the alternative tax-devolution scheme recommended by the Tenth Finance Commission, bringing the net proceeds of a wider range of Union taxes into the shareable arrangement. It is an important bridge between older tax-by-tax sharing and the modern divisible-pool approach.

The Planning Commission and National Development Council shaped Five-Year Plans and plan assistance. The Planning Commission’s fund-allocation role gave it substantial leverage over State development choices.

The system’s strength was coordinated national investment. Its weaknesses were executive discretion, overlapping transfer channels and bargaining unequal to the Finance Commission’s constitutional process.

Article 282 supplied broad authority for many development grants, but also made scheme conditionality a continuing federal concern.

### 4. NITI Aayog after 2015

NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission by executive resolution on 1 January 2015.

| Planning Commission | NITI Aayog |
|---|---|
| executive and non-statutory | executive and non-statutory |
| fund-allocation leverage | no tax-devolution or general fund-allocation power |
| Five-Year Plan orientation | policy advice, monitoring and strategic coordination |
| top-down criticism | cooperative/competitive-federalism claim |

NITI’s Governing Council gives Chief Ministers and Union Territory leadership a national forum. Its indices and programmes encourage competitive federalism. Yet voice without fiscal authority may not substitute for the leverage once attached to plan grants.

### 5. Fourteenth and Fifteenth Finance Commission transition

The Fourteenth Finance Commission raised the States’ vertical share from 32 to 42 per cent for 2015–20, enlarging untied tax devolution. The Fifteenth Finance Commission used 41 per cent, reflecting the changed territorial calculation after the reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir.

The transition improved predictability and formula-based sharing. It did not settle:

- the growth of cesses and surcharges outside the divisible pool;
- centrally sponsored scheme conditions;
- disputes over horizontal criteria;
- State borrowing constraints;
- GST-related dependence.

### 1. Verified status

The Sixteenth Finance Commission, chaired by Arvind Panagariya, submitted its report on 17 November 2025 for the award period 2026–31. The award took effect from 1 April 2026.

The Union Government accepted:

- 41 per cent of the net proceeds in the divisible pool as the collective States’ vertical share;
- a horizontal formula containing a new 10 per cent **Contribution to GDP** criterion.

The official report explains that the new criterion recognises growth contribution while gradualism limits abrupt changes in State shares.

### 2. What each criterion tries to do

| Criterion | Constitutional-policy value | Predictable criticism |
|---|---|---|
| Per-capita GSDP distance, 42.5% | Equalises fiscal capacity | May appear to weaken reward for performance |
| Population 2011, 17.5% | Reflects scale of expenditure needs | Population-control performers fear penalty |
| Demographic performance, 10% | Rewards lower population growth between the 1971 and 2011 Censuses | TFR was replaced and the weight reduced |
| Area, 10% | Recognises service-delivery scale | Larger area does not always mean higher need |
| Forest and ecology, 10% | Compensates ecological service and cost disability | Measurement design affects incentives |
| Contribution to GDP, 10% | Rewards output and economic management | May favour richer, industrialised States |

The formula does not choose equity over efficiency. It creates a negotiated mix, with income distance retaining the largest single weight.

### 3. Why Contribution to GDP matters

The Commission defines contribution through a moderated measure based on State GSDP shares. It treats the criterion as a proxy for efficiency-related performance such as economic management, while avoiding the extreme distribution that a raw share could produce.

**Strongest objection:** States already contributing more to national output possess stronger tax bases; rewarding them again may reduce equalisation.
**Reply:** A federation also needs incentives for growth and revenue generation, and the criterion carries only 10 per cent while equalisation remains dominant.
**Residual:** Even a moderate weight creates zero-sum distributional effects and may sharpen richer-versus-poorer State politics.

### 4. Vertical share versus effective fiscal space

Retaining 41 per cent does not guarantee unchanged effective State resources. Fiscal space also depends on:

- the size of the divisible pool;
- the use of cesses and surcharges outside it;
- grant design;
- centrally sponsored scheme conditions;
- expenditure mandates;
- State own-tax capacity and debt.

Transparency in the CAG-certified net proceeds and actual devolution is therefore as important as the percentage.

### 5. What changed from the Fifteenth Commission

The accepted comparison includes:

- income distance: 45 to 42.5 per cent;
- population: 15 to 17.5 per cent;
- demographic performance: 12.5 to 10 per cent;
- area: 15 to 10 per cent;
- Forest and ecology: 10 per cent retained, with revised measurement;
- tax and fiscal effort: separate 2.5 per cent criterion removed;
- Contribution to GDP: new 10 per cent criterion.

The analytical shift is from need plus tax effort toward need plus output. Yet income distance remains over two-fifths, preserving the redistributive centre.

The demographic variable also changed internally. The Fifteenth Commission used inverse total fertility rate. The Sixteenth Commission considered that basis increasingly weak and replaced it with inverse population growth between the 1971 and 2011 Censuses, multiplied by the 2011 population to obtain the State total. It retained the variable only as a gradual transition and reduced its weight from 12.5 to 10 per cent.

### Visual: Neither side can decide alone

```text

                 ↓

```

GST is not ordinary concurrent taxation. It is a special constitutional sharing arrangement.

### 1. The 101st Amendment

The 101st Constitutional Amendment, 2016 created:

- Article 246A: simultaneous GST legislative competence;
- Article 269A: inter-State GST levy, collection and apportionment;
- Article 279A: the GST Council.

GST subsumed many Union and State indirect taxes, reduced cascading and supported a common market. States exchanged independent rate space for participation in a larger shared tax system.

### 2. Council design

The Council includes the Union Finance Minister, the Union Minister of State responsible for finance or revenue, and a minister nominated by each State. State members choose one among themselves as Vice-Chairperson.

The voting formula is deliberate:

- Union: one-third of weighted votes;
- States collectively: two-thirds;
- decision: at least three-fourths of weighted votes of members present and voting;
- quorum: one-half of the total membership.

Neither constitutional level can carry a decision alone. The Union can block because one-third exceeds the one-quarter blocking threshold; States must combine but cannot pass without Union support.

### 3. Recommendation versus command

In *Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022), the Supreme Court held that Council recommendations are persuasive rather than binding. Parliament and State legislatures retain simultaneous Article 246A competence.

The ruling protects legislative autonomy. A binding reading could subordinate elected legislatures to an inter-governmental body not described as a legislature.

**Limit:** Legal non-bindingness does not make recommendations politically trivial. Divergent GST laws may fragment the market, so consensus remains functionally powerful.

### 4. Compensation bargain

The GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017 guaranteed compensation for a five-year period ending in June 2022, financed through a compensation cess.

The guarantee was the political price for State surrender of several taxes. Its end intensified the trust debate. The lesson is institutional rather than rate-specific: a pooled-sovereignty arrangement depends on credible commitments.

### 5. Is GST accommodative?

**Case for accommodation**

- shared legislative competence;
- constitutionally mixed Council;
- supermajority voting;
- compensation bargain;
- consensus practice;
- *Mohit Minerals* protection of legislative autonomy.

**Case for centralisation**

- loss of independent State rate-setting;
- dependence on apportionment and transfers;
- unequal bargaining capacity;
- Union blocking power;
- pressure for uniformity.

**Qualified verdict:** GST is accommodative in design but conditionally cooperative in practice.

8. Decisions need at least three-fourths weighted votes.
9. Quorum is one-half of membership.
10. Neither level can decide alone.
11. *Mohit Minerals* makes recommendations persuasive, not binding.
12. Legislative autonomy survives Council recommendations.
13. The statutory compensation period ended in June 2022.
14. Consensus remains politically necessary despite legal non-bindingness.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can GST Council recommendations be non-binding yet essential to the system’s functioning?

**Model answer:** Legislatures retain constitutional authority under Article 246A, so recommendations cannot legally command them. But harmonised rates and rules require coordinated action; widespread divergence would fragment the market. Recommendations therefore possess strong persuasive and political force without replacing legislative power.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say either that the Council binds legislatures or that its recommendations have no practical significance.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** “GST institutionalises pooled sovereignty but not fiscal equality.” Analyse.

**Model answer — 151 words:**
The 101st Amendment created pooled sovereignty through Articles 246A, 269A and 279A. Parliament and State legislatures share GST competence; inter-State GST is apportioned; and a mixed Council continuously negotiates rates and rules. Its voting design—Union one-third, States two-thirds and a three-fourths decision threshold—prevents either level from deciding alone.

Accommodation is reinforced by the original compensation bargain and *Mohit Minerals* (2022), which held Council recommendations persuasive rather than binding. Legislative autonomy therefore survives institutional coordination.

Fiscal equality, however, does not follow. States differ in consumption bases, administrative capacity and dependence on transfers. GST reduced independent State rate space, compensation disputes weakened trust, and the Union possesses effective blocking power. Harmonisation can therefore operate within unequal bargaining conditions.

GST is India’s strongest experiment in negotiated tax sovereignty, but not a cure for vertical or horizontal imbalance. Its federal legitimacy depends on consensus, transparent apportionment, credible dispute resolution and respect for State legislative choice.

Adjudication answers a legal allocation question. It does not automatically create trust, shared data or day-to-day compliance.

### 1. Article 262

Article 262 permits Parliament to:

1. provide for adjudication of disputes concerning use, distribution or control of waters of an inter-State river or river valley; and
2. exclude the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and other courts over the defined dispute.

The provision recognises that river disputes combine law, hydrology, engineering, agriculture and inter-governmental politics.

### 2. The two 1956 Acts

The **River Boards Act, 1956** permits institutional coordination for river development, but its river-board vision has not become the principal dispute-resolution route.

The **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956** provides dispute-specific tribunal adjudication. Tribunals have included Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Narmada, Ravi-Beas and Mahanadi.

Article 262 does not itself create a tribunal for every dispute. Parliamentary legislation supplies the machinery.

### 3. Court-jurisdiction trap

Article 262 permits Parliament to exclude judicial jurisdiction over the water dispute. It does not mean every legal question touching a river is beyond all courts. Questions concerning statutory implementation, constitutional procedure or matters outside the defined dispute may raise distinct issues.

The safe answer is precise: Parliament may exclude court jurisdiction over the adjudicated inter-State water dispute to the statutory extent.

### 4. Why delays persist

| Weakness | Mechanism of failure |
|---|---|
| Delay in constituting or completing tribunals | Political positions harden before adjudication |
| Conflicting hydrological data | Governments litigate different factual worlds |
| Implementation deficit | Legal allocation lacks routine compliance machinery |
| Variable rainfall and climate stress | Historical averages become less reliable |
| Agriculture and urban demand | Allocation disputes become livelihood conflicts |

### 5. Reform without pretending that a proposal is law

A 2019 Amendment Bill proposed a dispute-resolution committee and a permanent tribunal with benches. It passed the Lok Sabha but lapsed with the dissolution of the Seventeenth Lok Sabha. Later revised proposals are not current law.

The existing system remains dispute-specific. Reform should focus on:

- time-bound preliminary negotiation and tribunal stages;
- an independent shared basin-data platform;
- technically capable permanent support staff without falsely claiming a permanent tribunal already exists;
- implementation and compliance bodies;
- ecological-flow and climate-variability review;
- transparent reasons and periodic monitoring.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Excluding ordinary judicial jurisdiction weakens constitutional accountability.
**Reply:** Specialised adjudication can handle technical basin evidence and prevent parallel litigation; statutory and constitutional review questions do not necessarily vanish.
**Residual:** Without strong implementation and credible data, specialised adjudication may merely relocate delay.

The table prevents a frequent mistake: every inter-governmental body is not “constitutional” merely because it works within the constitutional system.

### 1. Inter-State Council

Article 263 authorises the President to establish a Council where public interest would be served. Its possible duties include:

- inquiring into and advising upon disputes between States;
- investigating and discussing subjects of common interest;
- recommending better coordination of policy and action.

The permanent Inter-State Council was established in 1990 following the Sarkaria Commission recommendation. It is chaired by the Prime Minister and includes Chief Ministers, relevant Union Territory leadership and Union Cabinet ministers. Its Standing Committee, chaired by the Union Home Minister, supports continuing work.

The distinction between constitutional possibility and assigned duty is crucial. The 1990 Presidential Order gave the permanent Council the common-interest investigation/discussion and policy-coordination functions corresponding to Article 263(b) and (c); it did not assign the formal dispute-inquiry function in clause (a).

Its procedural framework envisages regular meetings, ordinarily in camera and by consensus. The expectation of at least three meetings a year underlines the gap between institutional design and the much less regular practice of the full Council.

Its function is advisory. This distinguishes it from:

- Article 131 adjudication by the Supreme Court, which produces legal judgment;
- Article 262 tribunal adjudication under statute;
- political negotiations without a standing constitutional forum.

### 2. Why advisory institutions matter

An advisory institution may still alter incentives:

```text
early consultation
      ↓
States disclose objections before legislation
      ↓
technical and political issues separated
      ↓
common options developed
      ↓
lower litigation and implementation resistance
```

The strongest criticism is under-use: constitutional permission cannot build trust if the full Council rarely becomes the regular site of national bargaining. The reply is that Zonal Councils and ministerial forums handle many operational matters. The residual need is escalation—regional issues requiring structural settlement should reach a functioning national council.

### 3. Zonal Councils

Five Zonal Councils—Northern, Central, Eastern, Western and Southern—are statutory bodies under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.

- common Chairperson: Union Home Minister;
- a Chief Minister serves as Vice-Chairperson by annual rotation;
- character: advisory and deliberative;
- use: regional security, boundaries, transport, language, development and coordination.

They are not constitutional bodies and not substitutes for the Inter-State Council.

The National Security Council and National Integration Council are executive, non-statutory consultative bodies. Their executive source distinguishes them from Article 263’s constitutional provision and the statutory Zonal Councils.

The wider coordination network also includes sectoral councils and recurring conferences. Standard examples include councils concerned with health and family welfare, local government, transport, higher education and traditional systems of medicine, together with conferences of Governors, Chief Ministers, Chief Secretaries, police leadership, Chief Justices, Vice-Chancellors, Home Ministers and Law Ministers. Their names and administrative arrangements may evolve, but the constitutional point is stable: much inter-governmental cooperation occurs through executive consultation rather than a single federal chamber.

### 4. North-Eastern Council

The North-Eastern Council is a separate statutory institution under the North-Eastern Council Act, 1971. It is not a “sixth Zonal Council”. Sikkim was added later to the Council’s regional membership.

The distinction is examination-worthy because similar regional functions arise from different statutes and institutional designs.

### 5. Full faith and credit under Article 261

Article 261 requires full faith and credit throughout India for public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State. Parliament may prescribe their mode of proof and effect.

Final judgments or orders of civil courts are capable of execution anywhere in India according to law.

**Limit:** The civil-judgment clause does not mean that every State penal law automatically operates across India. Recognition of a judgment is not identical to extra-territorial legislative competence.

Part XIII protects an integrated market. It does not constitutionalise a tax-free or regulation-free economy.

### 1. Article 301

Trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India shall be free. The guarantee covers both inter-State and intra-State activity and may be affected by a direct restriction at a frontier or at an earlier or later stage. The provision restrains both legislative and executive barriers.

The freedom is not a Fundamental Right and does not by itself create an Article 32 claim. It is a structural constitutional guarantee of economic integration.

### 2. Parliament’s power

Article 302 permits Parliament to impose restrictions in the public interest.

Article 303(1) generally prevents Parliament and State legislatures from preferring one State over another or discriminating between States. Article 303(2) permits Parliament—not State legislatures—to depart from this rule where necessary to deal with scarcity of goods.

### 3. State power

Article 304(a) permits a State to tax goods imported from other States only where similar locally produced goods bear equivalent taxation. The controlling idea is non-discrimination.

Article 304(b) permits reasonable public-interest restrictions, but the Bill requires previous Presidential sanction.

The two clauses are distinct. A State cannot justify discriminatory taxation merely by invoking public interest under clause (b).

### 4. Article 305 and public monopolies

Article 305 preserves the constitutionally authorised operation of specified existing laws and laws creating State or Union monopolies. Parliament or a State legislature may provide for government carrying on a trade, business, industry or service, wholly or partly excluding citizens, within the constitutional framework.

This nationalisation/public-monopoly exception does not authorise discriminatory protection for private traders.

### 5. Judicial evolution

*Atiabari Tea Co.* (1961) focused Article 301 scrutiny on restrictions having a direct and immediate effect on trade.

*Automobile Transport (Rajasthan)* (1962) accepted a regulatory/compensatory understanding for some levies.

In *Jindal Stainless v. State of Haryana* (2016), a nine-judge Bench rejected the compensatory-tax doctrine as lacking textual foundation. The central tax inquiry is Article 304(a) non-discrimination.

Article 307 permits Parliament to appoint an authority for Articles 301–304, but no such authority has been constituted. Judicial interpretation therefore remains the central enforcement mechanism.

### 6. GST and Part XIII

Part XIII supplied the constitutional command of a common market but could not alone remove every fiscal border. GST created shared tax competence and administrative harmonisation.

**Answer line:** GST did institutionally what Part XIII had largely attempted through constitutional litigation—replace fragmented indirect taxation with negotiated national integration.

The analogy has limits. Part XIII remains relevant to restrictions and discrimination beyond GST.

### 7. Minor minerals: a separate tested split

The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 creates a close-option competence distinction:

| Function | Authority |
|---|---|
| Define “minor minerals” and notify additional minerals under Section 3(e) | Central Government |
| Make rules regulating quarry leases, mining leases and other concessions for minor minerals under Section 15 | State Governments |

Central power to classify the mineral does not eliminate State rule-making for concessions.

### 1. Representation as a federal variable

Article 81 links Lok Sabha representation to population. Article 80 and the Fourth Schedule allocate Rajya Sabha seats unequally; India does not possess an equal-State upper chamber like the United States Senate. Articles 54–55 also connect parliamentary representation to the presidential electoral college.

Representation therefore affects:

- law-making power;
- government formation;
- constitutional-amendment politics;
- presidential election weight;
- bargaining over fiscal and administrative policy.

### 2. The seat-freeze bargain

The Forty-second Amendment froze inter-State seat allocation on the 1971 census. The Eighty-fourth Amendment extended the freeze until the relevant figures of the first census after 2026 are published, while permitting boundary readjustment within States. The Eighty-seventh Amendment shifted that internal readjustment basis to the 2001 census.

This bargain protected States that achieved population stabilisation from losing relative representation. The counter-value is democratic equality: indefinitely freezing allocation means voters in more populous States may receive systematically lower relative representation.

### 3. Static legislative-status control

The Constitution (One Hundred and Thirty-First Amendment) Bill, 2026 and linked delimitation legislation proposed removing the existing freeze mechanism, allowing Parliament to determine the census basis and raising the Lok Sabha ceiling from 550 to 850—up to 815 members from States and 35 from Union Territories. The linked Bill contemplated use of the latest published census, which at introduction meant the 2011 census. The constitutional amendment did not pass. Every figure is therefore only the defeated Bill’s text, not law, precedent or forecast.

The proposal also attempted to detach commencement of women’s legislative reservation from waiting for a fresh post-commencement census. That change did not occur. The 106th Amendment has commenced, but the reservation remains non-operational until the Article 334A census-publication and delimitation conditions are fulfilled. Census conduct or reference dates must not be confused with publication of final figures, and no delimitation or reserved-election date is presently asserted.

The safe constitutional position remains:

- Articles 81 and 82 continue to govern representation and readjustment;
- the Forty-second/Eighty-fourth/Eighty-seventh Amendment chain remains operative;
- no State-wise seat projection should be stated;
- no future delimitation or reserved-election date should be invented.

The analytical lesson—not the proposal—is that representation and devolution can become the same grievance in two currencies. A State may fear losing both relative seats and fiscal weight through population-based criteria.

### 4. Executive federalism

Much practical federalism occurs through executives:

- Prime Minister and Chief Ministers;
- Union and State ministers;
- officials and standing committees;
- GST, NITI, Zonal and sectoral meetings.

Executive federalism is fast and technically capable. It may, however, reduce legislative scrutiny, public transparency and opposition participation.

The answer is not to eliminate executive negotiation but to make it accountable through agendas, recorded reasons, follow-up reports and legislative information.

### 5. Three modes of practical federalism

| Mode | Mechanism | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cooperative | councils, tax sharing, joint programmes | coordination and common standards | symbolic consultation |
| Competitive | investment and performance comparison | innovation and peer learning | race to the bottom or data gaming |
| Confrontational/coercive | assent disputes, agency consent, scheme conditions | exposes genuine constitutional disagreement | partisan institutional use |

Parties do not permanently belong to one mode. National parties may centralise when governing the Union and demand autonomy when governing a State. Regional parties may bargain nationally while centralising within their own States.

### 6. How the autonomy debate developed

Centre-State relations became more openly contested after the 1967 elections weakened one-party dominance across the Union and the States. The disagreements were not confined to constitutional theory. They concerned the everyday instruments through which the Union could influence State government.

| Tension family | Recurring issues identified in the standard account |
|---|---|
| Constitutional offices and emergencies | appointment, removal and partisan conduct of Governors; Article 356; reservation of State Bills |
| Security and administration | deployment of central forces; management of All-India Services; commissions of inquiry against Chief Ministers |
| Money and development | discriminatory allocations; tax sharing; Planning Commission project approval; centrally sponsored schemes |
| Competence and agencies | encroachment on the State List; working methods of central agencies such as CBI and ED |
| Public communication | alleged partisan use of publicly controlled electronic media |

The list explains why later committees proposed different cures. Some sought better cooperation within the existing strong-Union design. Others demanded a basic redistribution of power.

#### First Administrative Reforms Commission

The Union appointed the First Administrative Reforms Commission in 1966, initially chaired by Morarji Desai and later by K. Hanumanthayya. Its study team on Centre-State relations was led by M.C. Setalvad. The Commission reported in 1969 and made 22 recommendations.

Its major proposals included:

- establish an Inter-State Council under Article 263;
- appoint experienced, non-partisan Governors;
- delegate powers to States to the maximum practicable extent;
- transfer more financial resources to reduce State dependence;
- regulate deployment of central armed forces in States through clearer federal practice.

The recommendations did not receive comprehensive Union implementation. Their importance lies in diagnosing coordination and neutrality problems before the later Sarkaria Commission.

#### Rajamannar Committee

The Tamil Nadu Government appointed the Rajamannar Committee in 1969; it reported in 1971. It attributed centralisation to Union-weighted constitutional provisions, one-party dominance, State fiscal weakness and central planning.

Its autonomy programme included an immediate Inter-State Council, a permanent Finance Commission, replacement of the Planning Commission by a statutory body, removal of Articles 356, 357 and 365, deletion of the rule that a State ministry holds office during the Governor’s pleasure, transfer of selected Union/Concurrent subjects and residuary power to States, and abolition of All-India Services. These demands were not accepted by the Union.

#### Anandpur Sahib Resolution

The Akali Dal’s 1973 Anandpur Sahib Resolution combined political and religious demands. Its federal claim would restrict Union jurisdiction largely to defence, foreign affairs, communications and currency, place residuary power with States, and seek equal State authority and representation at the Centre.

Its value in an answer is not to treat it as constitutional law, but as evidence of a strong regional-autonomy response to perceived centralisation.

#### West Bengal Memorandum

The West Bengal Government’s 1977 memorandum demanded a more explicitly federal constitutional order. It proposed replacing “Union” with “federal”, limiting the Centre principally to defence, foreign affairs, currency, communications and economic coordination, vesting other and residuary subjects in States, repealing Articles 356, 357 and 360, requiring State consent for reorganisation, allocating 75 per cent of total central revenue to States, equalising Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha powers, and abolishing All-India Services.

The Union did not accept these demands. Their analytical limit is also their strength: they show the maximalist autonomy position against which Sarkaria’s cooperative strong-Union compromise can be evaluated.

```text
FIRST ARC
improve cooperation within the existing system
        ↓
RAJAMANNAR / ANANDPUR / WEST BENGAL
redistribute constitutional and fiscal power toward States
        ↓
SARKARIA / PUNCHHI
retain a strong Union but discipline its instruments
```

The historical sequence shows that federal reform has always involved two rival strategies: **activate and neutralise common institutions**, or **transfer more constitutional power to States**.

### 8. Trust-building architecture

The 2024 demand asks for changes and remedies together:

**UPSC GS-II 2024, Q13:** “What changes has the Union Government recently introduced in the domain of Centre-State relations? Suggest measures to be adopted to build the trust between the Centre and the States and for strengthening federalism.” **15 marks, 250 words.**

**Answer approach:** plan-to-NITI shift, Finance Commission devolution, GST, cesses, schemes, Governor and CBI friction → pair each diagnosis with one reform.

An integrated reform ladder is:

1. regular Inter-State Council meetings with tracked responses;
2. structured consultation before major Concurrent-field legislation;
3. prompt, reasoned and politically neutral constitutional-office decisions;
4. predictable devolution and transparent treatment of cesses/surcharges;
5. flexible, jointly designed centrally sponsored schemes;
6. credible GST dispute settlement and consensus practice;
7. shared river data and implementation institutions;
8. a comprehensive and independent CBI statutory framework;
9. accountable executive-federal processes;
10. negotiated representational safeguards when reapportionment is constitutionally undertaken.

### 9. Strongest objection, reply and final residual

**Objection:** India’s structural Union dominance—residue, Governors, fiscal imbalance, emergency power and unequal bargaining—cannot be cured merely by convening councils.
**Reply:** Textual asymmetry is real, but institutions alter how power is exercised. *Bommai*, Finance Commission rules, GST bargaining, judicial review and electoral federalisation have constrained unilateralism.
**Residual:** Institutional safeguards themselves depend on political willingness. Durable federalism therefore needs both legal rules and repeated cooperative practice.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why are representation and tax devolution described as two currencies of the same federal grievance?

**Model answer:** Both determine a State’s effective weight within the Union. Population-based reapportionment can alter parliamentary influence, while population-based fiscal criteria affect resource shares. A State that achieved demographic stabilisation may therefore fear relative loss in both political voice and transfers.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not describe the Rajya Sabha as an equal-State chamber or treat a defeated delimitation proposal as existing law.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Centre-State trust requires more than judicial enforcement of federal boundaries. Critically examine and propose an integrated reform architecture.

**Model answer — 183 words:**
Judicial review protects federal boundaries: competence doctrines preserve State fields, *S.R. Bommai* restrains Article 356, constitutional courts balance CBI consent, and *Mohit Minerals* protects GST legislative autonomy. Yet litigation is reactive and cannot itself create routine cooperation.

Trust deficits arise earlier. Major Concurrent legislation may lack structured consultation; Governors and agencies become partisan flashpoints; cesses and conditional schemes weaken predictable devolution; water awards face data and implementation failures; executive negotiations lack transparency. Representation adds a fourth concern because seat weight and fiscal weight may shift together.

Reform must connect institutions to each deficit. The Inter-State Council should meet regularly with published follow-up. Concurrent-field Bills need advance State consultation. Constitutional offices should give prompt reasons within controlling legal limits. The divisible pool and scheme conditions require transparency and flexibility. GST needs credible dispute settlement; river basins need common data and compliance bodies; the CBI requires an independent statute. Executive forums should publish agendas and outcomes, while future reapportionment must be negotiated with compensating federal safeguards.

Union strength remains necessary for redistribution and national capacity. The objective is not weaker government but rule-bound shared government.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Legislative block

**Question:** A State law incidentally touches a Union entry, while a parliamentary law regulates a related field. What sequence should a court follow before declaring the State law void?

**Model answer:** Identify the State law’s pith and substance, locate both laws in their proper entries, test whether the encroachment is merely incidental, and attempt harmonious construction. Article 254 applies only if both laws occupy a Concurrent field and create a real conflict. Union supremacy resolves only the irreconcilable remainder.

**Misconception to avoid:** Similar subject matter does not automatically establish repugnancy.

## Check 2 — Administrative block

**Question:** Distinguish Union direction, executive entrustment and judicially ordered central investigation.

**Model answer:** A Union direction under Articles 256–257 secures constitutionally authorised implementation or non-impediment. Entrustment under Articles 258/258A reallocates specified executive work by consent. A constitutional court ordering CBI investigation exercises Articles 32/226 power and does not create a general Union-executive right to bypass State consent.

**Misconception to avoid:** These mechanisms have different sources, actors and consequences.

## Check 3 — Fiscal block

**Question:** How can GST, Finance Commission devolution and Article 282 grants simultaneously strengthen and constrain States?

**Model answer:** GST gives States shared constitutional tax power and institutional bargaining; Finance Commission devolution provides formula-based resources; Article 282 enables flexible development grants. Yet GST narrows independent rate space, devolution applies only to the divisible pool, and Article 282 conditions may steer State priorities.

**Misconception to avoid:** Fiscal federalism is neither complete autonomy nor simple Union charity.

## Check 4 — Final synthesis

**Question:** What single principle connects conflict doctrines, councils, fiscal formulas and water tribunals?

**Model answer:** Each converts raw political power into a rule-bound relationship. Doctrines require legal sequencing, councils require consultation, fiscal formulas require predictable distribution, and tribunals require reasoned adjudication. Federal trust depends on governments experiencing these rules consistently rather than relying on partisan goodwill.

**Misconception to avoid:** Institutional existence alone does not guarantee effective federalism; design, use and compliance all matter.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Distinguish federal supremacy, repugnancy and Union legislation in the State field.

**Model answer — 133 words:**
Federal supremacy is the constitutional priority that resolves irreconcilable Union-State conflict after competence and harmonious construction have been examined. Article 246 gives Union List priority, while Article 254 governs repugnancy.

Repugnancy is narrower. It ordinarily arises where valid Union and State laws occupy the same Concurrent field and cannot operate together. Union law prevails; a President-assented State law may operate within that State until Parliament overrides it.

Union legislation in the State field does not necessarily involve conflict. Parliament may acquire competence through Article 249, 250, 252, 253 or 356. Each route has its own trigger, duration and safeguard.

Thus, State-field entry concerns the source of parliamentary competence; repugnancy concerns conflict between valid laws; federal supremacy is the final constitutional priority rule. Treating all three as “central dominance” conceals their different legal functions.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** three accurate definitions 4; Articles and routes 3; relationship among concepts 2; concise conclusion 1.

## Final 15-mark question

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** “Indian fiscal federalism has become more rule-based without becoming less contested.” Examine.

**Model answer — 170 words:**
India’s fiscal relationship has moved from parallel Finance Commission and Planning Commission channels toward a clearer constitutional and negotiated architecture. Article 280 devolution is formula-based; the Fourteenth Finance Commission enlarged untied tax sharing; NITI Aayog replaced fund-allocating plan machinery with advisory coordination; and GST created shared competence and a permanent Council.

The Sixteenth Finance Commission retains 41 per cent vertical devolution and combines income distance, population, demography, area, the Forest and ecology criterion, and Contribution to GDP. This strengthens transparency and predictability. *Mohit Minerals* also protects State legislative autonomy by treating GST Council recommendations as persuasive.

Contest persists because every formula distributes gains and losses. Population and GDP criteria divide States differently. Cesses and surcharges shrink the divisible pool, Article 282 schemes carry conditions, GST narrows independent rate space, and Article 293 borrowing consent preserves Union leverage.

Fiscal federalism is therefore more institutionalised, not fiscally equal. Reform should widen transparency around the divisible pool, rationalise conditional schemes, strengthen GST dispute settlement and preserve predictable rules while permitting genuine State flexibility.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** evolution 3; rule-based institutions 4; current formula application 2; contest sources 4; reform/verdict 2.

## Final 20-mark question

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Critically evaluate whether India’s Centre-State machinery is adequate for managing a mature, diverse federation.

**Model answer — 173 words:**
India possesses a dense federal machinery. The Seventh Schedule and competence doctrines structure law-making; Articles 256–258A manage implementation and delegation; Finance Commissions, grants and GST organise fiscal interdependence; Article 262 tribunals, Article 263 councils and Article 261 legal recognition support inter-State relations. Judicial review through *Bommai*, *CPDR*, *Jindal Stainless* and *Mohit Minerals* restrains excess.

The machinery is nevertheless uneven. Union residue, emergency powers, Governors, cesses, scheme conditionality and borrowing consent create structural leverage. The Inter-State Council is under-used, water disputes suffer data and implementation deficits, CBI consent becomes partisan, and executive negotiation often lacks transparency. Representation adds a growing conflict because population may affect both seats and fiscal shares.

Adequacy therefore depends less on creating another institution than on activating and connecting existing ones. India needs regular ISC consultation, reasoned constitutional-office decisions, transparent divisible-pool data, flexible schemes, a credible GST dispute process, shared river information, an independent CBI statute and accountable executive forums.

The system is constitutionally adequate but operationally incomplete. A mature federation requires predictable consultation before compulsion and compliance after adjudication.

**Rubric — 20 marks:** constitutional machinery 6; judicial safeguards 3; structural limitations 5; reform architecture 4; critical verdict 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## The operating map

- Legislative relations: Articles 245–255.
- Administrative relations: Articles 256–263.
- Financial relations: Articles 265–293.
- Inter-State/economic relations: Articles 261–263 and 301–307.
- Always identify the relevant channel before alleging centralisation.

## Legislative competence

- Article 245: Parliament for whole/part of India; extra-territorial operation not by itself invalid.
- State extra-territorial effect needs real and pertinent territorial nexus.
- Examination convention: Union 100, State 61, Concurrent 52; literal operative-entry counting may yield 98/59/52 because of omissions and insertions.
- Article 246 priority: Union → Concurrent → State.
- Article 248 + Entry 97: genuine residue, including tax, belongs to Parliament.
- Forty-second Amendment shifted education, forests, weights and measures, wildlife protection and administration of justice from State to Concurrent.
- State-field routes: Articles 249, 250, 252, 253 and 356.
- Articles 249 and 250 cover State List matters and the specified Article 246A GST field.
- Article 249: Rajya Sabha two-thirds present and voting; no Emergency required.
- Article 252: two or more States request; Parliament alone later amends/repeals.
- Article 253: implementation of international obligations.
- Article 251: parliamentary law under Articles 249/250 temporarily prevails over inconsistent State law.
- Article 255: specified prior-sanction/recommendation defects may be procedurally cured by proper final assent, but competence defects cannot.

## Competence-doctrine sequence

1. territorial reach;
2. pith and substance;
3. proper List entry;
4. ancillary/incidental effect;
5. harmonious construction;
6. Article 254 repugnancy in a Concurrent field;
7. federal supremacy only for irreconcilable conflict.

- Colourable legislation tests disguised lack of power, not bad motive.
- Article 254(2): President-assented State law may prevail in that State.
- Parliament may later override it.
- Assent cannot cure absence of State competence.

## State Bills and assent

- Previous Presidential sanction differs from post-passage reservation.
- Article 200 controlling options: assent; reserve; withhold and return non-Money Bill with comments.
- High Court-endangering Bill must be reserved.
- Article 201: President may assent, withhold or direct return of non-Money Bill.
- After Article 200 re-passage, the Governor must assent or reserve the Bill for the President and cannot withhold again.
- Article 201 gives six months for reconsideration but does not compel presidential assent after re-passage.
- Article 213: specified State ordinances require Presidential instructions where comparable Bills would attract Union control.
- 2025 INSC 1333: no court-created rigid timeline or deemed assent.
- Article 200 choice is discretionary and not merits-reviewed.
- Prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction permits limited mandamus to act.

## Administrative relations

- Executive power generally follows legislative distribution; States ordinarily execute Concurrent-field law unless Union power is specially assigned.
- Article 256: State compliance with parliamentary/applicable law; necessary Union directions.
- Article 257: State must not impede Union; communications and railway-protection directions.
- Article 339(2): ST-welfare directions.
- Article 350A: primary mother-tongue facilities.
- Article 355: Union duty, not automatic takeover.
- Article 365: relevant inference, not automatic President’s Rule.
- Article 356: parliamentary approval and *Bommai* review.
- Article 357: temporary State legislative arrangements during President’s Rule.
- Article 258: Union functions entrusted to State with consent.
- Article 258A: State functions entrusted to Union with consent.
- SPSC members: Governor appoints, President removes under the constitutional process.
- Parliament may create a Joint State PSC after concerned State-legislature resolutions.
- UPSC may serve a State on gubernatorial request with presidential approval.

## Services and investigation

- Article 312: Rajya Sabha two-thirds present-and-voting trigger for new AIS.
- AIS: IAS, IPS, Indian Forest Service.
- Indian Foreign Service is not AIS.
- Common recruitment and State field service create national standards plus cadre tension.
- DSPE Sections 5–6: extension to State and State consent.
- General consent differs from case-specific consent.
- Articles 32/226 courts may direct CBI investigation without State consent.
- Court exception does not give the Union executive general bypass power.

## Fiscal Constitution

- Article 265: no tax except authority of law.
- Concurrent List has no general tax entry; GST uses Article 246A.
- Standard textbook tax-entry count: Union 13 and State 18; taxing power still depends on the exact entry or special article.
- Article 268: specified Union levy, State collection/appropriation.
- Article 269: Union collection, assignment to States.
- Article 269A: inter-State GST apportionment.
- Article 270: divisible pool.
- Article 271: Union surcharge outside sharing.
- Article 274: Presidential recommendation for specified Bills affecting State tax/distribution interests.
- Article 286: States cannot tax outside-State or import/export supplies; Parliament may formulate determining principles.
- Article 287 protects specified Union and railway electricity consumption/sales from State tax.
- Article 288 requires reservation and Presidential assent for specified State taxes on inter-State river-valley authorities.
- Union and State non-tax revenues ordinarily remain outside the Article 270 divisible pool.
- Article 275: constitutionally structured grants.
- Article 275 also supports specific ST-welfare and Scheduled-Area administration grants.
- Article 282: any-public-purpose discretionary grants.
- Article 276 profession-tax cap: ₹2,500 annually per person.
- Article 285/289: qualified Union/State tax immunities.
- Government corporations are separate taxable entities; local authorities lack State immunity; State immunity does not automatically cover customs/excise or taxable commercial operations.
- Article 292: Union borrowing.
- Article 293: State borrowing within India; consent where Union debt/guarantee remains.
- National Emergency may temporarily modify revenue distribution; Financial Emergency permits financial-propriety and State financial-Bill directions.

## Finance Commission and fiscal evolution

- Article 280: President constitutes every fifth year or earlier; Chairperson plus four members.
- Vertical = Union–States split; horizontal = distribution among States.
- Article 281: report and action-taken explanation laid before Parliament.
- Planning Commission/NDC once operated a discretionary plan channel alongside Finance Commission.
- Eightieth Amendment, 2000: wider alternative tax-devolution scheme based on the Tenth Finance Commission.
- NITI replaced Planning Commission in 2015; it advises and coordinates but does not devolve taxes.
- Fourteenth Finance Commission: 32 to 42 per cent.
- Fifteenth Finance Commission: 41 per cent.
- Sixteenth Finance Commission, 2026–31: 41 per cent retained.
- Horizontal weights: GSDP distance 42.5; population 17.5; demographic performance 10; area 10; Forest and ecology 10; Contribution to GDP 10.
- Sixteenth-FC demographic performance uses inverse population growth between the 1971 and 2011 Censuses; it replaced the Fifteenth-FC inverse-TFR method.
- Contribution to GDP is new; separate tax-effort weight removed.
- Cesses/surcharges and Article 282 conditionality remain major grievances.

## GST

- 101st Amendment, 2016.
- Articles 246A, 269A and 279A.
- Union vote one-third; States collectively two-thirds.
- Decision needs at least three-fourths weighted votes; quorum one-half.
- Neither level can carry a decision alone.
- *Mohit Minerals* (2022): recommendations persuasive, not binding.
- Statutory five-year State compensation period ended in June 2022.
- GST is accommodative in design but bargaining remains unequal.

## Inter-State water and coordination

- Article 262 enables statutory adjudication and jurisdiction exclusion.
- River Boards Act, 1956 and Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956.
- Existing principal model: dispute-specific tribunals.
- 2019 permanent-tribunal Bill lapsed; proposal is not law.
- Durable settlement requires common data, timely adjudication and implementation bodies.
- Article 263 offers dispute-advice, common-interest and coordination functions; the 1990 permanent ISC was assigned clauses (b) and (c), not formal clause (a) dispute inquiry.
- Zonal Councils: five statutory bodies under SRA 1956; Union Home Minister chairs.
- NEC: separate 1971 Act; not sixth Zonal Council.
- National Security Council and National Integration Council are executive/non-statutory.
- Sectoral councils and ministerial/official conferences form a wider executive-coordination network.
- Article 261: nationwide full faith and credit; civil judgments executable according to law.

## Common market and tested distinctions

- Article 301: freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse.
- Article 301 covers intra-State as well as inter-State trade.
- Article 302: parliamentary public-interest restrictions.
- Article 303: general prohibition on State preference; Parliament scarcity exception.
- Article 304(a): non-discriminatory State taxation.
- Article 304(b): reasonable restriction with previous Presidential sanction.
- Article 305 preserves specified existing laws and constitutionally authorised public monopolies.
- *Jindal Stainless* (2016) rejected the compensatory-tax doctrine.
- Article 301 does not promise tax-free trade.
- Article 307 authority has not been constituted.
- Inter-State trade: Union Entry 42.
- Inter-State migration and quarantine: Union Entry 81.
- MMDR Section 3(e): Centre notifies minor minerals.
- MMDR Section 15: States regulate minor-mineral concessions.

## Representation and trust

- Article 81 links Lok Sabha allocation and population.
- Rajya Sabha does not give equal State representation.
- Freeze chain: Forty-second, Eighty-fourth and Eighty-seventh Amendments.
- Inter-State allocation freeze differs from intra-State boundary readjustment.
- The 2026 delimitation-linked constitutional proposal did not become law.
- The defeated Bill proposed a ceiling of 850—up to 815 State and 35 Union Territory members—and a different census route; neither became law.
- The proposed decoupling of women’s reservation from the post-commencement census condition did not occur.
- The 106th Amendment has commenced, but the reservation remains non-operational pending the Article 334A publication-and-delimitation sequence.
- Never invent seat projections or implementation dates.
- Representation and devolution may become two currencies of one grievance.
- First ARC, 1966–69: ISC, neutral Governors, greater delegation and stronger State finances.
- Rajamannar: strong autonomy.
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution: narrow Union fields and State residuary power.
- West Bengal Memorandum: an explicitly federal redesign, greater State revenue and removal of emergency/AIS levers.
- Sarkaria: strong Union plus cooperation, neutral Governors, ISC and Article 356 restraint.
- Punchhi: modernised Governor/council machinery and localised-emergency proposal.
- Trust formula: consultation before compulsion + reasons with discretion + predictable finance + implementation after adjudication.
