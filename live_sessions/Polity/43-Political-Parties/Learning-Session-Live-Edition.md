# Political Parties — Live Session Edition

A new association registers itself with the Election Commission, swears allegiance to the Constitution, and calls itself a political party. Three elections later it still cannot get a reserved symbol, still gets only twenty star campaigners instead of forty, and still has no seat in Parliament — yet it is, in every legal sense, a party. Meanwhile a seventy-year-old party that has not contested an election in a decade remains on the register because nobody has the power to strike it off. A third party splits down the middle, and within weeks the Election Commission — not a court — decides which half gets to keep the party's name and its reserved symbol. None of this is written in the part of the Constitution students read first, because for nearly four decades after 1950 the Constitution did not mention the word "political party" at all.

This session teaches the party the way an examiner tests it: what a party actually is before any Indian law touches it, the three separate legal layers that regulate it — a constitutional amendment, a statute and an Election Commission order — and why collapsing those three layers into one is the single most common error in a Political-Parties answer. From there the session moves to how a party is funded and how that funding is policed, why criminal candidates keep winning despite three decades of court-ordered disclosure, why no law yet compels a party to hold its own elections, and how the party system itself has changed shape four times since 1952. It closes with the one demand examiners return to most often — whether national and regional parties disagree about centralisation because of conviction or because of position — and with the architecture needed to answer any directive on this topic to time.

### The learner's problem

Before any Indian statute or court judgment can be taught, you need to be able to answer a more basic question without hesitating: what, exactly, is a political party, and why does a representative democracy organise itself around a small number of them rather than around individual candidates alone? Get this wrong and every later lesson about registration, recognition or regulation floats without a foundation.

*Caption: a party's defining job is aggregation — turning scattered individual opinion into a small number of governable choices that can actually form and run a government.*

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine an election with no parties at all: every one of the thousands of candidates across the country would run on a private manifesto, and a legislature built from them would be a room full of free agents with no basis for stable coalitions, collective responsibility or a shared programme to implement. A party solves this coordination problem. It is a voluntary association of people who already share political views, and it exists to win power constitutionally — through the ballot, not through force — in order to put a programme into practice "for the national interest" (or, as later lessons show, often quite explicitly for a regional one).

### Technical doctrine: definition, ideological types, and party systems

A **political party** is a voluntary, organised group of individuals sharing similar political views, that seeks to capture political power through constitutional means and professes to work for the national interest. Three structural facts follow from this definition before any Indian-specific detail is added:

1. **Voluntary association, not a State organ.** A party is privately formed; the State does not create it, though — as Lessons 3-4 show — the State later registers and classifies it.
2. **Constitutional means only.** The definition itself excludes organisations seeking power through violence or unconstitutional methods from the ordinary category of "political party" for this purpose.
3. **A claimed, not guaranteed, public orientation.** "Desire to work for the national interest" is the party's own claim; it is not independently verified before registration, which is precisely why later lessons on finance and criminalisation matter.

Political scientists classify parties along an ideological spectrum into four types, and India is normally taught with illustrative placements on it:

| Type | Core disposition | Spectrum position | Indian illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reactionary | Clings to older socio-economic and political institutions | Right | Used as a textbook category; avoid naming a specific current Indian party as "reactionary" in an answer |
| Conservative | Believes in the status quo | Right | — |
| Liberal | Aims at reforming existing institutions | Centre | The Congress is the standard centrist illustration |
| Radical | Aims at a new order, by overthrowing existing institutions | Left | CPI and CPM are the standard leftist illustrations |

> 🔑 **Spectrum shorthand:** radical = left, liberal = centre, reactionary/conservative = right. The BJP is conventionally placed as a rightist illustration in this scheme; treat all three placements as textbook classification, not as a claim about any party's full contemporary programme.

A country's **party system** — how many parties genuinely compete for power — falls into three types:

### Example and its limit

The CPI/CPM-as-left, Congress-as-centre, BJP-as-right placement is a convenient teaching anchor for the ideological-type question. Its limit: a party's position in this scheme describes a general ideological tendency at the time the textbook was written, not a verified, timeless or complete description of every policy a party holds today — treat it as classification, not as current political commentary.

### UPSC application

A Prelims or Mains opening line that confuses "multi-party system" with "two-party system", or that calls India a "one-party system" because one party has dominated for long periods, is a basic-recall failure. Use the three-system table as your first filter whenever a question asks you to classify a country's party system, and use the four-type ideological table only when a question specifically asks for ideological classification — most India-specific questions (the next twelve lessons) do not need it at all.

### Revision notes

1. A political party is a voluntary association seeking political power through constitutional means, claiming to serve the national interest.
2. Four ideological types: reactionary and conservative (right), liberal (centre), radical (left).
3. CPI/CPM are textbook left illustrations, Congress is the textbook centre illustration, BJP is the textbook right illustration — classification, not current commentary.
4. Three party systems: one-party (USSR-era), two-party (USA, Britain), multi-party (France, Switzerland, Italy).
5. A party is privately (voluntarily) formed; the State registers and classifies it afterward — it does not create it.
6. "Constitutional means" in the definition excludes organisations seeking power unconstitutionally.
7. India's own classification is taught in the next lesson as a multi-party system carrying eight named, India-specific features.
8. A party's professed "national interest" is its own unverified claim at the point of definition — registration does not test it, which is why later lessons on finance, candidate disclosure and criminalisation matter.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to call India's party system simply a "multi-party system" without further qualification, even though that classification is technically correct?

**Model answer:** "Multi-party system" correctly places India among countries with many competing parties rather than one or two dominant ones, but the bare label captures only the count of parties. It says nothing about the additional, India-specific features — a historical phase of one-party dominance, weak ideological differentiation, personality-centred organisation, parties built on caste/religion/language/region, the rise of regional parties, chronic factions and defections, and a historically weak Opposition — that the next lesson shows are necessary to describe India's party system accurately.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the three-way classification (one-party/two-party/multi-party) as a complete description of any country's party system; it only answers "how many parties compete", not "how do they behave".

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish a "party system" from a "political party", and explain why a representative democracy needs the former even though individual candidates could, in principle, contest elections alone.

**Model solution:** A political party is a single voluntary association of individuals with shared political views, organised to capture power constitutionally; a party system is the pattern — one-party, two-party or multi-party — that describes how many such associations genuinely compete for power in a country. Individual, party-less candidates could in principle contest elections, but a legislature composed entirely of free agents would lack a shared programme, a basis for collective responsibility, or a mechanism for forming and sustaining a government, since majorities would have to be assembled anew on every single issue. Parties solve this by aggregating scattered individual opinion into a manageable number of programmatic choices before the voter ever reaches the ballot, which is what allows a parliamentary system to produce a government with a coherent mandate rather than an unstable collection of individuals.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Correct definition of a political party — 2; correct definition of a party system with the three types named — 3; reasoned explanation of why aggregation is necessary for stable government — 4; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### Plain-language intuition

Picture the same photograph of Indian politics examined through eight separate lenses. Through the "scale" lens you see an enormous number of parties, a direct consequence of a continental, socially diverse country voting as one. Through the "dominance" lens you see one party (the Congress) occupying the centre of the picture for decades while still absorbing internal factions rather than suppressing them. Through the "ideology" lens most parties look alike: almost all profess democracy, secularism, socialism and Gandhism, and are separated in practice by the pursuit of power rather than by programme. Through the "personality" lens the party dissolves into its leader — parties are known by who leads them, and several carry a leader's name. Through the "traditional-factors" lens parties organise around religion, caste, language, culture or race rather than socio-economic programme alone. Through the "regional" lens you see parties that govern States outright and, since coalition politics began, bargain at the Centre. Through the "factions" lens the same party name keeps fracturing into two or three competing claimants. Through the "Opposition" lens, no single alternative force has matched the ruling party's organisation for long, continuous stretches. No single lens is the whole picture; a complete answer layers several of them.

### Technical doctrine: the eight features, with evidence

1. **Multi-party system at continental scale.** The size and social diversity of the country, universal adult franchise and the specific texture of Indian political competition together produce an unusually large number of parties, spanning left, centrist, right, communal and non-communal orientations; this is routinely described as giving India among the largest party counts of any democracy.
2. **One-dominant-party phase — the "Congress system".** Political analyst **Rajni Kothari** described early Indian politics as a **"one-party dominance system"** or **"Congress system"**: the Congress was hegemonic yet internally plural, functioning as an umbrella that absorbed factions and tolerated internal dissent rather than suppressing it outright, with real opposition surviving mainly at the margins. Its dominant position began declining unevenly from **1967**, with the rise of regional parties and later national competitors such as the **Janata Party (1977)**, **Janata Dal (1989)** and the **BJP (1991)**.
3. **Lack of clear ideology.** Apart from the BJP and the two communist parties (CPI, CPM), parties do not carry a clear-cut ideology; they lie close to one another in policy and programme, and almost every one of them advocates democracy, secularism, socialism and Gandhism. In practice a single consideration — the capture of power — guides even the self-described ideological parties, so politics has become issue-based rather than principled.
4. **Personality cult.** Parties are frequently organised around an eminent leader who becomes more important than the party or its ideology, so that a party is known by its leader rather than by its manifesto: the Congress's popularity owed much to Nehru, Indira Gandhi and Rajiv Gandhi; the AIADMK in Tamil Nadu and the TDP in Andhra Pradesh became identified with M.G. Ramachandran and N.T. Rama Rao respectively. Several party names (Biju Janata Dal, Lok Dal (A), Congress (I)) literally carry a leader's name — hence the remark that India has political personalities rather than political parties.
5. **Based on traditional factors.** Unlike Western parties organised chiefly around socio-economic programme, a large number of Indian parties form around religion, caste, language, culture or race, pursuing communal or sectional interests that can work against general public interest.
6. **Emergence of regional parties.** Regional parties have become the ruling parties in numerous States (treated in full in Lesson 13), and coalition government at the Centre has given them a national role as well; in the **1984** general election the **Telugu Desam Party emerged as the largest Opposition party in the Lok Sabha**. ⚠️ Treat that 1984 result as a dated illustration of how far a State-based party can rise nationally, not as a statement about any party's present standing.
7. **Factions and defections.** Factionalism, defections, splits, mergers, fragmentation and polarisation are a structural aspect of how Indian parties function, driven by the pursuit of power and material advantage; the practice gained greater currency after the **fourth general election (1967)**, causing instability at the Centre and in the States and producing parallel claimants such as two Janata Dals, two Telugu Desam Parties, two DMKs, two Communist Parties, two Congresses, three Akali Dals and three Muslim Leagues.
8. **Lack of an effective, united Opposition.** Despite the need for a strong Opposition to check the ruling party's autocratic tendencies and offer an alternative government, India has rarely sustained an organised, united and viable national Opposition for long; Opposition parties have often held mutually conflicting positions rather than functioning as a coherent alternative.

### Comparison: Congress system against ordinary one-party rule

| Question | Genuine one-party State (e.g., former USSR) | India's "Congress system" |
|---|---|---|
| Is competition legally permitted? | No — opposition is not allowed | Yes — opposition existed at every election |
| Is the dominant party internally plural? | Typically no | Yes — Kothari stressed its internal factions and consensus-building |
| Is dominance permanent? | By design, yes | No — it declined unevenly from 1967 onward |

Calling the "Congress system" a one-party State in the USSR sense is a common and serious conceptual error; Kothari's label describes a *competitive* system with one dominant, internally plural party, not the suppression of competition.

### Example and its limit

The pattern "two Janata Dals, two TDPs, two DMKs" is a vivid, examinable illustration of feature 7. Its limit: it is a static historical snapshot from a particular period; do not present it as describing the current organisational state of any named party without independently verifying today's status, since splits, mergers and renamings continue.

### UPSC application

A frequent Prelims trap asks you to match early post-Independence parties with their founding leaders — for example, the **Bharatiya Jana Sangh** with **Dr. Shyama Prasad Mukherjee**. Such questions test historical party formation rather than this topic's legal-regulatory content, but they draw on the same personality-and-traditional-factor features taught here. In Mains, examiners most often invoke features 2, 6 and 7 (dominance, regional parties and factions) as the backdrop to the centralisation-versus-autonomy debate developed fully in Lesson 14.

### Revision notes

1. The Indian party system has **eight** named characteristic features; the six-lens shorthand is only a recall grouping.
2. Feature 1 — a multi-party system reflecting continental scale, social diversity and universal adult franchise.
3. Feature 2 — the one-dominant-party phase: Rajni Kothari's "Congress system" describes a hegemonic but internally plural Congress, not a USSR-style absence of competition; it declined from 1967, with Janata (1977), Janata Dal (1989) and the BJP (1991) as rising national competitors.
4. Feature 3 — lack of clear ideology: apart from the BJP, CPI and CPM, parties resemble one another and are guided chiefly by power capture.
5. Feature 4 — personality cult: parties known by leaders rather than manifestos, several bearing a leader's name.
6. Feature 5 — based on traditional factors: religion, caste, language, culture and race rather than economic programme alone.
7. Feature 6 — emergence of regional parties: ruling parties in many States, nationally significant since coalition politics; the TDP was the largest Opposition party in the Lok Sabha after the 1984 election (a dated illustration).
8. Feature 7 — factions and defections: structural, gaining currency after the fourth general election (1967), producing two Janata Dals, two TDPs, two DMKs, two Communist Parties, two Congresses, three Akali Dals and three Muslim Leagues.
9. Feature 8 — lack of an effective, united national Opposition for sustained periods.
10. The "Congress system" is a characterisation of an early historical phase, not a permanent constitutional fact.
11. These eight features, not the bare "multi-party" label, are what an examiner expects in an India-specific answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student writes: "India's Congress system proves that India was once a one-party State." What is wrong with this claim?

**Model answer:** The claim conflates dominance with the absence of competition. Rajni Kothari's "Congress system" described a period in which the Congress was electorally dominant yet internally plural, absorbing factions and tolerating internal dissent, while genuine opposition parties continued to exist and contest elections, even if they rarely won power. A one-party State, by contrast, legally forecloses opposition altogether. India never forbade opposition parties; the Congress's dominance was a competitive outcome, not a constitutional or legal monopoly.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "dominant party" and "only party" as equivalent; dominance is a political/electoral condition, while a one-party system is a constitutional/legal arrangement that excludes competition altogether.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "The Congress system was less a monopoly than a managed plurality." Explain this characterisation of early Indian party politics.

**Model solution:** Rajni Kothari's description of early Indian politics as a "Congress system" or "one-party dominance system" captured a party that won most elections yet functioned internally as a coalition of factions representing diverse regional, caste and ideological tendencies. Rather than suppressing disagreement, the party absorbed it, allowing dissent to be expressed and partially accommodated within its own organisation while opposition parties continued to exist at the margins and occasionally won State-level power. This internal plurality is what distinguishes a "dominant party" from a "sole legal party": competition was permitted and real, even though one party consistently prevailed. The characterisation also explains the dominance's eventual, uneven decline from 1967 onward — as factions that could no longer be accommodated broke away, forming or strengthening regional and national rivals such as the Janata Party, rather than the dominant party collapsing all at once.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Correct statement of the Congress-system thesis with Kothari named — 3; internal plurality/faction-absorption explained — 3; distinction from a legally exclusive one-party State — 2; link to the uneven post-1967 decline — 1; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### Three departments, three files on the same organisation

Think of three separate government departments that each keep a different file on the same organisation. The Constitution's file is thin and late: it opens only in 1985, to record one fact — that this organisation's members can lose their seats for defying it — and is added to once more in 2003, only to record what else such a member forfeits. The statute's file is the "birth certificate" file: anyone forming a qualifying association can walk in, register under Section 29A, and receive a basic legal identity as a political party under the Act — a courtesy extended to new entrants as readily as to century-old ones. The ECI's file is the "performance report" file: it is the one place that actually asks how the party did at the polls, and it is the only file that hands out a reserved symbol. A learner who treats these as one undifferentiated "law on parties" will, under exam pressure, attribute registration to the Constitution or recognition to the statute — exactly the error the Prelims setter is testing for.

### Technical doctrine: what each layer actually says

1. **the constitutional layer — the Constitution, and only inside the defection regime.** The Constitution's original 1950 text did not mention political parties. The **Tenth Schedule**, inserted by the **Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985**, is the **first** constitutional text to use and define the term "political party", and it does so for one purpose: disqualifying a legislator who defies or abandons that party, operating through **Articles 102(2)** (Parliament) and **191(2)** (State Legislatures). It is not, however, the only constitutional place the expression now appears. The **Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003** inserted **Article 75(1B)**, **Article 164(1B)** and **Article 361B**, each of which speaks of a member "belonging to any political party" who stands disqualified under the Tenth Schedule, and bars that member from being appointed a Minister (at the Union and in the States respectively) or from holding a remunerative political post for the stated period. Every one of these constitutional uses sits inside the defection regime: none of them registers a party, recognises it, or regulates its finances or internal functioning.
2. **the statutory layer — an ordinary statute, for registration only.** **Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951** lets an association of Indian citizens apply to the Election Commission for registration as a political party, provided its memorandum or rules carry the statutory declaration that it **shall bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India as by law established, and to the principles of socialism, secularism and democracy, and would uphold the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India**. Registration gives the association statutory status under the RPA and connects it to later tax and disclosure consequences — but registration by itself does **not** create the association (it already existed as a voluntary body) and does **not** confer National or State recognition.
3. **the ECI-order layer — a subordinate ECI order, for recognition and symbols.** The **Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968** — issued by the Election Commission under **Article 324**, read with **Rules 5 and 10 of the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961** — is what actually classifies a registered party as **National**, **State** or **registered-unrecognised**, allots reserved or free symbols, and empowers the ECI to decide disputes between rival factions claiming a party's identity and symbol (Lesson 6). Because it is an executive order, not a statute or a constitutional provision, its criteria and thresholds can be, and have been, revised by the Commission itself from time to time: the fifth State-party condition was added in **2011**, and **paragraph 6C**, which governs continued recognition, was substituted in **2016** (Lesson 5).

Keep the separate dates and the separate purposes together.

### Is the familiar "the Constitution never mentions parties" claim wrong?

**Objection:** "If parties are regulated this thoroughly, why does the common claim that 'the Constitution does not mention political parties' persist, and is it simply wrong?"

**Reply:** The claim is accurate about the Constitution's **original** 1950 text, which was silent. It is not accurate about the Constitution as it now stands. The Tenth Schedule, added thirty-five years later, was the first place the expression appeared, and the 91st Amendment (2003) added three more locations — Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B — each using the phrase "belonging to any political party". What survives of the familiar claim is not the absence of the term but the **narrowness** of its use: every constitutional appearance sits inside the defection regime, and none of it registers, recognises or regulates a party as an organisation.

**Qualified residual:** This is exactly the ambiguity a well-drafted Prelims statement can exploit. Decide carefully whether a statement asks about the Constitution's **original** text (where the silence holds), about its **current** text (where both the Tenth Schedule and the 91st Amendment articles matter), or about party **regulation** generally (where the statutory and ECI-order layers also matter) before judging it true or false.

### Opening an answer with the three layers

This lesson is the background every Mains answer on "the constitutional and statutory basis of parties" must open with, and the control for any objective statement about what the constitutional text does or does not say. A two-line opening — "the original Constitution was silent on political parties; the Tenth Schedule (1985) and the 91st Amendment's Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B (2003) name them only inside the defection regime; registration is statutory (Section 29A) and recognition is by ECI order (1968)" — earns structural credit before any substantive argument begins.

### Rapid recall — the three layers

1. The original 1950 Constitution did not mention "political party".
2. The Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985) was the **first** constitutional text to use and define "political party", for defection alone, via Articles 102(2)/191(2).
3. The 91st Amendment (2003) added three further constitutional uses — Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B — barring a member disqualified for defection from ministerial office or a remunerative political post.
4. Every constitutional appearance of "political party" therefore lies inside the defection regime; none of it registers, recognises or funds a party.
5. ### Concept check

**Question:** A party is refused a reserved symbol. Which of the three legal layers governs that refusal, and why is it wrong to challenge it as a violation of the Tenth Schedule?

**Model answer:** Symbol allocation is governed by the ECI-order layer — the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, an ECI order issued under Article 324 — because it concerns recognition and symbols, not legislative defection. The Tenth Schedule (the constitutional layer) has no provision about symbols at all; it concerns only a sitting legislator's disqualification for voluntarily giving up party membership or defying the party whip. Framing a symbol refusal as a Tenth Schedule question confuses the layer that creates legislative consequences with the layer that creates electoral-administrative ones.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that because both the Tenth Schedule and the Symbols Order use the words "political party", they form a single unified legal code; they are enacted by different authorities, for different purposes, and can be amended independently of each other.

### Mains practice — many points of law, no single code

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "India regulates the political party at several different points of law, but not through a single code." Explain, with reference to the Constitution, statute and subordinate legislation.

**Model solution:** India's regulation of political parties is layered, not unified. The Constitution names "political party" only inside the defection regime: the Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985) disqualifies a legislator who defies the whip or gives up membership, through Articles 102(2) and 191(2), and the 91st Amendment (2003) added Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B, barring a member so disqualified from ministerial office or a remunerative political post. Nothing in the Constitution registers, recognises or funds a party. Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 — an ordinary statute — lets an association register with the Election Commission on declaring allegiance to the Constitution, socialism, secularism and democracy and undertaking to uphold India's sovereignty, unity and integrity. ### The learner's problem

If registering a party is this easy — an association, a declaration of allegiance, an application to the ECI — a natural question follows: can the Commission just as easily strike a party off the register once it stops contesting elections, turns inactive, or even acts unlawfully? The answer is a qualified, and frequently mistested, "no".

### Visual: the registration gate, and the missing exit

```text
AN ASSOCIATION OF INDIAN CITIZENS
        |
        v
APPLIES TO THE ELECTION COMMISSION UNDER SECTION 29A, RPA 1951
(furnishes a memorandum declaring true faith and allegiance to the

        v
```

*Caption: the gate into registration is wide and procedural; the gate back out is narrow and exceptional — this asymmetry is itself a standard UPSC criticism of the regime.*

### Plain-language intuition

Registering under Section 29A works like opening a bank account with a basic identity declaration: the bank (the ECI) checks your paperwork and your declared commitments, then opens the account. What the bank cannot easily do afterward is close the account merely because you stopped using it, or because you turned out to disagree with the bank's broader judgment of your conduct — it can act only within specific, narrow grounds written into the rules (fraud in opening the account, serious non-compliance, or using the account for an unlawful purpose). The Election Commission's registration power works the same way: generous and procedural going in, and narrow and exception-bound going back out.

### Technical doctrine: registration, its quasi-judicial character, and the bounded exit

- **Registration is quasi-judicial, not merely clerical.** The Commission does not simply record an application; it examines it and decides whether the statutory conditions — including the allegiance declaration — are met, which is why its registration function has been treated by the courts as quasi-judicial in character.
- **No general, merits-based deregistration power.** In ***Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002)**, the Supreme Court held that Section 29A's registration function is quasi-judicial, and it rejected a general, merits-based power for the Commission to deregister a party simply because it considers the party inactive, errant, or in breach of its declared principles in the Commission's own subsequent assessment.
- **Narrow exceptions remain available.** The judgment preserves bounded situations in which deregistration (or treating registration as void from the outset) is legally available — notably where registration was obtained by **fraud**, where there is clear **non-compliance** with the statutory conditions of registration, or where the party's **object is shown to be unlawful**. These are exceptions grounded in the integrity of the registration itself, not a general power to review a party's ongoing conduct or performance.
- **The practical consequence — a recognised statutory gap.** Because the ECI can register but cannot generally deregister on the merits, long-inactive or defunct "registered-unrecognised" parties can remain on the rolls indefinitely; this is one of the standard, examinable criticisms of the current regime (see Lesson 15's register notes) and is distinct from the loss of National or State recognition under the 1968 Order, where status turns on poll performance and, under paragraph 6C, survives one failed general election before it can be withdrawn (Lesson 5).

### Comparison: deregistration versus loss of recognition

| Question | Deregistration (Section 29A status) | Loss of National/State recognition (1968 Order) |
|---|---|---|
| What is lost? | Statutory existence as a registered party | Only the privileges of recognition (symbol, broadcast time, star campaigners) |
| How common is it? | Rare; confined to narrow exceptions after *Institute of Social Welfare* | Routine; status is reassessed on poll performance, though paragraph 6C lets a recognised party survive one failed general election |
| Legal basis | Section 29A and the limited exceptions recognised in case law | The 1968 Symbols Order's performance thresholds |
| Does the party cease to exist legally? | Only in the narrow exception cases | No — it simply becomes a registered-unrecognised party |

Keep these two consequences separate: almost every party that "loses" something in the news has lost recognition (routine, performance-based, the ECI-order layer), not registration (rare, exception-bound, the statutory layer).

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If the Commission can register thousands of parties but can almost never deregister them, is registration not a meaningless formality that invites abuse — for instance, by inactive parties used only for tax or funding advantages?"

**Reply:** The formality is deliberate, not accidental: a quasi-judicial registration gate that could also be reopened on a general merits review would let the Commission, an unelected body, pass continuing political judgment on which associations deserve to exist as parties — a far larger power than recognising electoral performance. The narrow fraud/non-compliance/unlawful-object exceptions preserve the Commission's ability to act against genuine abuse of the registration process itself.

**Qualified residual:** The reply justifies the narrowness of the exit, but it does not resolve the practical problem of thousands of dormant registered-unrecognised parties remaining on the rolls; that is a policy gap about which commentators disagree on the right fix (tightening the exceptions by statute, versus accepting the gap as the price of protecting genuine parties from arbitrary deregistration).

### UPSC application

A frequent close-option trap asks whether the Election Commission can "deregister a political party for inactivity" — the correct position is that it generally cannot, short of the narrow *Institute of Social Welfare* exceptions, even though it reassesses and revises **recognition** routinely on poll performance. Keep the registration/recognition distinction visibly separate in any answer that uses the word "deregister".

2. Registration confers statutory status under the RPA; it does not by itself confer National or State recognition.
3. The ECI's Section 29A function is quasi-judicial, as held in *Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002).
4. The same judgment rejected a general, merits-based power to deregister a party.
5. Narrow exceptions survive: fraud in registration, clear non-compliance with statutory conditions, or an unlawful object.
6. This produces a recognised statutory gap — long-defunct registered-unrecognised parties can remain on the rolls.
7. Deregistration (rare, Section 29A) must not be confused with loss of recognition (performance-based under the 1968 Order and subject to its continued-recognition rule, Lesson 5).

### Concept check

**Question:** A party has not contested any election for fifteen years. Can the Election Commission deregister it on that ground alone?

**Model answer:** Generally, no. After *Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002), the Commission's Section 29A function does not include a general, merits-based power to deregister a party for inactivity by itself; deregistration (or treating the registration as void) is confined to narrow situations such as fraud in the original registration, clear non-compliance with the statutory conditions, or an unlawful object. Prolonged electoral inactivity, without more, does not fall within these exceptions; the party may at most lose any recognition it held under the 1968 Symbols Order — and even that follows the Order's own continued-recognition rule rather than a single bad result (Lesson 5), a performance-based consequence quite distinct from deregistration.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating "losing recognition" with "being deregistered"; recognition is reviewed on poll performance but remains subject to paragraph 6C's one-election carry-over, while deregistration of an already-registered party remains legally rare.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Examine the statutory gap identified by the Supreme Court in *Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002) regarding deregistration of political parties.

**Model solution:** Section 29A, Representation of the People Act, 1951 empowers the Election Commission to register political parties through a quasi-judicial process, but does not expressly empower it to deregister a party for its subsequent conduct, inactivity or deviation from declared principles. In *Institute of Social Welfare* (2002), the Supreme Court confirmed registration's quasi-judicial character while declining to read in a broad deregistration power, preserving only narrow exceptions: registration obtained by fraud, clear non-compliance with statutory conditions, or an unlawful party object. Consequently, defunct or dormant registered-unrecognised parties can remain on the Commission's rolls indefinitely, since mere inactivity fits none of these exceptions. This gap is distinct from the loss of National or State recognition, which the Commission reassesses on poll performance under the 1968 Symbols Order and subject to that Order's continued-recognition rule, and it remains unresolved in reform discussion on cleaning up the register of parties.

### "How many national parties are there?" is a trap

"How many national parties does India have?" looks like a one-fact recall question. It is actually a trap, because the honest, examinable answer is "it depends on which general election you are counting from, and the number has changed almost every election since 1952." What an examiner actually wants is the **threshold structure** — the exact conditions that turn a registered party into a National or State party — not a memorised headcount that will be stale within one election cycle.

### Visual: the three-tier ladder of status


*Caption: status is climbed by poll performance alone — but it is not lost on a single bad election. This is why no headcount should be memorised as permanent, and why a party can lawfully hold recognition on a lapsed performance for a full electoral cycle.*

### Technical doctrine: the exact thresholds

A party is recognised as a **National party** if it satisfies **any one** of these three conditions:

1. It secures **6% of valid votes** polled in **any four or more States** (at a general election to the Lok Sabha or to a Legislative Assembly), **and**, in addition, wins **4 Lok Sabha seats** from any State or States; **or**
2. It wins **2% of Lok Sabha seats** at a general election, with these winning candidates elected from **3 different States**; **or**
3. It is recognised as a **State party in four States**.

A party is recognised as a **State party** within a given State if it satisfies **any one** of these five conditions:

1. **6% of valid votes** in the State (Assembly election) **and** wins **2 Assembly seats**; **or**
2. **6% of valid votes** (Lok Sabha election from that State) **and** wins **1 Lok Sabha seat** from that State; **or**
3. **3% of Assembly seats**, or **3 seats**, whichever is more; **or**
4. **1 Lok Sabha seat for every 25 seats** (or any fraction) allotted to the State; **or**
5. **8% of the total valid votes** polled in the State (Lok Sabha or Assembly election) — **this fifth condition was added in 2011**.

A party meeting none of these is simply a **registered-unrecognised party** — still a lawful political party under Section 29A, but without recognition's privileges (Lesson 6).

**Keeping the status — the continued-recognition rule.** Recognition is not re-earned from scratch at each election. **Paragraph 6C** of the 1968 Order, as **substituted in 2016**, provides that where a party is recognised as a National or State party, the question whether it shall continue to be so recognised is determined **after the next general election**; and if it fails to fulfil the conditions at that election, it **shall nevertheless continue to be so recognised**, the question being reopened only **after the subsequent general election**. One failed general election therefore does not demote a recognised party; de-recognition follows only on failure at a second, later election. The same paragraph is why the Order must be described as revised periodically rather than as last amended on any single date — the fifth State-party condition was added in 2011, and paragraph 6C itself was substituted in 2016.

|---|---|---|
| National party | Any one of 3 conditions above | "All-India party" |
| State party | Any one of 5 conditions above, within a State | "Regional party" |
| Registered-unrecognised | Fails every threshold | — |

State needs **6% + 2 Assembly seats**, **or** 6% + 1 LS seat, **or** 3% (or 3) Assembly seats, **or** 1 LS seat per 25 allotted, **or** 8% of the State's valid votes (2011 addition). And once recognised, paragraph 6C lets a party survive **one** failed general election before the question of de-recognition is reopened.

### Recognition is a report card, not a title deed

Treat recognition as a report card rather than a title a party earns once and keeps forever — but a report card that allows one carry-over. A party that cleared the National threshold decades ago and has since collapsed electorally is tested again at the next general election; if it misses, paragraph 6C keeps it recognised for that cycle, and only a second failure at the following general election costs it the status. Conversely, a State party that keeps expanding can climb into the National tier by crossing the "State party in four States" route without ever directly meeting the vote-share or seat conditions itself.

The recurring Prelims trap is treating any one of these conditions as the **only** route to recognition ("needs 6% in all states", "always needs a Lok Sabha seat"); the correct framing is always "any **one** of" the listed conditions. A second, less-noticed trap assumes that recognition evaporates the moment a party misses the thresholds once — paragraph 6C says otherwise. ### Concept check

**Question:** At the last general elections a party polled 6% of the valid votes and won 2 Assembly seats in the Assembly election of each of three States, and also won one Lok Sabha seat from each of those same three States, though its Lok Sabha vote share in each stayed below 6%. In no other State did it meet any State-party condition. Can it claim National party status?

**Model answer:** No — all three National routes fail on these facts. Route 1 requires 6% of valid votes in **four or more** States together with 4 Lok Sabha seats: the 6% figure is reached in three States, not four, and the party holds 3 Lok Sabha seats, not 4. Route 2 requires 2% of Lok Sabha seats won from three different States: the three-State spread is satisfied, but 2% of the Lok Sabha's 543 elected seats is about eleven seats, and 3 seats falls far short. Route 3 requires State-party recognition in four States: the party does qualify as a State party in each of the three States, under the first State condition (6% of Assembly votes plus 2 Assembly seats), but three recognitions are one short of the four the route demands. It is therefore a State party in three States and not a National party.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that performance "spread thinly" across several States adds up to National recognition; each route sets its own exact combination of vote share, seat count and State count, and falling one State or one seat short defeats that route completely.

### Mains practice — criteria as administrative policy

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the criteria the Election Commission uses to recognise a party as a National party, and why these criteria should be treated as administrative policy rather than fixed law.

**Model solution:** A registered party qualifies as a National party on satisfying any one of three conditions: 6% of valid votes in four or more States at a Lok Sabha or Assembly election plus 4 Lok Sabha seats; 2% of Lok Sabha seats with winners from three different States; or State-party recognition in four States. These criteria are not fixed law. They are laid down by the Election Commission itself through the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, under Article 324, and revised by it from time to time: a fifth State-party condition was added in 2011, and paragraph 6C — which lets a recognised party survive one failed general election before de-recognition is reconsidered — was substituted in 2016. ### Plain-language intuition

Think of the reserved symbol as a long-held trademark: a recognised party's symbol belongs to it exclusively across the area of its recognition, which is precisely why, when a party splits, both halves fight so hard over who gets to keep it — losing the symbol can mean losing instant voter recognition built over decades. A registered-unrecognised party, by contrast, is choosing from a shared, unprotected shelf of "free" symbols available to anyone in that position, with no guarantee the same symbol will be available next time.

### Technical doctrine: privileges, the symbol test, and its two judicial checks

- **Privileges of recognition.** A recognised party — National or State — is allotted a **symbol exclusively reserved** for its use (nationally, for a National party; within the recognised State(s), for a State party). It needs only **one proposer** for a nomination, gets **broadcast time** on state-owned television and radio, and has **access to electoral rolls**. A registered-unrecognised party instead selects from a list of **free symbols** available to all such parties and independents.
- **Star campaigners.** Under the Explanation to **Section 77 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951**, a **recognised political party** may have **40 "star campaigners"**, while **another (unrecognised) political party** may have **20**; the travel expenses of these campaigners are **not** counted in the election expenditure of the party's own candidates — a meaningful financial advantage.
- **The *Sadiq Ali* test of majority.** When a party splits and two factions each claim to be the "real" party entitled to its name and symbol, the dispute is decided by the **Election Commission under the 1968 Order**, not in the first instance by a court. In ***Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India* (1972)**, the Supreme Court sustained this ECI power, applying what is taught as the **"test of majority"** — assessing which faction commands majority support across both the party's **organisational** wing and its **legislative** wing, rather than treating either alone as decisive.
- **The 2026 current test of the whole scheme.** On **9 January 2026**, a Bench of the **Delhi High Court** (Justices **Nitin Sambre** and **Anish Dayal**) dismissed a petition by a registered party challenging the **validity of the 1968 Symbols Order itself** and the Commission's power to classify parties as National or State. The petitioner argued that recognised parties enjoy symbol advantages well before scrutiny while a "newly-born" party must wait until after scrutiny for a symbol, and that this amounted to discrimination. The Court held that the issues had already been settled by the Supreme Court, that **allotment of an election symbol cannot be claimed as a fundamental right**, and that the 1968 Order is not without statutory power nor discriminatory — recognised parties' privileges flow from qualifications the law itself permits and that the petitioner had not met.

| Feature | *Sadiq Ali v. ECI* (1972) | Delhi High Court, 9 January 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| What was challenged | The ECI's power to decide which faction is the "real" party in a split | The validity of the 1968 Order itself, and the ECI's recognition power generally |
| Court's holding | Upheld the ECI's quasi-judicial symbol-dispute jurisdiction; introduced the test of majority | Upheld the 1968 Order's statutory validity; held a symbol is not a fundamental right; found no discrimination |
| Scope | A specific faction dispute | A general, structural challenge to the recognition/symbol scheme |
| What it settles for an answer | Who decides a split, and how | That courts continue to treat the scheme itself as valid and non-discriminatory |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If a brand-new party only gets its symbol after scrutiny while an established, recognised party already holds a reserved symbol for every election, is the scheme not structurally unfair to new entrants, exactly as the 2026 petitioner argued?"

**Reply:** The Delhi High Court's answer was that this is not unfairness but **differentiation by qualification**: recognised status is earned through verified electoral performance against public thresholds that any party, in principle, can meet over time, and a symbol has never been held to be a fundamental right that every contestant is owed on identical terms from day one.

**Qualified residual:** The reply defends the scheme's **legality**, not necessarily its **political desirability**; the practical head-start recognised parties enjoy in voter recall remains a genuine, if lawful, competitive advantage that newer or smaller parties continue to raise as a fairness concern in political, if not legal, debate.

### Plain-language intuition

Think of party finance regulation as a tap with a filter and a meter, rather than a sealed pipe. Most lawful sources — Indian individuals, Indian companies through the Companies Act route, and electoral trusts — can flow through the tap; a foreign-source filter blocks one category outright under the FCRA. ### Technical doctrine: sources, disclosure, and the foreign bar

- **Permitted contribution routes.** Companies may donate to political parties under **Section 182 of the Companies Act, 2013**, and **electoral trusts** are a separately recognised, permitted channel for routing donations to parties. **Section 29B of the RPA, 1951** confirms that parties may accept contributions, subject to the statutory foreign-source prohibition.
- **The disclosure threshold.** **Section 29C, RPA 1951** requires the party's treasurer or an authorised person to report to the Election Commission any contribution **exceeding a statutory threshold** (commonly cited as ₹20,000) received from any person or company, before the due date for filing the party's income-tax return; failure to report carries tax-relief consequences for the party. This reporting threshold is a distinct figure from cash-receipt limits under tax law and from candidate-level election-expenditure ceilings — the three must never be interchanged in an answer, and the exact current rupee figure should be verified before being quoted, since such thresholds are revisable by statute or rules.
- **The foreign-contribution bar.** The **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 (FCRA)** prohibits political parties and specified political actors from accepting a **"foreign contribution"** as the Act currently defines that term; because the statutory definition of "foreign source" itself has been subject to legislative change over time, an answer should apply the Act's **current** definition rather than assume every Indian-incorporated company with foreign shareholding is automatically a foreign source.
- **A dated loosening, later reversed.** The **Finance Act, 2017** had removed the Companies Act's earlier **profit-linked ceiling** on how much a company could donate, and the requirement to name the recipient party in company accounts. As the next lesson shows, the 2024 Electoral Bonds judgment struck down those 2017 amendments, which **revived the pre-2017 profit-linked ceiling and the recipient-naming requirement**; the Court itself imposed no new cap of its own.

### Whose right was actually violated?

Imagine a ballot box that is secret from other voters but is, unknown to those voters, logged by name in a ledger the bank keeps. That is roughly the structure the Electoral Bond Scheme created for donations: anonymous to the public and to rival parties, but not beyond institutional traceability. The Supreme Court's 2024 judgment reframed the entire debate: the question was not "does the donor deserve privacy from competitors", but "does the voter have a right to know who funds the party they are about to vote for" — and the Court held that right outweighs the donor's claimed interest in anonymity from the public.

### Technical doctrine: the scheme, the verdict, and its limits

- **The scheme's design (2018).** Under the Electoral Bond Scheme, a donor purchased a bond from specified State Bank of India branches and handed it to a political party, which then encashed it; the donor's identity was **not** disclosed to the public, to rival parties, or on the face of the party's own accounts.
- **The 2017 enabling changes.** The **Finance Act, 2017** had amended the **Representation of the People Act**, the **Companies Act** and the **Income-tax Act** to make the scheme possible — including, as Lesson 8 noted, removing the Companies Act's profit-linked donation cap and the requirement to name the recipient party.
- **The verdict (exact proposition).** In ***Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India*** (**February 2024**, five-judge Constitution Bench), the Supreme Court **struck down the Electoral Bond Scheme, 2018 and the 2017 enabling amendments** as **unconstitutional for violating the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a)**, holding that unlimited, anonymous corporate funding is **not saved by donor privacy**, and **directed the State Bank of India to disclose** bond purchaser and encashment data to the Election Commission.
- **What the verdict restored, and what it left open.** Striking down the 2017 amendments revived the **pre-2017 Companies Act position** — the profit-linked ceiling on company donations and the requirement to name the recipient party in company accounts — re-anchoring campaign-finance law in the voter's informed choice rather than donor confidentiality. The Court itself, however, **imposed no new cap** and enacted **no comprehensive disclosure code**: striking down one instrument closed one anonymity route without resolving the wider funding-transparency debate.
- **Reform proposals that predate the verdict.** The **Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998)** had recommended partial **State funding** of elections in kind as an alternative route to reducing private-money dependence; the **Law Commission's 255th Report (2015)** had proposed tighter disclosure and donation controls. Neither has been enacted as a complete scheme; both remain recommendations, not law.

### Was it anonymity, or only weak disclosure?

**Objection:** "If donor identity was only hidden from the public and not from the State (via the banking system), was the scheme really 'anonymous', or was the real problem simply insufficient public disclosure rather than true anonymity?"

**Reply:** The Court's reasoning focused precisely on this point: the relevant constitutional injury was to the **voter**, who had no way to know who funded a party before casting a vote, regardless of what records existed elsewhere in the banking system. Selective opacity toward the electorate — even where some institutional trace remained — was enough to violate Article 19(1)(a), because the right at stake belonged to the voter, not to the donor or the bank.

**Qualified residual:** This is a sound answer to "was the scheme constitutional", but it is not, by itself, a complete answer to "is party finance now transparent" — the judgment removed one specific instrument of selective opacity and revived the pre-2017 corporate ceiling by striking down the amendment that had abolished it, but the Court designed no new cap and no ongoing real-time disclosure regime for every funding route that remains lawful.

### Using the exact 2024 holding in an answer

Mains questions on "money power" or "opacity" in Indian democracy should be answered with this lesson's precise sequence — 2017 enabling changes, the 2018 scheme's actual mechanics, the exact 2024 holding and its Article 19(1)(a) ground, the SBI disclosure direction, and the explicit qualification that the verdict is a floor, not a ceiling, on transparency. A frequent Prelims/Mains confusion is citing the electoral-bonds case and *Lily Thomas* (2013, Lesson 10) interchangeably; they concern entirely different problems — anonymous funding versus criminal candidature — and must be kept separate.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did the Supreme Court frame the Electoral Bond Scheme as a violation of the voter's rights rather than as a question of donor privacy?

**Model answer:** The Court located the constitutional harm in Article 19(1)(a)'s protection of the voter's right to information, reasoning that a citizen's ability to make an informed electoral choice depends on knowing who funds the parties and candidates competing for that vote. On this framing, the donor's interest in anonymity from the public had to be weighed against, and could not override, the voter's right to know — a reframing that let the Court strike down unlimited anonymous corporate funding without needing to find that donor privacy itself carried no weight at all, merely that it could not defeat the voter's informational right at this scale.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the judgment was primarily about preventing corruption or quid-pro-quo arrangements as such; its stated constitutional ground is the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a), not an anti-corruption provision.

### Mains practice — one route closed, one gap open

The statement is substantially correct. ### Two decades of disclosure, and the paradox it leaves

Indian courts have, since 2002, repeatedly forced more information about candidates' criminal records into the open. If disclosure has steadily increased for over two decades, why does the share of candidates and legislators facing criminal cases not seem to have fallen in proportion? This lesson supplies the legal sequence and the honest answer to that apparent paradox.

### Two levers: more labels, or a recall

Picture two separate levers a reformer can pull. One lever adds more labels to the package — disclosure — so a shopper (the voter) can read the ingredients before buying. The other lever recalls the product after a defect is proven — disqualification — but only once a court has actually convicted, which can take years. Indian law has pulled the "more labels" lever hard and repeatedly since 2002; it has pulled the "recall" lever only once decisively, in 2013, and even then only for a conviction that has already occurred. Nothing yet pulls a third lever — refusing the product at the factory gate merely because a defect is alleged but unproven — which is precisely the gap the Vohra Committee flagged and which pending-charge disqualification proposals continue to target.

### Technical doctrine: the two tracks, in exact sequence

- **Disclosure track.** ***Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms* (2002)** and ***PUCL v. Union of India* (2003)** established the voter's **right to know** and created the **candidate-affidavit** requirement, compelling disclosure of criminal, financial and educational background before nomination. ***Rambabu Singh Thakur v. Sunil Arora* (2020)** went further, directing **political parties themselves** to publish, on their own websites, their candidates' criminal antecedents and the **reasons for selecting them** over candidates without a criminal background — shifting part of the accountability burden onto the party, not only the individual candidate.
- **Disqualification track.** ***Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2013)** struck down **Section 8(4) of the RPA, 1951**, which had previously let a sitting MP or MLA continue in office pending appeal even after a disqualifying conviction. After *Lily Thomas*, a sitting member convicted of a disqualifying offence is disqualified **immediately**, aligning the position of sitting members with that of ordinary candidates, who were never protected by such a shield.
- **Why disclosure has not reduced the share of candidates with criminal cases.** Publicity leaves the actual choice with voters and with parties exercising their own candidate-selection discretion; where a candidate's "winnability" (ability to win, regardless of record) is the dominant selection criterion, disclosure alone creates no automatic cost to selecting that candidate. *Rambabu Singh Thakur*'s requirement that parties publish their **reasons** for selecting such candidates was designed to raise exactly this reputational cost, but it remains a disclosure duty, not a bar on selection.
- **Why disqualification still under-bites.** Disqualification under *Lily Thomas* operates only **after conviction**, and criminal trials, especially against sitting legislators, are frequently slow; a candidate or sitting member facing even serious pending charges is not disqualified merely on that basis. This is the standard basis for demands for special fast-track courts for cases against legislators and for a more extensive, pre-conviction disqualification regime — demands that remain policy proposals, not enacted law.
- **The documented scale of the problem.** The **Vohra Committee Report (1993)** officially recorded the existence of a **criminal–politician–bureaucrat nexus**; it remains the standard citation for official acknowledgement of the **scale** of the criminalisation problem, even though its full findings were never published and it should be cited as an acknowledgement, not as a data source for current figures.

### Is disclosure merely symbolic?

**Objection:** "If disclosure alone cannot reduce criminalisation, is it not a largely symbolic reform that has achieved little beyond generating paperwork?"

**Reply:** Disclosure is not symbolic in its own terms — it genuinely gives voters and civil-society monitors information that was previously unavailable, and *Rambabu Singh Thakur*'s reasons-for-selection requirement specifically targets the party's own decision-making, not merely the candidate's paperwork. Its limitation is one of **incentive structure**, not of information quality: information does not automatically outweigh a party's electoral calculation that a candidate is winnable.

**Qualified residual:** The honest verdict is that disclosure has succeeded as a transparency reform while failing, on its own, as a deterrence reform; closing that gap requires either a pre-conviction disqualification mechanism or a structural change to how parties weigh "winnability" against clean candidature — both of which remain contested policy questions rather than settled law.

### Citing the right case for the right problem

A frequent close-option error is to cite *Lily Thomas* when a question actually concerns disclosure (it does not create any disclosure requirement; it removes a conviction-survival shield) or to cite the 2002/2003 affidavit cases when a question actually concerns disqualification (they do not disqualify anyone; they require disclosure only). Keep the two tracks, and their four distinct dates, cleanly separated in any answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate facing serious, pending (unconvicted) criminal charges contests and wins an election. Can this candidate be disqualified from the legislature on the basis of those pending charges alone?

**Model answer:** No. Under the current legal position taught in this lesson, disqualification under *Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2013) operates only once a sitting member is **convicted** of an offence carrying a disqualifying sentence; mere pending, unconvicted charges — however serious — do not themselves disqualify a candidate or a sitting legislator. The disclosure track (the 2002/2003 affidavit requirement and the 2020 *Rambabu Singh Thakur* party-publication duty) only requires that such pending charges, and the party's reasons for selecting the candidate despite them, be made public; it creates no bar on contesting or on taking the seat.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that "disclosure of criminal antecedents" and "disqualification for criminal antecedents" are the same legal consequence; disclosure informs the voter and the public, while disqualification removes a member from office, and only the latter currently requires an actual conviction.

### Mains practice — disclosure without deterrence

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "Two decades of judicially mandated disclosure have not visibly reduced the number of candidates with criminal cases contesting elections." Analyse the reasons for this gap between disclosure and deterrence.

**Model solution:** Since *Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms* (2002) and *PUCL v. Union of India* (2003) established the voter's right to know, candidates have been required to disclose criminal, financial and educational antecedents on affidavit, and since *Rambabu Singh Thakur v. Sunil Arora* (2020), parties themselves must publish such antecedents and their reasons for selecting these candidates. Despite more than two decades of expanding disclosure, the share of candidates and sitting legislators facing criminal cases has not fallen proportionately. The reason lies in the gap between information and incentive: disclosure leaves the decision entirely with voters and with the party's own candidate-selection process, and where "winnability" dominates that process, a clean record creates no automatic electoral advantage over a criminal record with strong local support. The complementary disqualification track is narrower still: *Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2013) struck down the appeal-pending shield of Section 8(4), RPA 1951, so a sitting member is disqualified immediately on conviction, but this operates only after conviction, and trials against sitting legislators are frequently slow, so even serious pending charges disqualify no one. The Vohra Committee (1993) had already officially acknowledged the scale of a criminal-politician-bureaucrat nexus. Closing the gap would require either a pre-conviction disqualification mechanism, time-bound trials for legislators, or a change in party incentives around winnability — all of which remain contested reform proposals rather than enacted law.

Every earlier lesson showed a layer of law reaching into some part of a party's life — registration, recognition, finance, candidate disclosure. ### Plain-language intuition

Picture a building with a strict, well-staffed security desk at every entrance — checking registration papers, recognition credentials, funding sources, and candidates' backgrounds — but with no rules at all governing who gets to sit in the corner office or how promotions are decided once you are inside. ### Technical doctrine: what is, and is not, regulated internally

- **No statutory code compelling internal elections.** There is **no statute** requiring a political party to hold genuine internal elections for its own leadership or to follow a prescribed, transparent process for selecting candidates. Parties do file organisational-election particulars with the Election Commission, but compliance with this filing requirement is widely treated as **formal** rather than a substantive guarantee of internal democracy.
- **The high-command / dynastic pattern.** In the absence of a binding code, leadership succession and ticket distribution in many parties remain discretionary, and a **high-command** model — concentrated decision-making at the top, sometimes along dynastic lines — dominates candidate selection and internal promotion in practice. This is an **analytical observation about a recurring pattern**, not a claim that it describes every party identically or permanently.
- **The RTI debate.** The **Central Information Commission**, in **2013**, held that **six national parties** qualified as **"public authorities"** under the Right to Information Act — meaning they would, on that finding, be obliged to respond to RTI requests like a government body. This finding has **not been complied with** by the parties concerned, and the underlying question of whether political parties should be brought under the RTI Act remains a live, unresolved policy debate rather than settled, enforced law.
- **Reform proposals, all unenacted.** The **Law Commission's 170th Report (1999)** and the Election Commission's own recurring submissions have proposed legislating inner-party democracy; the **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002)** separately proposed regulating parties' internal functioning and finances by law. None of these proposals has been enacted as a comprehensive internal-democracy code; they remain recommendations.

**Objection:** "If the CIC itself has held that national parties are public authorities under the RTI Act, why does the absence of compliance not simply count as unlawful defiance that courts should enforce?"

**Qualified residual:** This does not mean the CIC's finding is irrelevant — it remains the standard citation establishing that a credible official body has already concluded that parties meet the public-authority test on the facts it examined; the open question is one of enforcement and political will, not of whether the argument for coverage has been made.

### Plain-language intuition

Think of the party system's shape as a pendulum rather than a one-way escalator. It swung from one dominant, internally plural party, through genuine multi-party fragmentation, into an extended period where no single party could govern alone and smaller, often regional, parties held real bargaining power, and then swung back toward single-party dominance — only to swing toward coalition dependence again after a single subsequent election. Treating any one phase as "where India's party system has ended up" misreads a system that keeps moving.

### Technical doctrine: the four phases, with their analytical logic

| Phase | Character | What changed, and why |
|---|---|---|
| **One-party dominance (1952-1967)** | Rajni Kothari's "Congress system" | Congress was hegemonic yet internally plural — an umbrella absorbing factions and functioning through consensus with margins of pressure; opposition survived at the edges rather than being suppressed. |
| **Fragmentation and first coalitions (1967-1989)** | Congress loses many States in 1967; Janata forms government in 1977 | Social coalitions that had sustained Congress broke apart; regional and backward-class political assertion rose; the dominance model ended in practice even before the Centre itself changed hands durably. |
| **Coalition era (1989-2014)** | National Front / United Front / NDA / UPA governments | No single party commanded a Lok Sabha majority; regional parties became pivotal to government formation, extracting fiscal and portfolio concessions — arguably federalism's most actively bargained phase. |
| **Dominance and renewed coalition dependence (2014 onward)** | Single-party majorities in 2014 and 2019; a coalition-supported government after the 2024 general election | Central dominance has coexisted with persistently strong State parties, and the 2024 result restored coalition dependence at the Centre — a reminder that these labels are periodised analysis, not fixed constitutional facts. |

> 🔑 **Four phases, four one-word anchors:** **Dominance** (1952-67) → **Fragmentation** (1967-89) → **Coalition** (1989-2014) → **Dominance-with-renewed-coalition-dependence** (2014 onward).

### Comparison: what the phases explain about the centralisation debate

The next lesson's central PYQ asks whether national parties favour centralisation while regional parties favour autonomy. This lesson's phases supply the causal mechanism for that debate: dominance and single-party majorities (Phases 1 and 4's majority years) pull political behaviour toward the Centre, while fragmentation and genuine coalition dependence (Phases 2, 3, and 4's post-2024 turn) pull it toward the States, because a Union government that needs regional allies to survive must bargain for their support rather than dictate to them.

### Example and its limit

The 1989-2014 coalition era is the strongest textbook illustration of regional pivotality — National Front, United Front, NDA and UPA governments all depended on regional-party support to survive, and extracted real policy concessions in return. **Limit:** do not present this period as "when Indian federalism finally worked" in an unqualified sense; it also produced documented costs — frequent government instability, portfolio-driven coalition bargaining, and policy incoherence across coalition partners — which the Advanced criticism of regionalisation (Lesson 13) develops further.

### UPSC application

"Analyse the evolution of the party system" or "from one-party dominance to coalition politics" questions require phase-by-phase causal explanation, not a bare list of years; the standard trap is writing only two phases (dominance, then coalition) and missing the 2014-onward reversal and its own 2024 qualification. State the current phase as a **characterisation**, not as settled constitutional fact.

1. Phase 1 (1952-1967): one-party dominance, the "Congress system" — hegemonic but internally plural.
2. Phase 2 (1967-1989): fragmentation and first coalitions, following Congress's 1967 State losses and the 1977 Janata government.
3. Phase 3 (1989-2014): coalition era — National Front, United Front, NDA, UPA; regional parties pivotal.
4. Phase 4 (2014 onward): single-party majorities in 2014/2019, with coalition dependence restored after the 2024 general election.
5. Each transition has a distinct cause: social-coalition breakdown (Phase 1→2), electoral arithmetic denying any single majority (Phase 2→3), and its reversal and re-reversal (Phase 3→4→post-2024).
6. Phase labels are analytical characterisations, not constitutional facts — mark them as analysis in an answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student writes: "India's party system evolved from one-party dominance to coalition politics, and has remained a coalition system ever since 1989." What is wrong with this claim?

**Model answer:** The claim stops the story in 1989 and misses two further movements: single-party Lok Sabha majorities returned in 2014 and 2019, interrupting the coalition era, before a coalition-supported government after the 2024 general election restored coalition dependence once again. ### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Trace the evolution of the Indian party system since 1952, explaining the cause of each major transition.

**Model solution:** India's party system has moved through four analytically distinct phases. From 1952 to 1967, the Congress enjoyed what Rajni Kothari termed a "one-party dominance system" or "Congress system" — hegemonic in electoral terms yet internally plural, absorbing factional disagreement rather than suppressing it, while opposition parties survived at the margins. This dominance broke down from 1967, when Congress lost power in several States and social coalitions that had sustained it fragmented along regional and backward-class lines, producing the Janata government of 1977 — a second phase of fragmentation and intermittent coalition. From 1989 to 2014, no single party commanded a Lok Sabha majority, and successive National Front, United Front, NDA and UPA governments depended on regional parties for survival, making this the system's most actively bargained, federalism-sensitive phase, with regional parties extracting real fiscal and portfolio concessions. From 2014, single-party Lok Sabha majorities returned in two successive general elections, suggesting a fourth phase of renewed central dominance — but the 2024 general election restored coalition dependence at the Centre, showing that even this phase is provisional rather than a settled end-state. Each transition therefore has its own distinct cause: social-coalition breakdown drove the first transition, electoral arithmetic denying any majority drove the second, and shifting electoral verdicts have driven the oscillation since, confirming that the party system's shape is a product of changing political and social conditions rather than a one-way, completed evolution.

### The local business and the national chain

Treat a regional party the way you would treat any specialised local business competing against national chains: it survives and grows by meeting a local demand the national competitor under-serves — a regional language, a regional grievance, a regional leader's local standing. That local focus is simultaneously its greatest strength (closeness to local needs, responsiveness, governance tailored to the region) and its characteristic weakness (an incentive to prioritise the region even when national coordination would serve citizens better, and a tendency to lean on sub-national identity in ways that can also fuel division).

### Technical doctrine: classification, causes, role and dysfunctions

**Features of a regional party.** A regional party typically (i) operates within a particular State or specific region, with its electoral base limited there; (ii) articulates regional interests and identifies with a particular cultural, religious, linguistic or ethnic group; (iii) is primarily concerned with local grievances or primordial demands based on language, caste, community or region; (iv) focuses on regional issues and aims to capture power at the State level, without seeking to control the central government directly; and (v) carries a political desire for greater regional autonomy within the Indian Union.

**Four categories, by origin:**

1. Parties based on **regional culture or ethnicity** — illustrated by the Shiromani Akali Dal, the National Conference, the DMK, the AIADMK, the Telugu Desam, the Shiv Sena, the Asom Gana Parishad, the Mizo National Front and the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha.
2. Parties with an **all-India outlook but no national electoral base** — illustrated by the Indian National Lok Dal, the All-India Forward Bloc, the Revolutionary Socialist Party, the Samajwadi Party and the Nationalist Congress Party.
3. Parties formed by a **split in a national party** — illustrated by the Bangla Congress, the Bharatiya Kranti Dal, the Utkal Congress, the Kerala Congress, the Biju Janata Dal, the Rashtriya Janata Dal, the Samata Party, the Trinamool Congress and the YSR Congress.
4. **Personalised parties**, formed by individual leaders on the strength of a charismatic personality and typically short-lived — illustrated by the Lok Janshakti Party, the Haryana Vikas Party, the Himachal Vikas Congress and Congress (J).

**Twelve enumerated causes of rise** (grouped, not a rote list to recite in full in a short answer): the cultural and ethnic pluralism of Indian society; economic disparities and regional imbalances in development; the desire of certain sections or areas to maintain a separate identity for historical reasons; the self-interest of the deposed Maharajas and dispossessed Zamindars; the failure of national politics to meet regional aspirations; the reorganisation of States on the basis of language; the charismatic personality of regional leaders; factional fights within the larger parties; the centralising tendencies of the Congress party; the absence of a strong Opposition party at the central level; the role of caste and religion in the political process; and alienation and discontentment among tribal groups.

**Ten points on the positive role** regional parties have played:

1. They have provided better governance and a stable government at the regional level.
2. They have posed a challenge to the one-party dominant system and led to the decline of Congress domination.
3. They have made a strong impact on the nature and course of Centre-State relations: the tension areas, and the demand for greater autonomy, made the central leadership more responsive to the needs of regional actors.
4. They have made politics **more competitive** and popular participation in the political process more extensive **at the grass roots**.
5. They have **widened the choice for voters** in both parliamentary and Assembly elections, allowing a voter to support a party that aims to promote the interest of that State or region.
6. They have increased the political consciousness of the people and their interest in politics, bringing local and regional issues into focus.
7. They have provided a check against the dictatorial tendencies of the central government, opposing the ruling party at the Centre on particular issues and forcing a more reasonable approach to conflict resolution.
8. They have contributed significantly to the **successful functioning of parliamentary democracy**: in a parliamentary democracy the minority must have its say and the majority must have its way, and regional parties have played exactly that role by being ruling parties in some States while sitting in Opposition at the Centre.
9. They have succeeded in **exposing the partisan role of Governors** in appointing and dismissing Chief Ministers, in issuing ordinances, and in reserving Bills for the consideration of the President.
10. Since the dawn of the era of coalition politics, they have assumed an important role in national politics, joining coalition governments at the Centre and sharing power with national parties.

**Six documented dysfunctions:** privileging regional interest over national interest in resolving national issues; encouraging regionalism, casteism, linguism, communalism and tribalism that can obstruct national integration; contributing to unresolved inter-State water and border disputes; indulging, like any party, in corruption, nepotism and favouritism to serve self-interest; focusing on populist measures to expand electoral bases, with adverse effects on State finances and development; and injecting regional, coalition-driven bargaining into central decision-making regardless of its national-interest cost.

### Which side of the table are they sitting at?

Imagine judging two debaters not by what they say they believe, but by which side of the table they are sitting at. A debater seated at the "I run the whole company" table will naturally argue for head-office control; the same debater, moved to the "I run one branch office" table, will naturally argue for branch autonomy — not because their convictions flipped, but because their position did. This lesson argues that national and regional parties behave exactly this way: their centralisation-or-autonomy stance tracks which table they are sitting at (Union power versus State-based power), not a settled philosophical commitment that would survive a change of seats.

### Technical doctrine: the party-system route, built from earlier lessons

1. **A national party's centralising incentive.** A party that holds, or realistically seeks, the Union internalises the gains of a strong Centre — control over the All-India Services, dominance of the Union List, and the political credit of central schemes — and therefore tends to prefer uniform, national programmes over State-specific variation.
2. **The internal-organisation reinforcement.** Lesson 11's high-command model — weak internal federalism within the party itself, with no statutory internal-democracy code forcing devolved decision-making — reinforces this preference for centralised control, inside the party and, when in government, inside the polity.
3. **A regional (State) party's autonomy incentive.** A party recognised as a **State party** under the 1968 Symbols Order (Lesson 5) draws its entire electoral base from one State's identity, language or sub-region; for such a party, fairer tax devolution, a restrained Governor's office, and fewer conditionalities attached to centrally sponsored schemes are not abstract preferences but **electoral necessities** — its voters' material interests are tied directly to State-level autonomy.
4. **Counter-evidence that the correlation tracks position, not ideology.** During the coalition era (1989-2014, Lesson 12), regional parties sitting inside central coalitions bargained hard **for** State interests, while national parties needing their support **conceded** autonomy they might otherwise have resisted; regional parties, once in power within their own States, frequently **centralise** authority there just as a national party would at the Centre; and a national party that finds itself in **Opposition within a State** often argues for State autonomy against the very Centre its own national leadership controls.
5. **The institutional verdict.** Because preference tracks incentive and organisational form rather than fixed conviction, the durable safeguard for State autonomy is **institutional** — the Finance Commission's devolution formula, a functioning Inter-State Council, and judicial review grounded in federalism as a basic feature (*S.R. Bommai*, 1994, developed fully in Federal System) — rather than reliance on any party's professed ideology remaining stable once its position in the power structure changes.

### Is the original statement simply false, then?

**Objection:** "If the correlation reverses depending on which party holds the Union, is the original statement — that national parties favour centralisation and regional parties favour autonomy — simply false?"

**Reply:** Not false, but incompletely specified: the statement is broadly, observably true **at any given moment**, because whichever party currently holds or realistically seeks the Union does tend to behave in a centralising way, and whichever party's base is confined to a State does tend to press for autonomy. ### Concept check

**Question:** A regional party that has spent a decade in a central coalition demanding greater fiscal devolution later wins a Lok Sabha majority on its own and governs the Union alone. Would you expect its stance on centralisation to change, and why?

**Model answer:** On this lesson's incentive-based account, yes — its stance would plausibly shift toward the centralising behaviours typical of any Union-holding party (favouring uniform national programmes, internalising the gains of Union List dominance and central schemes), because the shift follows from its changed position in the power structure rather than from any fixed ideological commitment to autonomy. This is precisely the mechanism the coalition-era counter-evidence illustrates: parties argue for the arrangement that favours whichever table they currently sit at.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that a party's past advocacy for State autonomy is a permanent ideological marker that would survive its becoming the sole party controlling the Union; this lesson's evidence suggests position, not fixed conviction, is the better predictor.

### Mains practice — position against ideology

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** "A party's stance on centralisation is a function of its position in the structure of power, not of its ideology." Critically evaluate, with reference to the behaviour of national and regional parties in India.

**Model solution:** The claim is substantially well supported. A party holding or realistically seeking control of the Union internalises the gains of a strong Centre — command of the All-India Services, dominance of the Union List, and the political credit of centrally run schemes — and its high-command style of internal organisation, with no statutory internal-democracy code forcing devolved decision-making, reinforces that preference for centralised control. A party recognised only as a State party, by contrast, draws its electoral base from one State's identity, language or region, making fairer tax devolution, a restrained Governor's office and fewer scheme conditionalities electoral necessities rather than abstract convictions. The strongest support for a positional, not ideological, reading comes from reversal: during the 1989-2014 coalition era, regional parties inside central coalitions bargained hard for autonomy while national parties needing their support conceded it; regional parties, once in power within their own States, routinely centralise authority there just as a national party would at the Centre; and a national party in opposition within a State frequently argues for that State's autonomy against its own central government. The claim therefore holds well as description, with one qualification: because incentives, not fixed conviction, drive the correlation, no party's professed position survives a change in its place in the power structure — which is precisely why durable safeguards for autonomy must be institutional (the Finance Commission, the Inter-State Council, and judicial review grounded in federalism as a basic feature), not dependent on any party's ideology remaining stable.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** National-party centralising incentive correctly explained — 4; regional/State-party autonomy incentive correctly explained — 4; coalition-era and in-State-opposition counter-evidence correctly used to show reversal — 5; institutional-safeguard conclusion correctly drawn — 4; graded, qualified verdict — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

### Original Mains question

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** "Indian law regulates a political party most tightly at its edges — birth, symbol and candidate disclosure — and leaves its interior almost entirely to convention." Critically evaluate.

**Model solution:** The edges of a party's legal life are regulated in detail. Registration under Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 requires a constitutional-allegiance declaration; recognition and symbols under the Election Symbols Order, 1968 depend on verified poll-performance thresholds, with faction disputes settled by the Election Commission under a test upheld in *Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India* (1972) and reaffirmed as recently as a Delhi High Court ruling of January 2026; and candidate disclosure has been built up through *Association for Democratic Reforms* (2002), *PUCL* (2003) and *Rambabu Singh Thakur* (2020). The interior, by contrast, is far less regulated: no statute compels internal party elections or a transparent candidate-selection process, the Election Commission's registration power carries no general deregistration function after *Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002), and the Central Information Commission's 2013 finding that national parties are public authorities under the RTI Act remains uncomplied with. Party finance sits between these extremes: contributions and disclosure are statutorily governed, and the 2024 Electoral Bonds judgment restored a floor on the voter's right to know, yet the Court imposed no new cap — striking down the 2017 amendments merely revived the pre-2017 company ceiling — and no comprehensive disclosure code followed. The statement is therefore substantially correct: regulation is dense at entry, symbol and candidate-disclosure stages, thin on internal governance, and only partly strengthened on finance — internal democracy and full funding transparency remain this topic's unresolved reform frontier.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** Edge regulation (registration, recognition/symbols, candidate disclosure) correctly detailed with named provisions and cases — 7; interior under-regulation correctly detailed (deregistration gap, internal democracy, RTI non-compliance) — 6; finance placed correctly as a partial, intermediate case with the 2024 verdict — 4; graded, qualified verdict — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cross-lesson check 1 (Lessons 3, 4, 6)

**Question:** A party is refused a reserved symbol because it has never crossed a State-party threshold, and separately a court is asked to compel the Election Commission to deregister a rival, long-inactive party. Which legal layers, and which case law, govern each request?

**Model answer:** The symbol refusal is governed by the ECI-order layer — the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968 — whose recognition thresholds determine eligibility for a reserved symbol; its validity and the ECI's power to classify parties were most recently reaffirmed by the Delhi High Court on 9 January 2026, building on *Sadiq Ali v. ECI* (1972). The deregistration request concerns the statutory layer — Section 29A registration — where *Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002) denies any general, merits-based deregistration power, confining it to narrow fraud, non-compliance or unlawful-object exceptions that mere inactivity does not satisfy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "no reserved symbol" and "should be deregistered" as the same legal complaint; one concerns recognition thresholds under an ECI order, the other concerns the separate, much narrower question of registration itself under a statute.

### Cross-lesson check 2 (Lessons 8, 9, 11)

**Question:** After the 2024 Electoral Bonds verdict, is it accurate to say that Indian party finance is now fully transparent and that internal party democracy has also been strengthened as a result?

**Model answer:** No, on both counts. The 2024 verdict in *Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India* restored the voter's right-to-know by striking down the anonymous Electoral Bond Scheme and its 2017 enabling amendments, but it did not impose a donation cap or enact a comprehensive new disclosure code, leaving other lawful funding routes under Sections 29B-29C, the Companies Act and the FCRA only partially more transparent than before. It also has no bearing on internal party democracy, which remains governed by no statutory code at all, with the Central Information Commission's unimplemented 2013 RTI finding and the unenacted Law Commission 170th Report and NCRWC proposals showing that internal governance is a wholly separate, unresolved reform frontier from funding transparency.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that a reform in one domain (funding disclosure) automatically improves an unrelated domain (internal party governance); the two reform frontiers have different legal sources and different, independent statuses.

### Cross-lesson check 3 (Lessons 2, 12, 13)

**Question:** How do India's "eight defining features" (Lesson 2), the "four phases" (Lesson 12) and the causes of regional-party rise (Lesson 13) fit together without repeating each other?

**Model answer:** Lesson 2's eight features describe the party system's standing character (multi-party scale, past dominance, weak ideology, personality cult, traditional-factor basis, regional parties, factions and defections, weak Opposition) as a snapshot. Lesson 12's four phases describe how that snapshot has changed over time — the "one-dominant-party" feature, for instance, corresponds exactly to Phase 1, while the "emergence of regional parties" feature is the seed of Phases 2-4. Lesson 13 then explains, causally, **why** one specific feature (regional parties) arose in the first place, supplying the enumerated causes behind the trend that Lesson 12 periodises and Lesson 2 first names. Each lesson therefore adds a different layer — description, periodisation, causation — to the same underlying phenomenon rather than repeating it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Lessons 2, 12 and 13 as three separate topics; they describe, periodise and explain the same set of facts about India's party system from three different analytical angles.

### Cross-lesson check 4 (Lessons 7, 11, 14)

**Question:** A high-command decision inside a national party disciplines a legislator for voting against the party's declared position in the House. Which of this session's lessons, and which other topic, does a full analysis of this fact pattern require?

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These three questions are new and distinct from the lesson questions and from every previous-year question listed above.

### Synthesis Mains — 10 marks, strict ceiling 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish "registration" from "recognition" of a political party, explaining why confusing the two is a common examinable error.

**Model solution:** Registration and recognition are governed by entirely different instruments and answer different questions. Registration, under Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, is a one-time statutory step: an association furnishes a constitutional-allegiance declaration and the Election Commission, acting quasi-judicially, registers it as a political party, conferring basic statutory identity regardless of electoral performance. Recognition, under the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, is a recurring, performance-based classification: a registered party is assessed at general elections against specific vote-share and seat thresholds to determine whether it qualifies as a National or State party — keeping that status, under paragraph 6C, through one failed election — and recognition alone unlocks a reserved symbol, broadcast time and additional star campaigners. A party can therefore be validly registered for decades while never being recognised — exactly the confusion examiners test by asking what a party "needs" to be called national.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Registration correctly explained with Section 29A — 3; recognition correctly explained with the 1968 Order and its continued-recognition rule — 3; explicit statement that registration does not guarantee recognition — 3; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 15 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "The Indian political party is a creature of statute and administrative order far more than of the Constitution." Discuss, with reference to registration, recognition and the Constitution's narrow defection-regime mentions of the party.

**Model solution:** The claim is well supported. The original 1950 Constitution did not mention political parties, and even today the Constitution names "political party" only inside the defection regime — the Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985), operating through Articles 102(2) and 191(2), and Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B (91st Amendment, 2003), which bar a member so disqualified from ministerial office or a remunerative political post. The party's basic legal existence instead flows from an ordinary statute: Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 lets any qualifying association register with the Election Commission on a constitutional-allegiance declaration, with no constitutional provision governing that process. Its most consequential privileges — a reserved symbol, broadcast time, forty star campaigners, and classification as National or State — flow not from a statute at all but from the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, a subordinate instrument the Commission itself issues and revises under Article 324, adding a fifth State-party condition in 2011 and substituting the continued-recognition paragraph in 2016. Even disputes over a party's identity after a split are settled administratively, under a test upheld in *Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India* (1972) and reaffirmed by the Delhi High Court in January 2026. The claim is therefore accurate: the party's day-to-day legal life is overwhelmingly statutory and administrative, the Constitution's role being confined to a narrow, late-arriving set of defection consequences.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Constitution's narrow defection-regime appearances (Tenth Schedule, 1985; 91st Amendment articles, 2003) correctly stated — 4; registration correctly attributed to statute (Section 29A) — 3; recognition and symbols correctly attributed to the 1968 Order, with its administrative revisability — 4; *Sadiq Ali* and the 2026 reaffirmation correctly used as evidence — 3; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 20 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "Each reform of the Indian political party — in finance, in candidate disclosure, in internal governance — has closed one gap while leaving a wider one open." Critically evaluate.

**Model solution:** The pattern holds across every reform strand taught here. In finance, the 2024 Electoral Bonds judgment closed the anonymity route the 2018 Scheme and its 2017 enabling amendments had created, restoring the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a) and directing SBI disclosure — yet the Court imposed no new cap, striking down the 2017 amendments merely reviving the pre-2017 profit-linked company ceiling, while the Indrajit Gupta Committee's state-funding proposal and the Law Commission's 255th Report remain unenacted. In candidate disclosure, *ADR* (2002), *PUCL* (2003) and *Rambabu Singh Thakur* (2020) progressively widened what must be disclosed and by whom, culminating in parties justifying their selection of candidates with criminal antecedents — yet *Lily Thomas* (2013) shows disqualification bites only after conviction, so disclosure has not reduced pending criminal cases among candidates, a gap the Vohra Committee flagged in 1993. In internal governance, the Central Information Commission's 2013 finding that national parties are RTI "public authorities" accepted in principle that parties should answer publicly — yet it remains uncomplied with, and no statutory code has followed the Law Commission's 170th Report or the NCRWC's 2002 proposals. The common thread: each reform worked through disclosure, an administrative finding or a single struck-down instrument rather than binding structural change, closing a named gap while leaving the underlying incentive untouched — winnability over clean candidature, discretionary succession, and a corporate ceiling restored by default rather than redesigned. The qualification: closing one gap is genuine progress, not cosmetic.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** Finance strand correctly analysed with the 2024 verdict and its limits — 5; disclosure/disqualification strand correctly analysed with all four named cases — 5; internal-democracy strand correctly analysed with the CIC finding and unenacted proposals — 5; synthesis of the common pattern across all three strands — 3; graded, qualified verdict — 1; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

| Saying parties are "registered under the Constitution" | The Tenth Schedule's mention of "political party" is well known | Registered under Section 29A, RPA 1951 — a statute (Lessons 3-4) |
| Treating the Tenth Schedule as the source of registration/recognition | It is the only constitutional mention learners usually know | It governs only legislative defection; registration and recognition sit in the statutory and ECI-order layers (Lesson 3) |
| Quoting a specific current count of national/State parties as a fixed fact | A remembered textbook figure feels authoritative | Counts turn on general-election performance, and paragraph 6C lets a recognised party survive one failed election before de-recognition; state the threshold structure instead (Lesson 5) |
| Believing "national party" means a party that contests only national (not State) elections | The word "national" suggests scope of contest | It is a recognition/privilege category unrelated to where the party contests (Lesson 5) |
| Giving recognised and unrecognised parties the same star-campaigner allowance | Both terms sound like formal categories of equal weight | 40 for recognised, 20 for unrecognised, under RPA Section 77 (Lesson 6) |
| Assuming a court decides a party-split/symbol dispute in the first instance | Splits are reported as court battles in the media | The ECI decides first, under the 1968 Order and the *Sadiq Ali* test (Lesson 6) |
| Assuming the ECI can deregister any inactive or errant party | Registration and deregistration seem like mirror powers | No general merits-based power exists after *Institute of Social Welfare* (2002); only narrow exceptions (Lesson 4) |

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### 1. What a political party is, and India's party system (Lessons 1-2)

- A political party is a voluntary association seeking power through constitutional means, claiming to serve the national interest; four ideological types (reactionary, conservative, liberal, radical) map to right/centre/left.
- Three party systems: one-party (USSR-era), two-party (USA, Britain), multi-party (France, Switzerland, Italy, India).
- India's eight defining features: continental multi-party scale; the one-dominant-party phase (Rajni Kothari's "Congress system"); lack of clear ideology; personality cult; basis in traditional factors (religion, caste, language, culture, race); emergence of regional parties (the TDP was the largest Opposition party in the Lok Sabha after 1984 — a dated illustration); factions and defections ("two Janata Dals, two TDPs..."); and the absence of an effective, united Opposition. The six-lens grouping used in Lesson 2 is a recall device, not a rival count.
- Congress dominance declined unevenly from 1967, with Janata (1977), Janata Dal (1989) and the BJP (1991) as rising competitors.

### 2. Three legal layers (Lesson 3)

- Original 1950 Constitution: silent on "political party".
- the constitutional layer — Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985): the first constitutional use of "political party", for defection only, via Articles 102(2)/191(2); the 91st Amendment (2003) added Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B, which use "belonging to any political party" to bar a member disqualified for defection from ministerial office or a remunerative political post. Every constitutional use sits inside the defection regime.
- the statutory layer — Section 29A, RPA 1951: statutory registration, on a declaration of true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, to socialism, secularism and democracy, and an undertaking to uphold the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.
- the ECI-order layer — Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968: ECI order under Article 324, governing recognition and symbols; revised periodically (fifth State-party condition added 2011; paragraph 6C on continued recognition substituted 2016).
- The three layers have different legal pedigrees, dates and amending procedures; do not merge them.

### 3. Registration and the deregistration gap (Lesson 4)

- Section 29A registration is a quasi-judicial ECI function.
- *Indian National Congress (I) v. Institute of Social Welfare* (2002): no general, merits-based deregistration power; narrow exceptions only — fraud, non-compliance, unlawful object.
- Deregistration (rare) must not be confused with loss of recognition (performance-based and subject to paragraph 6C's one-election carry-over).

### 4. Recognition thresholds (Lesson 5)

- National party: any one of — 6% votes in 4+ States + 4 LS seats; OR 2% LS seats from 3 States; OR State-party status in 4 States.
- State party: any one of five conditions, the fifth (8% of the State's total valid votes) added in 2011.
- Continued recognition (paragraph 6C, substituted 2016): a recognised National or State party that fails the conditions at the next general election stays recognised; de-recognition follows only on failure at a subsequent general election.
- Counts turn on general-election performance (7 national/52 State/2,354 registered-unrecognised parties as of 2019 — a historical, not current, figure).

### 5. Symbols, *Sadiq Ali* and the 2026 current linkage (Lesson 6)

- Recognised parties: reserved symbol, one proposer, broadcast time, electoral-roll access, 40 star campaigners (RPA Sec 77); unrecognised parties: free symbol, 20 star campaigners.
- *Sadiq Ali v. ECI* (1972): ECI decides faction/symbol disputes first, using the test of majority across organisational and legislative wings.
- Delhi High Court, 9 January 2026 (Justices Sambre and Dayal): dismissed a challenge to the 1968 Order's validity and the ECI's classification power; held a symbol is not a fundamental right.

### 6. The defection bridge (Lesson 7)

- The Tenth Schedule's paragraph 1 definitions depend on the same party identity this topic registers and recognises.
- This topic owns the party as an organisation; Anti-Defection-Law owns the Tenth Schedule's complete scheme (grounds, defences, deciding authority, case law, 91st Amendment).

### 7. Party finance I (Lesson 8)

- Permitted routes: Companies Act Sec 182 corporate donations; electoral trusts; RPA Sec 29B (subject to the foreign-source bar).
- RPA Sec 29C: disclosure of contributions above a statutory threshold to the ECI, before the income-tax return due date.
- FCRA, 2010: bars a "foreign contribution" as currently defined.
- Finance Act, 2017 had removed the Companies Act's profit-linked donation ceiling and the recipient-naming duty; striking those amendments down in 2024 revived the pre-2017 position.

### 8. Party finance II — Electoral Bonds (Lesson 9)

- Electoral Bond Scheme (2018): donor anonymity from the public via SBI-issued bonds.
- *Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India* (February 2024): Scheme and 2017 enabling amendments struck down as unconstitutional, violating Article 19(1)(a); SBI directed to disclose data to the ECI.
- Striking down the 2017 amendments revived the pre-2017 profit-linked company ceiling and recipient-naming duty; the Court itself imposed no new cap and wrote no comprehensive code. Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998, State funding) and Law Commission 255th Report (2015, disclosure) remain unenacted.

### 9. Criminalisation of politics (Lesson 10)

- Disclosure track: *ADR* (2002), *PUCL* (2003) — candidate affidavits; *Rambabu Singh Thakur v. Sunil Arora* (2020) — parties must publish antecedents and reasons for selection.
- Disqualification track: *Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2013) — struck Section 8(4), RPA; immediate post-conviction disqualification.
- Vohra Committee (1993): official acknowledgement of the criminal-politician-bureaucrat nexus.
- Disqualification still requires conviction; disclosure alone has not reduced criminalisation, because "winnability" dominates candidate selection.

### 10. Internal democracy (Lesson 11)

- No statutory code compels internal party elections or transparent candidate selection; the high-command/dynastic pattern fills the gap.
- CIC (2013): six national parties held to be RTI "public authorities" — finding not complied with.
- Law Commission 170th Report (1999) and NCRWC (2002): unenacted proposals for an internal-democracy/finance code.
- Keep the Vohra Committee (criminal nexus), Goswami Committee (electoral reform/whip) and NCRWC (constitutional review) separate.

### 11. Four phases of the party system (Lesson 12)

- Phase 1 (1952-1967): one-party dominance, the Congress system.
- Phase 2 (1967-1989): fragmentation and first coalitions, after the 1967 losses and the 1977 Janata government.
- Phase 3 (1989-2014): coalition era — National Front/United Front/NDA/UPA; regional parties pivotal.
- Phase 4 (2014 onward): single-party majorities in 2014/2019; coalition dependence restored after the 2024 general election.

### 12. Regional parties (Lesson 13)

- Four categories, with dated illustrations: culture/ethnicity-based (Akali Dal, National Conference, DMK, AIADMK, Telugu Desam, Shiv Sena, AGP, MNF, JMM); all-India outlook without a national base (INLD, Forward Bloc, RSP, Samajwadi Party, NCP); formed by a split in a national party (Bangla Congress, Utkal Congress, Kerala Congress, BJD, RJD, Samata Party, Trinamool Congress, YSR Congress); personalised and short-lived (Lok Janshakti Party, Haryana Vikas Party, Himachal Vikas Congress, Congress (J)).
- Twelve causes of rise span cultural pluralism, economic imbalance, historical identity, deposed Maharajas and dispossessed Zamindars, unmet regional aspirations, linguistic reorganisation, charismatic leadership, factional fights, Congress's centralising tendencies, weak central Opposition, caste/religion and tribal alienation.
- Ten points on role: better stable regional governance; challenged one-party dominance; reshaped Centre-State relations; made politics competitive and deepened grass-roots participation; widened voter choice in Assembly and parliamentary elections; raised political consciousness; checked the Centre's dictatorial tendencies; made parliamentary democracy work — minority has its say, majority its way, by ruling in States while in Opposition at the Centre; exposed partisan Governors in appointments, dismissals, ordinances and Bill reservation; shared power in central coalitions.
- Six dysfunctions: regional over national interest; regionalism/casteism/linguism/communalism/tribalism; unresolved inter-State water and border disputes; corruption and nepotism; populism straining State finances; regional bargaining forced into central coalition decision-making.
- Verdict: democratisation with a coordination cost.

### 13. Centralisation versus State autonomy (Lesson 14)

- Party-system half (this topic): Union-holding incentive and high-command organisation push national parties toward centralisation; State-based electoral necessity pushes regional/State parties toward autonomy.
- Counter-evidence shows the correlation tracks position, not ideology: coalition-era bargaining reversal; in-State regional-party centralisation; a national party's autonomy demand in State opposition.
- Institutional verdict: durable autonomy safeguards are the Finance Commission, the Inter-State Council and judicial review (*S.R. Bommai*), not party intent.
- The constitutional-tools half of the shared 2022 GS-II Q13 demand (15 marks, 250 words) is taught under the Federal System.
