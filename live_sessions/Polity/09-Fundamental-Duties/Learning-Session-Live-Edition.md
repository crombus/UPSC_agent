# Fundamental Duties - Live Learning Session

> Part IVA does not turn citizenship into obedience to every governmental command. It states a
> constitutional ethic for citizens, gives legislatures carefully bounded fields for enforcement,
> and assists courts when interpreting rights, statutes and public obligations. Its central problem
> is therefore not whether duties matter, but how duties can matter without displacing liberty,
> conscience, legality and proportionality.

### 1. Why did the original Constitution contain no separate list?

The Constitution adopted in 1950 guaranteed Fundamental Rights and placed duties on the State
through the Directive Principles. It did not contain a separate constitutional code of citizen
duties. This was not an accidental blank that makes the original Constitution morally empty.
Criminal law, taxation, public order, defence obligations and ordinary civic responsibilities
already existed. The omission means only that the framers did not place a consolidated list of
citizen duties beside Parts III and IV.

Rights and duties can be **correlative** without being identical. A person's right may correspond
to a duty on the State or another person. But a constitutional list addressed to every citizen is
a different legal device: it states a public ethic even where no directly enforceable claim or
offence follows.

### 2. Comparative inspiration - use the textbook claim carefully

The accepted textbook account says that Part IVA was inspired by the Constitution of the
erstwhile **USSR**, where rights and duties were presented together. It also notes that the
Japanese Constitution contains express citizen duties and that many major democratic constitutions
do not use a comparable consolidated list.

This is a comparative orientation, not an exhaustive survey of every constitutional system. The
safe examination proposition is:

```text
source of inspiration for India's codified Fundamental Duties -> erstwhile USSR
```

Do not turn the comparative note into a universal claim that democratic systems without a
constitutional list impose no civic or legal duties.

### 3. Codification of inherited civic values - with a pluralist qualification

A standard textbook feature is that the duties codify values associated with Indian tradition,
mythology, religions, practices and the wider way of life. The point is not that Article 51A
copied one ancient code. Respect for learning, social harmony, care for nature, compassion,
non-violence, preservation of shared culture and pursuit of excellence have appeared in multiple
Indian intellectual, religious, ethical and social traditions.

This feature needs a constitutional qualification. India has no single homogeneous tradition,
religion, mythology or “way of life.” An inherited practice does not become constitutionally
valid merely because it is old. Clauses (e), (f) and (h) themselves combine culture with women's
dignity, humanism, inquiry and reform. The safe reading is therefore:

```text
plural inherited values
          +
constitutional equality, dignity and reform
          =
living civic ethic, not cultural essentialism
```

### 4. Swaran Singh Committee

The **Congress Party** set up the **Sardar Swaran Singh Committee in 1976** during the Internal
Emergency. The Committee argued that citizens should remain conscious that enjoyment of rights is
accompanied by obligations to the country and society. The **Congress Government at the Centre**
then accepted the central recommendation and enacted the 42nd Amendment. The Committee had
recommended:

- a separate constitutional chapter on Fundamental Duties;
- **eight** duties;
- a legislative power to impose punishment for non-compliance;
- protection of such punishment laws against constitutional challenge; and
- inclusion of a duty to pay taxes.

The final three proposals listed above were **not accepted**. This distinction is a recurring
Prelims trap:

```text
COMMITTEE RECOMMENDED                CONSTITUTION ENACTED
eight duties                         ten duties in 1976
tax duty proposed                    no tax duty in Article 51A
penalty proposal                     no general self-executing penalty
immunity proposal                    no immunity from Fundamental Rights review
```

### 5. The 42nd Amendment's actual design

The **Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976** inserted:

- **Part IVA**;
- the single **Article 51A**; and
- ten duties, clauses **(a) to (j)**.

The amendment occurred during the Emergency, so its political setting cannot be erased. Critics
reasonably worry that a government restricting rights may invoke duties as a vocabulary of
obedience.

### 6. The recorded debate at insertion

The political arguments should be presented as competing stated positions, not as neutral proof
of either side:

- **H.R. Gokhale**, then Law Minister, argued that a duties chapter would have a **“sobering
  effect”** on the “restless spirits” whom he associated with disregard for the established legal
  order and anti-national or unconstitutional agitation. This explains the government's
  discipline-and-order rationale; it also sharpens the Emergency-era paternalism criticism.
- **Indira Gandhi**, then Prime Minister, defended the moral value of duties as **“not to smother
  rights”** but to establish a **democratic balance** by making citizens conscious of duties as
  well as rights.
- The **parliamentary Opposition strongly opposed** the inclusion by the Congress government.
  The opposition matters because Part IVA was contested at birth rather than adopted by an
  uncontested constitutional consensus.

Later history complicates both positions. The Janata government reversed several Emergency-era
changes through the 43rd and 44th Amendments but did not remove Part IVA. The 86th Amendment later
added another duty. Retention does not validate every original argument, but it shows that the
institution acquired broader acceptance after its contested origin.

### 7. Placement and legal meaning

Part IVA follows the Directive Principles in Part IV. The placement does not make duties a part of
the DPSP, and it does not place them on the State. Article 51A opens with:

> **“It shall be the duty of every citizen of India—”**

That opening establishes two controls:

1. the addressee is the **citizen**, not every person; and
2. the list states duties, not automatically defined offences.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Part IVA was an Emergency-era attempt to discipline citizens while the State was
curtailing rights.

**Reply:** The political origin requires caution, but the text includes duties of harmony,
women's dignity, environmental protection, scientific temper, non-violence and education that
cannot be reduced to obedience. Subsequent retention and expansion also matter.

**Residual:** Emergency provenance remains a warning against using vague duties to bypass precise
rights limitations. The democratic legitimacy of Part IVA depends on rights-respecting
interpretation and carefully framed legislation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why are “eight duties,” “ten duties” and “eleven duties” all correct in different
contexts?

**Model answer:** The Swaran Singh Committee recommended eight duties in 1976. The 42nd Amendment
did not copy that number mechanically; it inserted ten duties as Article 51A(a)-(j). The 86th
Amendment, 2002 later added the education duty in clause (k), making the present total eleven.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying that the Committee recommended ten duties merely because the
42nd Amendment enacted ten.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“The Emergency explains the origin of Fundamental Duties but does not exhaust their present
constitutional meaning.” Discuss. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Fundamental Duties were inserted by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 after the Swaran Singh Committee,
during the Internal Emergency. Their origin therefore invites concern that duties might be used
to demand obedience while rights are restricted. The rejection of the Committee's proposed
immunity for punishment laws confirms why rights review remains essential.

However, present meaning cannot be reduced to origin. Article 51A includes harmony, women's
dignity, environmental protection, scientific temper, non-violence and excellence. The Janata
government retained Part IVA while reversing several Emergency changes, and the 86th Amendment
added the education duty in 2002.

Thus, Emergency provenance supplies a caution against coercion, while later retention and
rights-consistent interpretation support a broader civic ethic. Duties are legitimate only when
implemented through precise law subject to Fundamental Rights and proportionality.

*Constitutional patriotism protects the constitutional order; it does not require citizens to
approve every government, judgment or policy.*

### 1. Exact constitutional wording

Article 51A(a) states:

> **“to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and
> the National Anthem;”**

The sentence contains related but distinct objects:

| Object | Learner-first meaning |
|---|---|
| Constitution | Accept the supreme legal framework and constitutional methods |
| Ideals | Justice, liberty, equality, fraternity, dignity, democracy and constitutional government |
| Institutions | Respect offices and procedures without placing office-holders beyond criticism |
| National Flag | Treat the Flag with dignity under valid law and protocol |
| National Anthem | Avoid insult or intentional obstruction; respect does not automatically require compelled singing |

### 2. “Abide by” does not mean surrender criticism

To abide by the Constitution means using constitutional methods and respecting lawful
institutional authority. It does not forbid:

- challenging a statute in court;
- criticising the executive, legislature or judiciary;
- peacefully seeking constitutional amendment;
- dissenting from a public policy;
- exposing institutional wrongdoing.

Indeed, criticism can express constitutional fidelity when it invokes equality, liberty,
accountability or due process. Institutions deserve constitutional respect because of their
functions and procedures, not because every institutional decision is infallible.

### 3. Bijoe Emmanuel - respect without compulsory singing

In ***Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986)**, schoolchildren belonging to Jehovah's
Witnesses stood respectfully during the National Anthem but did not sing because of a sincere
religious belief. The Supreme Court protected them under Articles 19(1)(a) and 25. It found no
disrespect merely in their refusal to sing while standing respectfully.

The case supplies a precise principle:

```text
respectful non-participation
          ≠
insult or disruption
```

The judgment does not create a licence to obstruct the Anthem or insult national symbols. It
shows that a Fundamental Duty must be read with freedom of expression and conscience.

### 4. Naveen Jindal - respectful display as expression

In ***Union of India v. Naveen Jindal* (2004)**, the Court recognised respectful display of the
National Flag as an aspect of expression under Article 19(1)(a). The right is not absolute. It
remains subject to valid statutory restrictions, including legislation protecting national
honour, and to rules governing the Flag's dignity.

The case prevents a false binary:

| Incorrect extreme | Constitutional position |
|---|---|
| The State may prohibit private display at will | Respectful display has expressive protection |
| Expression makes every mode of display permissible | Valid law may prevent insult, misuse and indignity |

### 5. Shyam Narayan Chouksey - courts and compulsory patriotism

The Supreme Court's reportable **9 January 2018 judgment** in ***Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union
of India*** modified the earlier cinema-hall direction: playing the Anthem before a feature film
became optional or directory rather than mandatory. The Court left broader policy work to the
Union's committee and disposed of the writ petition. The judgment teaches institutional
restraint: Article 51A(a) does not itself authorise courts to design an all-purpose compulsory
patriotism code.

The narrow lesson is not that the Anthem lacks constitutional protection. It is that specific
obligations should arise from valid, precise rules and should respect constitutional rights and
institutional competence.

### 6. Statutory protection is precise, not symbolic

The **Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971** creates defined offences. Section 2
addresses specified insults to the Constitution and National Flag. Section 3 addresses
intentional prevention of singing the National Anthem or disturbance of an assembly engaged in
such singing.

Article 51A(a) supplies constitutional value; the statute supplies defined legal ingredients.
The duty alone is not a free-standing criminal offence.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Any refusal to perform a common patriotic ritual weakens unity.

**Reply:** Constitutional unity is strengthened when patriotism is consistent with conscience,
expression and legality. *Bijoe Emmanuel* protected respectful conduct, not insult.

**Residual:** Context matters. Deliberate obstruction, contemptuous treatment or statutory insult
is legally different from quiet, conscientious non-participation.

### UPSC application

- Quote clause (a) accurately; it mentions the Flag and Anthem, not the National Song.
- Use *Bijoe Emmanuel* for conscience and respectful non-singing.
- Use *Naveen Jindal* for expressive flag display subject to valid restrictions.
- Use *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* cautiously for institutional limits on compulsory patriotism.
- Distinguish Article 51A value from offences defined by the 1971 Act.

### Revision notes

1. Clause (a) covers the Constitution, its ideals and institutions, the Flag and Anthem.
2. Institutional respect does not immunise institutions from criticism.
3. Constitutional methods include judicial challenge, peaceful dissent and lawful reform.
4. *Bijoe Emmanuel* protected respectful silence grounded in conscience.
5. The case did not protect insult or disruption.
6. *Naveen Jindal* linked respectful Flag display to Article 19(1)(a).
7. Flag expression remains subject to valid law and dignity requirements.
8. The 2018 *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* judgment made cinema-hall playing optional or directory.
9. Article 51A(a) does not itself create a complete compulsory-conduct code.
10. The National Honour Act supplies defined offences; the constitutional duty supplies value.
11. The National Song is not listed in clause (a).
12. Constitutional patriotism combines loyalty to constitutional values with protected dissent.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student stands respectfully during the National Anthem but does not sing because
of a sincere conscientious belief. Why can this conduct remain consistent with Article 51A(a)?

**Model answer:** Clause (a) requires respect, not compulsory vocal expression in every case.
*Bijoe Emmanuel* held that respectful standing without singing was not disrespect and protected
the students' freedom of expression and conscience. Deliberate obstruction or insult would be a
different legal situation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating any non-participation with an insult to the Anthem.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Constitutional patriotism demands respect for national symbols, but it cannot be built through
unbounded coercion.” Examine. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 51A(a) requires citizens to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals,
institutions, Flag and Anthem. This duty supports a shared constitutional identity and permits
precise legislation against insult or intentional obstruction, such as the Prevention of Insults
to National Honour Act, 1971.

Yet respect is not identical to uniform performance. In *Bijoe Emmanuel*, children who stood
respectfully but declined to sing the Anthem on sincere religious grounds were protected by
Articles 19(1)(a) and 25. In *Naveen Jindal*, respectful Flag display received expressive
protection under Article 19(1)(a), subject to valid regulation. The 2018 *Shyam Narayan Chouksey*
judgment also illustrates why courts should not derive broad compulsory rituals directly from
Article 51A.

Coercion is legitimate only through law pursuing a legitimate constitutional aim and satisfying
necessity and proportionality. Deliberate insult and obstruction may be punished; criticism,
conscience and respectful non-participation cannot be collapsed into disloyalty.

Constitutional patriotism is therefore loyalty to constitutional values and methods. It protects
national symbols while preserving the liberty that gives those symbols democratic meaning.

*The sequence moves from values inherited from the freedom struggle to protection and, when lawfully

### 1. Exact wording of clause (b)

> **“to cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our national struggle for freedom;”**

The clause does not constitutionalise one party's history or one leader's complete political
programme. Its plural phrase **“noble ideals”** points to values such as:

- freedom from arbitrary rule;
- equality of civic status;
- sacrifice for public purposes;
- plural participation;
- constitutional self-government;
- non-discrimination and human dignity.

“Cherish” concerns memory and valuation. “Follow” asks conduct to reflect those ideals. A citizen
cannot satisfy the clause merely by celebrating anniversaries while rejecting liberty or equality
in everyday life.

### 2. The vagueness problem in clause (b)

The phrase “noble ideals” is not defined. Different strands of the national movement disagreed on
methods, economic policy, religion, social reform and constitutional strategy. The clause should
therefore be read at the level of constitutional values rather than used to impose one contested
historical ideology.

This interpretive discipline avoids two errors:

```text
too empty: "any ideal anyone associates with history"
too rigid: "one official account of the freedom struggle"
safe route: ideals consistent with the Constitution's democratic settlement
```

### 3. Exact wording of clause (c)

> **“to uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India;”**

The three terms are related but not interchangeable:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sovereignty | India's authority to govern itself within the constitutional order |
| Unity | Political belonging across regions, languages, religions and communities |
| Integrity | Protection of territorial and constitutional wholeness |

Clause (c) cannot make every criticism of the Union, demand for federal reform or peaceful
regional assertion “anti-national.” The Constitution itself permits federal debate, amendment,
political opposition and lawful advocacy. Restrictions must still fit the express constitutional
grounds, valid law and proportionality.

### 4. Exact wording of clause (d)

> **“to defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so;”**

The final words are decisive: **“when called upon to do so.”** Clause (d) does not by itself order
every citizen into military or civilian service. A concrete obligation requires lawful
institutional authority and applicable legislation or rules.

National service may be understood broadly in public life, but legal compulsion cannot rest on a
vague appeal to Article 51A(d). The State must define who is called, by whom, for what purpose,
under what conditions and with what safeguards.

### 5. Patriotism and constitutional disagreement

Citizens may disagree about:

- defence policy;
- federal distribution of power;
- emergency decisions;
- borders and diplomacy;
- the conduct of public institutions.

Peaceful disagreement does not negate clauses (b)-(d). The Constitution protects political
speech because unity based only on silence is fragile. The boundary is crossed by conduct
lawfully prohibited for threatening sovereignty or integrity, not by every unpopular opinion.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Narrow rights-based qualifications weaken strong national duties.

**Reply:** Rights qualifications do not erase duties; they prevent vague patriotic language from
becoming an uncontrolled power. A democracy protects itself through law, evidence and
proportionality.

**Residual:** Some threats are real and urgent. Rights review must remain serious rather than
mechanically invalidating every security measure, but urgency does not remove legality.

### UPSC application

- Quote “which inspired **our** national struggle for freedom.”
- Distinguish sovereignty, unity and integrity.
- Remember the conditional phrase “when called upon to do so.”
- Do not infer a general compulsory-service regime directly from clause (d).
- Use federal dissent versus unlawful threat as the analytical qualification.

### Revision notes

1. Clause (b) concerns the noble ideals inspiring India's national freedom struggle.
2. “Cherish” is remembrance; “follow” is conduct.
3. The ideals should be tied to the Constitution's democratic values.
4. Clause (b) does not settle every historical disagreement.
5. Clause (c) separately names sovereignty, unity and integrity.
6. Unity does not mean cultural or political uniformity.
7. Peaceful federal or policy disagreement is not automatically disloyal.
8. Clause (d) concerns defence and national service.
9. Its legal trigger is “when called upon to do so.”
10. Article 51A(d) alone does not create a defined service offence.
11. Security legislation remains subject to competence, rights and proportionality.
12. Constitutional nationalism combines common allegiance with protected pluralism.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the phrase “when called upon to do so” constitutionally important in clause
(d)?

**Model answer:** It shows that clause (d) is not a self-activating command requiring continuous
national service from every citizen. A concrete obligation must arise through lawful authority
that defines the call, its purpose, scope and safeguards.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Article 51A(d) itself as a complete compulsory military
service law.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Explain how Article 51A(b)-(d) can promote national commitment without suppressing democratic
dissent. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 51A(b) asks citizens to cherish and follow the noble ideals of the freedom struggle;
clause (c) requires protection of India's sovereignty, unity and integrity; and clause (d)
requires defence and national service when lawfully called.

These duties promote commitment by linking citizenship to constitutional freedom, common
political belonging and willingness to serve. However, unity cannot mean uniformity. The freedom
struggle itself contained diverse views, while the Constitution protects political opposition,
federal claims and peaceful criticism.

Therefore, clauses (b)-(d) should be implemented through constitutional values and precise law.
Security restrictions must have a valid legal basis and satisfy rights and proportionality.
Clause (d)'s phrase “when called upon” also prevents the duty from becoming a self-executing
service command.

National commitment in a democracy is strongest when it protects both the republic and the
freedoms for which the national movement struggled.

*Culture deserves preservation, but no cultural label can automatically immunise a practice that

### 1. Exact wording of clause (e)

> **“to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India
> transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; to renounce practices
> derogatory to the dignity of women;”**

Clause (e) contains **two duties** joined in one clause:

1. a positive duty to promote harmony and common brotherhood; and
2. a negative-transformative duty to renounce practices derogatory to women's dignity.

The first works across social divisions. The second acknowledges that fraternity is incomplete
when women are subjected to degrading practices within families, communities, workplaces or
public institutions.

### 2. “Transcending” diversity does not erase diversity

Transcending religious, linguistic, regional or sectional differences means refusing to turn
difference into hostility or unequal civic worth. It does not require citizens to abandon their
languages, faiths, regions or identities.

```text
constitutional fraternity
    ≠ homogenisation
    ≠ denial of inequality
    = equal civic concern across real differences
```

Harmony is also not silence imposed on disadvantaged groups. Peace without dignity may preserve
hierarchy. Genuine harmony permits claims for equality, reform and representation.

### 3. Women's dignity as a citizen duty

The duty to renounce degrading practices complements, but does not replace:

- Articles 14 and 15 on equality and non-discrimination;
- Article 21 dignity and autonomy;
- Article 15(3)'s enabling power for special provisions;
- legislation addressing violence, harassment and discrimination.

Article 51A(e) addresses citizen conduct. Fundamental Rights and statutes provide enforceable
standards and remedies. The duty cannot by itself define a criminal offence, but it strengthens
the constitutional rejection of practices that objectify, exclude or subordinate women.

### 4. Exact wording of clause (f)

> **“to value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture;”**

“Composite culture” recognises that Indian culture is neither singular nor sealed. It has
developed through interaction among regions, languages, faiths, communities, artistic forms,
local traditions and historical encounters.

The verbs are different:

- **value** - understand and respect the inheritance;
- **preserve** - maintain, transmit, document and protect it.

Preservation may concern monuments, manuscripts, languages, music, crafts, shared practices and
living traditions. Article 51A(f) supplies the citizen-duty principle; questions about particular
monuments, institutions and conservation statutes require their own factual and legal detail.

### 5. Culture cannot defeat constitutional dignity

Clauses (e) and (f) must be read together:

| Preservation claim | Constitutional test |
|---|---|
| Tradition has historical value | Identify the value and evidence |
| Practice affects women or a disadvantaged group | Test dignity, equality and autonomy |
| Reform is proposed | Preserve legitimate heritage while removing degrading elements |
| State regulates | Require competence, law, necessity and proportionality |

Culture is dynamic. Reform does not necessarily destroy heritage; clause (h) itself values inquiry
and reform. Conversely, labelling every inherited practice oppressive would flatten cultural
complexity. The Constitution requires a reasoned, rights-based distinction.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Courts and governments may use “dignity” to impose elite preferences on diverse
communities.

**Reply:** The answer is not to abandon dignity but to anchor it in constitutional text, affected
persons' rights, evidence and proportionate remedies.

**Residual:** Conflicts between cultural autonomy and equality remain fact-sensitive. A slogan
cannot substitute for analysis of the practice, harm, legal basis and available less restrictive
alternatives.

### UPSC application

- Identify both limbs of clause (e).
- Explain fraternity as equal civic belonging, not forced sameness.
- Link women's dignity to enforceable equality and liberty provisions without transferring their
  full doctrine into this topic.
- Explain composite culture as interaction and inheritance, not one official culture.
- In a gender-justice answer, use clause (e) as the citizen-duty dimension after establishing the
  principal equality, dignity, autonomy and statutory protections.

### Revision notes

1. Clause (e) has a harmony limb and a women's-dignity limb.
2. It names religious, linguistic, regional and sectional diversities.
3. Transcending difference does not mean abolishing identity.
4. Harmony without equality can conceal domination.
5. The women's-dignity duty complements Articles 14, 15 and 21.
6. Article 51A(e) does not itself define every prohibited practice.
7. Clause (f) uses the phrase “our composite culture.”
8. Composite culture reflects interaction among multiple traditions.
9. Valuing is understanding and respecting; preserving is protecting and transmitting.
10. Cultural preservation remains subject to dignity and equality.
11. Reform and preservation can coexist.
12. A valid restriction still requires law, competence and proportionality.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does constitutional harmony require more than the absence of visible conflict?

**Model answer:** Harmony under clause (e) includes equal civic belonging across religious,
linguistic, regional and sectional differences. If peace is maintained by silencing women or
disadvantaged groups, it lacks dignity and fraternity. The clause therefore joins common
brotherhood with renunciation of practices derogatory to women.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating harmony as passive order or compulsory cultural sameness.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Article 51A(e) and (f) preserve plural culture while building an internal constitutional test
against degrading practices.” Analyse. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

linguistic, regional and sectional diversities and to renounce practices derogatory to women's

Together, the clauses reject two extremes. First, national unity cannot demand homogenisation:
composite culture recognises interaction among multiple languages, faiths, regions and artistic
traditions. Secondly, historical or cultural status cannot automatically immunise a practice
that violates dignity. The women's-dignity limb complements Articles 14, 15 and 21 and relevant
legislation, though Article 51A alone does not create an offence.

The proper method is contextual. Identify the genuine cultural value, the persons affected, the
nature of harm and the available reform. Preservation may protect language, art and shared
inheritance while reform removes exclusion or degradation.

Thus, constitutional culture is neither frozen tradition nor cultural erasure. It is plural,
rights-conscious and capable of reform. Harmony becomes constitutionally valuable when it rests
on fraternity and equal dignity rather than silence or hierarchy.

### 1. Exact wording of clause (g)

> **“to protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers and wild
> life, and to have compassion for living creatures;”**

The clause contains three movements:

1. **protect** - prevent degradation and destruction;
2. **improve** - restore and enhance ecological conditions; and
3. **have compassion** - treat living creatures as more than disposable objects.

The listed forests, lakes, rivers and wild life are illustrative components of the natural
environment. The clause is not confined to those four categories.

### 2. The constitutional environment triangle


These provisions perform different functions. Article 48A is a Directive Principle addressed to
the State. Article 51A(g) addresses citizens. Article 21 is an enforceable Fundamental Right whose
environmental content has been developed through cases. The triangle supports interpretation; it
does not make every environmental preference an automatic Article 21 violation.

### 3. M.C. Mehta and environmental education

In ***M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1991; AIR 1992 SC 382)**, the Supreme Court emphasised
environmental awareness and education. It observed that law cannot work effectively without
social acceptance and referred to Article 51A(g). The case illustrates a non-punitive route:
citizenship education can operationalise a duty without turning every breach into a criminal
offence.

Detailed pollution-control doctrine remains with Environment. Here the important mechanism is:

```text
constitutional duty -> awareness and education -> voluntary compliance
                       + precise environmental law where required
```

### 4. Compassion and animal welfare - two judgments, different outcomes

***Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja* (2014)** relied significantly on Articles
51A(g) and (h) while interpreting the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 and addressing
Jallikattu as then regulated.

The Constitution Bench in ***Animal Welfare Board of India v. Union of India* (2023)** considered
later State amendment laws. It upheld the relevant amendments and stated that the Constitution
does not recognise Fundamental Rights for animals in the manner argued. It also concluded that
the Tamil Nadu amendment did not violate Articles 51A(g) and (h), treating the statutory changes
as addressing defects identified earlier.

The cases must not be collapsed:

| 2014 | 2023 |
|---|---|
| Strong use of compassion and statutory animal-welfare duties | Review of later validating/amending laws |
| Earlier regulatory setting | Changed statutory setting and legislative findings |
| Cannot be quoted as the final result for every later law | Did not erase compassion; changed the legal outcome |

### 5. Exact wording of clause (h)

> **“to develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform;”**

The clause contains four connected ideas:

- **scientific temper** - willingness to test claims through reason, evidence and revision;
- **humanism** - concern for human dignity, welfare and moral worth;
- **inquiry** - asking questions rather than accepting authority mechanically;
- **reform** - changing institutions or practices when reason and justice require it.

Scientific temper is not identical to memorising scientific facts. A technically trained person
may still reason dogmatically. Nor does scientific temper require hostility to all faith or
tradition. It asks that public claims with legal or policy consequences remain open to evidence,
reason and constitutional scrutiny.

### 6. Humanism prevents technocracy

Evidence alone does not select every public value. A technically efficient policy may distribute
harm unfairly. Humanism therefore qualifies scientific temper:

```text
evidence: what is likely to happen?
humanism: who bears the cost and whose dignity matters?
inquiry: what assumptions are hidden?
reform: what should change?
```

Clause (h) supports reasoned reform, not rule by experts without democratic accountability.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** “Scientific temper” is too vague and may allow the State to stigmatise dissenting
beliefs.

**Reply:** The duty is best treated as a civic and educational value. Any coercive regulation
must rely on precise law, demonstrable harm and proportionality, not on an official declaration
that a belief is “unscientific.”

**Residual:** Public-health, environmental and fraud regulation may legitimately require
evidence-based standards. The difficult task is distinguishing regulation of harm from
suppression of belief.

  Articles 48A and 21 rather than treating the citizen duty as the complete answer.

*Clause (i) protects the common base of citizenship; clause (j) asks citizens to improve what they
do with that base.*

### 1. Exact wording of clause (i)

> **“to safeguard public property and to abjure violence;”**

Public property is not “government property” in the sense of belonging to an abstract ruler. It
is held and financed for public purposes. Damage to a bus, school, hospital, railway facility,
water installation or public record imposes costs on citizens, often most heavily on those who
depend on public services.

The duty has two linked limbs:

- **safeguard public property** - do not damage it and act responsibly toward common assets;
- **abjure violence** - reject violence as a method of political or social action.

### 2. Why the two limbs belong together

Public property is frequently damaged during protests, communal conflict or collective unrest.
Clause (i) insists that political grievance does not convert shared assets into legitimate
targets.

This does not delegitimise protest. Article 19 protects speech and peaceful assembly subject to
constitutional restrictions. The constitutional distinction is:

```text
peaceful protest + civil disagreement        constitutionally protected field
intentional violence or property damage      regulable and punishable by precise law
```

The State must also act proportionately. Alleged damage by some participants cannot justify
indiscriminate force against an entire peaceful assembly.

### 3. Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984

The **Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984** creates statutory offences relating to
mischief causing damage to public property and provides specific legal consequences. The Act,
not Article 51A(i) by itself, supplies offence elements.

The constitutional and statutory roles differ:

| Article 51A(i) | PDPP Act, 1984 |
|---|---|
| States a citizen duty and constitutional value | Defines punishable conduct |
| Helps explain why public property deserves protection | Requires proof under criminal process |
| Does not itself specify every offence or penalty | Remains subject to constitutional safeguards |

### 4. Exact wording of clause (j)

> **“to strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that
> the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement;”**

The clause concerns both:

- **individual activity** - professional competence, learning, integrity and improvement; and
- **collective activity** - institutional quality, teamwork, public services, science, sport,
  culture and democratic performance.

“Strive” is process-oriented. The duty does not guarantee equal outcomes or authorise punishment
for ordinary failure. “Excellence” also cannot be reduced to elite competition. Public
institutions achieve constitutional excellence when they are competent, inclusive, lawful and
responsive.

### 5. Mohan Kumar Singhania and excellence

***Mohan Kumar Singhania v. Union of India* (decided 1991; reported AIR 1992 SC 1)** concerned
Civil Services Examination and training rules. Article 51A(j) was relevant to the value of
excellence in public services.

Use the case narrowly. It supports clause (j)'s connection with standards and training. It should
not be cited as an unnamed general proposition that every law invoking a duty is automatically
reasonable under Articles 14 and 19.

### 6. Excellence needs equality and institutional support

An excellence policy can become exclusionary if it:

- mistakes inherited advantage for merit;
- ignores unequal access to education or training;
- uses opaque standards;
- rewards targets at the cost of legality or welfare.

Constitutional excellence is therefore not “results at any cost.” It operates within equality,
dignity, fair procedure and public purpose.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Clause (j) is merely motivational language with no constitutional content.

**Reply:** The clause can guide institutional standards, training, interpretation and public
ethics. Its open texture is useful when it encourages improvement without defining a criminal
offence.

**Residual:** Because “excellence” is contestable, it must never become a slogan for arbitrary
rankings, exclusion or excessive performance coercion.

2. Public property is held for shared public purposes.
3. Damage disproportionately harms users of public services.
4. Peaceful protest remains distinct from violent property destruction.
5. Collective punishment or disproportionate policing is not justified by clause (i).
6. The PDPP Act, 1984 defines statutory offences.
7. Article 51A(i) alone does not create an offence.
8. Clause (j) covers individual and collective activity.

*The child's right, the State's directive and the parent/guardian's duty are complementary but
legally different.*

### 1. Exact wording of clause (k)

> **“who is a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to his child or, as the
> case may be, ward between the age of six and fourteen years.”**

Clause (k) differs grammatically from the earlier clauses because it applies to a citizen **who is
a parent or guardian**. It does not impose the parent duty on every citizen regardless of family
role.

Two exact-text traps matter:

- it says **“provide opportunities for education”**, not “personally teach”;
- it covers a **child or ward between six and fourteen years**.

### 2. The 86th Amendment

The **Constitution (Eighty-sixth Amendment) Act, 2002**:

1. inserted **Article 21A**, making free and compulsory education for children aged 6-14 a
   Fundamental Right;
2. recast **Article 45** towards early childhood care and education for children below six; and
3. inserted **Article 51A(k)** as the parent/guardian duty.

The amendment distributes responsibility:

| Actor | Constitutional role |
|---|---|
| Child aged 6-14 | Holder of the Article 21A right |
| State | Must provide free and compulsory education in the manner determined by law |
| Parent/guardian | Must provide the opportunity for the child or ward to receive education |

### 3. Right versus duty

The child's right cannot be defeated by saying that a parent failed in clause (k). The State
retains constitutional and statutory obligations to make schooling available and accessible.
Poverty, migration, disability, distance, discrimination or lack of documentation may constrain
a parent's capacity. A rights-based system must address those barriers rather than treating every
out-of-school child as proof of parental fault.

### 4. RTE Act, 2009 and Section 10

The **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009** operationalises Article 21A.
Section 10 states a duty of every parent or guardian to admit or cause the child or ward to be
admitted to elementary education in a neighbourhood school.

The statute supplies a more concrete administrative context, but the education framework remains
child-centred. A complete education-policy answer must additionally examine school access,
standards, inclusion, teaching capacity and learning outcomes.

### 5. Why “opportunity” matters

Providing opportunity may include:

- enrolling the child;
- permitting regular attendance;
- not withdrawing the child for avoidable labour or discriminatory reasons;
- cooperating with schooling and basic learning support.

It does not mean that a parent guarantees educational quality, teacher availability or school
infrastructure. Those are institutional responsibilities.

### 6. Education as civic capability

Education enables later performance of other duties:

```text
education
   -> constitutional awareness
   -> scientific temper and inquiry
   -> respect for diversity
   -> capacity for work and excellence
   -> informed democratic participation
```

Clause (k) is therefore not isolated. It supports the civic agency needed for the entire Article
51A framework.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A parental duty unfairly shifts the State's educational failure onto poor families.

**Reply:** The constitutional triangle prevents that shift. Article 21A places an enforceable
obligation on the State; Article 45 guides early-childhood policy; clause (k) adds family
responsibility.

**Residual:** Even a formally available school may be inaccessible because of quality, safety,
language, disability or livelihood barriers. Legal design must address capability, not merely
enrolment.

### UPSC application

- Pair 86th Amendment with Articles 21A, 45 and 51A(k).
- Distinguish right-holder, State addressee and parent/guardian addressee.
- Quote “provide opportunities for education.”
- Name RTE Act Section 10 without converting it into a universal penal claim.
- Keep this constitutional explanation distinct from the broader analysis of education schemes,
  school quality and learning outcomes.

### Revision notes

1. Clause (k) was added by the 86th Amendment, 2002.
2. It raised the total number of duties from ten to eleven.
3. It applies to a citizen who is a parent or guardian.
4. It covers a child or ward aged 6-14.
5. The wording is “provide opportunities for education.”
6. Article 21A is the child's Fundamental Right.
7. Revised Article 45 concerns early childhood care and education below age six.
8. RTE Act, 2009 implements Article 21A.
9. RTE Section 10 states a parent/guardian admission duty.
10. Parental default does not cancel the child's right.
11. State capacity and access barriers remain constitutionally important.
12. Education enables performance of several other Fundamental Duties.

### Concept check

**Model answer:** The 86th Amendment created complementary obligations. Article 21A gives the
child an enforceable right and obliges the State to provide free and compulsory education.
Article 51A(k) adds a parent/guardian duty to provide opportunity. The parental duty cannot erase
the State's right-based responsibility.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the child's Fundamental Right as conditional on perfect
parental compliance.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

#### Model answer

The 86th Amendment, 2002 created a three-part constitutional design. Article 21A made free and
compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen a Fundamental Right. Article 45 was recast
to direct the State towards early childhood care and education below six. Article 51A(k) imposed
on a parent or guardian the duty to provide educational opportunity to a child or ward aged
six to fourteen.

The RTE Act, 2009 operationalises Article 21A; Section 10 gives the parental duty a statutory
admission context. However, the child's right cannot be defeated by poverty, migration,
disability or parental default. The State must ensure availability, access and non-discrimination.

The amendment therefore distributes, rather than transfers, responsibility: the child holds the
right, the State builds and finances the system, and the parent or guardian enables participation.

## Lesson 8 - Legal character: citizens-only, non-justiciable and non-self-executing

Progress: 8/11 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: legal status, rights-DPSP relationship, constitutional morality and limits

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried - Fundamental Duties with the necessary Fundamental Rights, DPSP and constitutional-morality links
CA search: "Not required - static constitutional doctrine"
CA found: None required
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: constitutional weight without direct offence

```text
ARTICLE 51A DUTY
      |
      +--> applies to citizens only
      |
      +--> non-justiciable: no direct writ compelling ordinary performance
      |
      +--> non-self-executing: clause alone does not define offence/penalty
      |
      +--> interpretive value for courts and institutions
      |
      +--> legislature may enact precise implementation law
                         |
                         v
     competence + Fundamental Rights + due process + proportionality
```

*Non-justiciability limits the remedy; it does not delete the text from the Constitution.*

### 1. Citizens only

Article 51A begins with “every citizen of India.” Fundamental Duties therefore differ from
rights such as Articles 14, 20, 21 and 25, which protect persons, including foreigners, subject
to their terms.

This is a scope distinction, not a claim that foreigners have no legal obligations. Foreigners
must comply with ordinary Indian law. They are simply not constitutional addressees of Article
51A.

### 2. Moral and civic duties - a conventional analytical classification

The Constitution does not label individual clauses “moral” or “civic.” The familiar classification
is a textbook aid based on the principal character of each duty:

| Clause | Conventional classification | Why it is placed there |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Civic | Public allegiance to the Constitution, institutions, Flag and Anthem |
| (b) | Moral | Internal commitment to freedom-struggle ideals |
| (c) | Civic | Protection of sovereignty, unity and integrity |
| (d) | Civic | Defence and national service when lawfully called |
| (e) | Moral | Fraternity and rejection of practices degrading women depend substantially on social conscience |
| (f) | Moral | Valuing and preserving composite culture requires appreciation and voluntary stewardship |
| (g) | Civic | Environmental protection is a shared public obligation capable of legislative support |
| (h) | Moral | Scientific temper, humanism, inquiry and reform describe habits of mind and ethical disposition |
| (i) | Civic | Public-property protection and non-violence directly govern public conduct |
| (j) | Moral | Striving for excellence is an aspirational standard of character and effort |
| (k) | Civic | A parent or guardian has a concrete social responsibility concerning education |

The classification explains differences in likely operation:

```text
civic duty  -> visible public conduct -> more readily supported by precise law
moral duty  -> conscience/value/character -> mainly education, persuasion and interpretation
```

It is not a rigid wall. Clause (e) has a public harmony dimension; clause (g) includes the moral
language of compassion; clause (j) can guide institutional standards. Nor does “civic” mean
automatically enforceable or “moral” mean constitutionally unimportant. Every clause has equal
constitutional location in Article 51A; any legal enforcement still requires a precise statute.

### 3. Non-justiciable

The Constitution does not provide a direct judicial remedy compelling a citizen to perform every
duty. A court ordinarily cannot issue a writ merely ordering a person to “develop scientific
temper” or “strive towards excellence.”

Non-justiciability means:

```text
no direct enforcement of the abstract clause
```

It does **not** mean:

```text
no constitutional significance
no legislative implementation
no interpretive use
automatic invalidity of every duty-linked law
```

### 4. Non-self-executing

A constitutional phrase such as “promote harmony” does not itself specify:

- prohibited conduct;
- required mental element;
- enforcement authority;
- procedure;
- defence;
- penalty.

Those elements require legislation. This is why Article 51A alone cannot be treated as a criminal
code.

### 5. Relationship with Fundamental Rights

Rights and duties are complementary but not symmetrical:

| Fundamental Rights | Fundamental Duties |
|---|---|
| Mostly enforceable against the State | Address citizens |
| Confer protected claims and remedies | Citizen obligations and civic norms |
| Restrictions require constitutional authority | May support a legitimate public purpose |
| Cannot be displaced by a vague duty | May influence interpretation within rights limits |

A law implementing a duty receives **no automatic immunity**. It must satisfy:

1. legislative competence;
2. the relevant restriction clause or constitutional source;
3. equality and non-arbitrariness;
4. fair procedure where applicable;
5. necessity and proportionality; and
6. basic-structure limits where constitutional amendment is involved.

### 6. Relationship with Directive Principles

DPSP and Fundamental Duties are both non-justiciable in the direct sense, but they have different
addressees:

```text
Part IV -> the State
Part IVA -> citizens
```

They often converge:

- Article 48A and Article 51A(g) on environment;
- Articles 45 and 51A(k) on education;
- social justice and clause (e)'s dignity/harmony concerns.

Their convergence supports coherent interpretation, not the creation of an unlimited combined
power.

### 7. Constitutional morality

Constitutional morality means fidelity to constitutional forms, procedures and values rather than
personal, partisan or majoritarian preference. Fundamental Duties can contribute to this civic
ethic:

- clause (a) supports constitutional methods;
- clause (e) supports fraternity and dignity;
- clause (h) supports inquiry and reform;
- clause (i) supports non-violence;
- clause (j) supports institutional quality.

However, constitutional morality is derived from the Constitution as a whole, including rights,
structure and democratic accountability. It cannot be reduced to Article 51A or used as a label
for a judge's personal morality.

### 8. Rights and duties as reciprocal citizenship

The popular “two wheels” metaphor is useful only with a qualification. Rights protect individuals
against majoritarian and State power. Duties encourage responsible exercise of citizenship.
Rights do not become rewards available only to dutiful people. A citizen who breaches a duty
does not lose Fundamental Rights except through constitutionally valid law and procedure.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Non-justiciable duties are legally meaningless moral advice.

**Reply:** They influence interpretation, support civic education and provide constitutional
purposes for precise legislation. Their indirect operation is a deliberate legal form.

**Residual:** Indirect force can become unpredictable if courts or governments invoke duties
without identifying the exact clause, statute and rights test. Discipline in reasoning is
essential.

### UPSC application

- Citizens-only is a constitutional-scope rule, not freedom from ordinary law for foreigners.
- Explain moral/civic as a conventional functional classification, not constitutional labels.
- Define non-justiciable and non-self-executing separately.
- State that implementing laws remain fully reviewable.
- Compare Part III, Part IV and Part IVA by addressee, enforceability and function.
- Use constitutional morality as a whole-Constitution concept.
- Never argue that rights depend on prior performance of duties.

### Revision notes

1. Article 51A applies to citizens only.
2. Foreigners remain bound by ordinary law.
3. Conventional civic duties: (a), (c), (d), (g), (i) and (k); moral duties: (b), (e), (f), (h) and (j).
4. The classification explains dominant function but permits overlap and creates no hierarchy.
5. Duties are non-justiciable in direct enforcement.
6. They are also non-self-executing.
7. Abstract duties do not define offence elements or penalties.
8. Legislatures may enact precise implementation laws.
9. Such laws receive no automatic constitutional immunity.
10. Competence, rights, due process and proportionality remain applicable.
11. Part IV addresses the State; Part IVA addresses citizens.
12. Rights and duties are complementary but not symmetrical.
13. Rights are not conditional rewards for obedient citizens.
14. Constitutional morality arises from the whole constitutional text and structure.

### Concept check

**Question:** A statute says that it implements Article 51A(c). Is that statement sufficient to
save the statute from an Article 19 challenge?

**Model answer:** No. Implementing sovereignty, unity or integrity may identify a legitimate
constitutional purpose, but the law must still be competent, fall within the applicable Article
19 restriction framework, use sufficiently precise means and satisfy necessity and
proportionality. Article 51A creates no automatic immunity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a duty label as a substitute for constitutional review.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Fundamental Duties are constitutionally weighted but neither directly enforceable nor legally
inert.” Explain. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 51A addresses every citizen and states eleven civic and moral duties. They are
non-justiciable: the Constitution creates no direct writ compelling ordinary performance.
They are also non-self-executing because phrases such as harmony, scientific temper or excellence
do not themselves define offence elements, procedure or penalties.

Yet duties are not legally inert. Courts may use them as interpretive guides, particularly where
statutes or constitutional values admit choice. Legislatures may enact precise laws protecting
national honour, public property, harmony, environment or education. Duties also support civic
education and constitutional morality.

Their force remains bounded. An implementing law must possess legislative competence and comply
with Articles 14, 19, 21 and other relevant provisions. It must satisfy due process and
proportionality; invocation of Article 51A creates no automatic immunity. Rights also do not
depend on prior dutiful conduct.

Thus, Part IVA occupies an intermediate constitutional space: stronger than ordinary moral advice
but weaker than a self-executing offence or directly enforceable claim. Its legitimate influence
is interpretive, educational and legislative, within the limits of the whole Constitution.

## Lesson 9 - Judicial use: interpretation without judicial legislation

*Judicial use is strongest when the court identifies the exact duty, the legal provision being

### 1. AIIMS Students' Union - interpretive guide

In ***AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS* (2001)**, the Supreme Court stated that Fundamental Duties,
though not enforceable by writ, provide a valuable guide and aid to interpretation. In a case of
doubt or choice, Article 51A may help resolve the issue and shape relief.

The proposition is interpretive, not penal:

```text
ambiguity or constitutional choice
              +
relevant Article 51A value
              =
guided interpretation
```

It does not permit a court to disregard clear statutory text or create an offence.

### 2. National symbols - a three-case discipline

| Case | Article 51A use | Limit preserved |
|---|---|---|
| *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986) | Respect for Anthem recognised | Conscience and expression protected respectful non-singing |
| *Naveen Jindal* (2004) | Flag dignity and constitutional values relevant | Respectful display protected as expression, subject to valid law |
| *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* judgment (2018) | Anthem respect remained relevant | Earlier compulsory cinema direction modified; institutional restraint |

These cases reject both extremes: national symbols are neither legally unprotected nor a basis
for boundless compulsion.

### 3. Environment and animal welfare

***M.C. Mehta* (1991)** used Article 51A(g) to support environmental education and public
awareness. This route addresses the social conditions for compliance.

***A. Nagaraja* (2014)** read compassion and humanism with the animal-welfare statute. The
**2023 Jallikattu Constitution Bench** then reviewed later amendment laws, declined to recognise
animal Fundamental Rights in the form argued and upheld the amendments. Case law therefore
develops through changed statutes and facts; one attractive quotation cannot replace the latest
holding.

### 4. Aruna Roy - value education and secularism

In ***Aruna Roy v. Union of India* (2002)**, the Court upheld value-based and comparative
religious education in the challenged curriculum framework, distinguishing education about
religions and shared values from prohibited religious instruction or indoctrination. Clauses
51A(e), (f) and (h) help explain harmony, composite culture and inquiry.

The case does not authorise State-sponsored proselytisation. Its value lies in the distinction
between plural knowledge and religious instruction.

### 5. Mohan Kumar Singhania - use narrowly

***Mohan Kumar Singhania* (1991/AIR 1992 SC 1)** provides a clause (j) link to training and
excellence in public services. It is not a safe authority for the broad textbook sentence that
any duty-implementing law is automatically reasonable under Articles 14 and 19.

An often-repeated but unnamed **1992** proposition says that a court may consider a law's genuine
Fundamental-Duty purpose while assessing reasonableness under Articles 14 and 19. Use it only as a
general proposition. Do not attribute it to *AIIMS Students' Union*, *Indra Sawhney* or any other
case unless the specific citation and holding have first been verified.

### 6. A disciplined judicial test

When Article 51A is invoked, ask:

1. Which exact clause applies?
2. Is the court interpreting a statute, reviewing a restriction or designing relief?
3. What independent legal source authorises the obligation?
4. Which right or institutional limit is affected?
5. Is the measure precise, necessary and proportionate?
6. Is the cited statement the ratio, an observation or a later-distinguished proposition?

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Interpretive use allows courts to make non-justiciable duties indirectly
enforceable.

**Reply:** The risk is controlled when courts anchor relief in enforceable rights, statutes or
jurisdiction and use duties only as interpretive values.

**Residual:** The line can blur. Courts should state the independent legal basis and avoid
transforming broad moral language into obligations that legislatures never defined.

### UPSC application

- Use *AIIMS Students' Union* for interpretive value.
- Use national-symbol cases as a rights-duty sequence, not isolated slogans.
- Distinguish *A. Nagaraja* (2014) from the 2023 Jallikattu judgment.
- Use *Aruna Roy* for value education versus indoctrination.
- Use *Mohan Kumar Singhania* narrowly for excellence.
- Label oral observations, interim orders and later-distinguished holdings accurately.

### Revision notes

1. Courts may use duties as interpretive guides.
2. Duties do not themselves authorise judicial creation of offences.
3. *AIIMS Students' Union* is the principal interpretive-value anchor.
4. *Bijoe Emmanuel* protects respectful conscientious non-singing.
5. *Naveen Jindal* protects respectful Flag display subject to valid law.
6. The 2018 *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* judgment modified compulsory cinema treatment.
7. *M.C. Mehta* supports environmental education and awareness.
8. *Aruna Roy* distinguishes plural value education from religious instruction.
9. *A. Nagaraja* and the 2023 Jallikattu decision must retain different statutory outcomes.
10. *Mohan Kumar Singhania* is a clause (j) excellence case.
11. The unnamed 1992 textbook proposition must not be misattributed.
12. Ratio, observation, interim direction and later law must remain separate.

### Concept check

**Question:** What fact would make a court's use of Article 51A interpretive rather than an
impermissible creation of a new offence?

**Model answer:** The court would identify an existing right, statute or jurisdictional source
and use the relevant duty to interpret ambiguity or constitutional purpose. If liability or
punishment rests only on broad Article 51A language without legislatively defined conduct and
procedure, the court would effectively be creating an offence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that every judicial reference to a duty makes the duty
directly enforceable.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Evaluate the Supreme Court's use of Fundamental Duties as aids to constitutional interpretation.
(15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

The Supreme Court has treated Article 51A as constitutionally significant despite its
non-justiciability. *AIIMS Students' Union* states that duties can guide interpretation where
there is doubt or choice. *M.C. Mehta* used Article 51A(g) to support environmental education,
while *Aruna Roy* connected harmony, composite culture and inquiry with value education.

The national-symbol cases show the necessary limit. *Bijoe Emmanuel* protected respectful
non-singing under expression and conscience; *Naveen Jindal* protected respectful Flag display;
and the 2018 *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* judgment illustrates restraint against deriving broad
compulsion directly from the duty. Animal-welfare jurisprudence likewise requires distinction
between *A. Nagaraja* and the later 2023 statutory setting.

This interpretive use is legitimate when the court identifies an independent right, statute or
remedial power and applies ordinary constitutional review. It becomes problematic if Article
51A alone supplies offence, liability or a legislative scheme.

Thus, duties may orient interpretation and relief, but legality, rights, institutional
competence and proportionality remain controlling.

*The Verma Committee's central insight was operational rather than amendment-based: several
duties already had legal support through specific statutes.*

### 1. What the Verma Committee did

The **Justice J.S. Verma Committee on Operationalisation of Fundamental Duties of Citizens
(1999)** examined how duties could be made effective. It did **not** add a new duty and did not
amend Article 51A. It:

- identified then-existing laws that implemented facets of duties;
- emphasised education, awareness and sensitisation;
- treated operationalisation as wider than punishment; and
- showed that precise legislation, not a single vague duty offence, was the practical route.

### 2. Historical accuracy: IPC then, BNS now

In 1999, the operative general criminal code was the **Indian Penal Code, 1860**. The Committee's
references must therefore remain historical IPC references. The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**
came into force on **1 July 2024**.

The correct comparison is:

```text
VERMA COMMITTEE, 1999              PRESENT COMPARISON
identified then-existing IPC       BNS now contains relevant fields
provisions                         such as sections 196 and 197
```

It is historically wrong to say that the 1999 Committee identified BNS sections.

### 3. National honour and harmony

| Law | Precise field | Article 51A connection |
|---|---|---|
| Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 | Defined insult to Constitution/Flag; intentional prevention or disturbance of Anthem singing | Clause (a) |
| Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 | Punishes enforcement of disabilities arising from untouchability | Fraternity, dignity and equality context; not a general religion-offence law |
| Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, sections 196-197 | Enmity between groups and assertions prejudicial to national integration, subject to exact statutory ingredients | Clauses (c) and (e) |

Do not replace statutory elements with a broad statement that “disharmony violates Article
51A.” Criminal liability follows the enacted provision and proof.

### 4. UAPA scope - precise formulation

The **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967** addresses unlawful activities, unlawful
associations and specified terrorism-related fields under its provisions. Its relevance lies in
conduct affecting sovereignty and territorial integrity.

It is inaccurate to describe UAPA simply as a law for declaring a “communal organisation”
unlawful. An association must satisfy the Act's statutory grounds and procedure; communal
identity as a label is not the legal test.

### 5. Elections and social harmony

The **Representation of the People Act, 1951** treats certain religious appeals and promotion of
enmity in connection with elections as corrupt practices, notably through sections 123(3) and
123(3A). Disqualification for corrupt practice operates through the Act's legal machinery,
including section 8A.

This supports the constitutional objective of elections based on civic choice rather than
religious hostility. A complete election-law answer must separately examine the statutory
ingredients, adjudicatory process and consequences of a proved corrupt practice.

### 6. Environment, public property and education

| Statute | Operational field |
|---|---|
| Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 | Species, hunting, protected areas and trade controls relevant to clause (g) |
| Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 1980 | Current short title of the former Forest (Conservation) Act; regulates specified forest-land use |
| Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984 | Defined damage to public property relevant to clause (i) |
| Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, section 10 | Parent/guardian admission duty relevant to clause (k) |

These laws do not “enforce Article 51A as a whole.” Each defines a bounded field with its own
elements, institutions and remedies.

### 7. The only current linkage: Durga Dutt

In ***Durga Dutt v. Union of India*, W.P.(C) 67/2022**, the Supreme Court's official written
order dated **11 September 2024** requested the Attorney General to file a short synopsis of
Central and State enactments that “effectuate and catalyse” different facets of Article 51A and
relisted the matter.

The status control as of **30 September 2026** is limited and precise:

- no later official written final merits disposition was located in the docket/order search;
- the 11 September 2024 order did not make Fundamental Duties directly enforceable;
- it did not dispose of the petition on merits; and
- reported oral observations about legislative directions are not a final ratio.

The genuine current relevance is the Court's focus on the **existing enactment map**, not a claim
that a new comprehensive enforcement code has been judicially created.

### 8. Why a single comprehensive penal code is risky

A general offence of “failing to perform Fundamental Duties” would face serious problems:

- vague standards such as noble ideals, composite culture or excellence;
- uncertain mental elements;
- unequal or selective enforcement;
- chilling effects on speech, conscience and dissent;
- disproportionate penalties;
- legislative-competence and federal questions.

Selective, precise statutes and civic education are more consistent with constitutional design.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Without penalties, duties remain symbolic.

**Reply:** Many duties are already supported by laws, while others are better promoted through
education, institutions and social norms. Not every constitutional value should become a
criminal prohibition.

**Residual:** Fragmented laws may leave implementation gaps. Reform should identify a concrete
harm and design a proportionate remedy rather than criminalising abstract disobedience.

### UPSC application

- Verma Committee year: 1999; function: operationalisation and mapping, not insertion.
- Preserve historical IPC context; use BNS only as the present successor comparison.
- State UAPA's unlawful-activity/association field precisely.
- Add PDPP Act and RTE Section 10 to the implementation map.
- Use the current forest statute's official short title.
- Treat *Durga Dutt* through the official written order and status caveat only.
- Every implementing law remains subject to review.

1. The Verma Committee was constituted for operationalisation of duties.
2. It reported in 1999 and identified existing laws.
3. It did not add duties or amend Article 51A.
4. Education and awareness were important implementation routes.
5. Its criminal-law context was the IPC, not BNS.
6. BNS came into force on 1 July 2024.
7. BNS sections 196-197 are present analogues in harmony/national-integration fields.
8. The Protection of Civil Rights Act concerns untouchability disabilities, not generic religious offences.
9. UAPA's test is statutory unlawful activity/association, not a “communal organisation” label.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Operationalising Fundamental Duties requires a map of precise laws, not a single vague penal

- public-service participation;
- assistance during disasters.

The tax duty was specifically recommended by the Swaran Singh Committee but not adopted.
Omission means these are **not Fundamental Duties under Article 51A**. It does not mean they can
never be legal duties under statutes.

The reply is that no constitutional list can exhaust responsible citizenship. Excessive
enumeration may produce symbolic clauses or constitutionalise ordinary policy.

### 2. Criticism: vague and difficult language

Terms such as:

- noble ideals;
- composite culture;
- scientific temper;
- excellence;

admit disagreement. Vagueness is especially dangerous when punishment is proposed. The strongest
reply is functional differentiation:

```text
broad clause -> civic education and interpretation
precise harm -> carefully drafted statute
criminal penalty -> strict legality and procedural protection
```

### 3. Criticism: a code of moral precepts

Because duties are non-justiciable, critics call them moral advice. This description is partly
correct but incomplete. Their functions include:

1. constitutional education;
2. guidance for public institutions;
3. interpretation of statutes and rights;
4. support for precise legislation;
5. a language of reciprocal citizenship.

Indirect force is still constitutional force, although weaker than a directly enforceable right.

### 4. Criticism: superfluous

Some argue that good citizens would respect the environment, reject violence or protect public
property even without Part IVA. Yet constitutions do more than prohibit misconduct. They identify
public values, allocate responsibility and educate institutions.

The limitation is real: constitutional declaration does not guarantee conduct. Without education,
credible institutions and fair enforcement, duties can become ceremonial slogans.

### 5. Criticism: wrong placement

Part IVA follows Part IV, which may visually suggest lower status than Fundamental Rights.
Critics argue that duties should have followed Part III.

The reply is legal rather than graphical:

- Part IVA is a distinct constitutional Part;
- placement does not merge it into DPSP;
- enforceability depends on text and remedy, not page order.

Still, the criticism reveals a pedagogical problem: many citizens know rights better than duties,
or hear duties only as moral commands detached from rights.

### 6. Emergency-origin debate as evidence

The insertion debate supports neither simple celebration nor simple dismissal. H.R. Gokhale's
“sobering effect” rationale shows the government's concern with discipline and established legal
order. Indira Gandhi's “not to smother rights” formulation defended duties as a democratic balance,
not a replacement for rights. The parliamentary Opposition's strong resistance records the
contemporaneous fear that the State was moralising citizens during an Emergency.

These positions should be evaluated rather than quoted decoratively. Gokhale's language supports
the paternalism objection; Indira Gandhi's formulation supplies the strongest rights-compatible
defence; Opposition and later Janata-era retention show a movement from contested insertion
towards qualified acceptance, not an original consensus.

### 7. Utility

Fundamental Duties can:

- remind citizens that democracy needs responsible conduct;
- support harmony, dignity and non-violence;
- strengthen environmental and educational citizenship;
- guide interpretation;
- justify precise laws protecting public goods;
- encourage institutional and individual excellence.

The textbook description of duties as a codification of values found in Indian traditions,
mythologies, religions and practices can strengthen this civic-education function only when read
pluralistically. It cannot define one official Indian way of life or protect an inherited
practice from equality, dignity, humanism and reform.

They are most useful when framed positively - as participation in constitutional democracy - and
least useful when deployed to stigmatise dissent.

### 8. Should duties be made justiciable?

A binary answer is weak. The duties differ:

| Duty type | Appropriate operational route |
|---|---|
| Precisely harmful conduct: Flag insult, property damage, wildlife offences | Statute with defined offence and procedure |
| Broad civic values: noble ideals, composite culture, scientific temper, excellence | Education, institutional practice and interpretation |
| Shared responsibility: environment, harmony, education | Combination of statute, policy, awareness and rights |

Blanket justiciability would ignore these differences. Selective legal implementation already
exists and is constitutionally safer.

### 9. Reform agenda

1. Teach duties together with Fundamental Rights and DPSP, never as obedience alone.
2. Use plain-language civic education with case-based examples.
3. Review laws for gaps, duplication and rights compliance.
4. Avoid adding new duties merely for symbolism.
5. If a new duty is proposed, identify its unique constitutional function.
6. Use non-penal tools before criminalisation where harm can be addressed effectively.
7. Require every coercive measure to satisfy legality, necessity and proportionality.
8. Publish transparent implementation and rights-impact reviews.

### 10. Qualified verdict

Fundamental Duties are neither empty slogans nor enforceable rights in reverse. They are
constitutional standards of responsible citizenship. Their democratic value depends on three
limits:

```text
duty must be identified precisely
law must remain rights-compatible
patriotism must not become compelled conformity
```

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A democratic Constitution should restrain the State, not moralise citizens.

**Reply:** Democracy also depends on non-violence, fraternity, environmental responsibility and
care for public institutions. A citizen ethic is legitimate when it does not condition rights or
authorise vague punishment.

**Residual:** Political actors may still use duties selectively against critics while ignoring
State obligations. The continuing safeguard is whole-Constitution reasoning and equal
application.

### UPSC application

- Present all five textbook criticisms and the Emergency-origin concern.
- Use the Gokhale-Indira-Opposition debate to show competing rationales, not as proof by quotation.
- Reply to each rather than listing criticism alone.
- Reject the false choice between total non-enforcement and blanket justiciability.
- Recommend selective precise legislation plus civic education.
- Conclude with rights, legality and proportionality.
- Do not propose vote or tax as existing duties; label them reform proposals only.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “make every Fundamental Duty judicially enforceable” an inadequate reform

**Model answer:** The duties differ in precision and function. Public-property damage can be

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that constitutional importance always requires direct court

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Critically examine whether Fundamental Duties should be made justiciable. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Fundamental Duties are criticised as vague, non-exhaustive and non-justiciable. Terms such as
“noble ideals,” “composite culture” and “excellence” are unsuitable for direct penal enforcement,
while the Emergency context creates a continuing risk of coercive patriotism.

However, complete non-enforcement is not the present position. Precise statutes protect national
honour, public property, electoral harmony, wildlife and education. Courts use Article 51A as an
interpretive aid, and education can promote scientific temper, fraternity and environmental
responsibility.

Blanket justiciability would erase these differences and could chill speech, conscience and
dissent. Conversely, leaving every duty purely symbolic would ignore genuine public harms.

The preferable model is differentiated operationalisation: defined offences for identifiable
harm; civil and administrative duties where suitable; civic education for broad values; and
judicial interpretation anchored in independent rights or statutes. Every coercive law must
satisfy competence, equality, due process and proportionality.

Thus, Fundamental Duties should not become directly justiciable as one undifferentiated code.
Their constitutional force should remain selective, precise and rights-respecting.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Block A - History and exact text

### Cumulative check A1 - committee, amendment and count

**Question:** A statement says: “The Swaran Singh Committee recommended ten Fundamental Duties,
which the 42nd Amendment inserted unchanged.” Identify every error.

**Model answer:** The Committee recommended eight duties, not ten. The 42nd Amendment inserted ten
duties as clauses (a)-(j), so it did not adopt the Committee's count unchanged. The Committee's
tax-duty and broad penalty/immunity proposals were not incorporated. Clause (k) was added only by
the 86th Amendment, 2002.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating recommendation, enactment and present count as one event.

### Cumulative check A2 - exact-clause allocation

**Question:** Allocate these ideas to their exact clauses: composite culture, national service,
scientific temper, public property and parent/guardian educational opportunity.

**Model answer:** Composite culture is clause (f); defence and national service when called is
clause (d); scientific temper, humanism, inquiry and reform are clause (h); public property and
abjuring violence are clause (i); and parent/guardian educational opportunity for a child or ward
aged six to fourteen is clause (k).

**Misconception to avoid:** Grouping duties by theme but losing their clause letters and exact
qualifiers.

## Block B - Rights, interpretation and enforcement

### Cumulative check B1 - national symbols

**Question:** A State circular requires every student to sing the National Anthem and treats
respectful silence as misconduct. What constitutional sequence should test it?

**Model answer:** Begin with Article 51A(a)'s duty of respect, then apply *Bijoe Emmanuel*: sincere
conscientious non-singing accompanied by respectful conduct is not automatically disrespect.
Identify the circular's legal authority and test it under Articles 19(1)(a) and 25, including
legality, necessity and proportionality. Deliberate obstruction or statutory insult is distinct.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that a Fundamental Duty automatically validates every
executive circular.

### Cumulative check B2 - environment and animals

**Question:** Why is it unsafe to cite *A. Nagaraja* (2014) as if it conclusively invalidates every
later law permitting a traditional animal event?

**Model answer:** The 2014 case interpreted the then statutory setting and gave significant weight
to Articles 51A(g) and (h). The 2023 Constitution Bench reviewed later State amendment laws,
treated the statutory defects as addressed and upheld them. Compassion remains relevant, but the
latest law, legislative findings and later holding must be considered.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating an earlier constitutional quotation as immune from later
statutory and precedential development.

## Block C - Operationalisation and reform

### Cumulative check C1 - law versus abstract duty

**Question:** Why can public-property damage be criminally punished while a general failure to
“strive towards excellence” cannot safely be treated as an offence?

**Model answer:** The PDPP Act defines damage to public property, legal ingredients, procedure and
consequence. “Excellence” is an evaluative civic standard with no inherent offence elements,
mental state or measurable threshold. Criminal legality requires precision; clause (j) is better
used for education, institutional standards and interpretation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing that every constitutional duty must use the same enforcement
instrument.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question

“Fundamental Duties are not merely the converse of Fundamental Rights.” Explain. (10 marks, 150
words)

### Model answer

Fundamental Rights and Fundamental Duties are complementary but not mirror images. Rights create
enforceable claims, principally against the State, and protect citizens and, for several rights,
all persons. Article 51A addresses citizens and states civic and moral obligations.

Duties are non-justiciable and non-self-executing: a citizen cannot ordinarily be compelled by
writ to develop scientific temper or excellence, and Article 51A alone defines no offence.
Nevertheless, duties guide interpretation, support civic education and provide purposes for
precise laws concerning national honour, harmony, environment, public property and education.

Most importantly, rights are not rewards conditional on good behaviour. Any law implementing a
duty must satisfy legislative competence, equality, the relevant restriction clause, due process
and proportionality.

Thus, rights restrain public power and protect human freedom; duties cultivate responsible
citizenship. They operate within one constitutional system but differ in addressee, remedy and
legal effect.

**Final 10-mark rubric:** **definition/distinction 3 + legal character 2 + operating mechanisms 2
+ rights-review limitation 2 + concise verdict 1.**

## Final 15-mark question

Assess the constitutional significance of Fundamental Duties in light of their non-justiciable
character. (15 marks, 250 words)

### Model answer

Inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 on the background of the Swaran Singh Committee,
Fundamental Duties now comprise eleven clauses in Article 51A. They apply only to citizens and
are non-justiciable: the Constitution supplies no direct remedy compelling ordinary performance.
They are also non-self-executing because broad terms such as harmony, scientific temper or
excellence do not define offences.

Their significance is nevertheless substantial. First, they articulate a civic constitutional
ethic: respect for the Constitution, fraternity, women's dignity, environmental care,
non-violence and educational responsibility. Secondly, *AIIMS Students' Union* recognises their
interpretive value. *Bijoe Emmanuel* and *Naveen Jindal* show that such use must remain compatible
with expression and conscience. Thirdly, legislatures have enacted precise laws protecting
national honour, electoral harmony, wildlife, public property and education. Fourthly,
*M.C. Mehta* illustrates education and awareness as operational tools.

However, duty-linked laws obtain no automatic immunity. They must satisfy competence,
Fundamental Rights, due process and proportionality. Vague duties should not become a blanket
penal code or a vocabulary for suppressing dissent.

Therefore, Article 51A has indirect but real constitutional force: educational, interpretive and
legislative, disciplined by the rights and structure of the whole Constitution.

**Final 15-mark rubric:** **history/text 2 + legal status 3 + civic significance 2 + cases 3 +
legislative examples 2 + limitations 2 + verdict 1.**

## Final 20-mark question

“The future of Fundamental Duties lies in differentiated operationalisation rather than blanket
justiciability.” Critically evaluate. (20 marks, 250 words)

### Model answer

Fundamental Duties range from relatively concrete obligations - respect national symbols,
protect public property and provide educational opportunity - to open civic values such as noble
ideals, composite culture, scientific temper and excellence. Treating all eleven as one
judicially enforceable code would ignore these differences.

Blanket justiciability creates serious risks. Vague offence standards would offend legality,
invite selective enforcement and chill expression, conscience and democratic dissent. It would
also bypass legislative competence, procedure and proportionality. Rights cannot become rewards
dependent on prior dutiful conduct.

Yet complete moralisation is also inadequate. The Verma Committee, 1999 identified existing
statutory routes. The National Honour Act defines symbol-related offences; election law and the
BNS address specified forms of enmity and prejudicial assertions; wildlife and forest laws
support clause (g); the PDPP Act supports clause (i); and RTE Section 10 supports clause (k).
Courts may use duties interpretively, as in *AIIMS Students' Union* and *M.C. Mehta*, while
*Bijoe Emmanuel* demonstrates anti-coercion limits.

Differentiated operationalisation therefore uses:

- precise offences for demonstrable harm;
- civil or administrative regulation where proportionate;
- education and institutional practice for broad values; and
- rights-based judicial interpretation.

The approach is not weak enforcement. It is constitutional calibration: each duty receives the
instrument suited to its precision, harm and institutional setting, while legality, equality,
due process and proportionality remain controlling.

**Final 20-mark rubric:** **evaluative thesis 3 + heterogeneity of duties 3 + blanket-model
critique 4 + Verma/statutory evidence 4 + case-law limits 3 + differentiated framework 2 +
decisive verdict 1.**

# REMEDIATION

## Error 1 - committee count confusion

**Error:** Swaran Singh recommended ten duties.

**Repair:** Eight were recommended; ten were enacted in 1976; eleven exist after the 86th
Amendment.

## Error 2 - original-Constitution error

**Error:** Fundamental Duties formed part of the Constitution from 1950.

**Repair:** The original Constitution had no separate citizen-duty code. Part IVA was inserted in
1976.

## Error 3 - scope error

**Error:** Duties apply to every person in India.

**Repair:** Article 51A expressly addresses every **citizen**. Foreigners remain bound by ordinary
law but are not Article 51A addressees.

## Error 4 - enforceability error

**Error:** Non-justiciable means courts can never refer to duties.

**Repair:** Courts may use duties interpretively; direct performance ordinarily cannot be
compelled solely through Article 51A.

## Error 5 - offence error

**Error:** Breach of any duty is automatically criminal.

**Repair:** Article 51A is non-self-executing. Liability requires a precise law defining conduct,
procedure and consequence.

## Error 6 - rights-subordination error

**Error:** A law is valid whenever government says it implements a duty.

**Repair:** Competence, Articles 14/19/21/25, due process and proportionality remain applicable.

## Error 7 - national-symbol error

**Error:** Respect always requires compulsory singing.

**Repair:** *Bijoe Emmanuel* protects respectful conscientious non-singing; obstruction or insult
is a different legal category.

## Error 8 - education-triangle error

**Error:** Article 51A(k) makes parents solely responsible for schooling.

**Repair:** Article 21A creates the child's right and State obligation; clause (k) adds a
parent/guardian duty; Article 45 addresses early childhood.

## Error 9 - Verma/BNS time error

**Error:** The 1999 Verma Committee identified BNS sections.

**Repair:** It identified then-existing IPC provisions. BNS is a present successor comparison
after 1 July 2024.

## Error 10 - UAPA scope error

**Error:** UAPA is simply a law for banning communal organisations.

**Repair:** It operates through statutory definitions and procedures concerning unlawful
activities and associations and specified terrorism-related fields.

## Error 11 - case-status error

**Error:** The 11 September 2024 *Durga Dutt* order made duties enforceable.

**Repair:** It requested an enactment synopsis and relisted the matter. No later official final
merits disposition was located through 30 September 2026.

## Error 12 - omitted-duty error

**Error:** Voting and paying taxes are Fundamental Duties.

**Repair:** Neither appears in Article 51A. Tax payment was a rejected Swaran Singh proposal.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional origin and amendment control

- Original Constitution: Fundamental Rights plus Directive Principles; no separate citizen-duty
  chapter.
- Inspiration: textbook source is the erstwhile USSR; comparative democracy claims must be
  qualified.
- Congress Party set up the Swaran Singh Committee in 1976; the Congress Government at the Centre
  accepted the central recommendation and enacted the amendment.
- Swaran Singh Committee: recommended a separate chapter and **eight** duties.
- Rejected Committee proposals: tax duty; general penalties; protection of penalty laws from
  constitutional challenge.
- 42nd Amendment, 1976: inserted Part IVA, Article 51A and ten clauses (a)-(j).
- 86th Amendment, 2002: inserted clause (k); present total eleven.
- Textbook feature: duties codify values associated with India's traditions, mythologies,
  religions, practices and way of life; constitutionally, this means plural inherited values
  filtered through equality, dignity, humanism and reform, not one essential culture.
- H.R. Gokhale offered the “sobering effect” discipline rationale; Indira Gandhi defended duties
  as democratic balance “not to smother rights”; the parliamentary Opposition strongly resisted
  their insertion.
- Emergency provenance supports caution; Janata-era retention and the 2002 addition support later
  constitutional acceptance.

```text
(g) protect/improve environment + compassion
(h) scientific temper + humanism + inquiry + reform
(i) public property + abjure violence
(j) individual/collective excellence
(k) parent/guardian educational opportunity, child/ward 6-14
```

Use the full constitutional quotations in an exact-text question; the rapid list is only a recall
device.

## National symbols and constitutional patriotism

- Clause (a) requires respect, not immunity of institutions from criticism.
- *Bijoe Emmanuel*: respectful standing with conscientious non-singing was not disrespect.
- *Naveen Jindal*: respectful Flag display is expression under Article 19(1)(a), subject to valid
  statutory restrictions and dignity.
- 2018 *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* judgment: cinema-hall playing became optional or directory; avoid
  deriving broad compulsory ritual directly from Article 51A.
- National Honour Act, 1971 supplies defined legal offences; Article 51A supplies value.
- National Song is not listed in clause (a).

## National ideals, integrity and service

- Clause (b): plural freedom-struggle ideals should be tied to constitutional democracy.
- Clause (c): sovereignty, unity and integrity are distinct.
- Unity does not eliminate lawful dissent, federal claims or policy criticism.
- Clause (d): service obligation is conditional - “when called upon to do so.”
- Security measures need law, evidence and proportionality.

## Fraternity, dignity and culture

- Clause (e) has two limbs: harmony/common brotherhood and renunciation of practices derogatory to
  women.
- Transcending diversity means equal civic belonging, not homogenisation.
- Women's dignity duty complements Articles 14, 15 and 21 and legislation.
- Clause (f): composite culture is plural, interactive and capable of reform.
- Culture cannot automatically immunise degrading practices; reform need not erase heritage.

## Environment, compassion and scientific temper

- Clause (g): protect **and improve** environment; compassion is separately stated.
- Constitutional triangle: Article 48A State directive + Article 51A(g) citizen duty + Article 21
  right.
- *M.C. Mehta*: education/awareness route.
- *A. Nagaraja* (2014) and 2023 Jallikattu decision must be distinguished.
- Clause (h): scientific temper is a method of evidence, revision and questioning.
- Humanism prevents technocratic indifference; inquiry leads to reform.
- Regulation of demonstrable harm differs from suppression of belief.

## Public property, non-violence and excellence

- Clause (i): public property is a common civic asset; violence is not protected protest.
- Peaceful protest remains protected; enforcement must target proven conduct proportionately.
- PDPP Act, 1984 supplies offence elements.
- Clause (j): excellence covers individual and collective activity.
- Excellence is effort and institutional quality, not elite outcome or performance at any cost.
- *Mohan Kumar Singhania*: narrow excellence/training anchor.
- Do not misattribute the textbook's unnamed 1992 general reasonableness proposition.

## Education duty

- Clause (k) applies to a citizen who is a parent or guardian.
- Exact phrase: “provide opportunities for education.”
- Child or ward: age six to fourteen.
- 86th Amendment triangle: Article 21A right; Article 45 below-six directive; Article 51A(k)
  parent/guardian duty.
- RTE Act, 2009 implements Article 21A; Section 10 states admission duty.
- Parent duty does not cancel the child's right or State responsibility.

## Legal character

- Citizens only; not all persons.
- Conventional textbook classification: civic - (a), (c), (d), (g), (i), (k); moral - (b), (e),
  (f), (h), (j). These are functional labels with overlap, not constitutional ranks.
- Non-justiciable: no direct writ for ordinary performance.
- Non-self-executing: no automatic offence, procedure or penalty.
- Interpretive: duties may guide courts and institutions.
- Legislative: precise statutes may implement defined facets.
- No automatic immunity: competence, Articles 14/19/21/25, due process and proportionality remain.
- Rights are not conditional rewards for dutiful citizens.
- Part IV addresses the State; Part IVA addresses citizens.

## Judicial and legislative operation

- *AIIMS Students' Union*: interpretive guide.
- *Aruna Roy*: value education is distinct from religious indoctrination.
- Verma Committee, 1999: mapped existing laws and awareness measures; added no duty.
- Historical context: Committee referred to IPC-era law.
- Present comparison: BNS sections 196-197 in harmony/national-integration fields after 1 July
  2024.
- Protection of Civil Rights Act concerns untouchability disabilities.
- UAPA operates through precise unlawful-activity/association definitions and procedure.
- RPA 1951 addresses specified religious appeals and enmity as corrupt practices.
- Wildlife law, Van Adhiniyam, PDPP Act and RTE Act support clauses (g), (i) and (k).
- *Durga Dutt* official order, 11 September 2024: enactment synopsis requested; no final merits
  disposition located through 30 September 2026.

## Criticism, utility and reform verdict

- Criticisms: non-exhaustive, vague, moral, superfluous, wrong placement and Emergency
  paternalism.
- Voting and tax payment are not present duties.
- Utility: civic education, public ethics, interpretation, precise legislation and reciprocal
  citizenship.
- Blanket justiciability is unsafe.
- Differentiated route:
  - precise offence for demonstrated harm;
  - administrative/civil regulation where suitable;
  - education and institutional practice for broad values;
  - interpretation anchored in independent law.
- Final line: **Fundamental Duties are constitutionally weighted civic standards whose legitimate
  force is educational, interpretive and selectively legislative, always bounded by rights,
  legality and proportionality.**
