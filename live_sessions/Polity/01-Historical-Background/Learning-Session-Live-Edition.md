# Historical Background of the Indian Constitution — Live Session Edition

The Indian Constitution did not emerge on a blank institutional page. Between 1773 and
1947, successive statutes changed who controlled government, how power was centralised or
devolved, how Indians entered legislatures, how responsibility was divided, and how
sovereignty finally passed to Indian hands. The task is therefore not to memorise a shelf of
Acts. It is to see a constitutional system being assembled, tested, criticised and partly
rejected.

## Roadmap

|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Why do these Acts form one constitutional story? | Build analytical axes instead of narrating dates |
| 2 | How did Parliament begin controlling the Company, 1773–1793? | Distinguish regulation, double government and executive control |
| 3 | How did the Charter Acts transform a trader into an administrator, 1813–1853? | Separate commercial, legislative and civil-service changes |
| 4 | How did Crown rule move from control to limited Indian association, 1858–1892? | Explain nomination, devolution and scrutiny without overstating representation |
| 5 | How did communal representation develop, 1909–1932? | Distinguish separate electorates from reserved seats in a joint electorate |
| 6 | Why did the 1919 dyarchy fail, and how did reform move toward 1935? | Explain divided responsibility and the Simon-to-1935 pipeline |
| 7 | What exactly did the Government of India Act, 1935 create? | Master federation, lists, autonomy, safeguards and non-commencement traps |
| 8 | How did the Indian Independence Act, 1947 transfer constituent sovereignty? | Explain dominions, paramountcy and constitutional transition |

### Visual: six tracks running through the chronology

```text
1600        1765          1773–1858                    1858–1947
Trade  →  territorial  →  COMPANY RULE  →  transfer  →  CROWN RULE
company     power
                           │                              │

                           ├─ RESPONSIBILITY: executive discretion → limited ministers

```

*The dates matter because each Act moves one or more of these six constitutional tracks.*

### 1. Begin with the real puzzle

⚠️ **Inference:** Why should a student of the Constitution care about statutes enacted by a colonial
Parliament? Because institutions have histories. The Constitution of 1950 transformed the
source and purpose of public power, but it inherited an already functioning administrative
territory, civil service, court structure, provincial machinery and legislative vocabulary.

✅ **Fact:** The constitutional story begins before 1773:

- In **1600**, the East India Company entered India under a charter granted by **Queen
  Elizabeth I**, which gave it the exclusive right to trade in India.
- In **1765**, it obtained the **Diwani** of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa: rights connected with
  revenue and civil justice. A merchant corporation had become a territorial power.
- From **1773 to 1858**, Parliament progressively regulated Company government.
- In **1858**, governance passed directly to the British Crown.
- On **15 August 1947**, British rule ended and Indian constitution-making became legally
  sovereign.

The boundary is important. This topic explains the legal-institutional development from
Company rule to independence. The detailed composition and deliberations of the Constituent
Assembly belong to the next stage of study; nationalist movements and the full politics of
Partition belong to modern history.

### 2. The two phases are not merely date boxes

| Phase | Formal governing agent | Central constitutional problem |
|---|---|---|
| Company Rule, 1773–1858 | East India Company under growing parliamentary supervision | How can Britain control a commercial corporation exercising public power? |
| Crown Rule, 1858–1947 | British Crown through the Secretary of State, Viceroy and councils | How far can Indians be associated with government without conceding full sovereignty? |

⚠️ **Inference:** The Company phase built control and central administration. The Crown phase retained that
administrative spine while cautiously widening consultation, election and provincial
responsibility.

### 3. Six analytical axes

⚠️ **Analytical frame:** The following six axes organise the sourced chronology for
understanding and answer-writing; they are not labels used by the colonial statutes themselves.

#### Axis A — Control

The first question was not democracy but supervision. Parliament had to make Company
officials answerable, separate commercial from political management and eventually replace
the Company altogether.

#### Axis B — Centralisation and devolution

From 1773 to 1833, authority moved upward toward Bengal and then an all-India
Governor-General. From 1861 onward, selected legislative powers moved back toward provinces.
The Constitution inherited both tendencies: a federal division of powers and a strong Union.

#### Axis C — Representation

Representation developed by stages:

```text
nomination → recommendation-based indirect election → direct election
```

But representation is not identical to responsible government. A council may contain elected
members and still be unable to remove or control the executive.

#### Axis D — Responsibility

**Responsible government** means that ministers exercising executive power are answerable to
an elected legislature and must retain its confidence. Colonial statutes approached this
principle unevenly: dyarchy divided responsibility; provincial autonomy widened it; governors'
safeguards limited it.

#### Axis E — Federal design

The division of subjects between Centre and provinces developed before the Constitution.
The 1935 Act supplied the most elaborate colonial federal design, although its proposed
All-India Federation never came into operation.

#### Axis F — Sovereignty

Before 1947, ultimate legal authority remained imperial. The Indian Independence Act enabled
the Constituent Assembly to frame any constitution and repeal British legislation. The change
was therefore not merely a new office-holder; it was a transfer in the source of legal power.

### 4. A useful analogy — and its limit

⚠️ **Inference and analogy:** Think of the colonial statutes as scaffolding around a building. Some beams—provincial
administration, public services and courts—were retained and strengthened. Other parts—racial
rule, restricted franchise, official majorities and communal electorates—were removed.

The analogy helps separate **institutional continuity** from **political legitimacy**. Its
limit is that the Constitution was not a simple renovation of a colonial building. Popular
sovereignty, universal adult franchise, justiciable rights and republican government changed
the building's owner, purpose and governing principles.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling colonial statutes the Constitution's historical background risks
presenting imperial concessions as a smooth democratic gift.

**Reply:** The chronology should not be taught as benevolent progress. Many reforms protected
imperial control and divided political claims. Yet their institutions produced legal and
administrative experiences that the framers could retain, alter or reject. A critical account
can recognise institutional inheritance without legitimising colonial rule.

**Balanced assessment:** The useful question is not whether the Constitution was either
colonial or original. It is **what was inherited, what was democratised, and what was
repudiated**.

### 6. UPSC application

- **Prelims:** office-title changes, firsts, commencement versus mere provision, and
  1909/1919/1935 distinctions.
- **Mains:** continuity and rupture; centralisation versus devolution; evolution of
  representation; the 1935 Act as the largest single statutory source.
- **Answer rule:** organise by constitutional strands, not by dumping every Act in sequence.

### Revision notes

1. Queen Elizabeth I's 1600 charter gave the East India Company the exclusive right to trade
   in India.
2. The Company became a territorial power after the Diwani grant of 1765.
3. Company Rule runs from 1773 to 1858; Crown Rule from 1858 to 1947.
4. Parliamentary control is the first constitutional problem.
5. Centralisation reaches a major colonial climax in 1833.
6. Legislative devolution begins to return in 1861.
7. Representation, election and responsibility are distinct ideas.
8. Dyarchy divides responsibility; provincial autonomy enlarges it.
9. Federal machinery develops before 1950 but remains colonial in authority.
10. The 1947 Act transfers constituent freedom to Indian bodies.
11. The Constitution combines institutional continuity with democratic rupture.
12. Universal franchise and justiciable rights cannot be reduced to colonial inheritance.
13. A strong answer uses the axes of control, scale, voice, responsibility, territory and
    sovereignty.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “a chronological list of British Acts” an inadequate description of this topic?

**Model answer:** The Acts form a connected constitutional process. They changed parliamentary control over the Company, centralised and later devolved authority, widened representation, experimented with responsible government, designed federal institutions and finally transferred constituent sovereignty. Chronology supplies evidence, but the examinable meaning lies in these institutional movements and in the distinction between machinery later retained and colonial principles later rejected.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that every later Act was automatically more democratic; several widened participation while retaining decisive imperial safeguards.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why should the historical background of the Indian
Constitution be studied as an institutional evolution rather than as a catalogue of statutes?

**Directive decoding:** “Why” requires reasons and significance. Establish a thesis, organise
the body by institutional strands, and conclude with continuity plus rupture.

**Model answer (139 words):**

The historical background of the Constitution records the gradual construction of India's
governing machinery, not merely a succession of dates. The Regulating Act, 1773 began
parliamentary supervision and central administration; the Charter Act, 1833 created an
all-India Governor-General and central legislature. After 1858, the Crown retained this
centralised apparatus while the Councils Acts gradually introduced Indian association,
budgetary scrutiny and election. The 1919 Act experimented with provincial dyarchy, whereas
the 1935 Act supplied provincial autonomy, legislative lists, public-service commissions and
the Federal Court. Finally, the 1947 Act released constitution-making from imperial
subordination. These developments explain both continuity and rupture in 1950: administrative,
judicial and federal machinery was substantially retained, but restricted franchise,
communal electorates, official majorities and imperial sovereignty were rejected. Thus,
institutional strands—not isolated statutory features—show how colonial machinery was
converted into a democratic constitutional order.

**Why this earns marks:** Direct thesis; six named statutes; analysis of institutional change;
qualified conclusion rather than praise of colonial “progress.”

**Scoring guidance (10):** thesis and scope 1; control/centralisation 2; representation and
responsibility 2; federal/sovereignty transition 2; continuity-versus-rupture analysis 2;
coherent conclusion 1.

**Bridge forward:** Once a trading corporation became a territorial ruler, the immediate
problem was how Parliament could supervise its executive, judicial and commercial powers.

*The sequence moves from regulating a corporation to supervising a territorial government.*

### 1. Regulating Act, 1773 — the first constitutional intervention

✅ **Fact:** The Regulating Act was Parliament's first major statutory intervention in Company
government.

The Company was exercising public power but remained a commercial corporation. Parliament's
first response was not to abolish it; it was to regulate it.

The Act:

1. redesignated the Governor of Bengal as the **Governor-General of Bengal**;
2. created a four-member Executive Council;
3. made **Warren Hastings** the first Governor-General of Bengal;
4. subordinated Bombay and Madras to Bengal in important matters;
5. provided for a Supreme Court at Calcutta, established in **1774**, with one Chief Justice
   and three other judges;
6. prohibited Company servants from private trade and accepting presents or bribes;
7. required the Court of Directors to report revenue, civil and military affairs.

⚠️ **Inference:** Its constitutional importance can be organised into three moves:

```text
Company's political role recognised
             ↓
British Parliament asserts supervision
             ↓
foundation of central administration
```

> **High-yield distinction:** The Act created the Governor-General of **Bengal**, not the
> Governor-General of India.

### 2. Why the Supreme Court created friction

A court and an executive had been placed within an uncertain jurisdictional relationship.
If a Company official acted in an official capacity, could the Supreme Court intervene? Did
the Court control revenue administration? Which law applied to Indian inhabitants?

The **Amending Act of 1781**, also called the **Act of Settlement**, clarified the arrangement:

- official acts of the Governor-General, Council and Company servants were exempted from the
  Supreme Court's jurisdiction;
- revenue matters were excluded;
- the Court's jurisdiction over Calcutta inhabitants was retained;
- Hindu and Muslim personal laws were to apply to their respective defendants;
- appeals from provincial courts went to the Governor-General-in-Council;
- the Governor-General-in-Council could frame regulations for provincial courts and councils.

This was an early lesson in institutional design: creating a court is not enough; the legal
system must define jurisdiction, applicable law and appellate routes.

### 3. Pitt's India Act, 1784 — double government

The 1784 Act distinguished the Company's **commercial** functions from its **political**
functions:

| Institution | Principal sphere |
|---|---|
| Court of Directors | Commercial affairs |
| Board of Control | Political affairs, civil and military government, revenue supervision |

This arrangement is called **double government** because two institutions exercised different
forms of control over Company administration. The Act also:

- described Company territories for the first time as **British possessions in India**;
- gave the British Government supreme control over political administration.

Do not confuse this with the **Dual Government in Bengal after 1765**, where the Company held
Diwani functions while the Nawab formally retained Nizamat responsibilities. The terms sound
similar but describe different arrangements.

### 4. Acts of 1786 and 1793 — executive authority consolidates

Lord Cornwallis accepted the Governor-Generalship on the conditions that he could override
his Council in special cases and also act as Commander-in-Chief. The **Act of 1786** enabled
these arrangements.

The **Charter Act of 1793** then:

- extended the override power to future Governor-Generals and Governors;
- strengthened the Governor-General's control over Bombay and Madras;
- continued the Company's trade monopoly for twenty years;
- provided that the Commander-in-Chief would not automatically sit in the Council;
- charged the salaries of the Board of Control and its staff to Indian revenues.

The constitutional movement is therefore not merely “more British control.” It also produces
a stronger executive at the Indian end of the chain.

### 5. Example and limit

Imagine a company operating a city while its shareholders, a government regulator and a new
court all claim authority. The 1773 and 1784 Acts progressively clarify this chain.

The analogy explains divided supervision, but it has a limit: the Company was not running an
ordinary municipal service. It commanded armies, collected revenue and governed subjects who
had no democratic control over the supervising institutions.

### 6. Objection, reply and residual problem

**Objection:** These statutes regulated corruption and clarified offices but did not create
constitutionalism because Indians remained subjects without representation.

**Reply:** In the democratic sense, the objection is correct. Yet constitutional development
also includes the legal allocation and limitation of public authority. Parliamentary
supervision, central executive offices and defined judicial jurisdiction were genuine
institutional developments.

**Residual:** Accountability ran upward to British authorities, not downward to the governed.
This democratic deficit explains why later association and representation became unavoidable.

2. Warren Hastings was the first Governor-General of Bengal.
3. Bombay and Madras were subordinated to Bengal.
4. The Calcutta Supreme Court was established in 1774.
5. The 1781 settlement clarified official, revenue and personal-law jurisdiction.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did Pitt's India Act create “double government” without ending Company rule?

**Model answer:** It left commercial management with the Court of Directors but placed political affairs, revenue and civil-military administration under a new Board of Control. The Company therefore continued as the governing instrument, while the British Government gained superior political supervision. The duality concerned institutional control in Britain, not two sovereign governments in India.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse Pitt's institutional double government with the post-1765 Diwani–Nizamat Dual Government in Bengal.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Trace how the Acts from 1773 to 1793 converted the East
India Company's territorial power into a government under parliamentary supervision.

**Directive decoding:** “Trace” requires chronological movement with causal links, not a
feature list. Show what each intervention corrected or added.

**Model answer (145 words):**

After acquiring the Diwani in 1765, the East India Company exercised territorial power
without an adequate public-law framework. The Regulating Act, 1773 marked Parliament's first
systematic intervention: it created the Governor-General of Bengal, subordinated Bombay and
Madras, established the Calcutta Supreme Court and required reporting by the Court of
Directors. Jurisdictional conflict then led to the Act of Settlement, 1781, which protected
official acts, excluded revenue matters and clarified personal-law and appellate rules.
Pitt's India Act, 1784 made supervision more direct by separating commerce under the Court of
Directors from political control under the Board of Control. The 1786 Act strengthened the
Governor-General through special override power and combined civil-military command under
Cornwallis; the 1793 Charter extended executive override and central control. Thus, Parliament
did not immediately abolish Company government. It progressively enclosed it within political,
judicial and administrative supervision, though without accountability to Indians.

**Why this earns marks:** Chronology is causal; institutions are named; “regulation without
abolition” answers the precise demand; democratic limitation is acknowledged.

**Scoring guidance (10):** 1773 mechanism 2; 1781 jurisdiction 1.5; 1784 institutional split
2; 1786/1793 executive consolidation 1.5; causal analysis 2; qualification and conclusion 1.

**Bridge forward:** Parliamentary supervision now existed; the next question was how repeated
Charter renewals would strip away the Company's commerce and build an all-India administration.

*The three Acts successively weaken the Company's commercial identity and strengthen the
specialised machinery of an all-India government.*

### 1. Charter Act, 1813 — monopoly ends, sovereignty is asserted

✅ **Fact:** The Act:

- ended the Company's trade monopoly in India, **except trade in tea and with China**;
- asserted the sovereignty of the British Crown over Company territories;
- permitted Christian missionaries to enter;
- is described in the standard textbook formulation as providing for the **spread of
  western education** among inhabitants of British territories in India;
- set aside at least **one lakh rupees annually** for the revival and improvement of
  literature, encouragement of learned Indians and promotion of scientific knowledge;
- allowed local governments to levy taxes and punish non-payment.

✅ **Textual qualification:** The textbook formulation and the precise statutory wording must
be held together. Section 43 earmarked one lakh rupees annually for the “revival and improvement of literature,”
encouragement of learned Indians, and “the introduction and promotion of a
knowledge of the sciences.” The Act did **not** expressly settle English as the language of
education. Thus “spread of western education” is a standard interpretive summary, not an
English-language clause that may be quoted as the Act's literal wording.

### 2. Charter Act, 1833 — all-India centralisation

This Act was the final major step in the centralisation of Company government:

1. the Governor-General of Bengal became the **Governor-General of India**;
2. **Lord William Bentinck** became the first holder of that office;
3. the office received civil and military authority over British India;
4. Bombay and Madras lost their legislative powers;
5. the Governor-General-in-Council received exclusive legislative authority;
6. enactments were called **Acts**, rather than the earlier Regulations;
7. the Company's commercial activities ended, leaving it a purely administrative body;
8. Company territories were held “in trust” for the Crown;
9. open competition for civil employment was attempted, with a declaration against
   excluding Indians, but the arrangement was defeated by opposition from the Court of
   Directors.

The change from **Governor-General of Bengal** to **Governor-General of India** marks a shift
in scale. A presidency-centred office became the head of an all-India government.

### 3. Charter Act, 1853 — government becomes a specialised function

The last Charter Act:

- separated the **legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General's Council;
- added six legislative councillors and created a distinct legislative wing, often described
  as the Indian or Central Legislative Council;
- made legislation a specialised governmental process, using procedures resembling a
  “mini-Parliament”;
- introduced open competition for civil-service recruitment, followed by the **Macaulay
  Committee of 1854**;
- made the covenanted civil service formally open to Indians;
- continued Company rule without a fixed renewal term, signalling that Parliament could end
  it when it chose;
- introduced local representation: four of the six legislative members came from Madras,
  Bombay, Bengal and Agra.

### 4. Why 1833 and 1853 must not be merged

| Question | 1833 | 1853 |
|---|---|---|
| What happened to Company commerce? | Ended; Company became administrative | Company administration continued |
| What happened to territorial authority? | GG of India and exclusive central legislation | Existing centre gained a specialised legislative wing |
| Civil-service reform | Open competition attempted but frustrated | Open competition introduced; Macaulay Committee followed |
| Representation | No comparable local legislative representation | Four locally appointed legislative councillors |
| Constitutional significance | Centralisation | Functional separation and specialisation |

The two Acts are connected but not interchangeable. **1833 creates the all-India central
government; 1853 differentiates functions within it.**

### 5. Example and limit

A growing organisation may first place all branches under one headquarters and later separate
policy-making from daily administration. That resembles 1833 followed by 1853.

The limit is political: the legislative wing was not a sovereign democratic Parliament.
Local representation remained narrow, and executive responsibility to an elected Indian
majority did not exist.

### 6. Criticism and reply

**Objection:** Describing 1853 as the beginning of a parliamentary system exaggerates a
Council dominated by colonial authority.

**Reply:** It should not be called responsible parliamentary government. Its significance is
narrower: legislation became a distinguishable function with additional members and
procedures. Institutional differentiation can be historically important without being
democratic.

### 7. UPSC application and traps

- **1813:** monopoly ended except tea and China; Crown sovereignty asserted.
- **1813 education wording:** remember both the standard “spread of western education”
  formulation and Section 43's precise literature, learned-Indians and sciences language; do
  not turn either into an express English-language settlement.
- **1833:** GG of India; all-India legislation; Company commerce ended.
- **1853:** legislative-executive functional separation; competitive civil service; local
  representation.
- **1858, not 1853:** Company rule was abolished.
- Do not convert the 1813 education grant into an express English-education mandate.

### Concept check

**Question:** What different constitutional problems did the Charter Acts of 1833 and 1853 solve?

**Model answer:** The 1833 Act solved a problem of scale by centralising civil, military and legislative authority under the Governor-General of India and ending the Company's commercial role. The 1853 Act addressed functional specialisation within that central government by separating legislative from executive council work, introducing competitive civil-service recruitment and adding local legislative representation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say that the 1853 Act abolished Company rule; abolition and transfer to the Crown occurred in 1858.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Examine the Charter Acts of 1813, 1833 and 1853 as stages
in the transformation of the East India Company from merchant to administrator.

**Directive decoding:** “Examine” requires a proposition tested through evidence and
qualification. Explain the distinct contribution of each Act and avoid treating the change as
instantaneous.

**Model answer (149 words):**

The Charter Acts progressively stripped the East India Company of commerce and equipped it
with the machinery of a central administration. The 1813 Act ended its Indian trade monopoly,
except tea and China trade, and asserted Crown sovereignty, weakening the idea of an
independent commercial corporation. The 1833 Act completed the commercial transition: the
Company became purely administrative, the Governor-General of Bengal became the
Governor-General of India, and legislative authority was centralised for British India. The
1853 Act then specialised this government by separating the legislative and executive
functions of the Governor-General's Council, adding legislative councillors and introducing
competitive civil-service recruitment. Its locally nominated members also supplied a narrow
beginning of territorial representation. However, these changes produced neither popular
sovereignty nor responsible government; the Company remained the governing instrument until
1858. The Acts therefore transformed its function and administrative form before Parliament
finally replaced it with direct Crown rule.

**Why this earns marks:** Each Act performs a different analytical role; the answer explains
sequence, names offices and preserves the democratic limitation.

**Scoring guidance (10):** 1813 transition 2; 1833 centralisation and commercial end 2.5; 1853
specialisation and recruitment 2.5; transformation argument 2; qualification 1.

**Bridge forward:** By 1853 the Company had become a specialised administrative instrument,
making its replacement by direct Crown machinery after the Revolt of 1857 the next issue.

*The Crown reorganised authority quickly, but representative accountability advanced only in
small, controlled steps.*

### 1. Government of India Act, 1858 — who replaced the Company?

⚠️ **Inference:** The Revolt of 1857 exposed the political weakness of governing India through a corporation.

✅ **Fact:** The **Government of India Act, 1858**, also associated with the phrase “good government of
India,”:

- abolished the East India Company;
- transferred its territories, revenues and governmental powers to the British Crown;
- changed the Governor-General of India's additional designation to **Viceroy**, the Crown's
  representative; **Lord Canning** became the first Viceroy;
- abolished the Board of Control and Court of Directors, ending the 1784 double-government
  arrangement;
- created the **Secretary of State for India**, a British Cabinet minister responsible to
  Parliament;
- established a **15-member Council of India** to advise the Secretary of State;
- made the Secretary of State the **chairman** of the Council of India;
- constituted the Secretary of State-in-Council as a body capable of suing and being sued in
  India and Britain.

⚠️ **Inference:** This was a change in the supervising machinery, not a grant of representative government.
Power became more directly imperial and centrally answerable.

### 2. Indian Councils Act, 1861 — association without election

✅ **Fact:** After 1857, the Crown sought Indian cooperation. The 1861 Act made several important changes:

1. the Viceroy could nominate Indians as non-official members for legislative work;
2. in 1862, Canning nominated the Raja of Benaras, the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao;
3. legislative powers were restored to Bombay and Madras;
4. new councils were authorised for Bengal, the North-Western Provinces and Punjab, established
   in 1862, 1886 and 1897 respectively;
5. the **portfolio system**, introduced by Canning in 1859, received statutory recognition;
6. the Viceroy could issue emergency ordinances with a life of six months.

#### What is the portfolio system?

Instead of every Council member jointly handling every matter, a member was placed in charge
of one or more departments and could issue final orders on those departmental matters.

```text
Collective executive council
          ↓ distributes work
Finance | Home | Law | Revenue | other departments
          ↓
departmental responsibility and administrative specialisation
```

⚠️ **Inference:** This resembles a ministerial division of work, but the resemblance has limits. These members
were not collectively responsible to an elected legislature in the modern cabinet sense.

### 3. Why 1861 reverses one constitutional tendency

✅ **Fact:** The period from 1773 to 1833 centralised authority. The 1861 Act restored legislative powers
to Bombay and Madras and thus began a process of **legislative devolution**.

This produces a recurring Indian constitutional pattern:

```text
need for national coordination
             ↕
need for provincial autonomy
```

⚠️ **Inference:** The Constitution later manages the same tension through Union, State and Concurrent fields,
although democratic federalism is fundamentally different from colonial devolution.

### 4. Indian Councils Act, 1892 — scrutiny without control

✅ **Fact:** The 1892 Act:

- increased additional non-official members in central and provincial councils;
- retained official majorities;
- allowed discussion of the budget;
- allowed questions to the executive;
- provided that the Viceroy would nominate some Central Council non-officials on the
  recommendations of provincial legislative councils and the **Bengal Chamber of Commerce**;
- provided that Governors would nominate some provincial-council non-officials on the
  recommendations of district boards, municipalities, universities, trade associations,
  zamindars and chambers.

✅ **Fact:** The Act avoided the word **election**. Formally, members were nominated on recommendations;
substantively, it introduced a limited and indirect electoral element.

### 5. Three concepts that must remain separate

| Concept | Meaning | Colonial example |
|---|---|---|
| Association | Indians are included in a governing body | Nominated non-official members, 1861 |
| Representation | Members enter through a process linked to constituencies or bodies | Recommendation-based indirect element, 1892 |
| Responsibility | Executive must retain legislative confidence | Still absent under these Acts |

This distinction prevents a common overstatement: an enlarged council with questions and
budget discussion is not automatically a responsible government.

### 6. Strongest criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Nominated elites and indirect recommendation gave the appearance of
representation without transferring real power. Official majorities and executive control
could defeat criticism.

**Reply:** The criticism correctly identifies the narrow franchise and imperial design.
Nevertheless, budget discussion, questioning and territorial representation created practices
of legislative scrutiny. These practices later acquired democratic force when backed by
election and ministerial responsibility.

**Qualified verdict:** The period created the grammar of legislative accountability before it
created an executive accountable to Indians.

### 7. UPSC application

- **1858:** Company abolished; Crown, Viceroy and Secretary of State architecture.
- **1861:** nomination, portfolio system, legislative devolution and ordinance power.
- **1892:** budget discussion, executive questions and indirect electoral principle.
- **Trap:** Indian participation in 1861 did not mean direct election.
- **Trap:** non-official members or even a non-official numerical presence did not necessarily
  create a ministry responsible to the legislature.

### Revision notes

1. The 1858 Act abolished the Company and ended Pitt's double-government machinery.
2. Lord Canning became the first Viceroy.
3. The Secretary of State was a Cabinet minister accountable to British Parliament.
4. The Council of India had fifteen members and was advisory.
5. The 1861 Act began Indian association with law-making through nomination.
6. Bombay and Madras regained legislative powers.
7. Provincial legislative devolution reversed the earlier centralising movement.
8. The portfolio system divided executive work departmentally.
9. A Viceroy's ordinance could last six months.
10. The 1892 Act permitted budget discussion and questions.
11. Official majorities continued.
12. “Election” was avoided in statutory language; recommendation produced a limited indirect
    electoral element.
13. Association, representation and responsibility are not synonyms.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the 1892 Act be called a step toward representation but not responsible government?

**Model answer:** It introduced a limited indirect electoral element through nomination on the recommendation of representative bodies and enlarged legislative scrutiny through budget discussion and questions. Yet official majorities remained and the executive did not depend on legislative confidence. Members could criticise administration but could not form or dismiss a responsible ministry.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate the power to ask questions or discuss a budget with control over the executive.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Examine the constitutional significance and limits of the
Indian Councils Acts of 1861 and 1892.

**Directive decoding:** “Examine” demands both contribution and limitation. Use a
change-versus-control structure.

**Model answer (149 words):**

The Councils Acts of 1861 and 1892 cautiously introduced Indians into legislative government
without conceding executive responsibility. The 1861 Act enabled nomination of Indian
non-officials, recognised Canning's portfolio system and restored legislative powers to Bombay
and Madras. It therefore combined administrative specialisation with a reversal of the
centralising trend that had culminated in 1833. The 1892 Act enlarged councils, permitted
budget discussion and questions, and used recommendations from local
bodies and organised interests to fill some non-official seats. Although the statute avoided
the word “election,” this supplied a limited indirect representative element. Their limits
were decisive: official majorities remained, participation was socially narrow, members could
not control grants effectively, and the executive did not depend on legislative confidence.
The Acts thus created practices of association, scrutiny and territorial representation, but
not responsible government. They were preparatory institutions whose democratic potential
exceeded the imperial purpose for which they were designed.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares rather than lists, uses named mechanisms and makes the
critical association–representation–responsibility distinction.

### Visual: representation can be widened in different ways

```text

all eligible voters  ───────────→  any eligible candidate
```

*The decisive issue is not only whether seats exist, but who forms the electorate that chooses
their representatives.*

### 1. Indian Councils Act, 1909 — representation becomes communal

✅ **Fact:** The **Morley–Minto Reforms** enlarged legislative councils and their deliberative functions:

- the Central Legislative Council rose from 16 to 60 members;
- the Centre retained an official majority;
- provincial councils could have non-official majorities;
- members could ask supplementary questions and move budget resolutions;
- Indians entered executive councils;
- **Satyendra Prasad Sinha** became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council,
  serving as Law Member;
- Muslims received **separate electorates**;
- presidency corporations, chambers of commerce, universities and zamindars received separate
  representation.

Lord Minto is consequently associated with the title **“Father of Communal Electorate.”**

### 2. How a separate electorate works

Under a separate electorate, Muslim voters voted for Muslim representatives in seats assigned
to them. The arrangement did more than guarantee presence. It made political representation
depend on a legally defined communal electorate.

The mechanism matters:

```text
State classifies voters by community
              ↓
community receives a distinct electoral roll or voting channel
              ↓
candidate's mandate comes from that community electorate
              ↓
political bargaining is organised around communal representation
```

⚠️ **Inference:** This is why the reform is described as having “legalised communalism.” The phrase does not
mean communal identities began in 1909. It means the electoral system formally embedded
community as a political category.

### 3. Extension under the 1919 reforms

The Government of India Act, 1919 retained communal representation and extended separate
electorates to **Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans**. Limited franchise
based on property, tax or education meant that political participation remained restricted
even while the range of represented communities expanded.

### 4. Communal Award, 1932

British Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** announced the Communal Award in August 1932. It:

- continued separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and
  Europeans;
- extended the principle to the **Depressed Classes**, later constitutionally identified with
  Scheduled Castes.

Gandhi opposed the separate electorate for the Depressed Classes and began a fast in Yerawada
Jail. The resulting **Poona Pact** replaced that arrangement with:

```text
reserved seats for Depressed Classes
                     +
joint Hindu electorate
```

The distinction is constitutionally important. Reservation can ensure group presence without
placing voters in separate communal electorates.

### 5. Why the Constitution rejected the colonial model

The Constitution established universal adult franchise and a common electoral framework.
Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, but voters generally participate
in a joint electorate. The aim is representation of disadvantaged groups without creating
separate political citizenships.

The comparison must be qualified:

| Colonial mechanism | Constitutional response |
|---|---|
| Restricted franchise | Universal adult suffrage |
| Official control over legislatures | Representative legislatures and responsible ministries |
| Separate communal electorates | Joint electorate |
| Political safeguards allocated by imperial authority | Constitutionally guaranteed representation |

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection to the standard narrative:** It is too simple to claim that separate electorates
alone caused Partition. Communal politics also reflected social anxieties, elite competition,
colonial strategies, provincial conditions and nationalist failures.

**Reply:** Separate electorates should not be treated as a single cause. Their constitutional
importance is narrower but substantial: they rewarded mobilisation through legally separated
communities and shaped bargaining incentives over time.

**Balanced assessment:** Separate electorates were neither the origin of all communal identity
nor a neutral technique. They institutionalised community as a channel of political
representation.

### 7. UPSC application

- **1909:** separate electorate for Muslims; S.P. Sinha enters the Viceroy's Executive Council.
- **1919:** separate electorates extended to additional communities.
- **1932:** Communal Award extends the principle to Depressed Classes.
- **Poona Pact:** reserved seats within a joint electorate.
- **Trap:** 1909 introduced communal electorates, not dyarchy.
- **Trap:** a reserved seat and a separate electorate are not the same arrangement.

2. The Central Council expanded from 16 to 60.

4. Satyendra Prasad Sinha became the first Indian in the Viceroy's Executive Council.
5. Muslims received separate electorates in 1909.
6. Separate electorates legally linked representation to community-specific voters.
7. The 1919 reforms extended communal electorates to more communities.
8. Franchise remained limited by property, tax or education.
9. Ramsay MacDonald announced the Communal Award in 1932.
10. It extended separate electorates to the Depressed Classes.
11. The Poona Pact retained reserved seats but restored a joint electorate.
12. The Constitution combines joint electorates with specified seat reservation.
13. Separate electorates were an important incentive structure, not a sufficient single-cause

### Concept check

**Question:** What is the constitutional difference between a separate electorate and a reserved seat in a joint electorate?

**Model answer:** In a separate electorate, only voters belonging to the designated community choose that community's representative. In a joint electorate with a reserved seat, the candidate must belong to the protected group, but the general electorate votes. The first separates voters politically by community; the second secures representation while retaining a common electorate.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not describe the Poona Pact as simply abolishing protection for the Depressed Classes; it changed the electoral mechanism while retaining reserved representation.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse how constitutional reforms between 1909 and 1932
institutionalised communal representation, and assess the significance of the Poona Pact.

**Directive decoding:** “Analyse” requires mechanism and consequences; “assess” requires a

**Model answer (224 words):**

Colonial reforms widened representation while increasingly organising it through legally
defined communities. The Indian Councils Act, 1909 introduced separate electorates for
Muslims: Muslim voters elected representatives to Muslim seats. By making community the
electoral channel, the state encouraged political claims to be framed as communal claims.
The Government of India Act, 1919 retained this system and extended separate electorates to
Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans. Representation expanded, but within a
restricted franchise and an executive still insulated from full popular control.

The Communal Award of 1932 extended separate electorates to the Depressed Classes. Gandhi
opposed this separation and fasted in Yerawada Jail. The Poona Pact substituted reserved
seats for the Depressed Classes within a joint electorate. Its significance was twofold. It
preserved guaranteed legislative presence while avoiding a separate electorate that could
politically detach oppressed castes from the wider electorate. This arrangement anticipated
the later constitutional preference for joint electorates with reserved SC/ST seats.

Yet a balanced assessment is necessary. Separate electorates did not alone produce communal
politics or Partition; elite competition, provincial variation, colonial strategy and failures
of inter-community accommodation also mattered. Nor did a joint electorate by itself end caste
domination. The period's lasting constitutional lesson is that representation depends not only
on the number of seats but also on who elects whom and what political incentives that mechanism
creates.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the electoral mechanism, traces extension, assesses the
Pact and qualifies the causal claim.

*Dyarchy was not merely a two-list classification; it split political responsibility inside
the same provincial government.*

### 1. The 1917 declaration and the Act's purpose

✅ **Fact:** On **20 August 1917**, the British Government announced the gradual introduction of
responsible government in India. The Government of India Act, 1919—known as the
**Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms**—came into force in 1921.

✅ **Fact:** Its reforms included:

- separation of central and provincial subjects, though the system remained centrally
  dominated;
- division of provincial subjects into transferred and reserved categories;
- **dyarchy** in provinces;
- bicameralism at the Centre: Council of State and Legislative Assembly;
- direct elections;
- a requirement that three of the six members of the Viceroy's Executive Council, **other
  than the Commander-in-Chief**, be Indian;
- extension of separate electorates;
- limited franchise based on property, tax or education;
- a High Commissioner for India in London;
- provision for a public service commission, leading to the Central Public Service Commission
  in **1926**;
- separation of provincial budgets from the central budget;
- a statutory commission after ten years.

### 2. How dyarchy was supposed to work

Transferred subjects were administered by the Governor with ministers responsible to the
legislative council. Reserved subjects remained with the Governor and Executive Council,
outside that responsibility.

⚠️ **Inference:** The stated logic was gradual training: Indian ministers would gain experience in selected fields
while imperial authorities retained sensitive powers. The central defect was structural:
responsibility was granted without unified authority.

```text
Legislature questions minister
          ↓
minister lacks control over a connected reserved department
          ↓
policy failure occurs
          ↓
responsibility is visible, power is divided
```

⚠️ **Inference:** The arrangement made it difficult for voters and legislators to know whom to hold answerable.

### 3. Dyarchy is not provincial autonomy

| Feature | Provincial dyarchy, 1919 | Provincial autonomy, 1935 |
|---|---|---|
| Subject arrangement | Provincial subjects split into reserved and transferred | Provincial dyarchy abolished |
| Ministerial sphere | Limited to transferred subjects | Ministers received a wider responsible sphere |
| Governor's position | Directly administers reserved subjects with Executive Council | Formally advised by ministers, but retains safeguards and discretion |
| Political logic | Divided responsibility | Enlarged, though qualified, responsibility |

This is one of the most frequently tested chronological distinctions.

### 4. Simon Commission and the constitutional pipeline

✅ **Fact:** The 1919 Act required later review. In **November 1927**, the British Government
announced the seven-member statutory commission chaired by **Sir John Simon**, earlier than
the ten-year schedule. All members were British, which provoked a boycott.

Its 1930 report recommended, among other things:

- abolition of provincial dyarchy;
- extension of responsible government in provinces;
- a federation of British India and princely states;
- continuation of communal electorates.

The route to the next statute was:

```text
Simon Commission
       ↓
Three Round Table Conferences
       ↓
White Paper on constitutional reforms
       ↓
Joint Select Committee of British Parliament
       ↓
Government of India Act, 1935
```

The Communal Award and Poona Pact occurred within this wider constitutional transition, but
their electoral mechanism was analysed in the previous lesson.

**Model answer:** Ministers were answerable to the legislature for transferred subjects, while the Governor and Executive Council controlled reserved subjects without that responsibility. Because policy fields interact, ministers could be blamed for outcomes without controlling all relevant powers. Authority and accountability were therefore divided, preventing coherent responsible government.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not define dyarchy merely as “two lists”; its constitutional defect lay in splitting executive power and responsibility within a province.

India and princely states. Three Round Table Conferences, a White Paper and a Joint Select
Committee then shaped the Government of India Act, 1935.

The 1935 Act responded by abolishing provincial dyarchy and introducing provincial autonomy.
However, gubernatorial safeguards and discretionary powers preserved imperial limits.
Therefore, 1919 mattered both as a failed structure of divided responsibility and as an
institutional bridge to a broader, though still qualified, provincial settlement.

*The central exam discipline is to distinguish what the Act provided from what actually came
into operation.*

### 1. Why the Act dominates this chapter

✅ **Fact:** With **321 sections and 10 schedules**, the 1935 Act was a detailed constitutional instrument.
It did not establish Indian popular sovereignty, but it supplied much of the administrative
and federal machinery with which the Constitution later worked.

⚠️ **Inference:** Calling it the “single largest source” of the Constitution means that many institutional
forms—legislative lists, provincial administration, governors, public-service commissions,
court structures and detailed governmental procedures—have a traceable statutory antecedent
there. It does not mean the Constitution simply copied the Act's purpose or retained every
feature.

### 2. Proposed All-India Federation

The Act proposed a federation of:

- British Indian provinces; and
- princely states.

Powers were distributed through:

| List | Items | Intended field |
|---|---:|---|
| Federal | 59 | Central legislature |
| Provincial | 54 | Provincial legislatures |
| Concurrent | 36 | Both levels |

Residuary powers were assigned to the **Governor-General**, not automatically to the federal
or provincial legislature.

The federation never commenced because the required accession of princely states did not
occur. Therefore:

> **Provided for an All-India Federation** is accurate.
> **Established an operating All-India Federation** is inaccurate.

### 3. Central dyarchy

The Act proposed dyarchy at the Centre. Federal subjects would be divided into reserved and
transferred fields. Reserved matters—**defence, external affairs, ecclesiastical affairs and
tribal areas**—would remain under the Governor-General and his councillors rather than
responsible ministerial control.

This central dyarchy did not operate because the federal part itself did not commence.

### 4. Provincial autonomy

At the provincial level, the Act:

- abolished the dyarchy introduced in 1919;
- treated provinces as autonomous units in their defined spheres;
- introduced ministries responsible to provincial legislatures;
- brought this arrangement into effect in **1937**;
- saw the period of Congress ministries end in **1939**.

Autonomy remained qualified. Governors possessed special responsibilities, safeguards and
discretionary powers. A provincial ministry therefore operated within a colonial constitution
whose ultimate authority remained imperial.

### 5. Bicameralism and electorate

Bicameral legislatures were created in six of eleven provinces:

> Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam and the United Provinces.

The Act further extended separate electorates to Depressed Classes, women and labour. The
franchise expanded but reached only about **10 per cent** of the population. This contrast is
essential: constitutional machinery became elaborate without becoming based on universal
citizenship.

### 6. Institutional provisions

✅ **Fact:** The Act:

- abolished the Council of India and provided advisers to the Secretary of State;
- provided for the Reserve Bank of India in the constitutional-administrative framework;
- provided Federal and Provincial Public Service Commissions and Joint Public Service
  Commissions for two or more provinces;
- provided for a Federal Court, established in **1937**;
- provided for the **separation of Burma from India**, which became operational in **1937**.

The RBI began operations in 1935 under separate legislation enacted in 1934. A safe answer
therefore says that the 1935 constitutional settlement **provided for or incorporated the RBI
framework**, not that this Act alone is the RBI's complete legal origin.

### 9. Act-to-Constitution continuity — with limits

| 1935 element | Later constitutional relevance | Essential qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Three-list division | Union, State and Concurrent distribution | Residuary allocation changed |
| Provincial autonomy | Responsible State ministries | Governors now operate under a democratic Constitution |
| Federal Court | Pre-constitutional judicial precursor | Supreme Court has a new constitutional source and jurisdiction |
| PSC structure | UPSC and State PSC framework | Constitutional independence is re-grounded in popular sovereignty |
| Governor's office | State constitutional head | Colonial discretionary control cannot be mechanically equated with the present office |
| Administrative detail | Continuity of governance | Rights, judicial review and democratic accountability alter its purpose |

### 10. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling the 1935 Act the Constitution's largest source understates the original
achievement of the Constituent Assembly and normalises colonial safeguards.

**Reply:** “Largest statutory source” is a claim about institutional material, not legitimacy
or constitutional philosophy. The Constitution re-grounded institutions in popular
sovereignty, universal franchise, Fundamental Rights and judicial review while rejecting
communal electorates and imperial supremacy.

**Balanced verdict:** The Act was an extensive quarry of governmental machinery, but the
Constitution redesigned the political foundation and accountability of that machinery.

### 11. UPSC traps

- Federation proposed does not mean federation commenced.
- Central dyarchy was provided but never operated.
- Provincial dyarchy was abolished and provincial autonomy introduced.
- Residuary powers lay with the Governor-General.
- Defence and external affairs belonged to the reserved central sphere.
- Bicameralism applied to six provinces, not all eleven.
- Franchise expanded to about 10 per cent, not universal adult suffrage.
- The Act provided for Burma's separation; the separation became operational in 1937.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can it be true that the 1935 Act provided a federation while the federation never existed in operation?

**Model answer:** A statute may legally design an institution subject to commencement conditions. The 1935 Act provided an All-India Federation of provinces and princely states, but the required accession of princely states did not occur. Consequently, the federal provisions and central dyarchy did not commence, even though the statutory design existed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat “provided for” and “came into operation” as equivalent expressions.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain why the Government of India Act, 1935 was both the
most important colonial source of the Constitution and an inadequate model for democratic
government.

**Directive decoding:** “Explain why” requires two connected propositions: institutional
inheritance and democratic inadequacy. Give a balanced, evidence-based verdict.

**Model answer (214 words):**

The Government of India Act, 1935 was the most elaborate colonial constitution and supplied
substantial machinery later adapted in 1950. Its proposed federation distributed powers
through Federal, Provincial and Concurrent Lists; it institutionalised provincial autonomy,
the Governor's office, Federal and Provincial Public Service Commissions and a Federal Court,
established in 1937. Bicameral provincial legislatures and detailed administrative provisions
also furnished working precedents. These elements made it the Constitution's largest single
statutory source.

Yet it was an inadequate democratic model. The All-India Federation never commenced because
the princely states did not accede, and proposed central dyarchy also remained inoperative.
Residuary power lay with the Governor-General. Provincial autonomy operated under governors'
safeguards and discretionary authority, while ultimate sovereignty remained British. Separate
electorates were extended and the franchise covered only about ten per cent of the population.
The executive at the Centre was not responsible to a legislature based on universal
citizenship.

The Constitution therefore borrowed selectively. It retained useful federal, judicial,
service and administrative forms but changed their normative foundation through popular
sovereignty, universal adult franchise, responsible government, Fundamental Rights and
judicial review. It rejected official imperial supremacy and communal electorates. The 1935
Act was thus an institutional quarry, not a democratic blueprint: continuity lay in
governmental machinery, while constitutional legitimacy and accountability were fundamentally
reconstructed.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer links named institutions to 1950, supplies at least five
precise limitations and ends with a graded continuity–rupture verdict.

**Scoring guidance (15):** source thesis 1; federal/list machinery 3; judicial/service/
administrative inheritance 2.5; democratic defects 4; selective constitutional transformation
3; reasoned verdict 1.5.

**Bridge forward:** The 1935 Act built the most detailed machinery of government, but only the
1947 Act could end imperial supremacy and release Indian constituent power.

### Visual: five legal transitions in 1947

```text
IMPERIAL INDIA
     │
     ├── territorial transition → India + Pakistan as two Dominions
     ├── sovereignty transition → British legislative supremacy ends
     ├── constituent transition → Assemblies may frame and repeal constitutional law
     ├── legislature transition → India's 1946 Constituent Assembly becomes
     │                            the Dominion legislature/Parliament
     └── treaty transition → paramountcy over princely states and
                             treaty relations with tribal areas lapse
                                      ↓
                             princely-state accession is not automatic
```

*Independence was simultaneously a territorial partition, a transfer of sovereignty, a
release of constituent authority, a transition of the Constituent Assembly into the Dominion
legislature, and the end of imperial paramountcy and tribal treaty relations.*

### 1. From announced deadline to immediate transfer

✅ **Fact:** On **20 February 1947**, British Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** announced that British rule
would end by **30 June 1948**. The **Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947** proposed Partition and
was accepted by the principal political parties. Parliament then enacted the Indian
Independence Act, making **15 August 1947** the transfer date.

### 2. Two Dominions and the end of British responsibility

✅ **Fact:** The Act:

- ended British rule and declared India independent and sovereign from 15 August 1947;
- partitioned British India into the Dominions of India and Pakistan;
- recognised their ability to leave the Commonwealth;
- abolished the office of Viceroy;
- provided a Governor-General for each Dominion, appointed by the King on the advice of the
  Dominion cabinet;
- ended the British Government's responsibility for the governance of either Dominion.

✅ **Fact:** **Lord Mountbatten** became the first Governor-General of the Dominion of India
and swore in **Jawaharlal Nehru** as the first Prime Minister of independent India.

“Dominion” did not mean that Britain retained the former imperial power to govern India. It
described the transitional constitutional form until India became a republic under its own
Constitution.

### 3. Constituent Assemblies acquire plenary authority

✅ **Fact:** The Constituent Assembly of India had been formed in **1946**. Under the
post-independence arrangement, it performed a dual role: it continued constitution-making and
functioned as the legislature or **Parliament of the Indian Dominion**.

✅ **Fact:** The Constituent Assemblies could:

- frame and adopt any constitution;
- repeal British Acts, including the Independence Act;
- legislate for their territories until new constitutions came into force.

✅ **Fact:** British Acts enacted after 15 August 1947 would not automatically extend to a Dominion. This
is the legal core of **constituent sovereignty**: the Assembly no longer depended on British
approval for the content of India's Constitution.

### 4. Transitional continuity under the 1935 Act

Independence did not make daily government disappear. Until new constitutions were framed,
the Dominions and provinces continued to be governed through the Government of India Act,
1935, as adapted by Dominion authorities.

```text
Political sovereignty changes immediately
                 ↓
administrative continuity prevents a legal vacuum
                 ↓
1935 machinery operates temporarily under new authority
```

This is an example of continuity serving transition rather than proving continued colonial
sovereignty.

### 5. Paramountcy and princely states

✅ **Fact:** The Act declared both the lapse of British paramountcy over princely states and
the lapse of Crown **treaty relations with tribal areas** from 15 August 1947. It did not
transfer paramountcy wholesale to India.

This produced a legal-political problem:

```text
British paramountcy lapses
          ↓
no automatic imperial link remains
          ↓
princely state is not automatically absorbed into India or Pakistan
          ↓
accession and integration require instruments, negotiation and political action
```

The Act's formal freedom for princely states to join either Dominion or remain independent
did not settle the strategic and political consequences. Integration was achieved through
subsequent Indian statecraft and legal instruments.

### 6. Other institutional changes

The Act:

- abolished the office of Secretary of State for India and transferred residual functions to
  the Secretary of State for Commonwealth Affairs;
- deprived the British monarch of the power to veto Dominion bills or reserve them for royal
  approval, while the Governor-General retained assent functions;
- made Governors-General and provincial Governors constitutional heads acting on ministerial
  advice;
- removed “Emperor of India” from the royal titles;
- discontinued new appointments and reservation of civil-service posts by the Secretary of
  State while protecting existing service members' benefits.

### 7. Example and limit

Consider a new owner taking charge of a complex institution at midnight. Ownership and
ultimate authority change immediately, but payroll, courts, departments and regulations must
continue the next morning. Temporary use of the 1935 machinery served this continuity.

The analogy stops at ownership. Independence was not a private transfer; it involved Partition,
mass politics, constitutional legitimacy and the integration of territories.

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If India initially remained a Dominion and used the 1935 Act, independence was
constitutionally incomplete.

**Reply:** Republican transformation was indeed completed only with the Constitution in 1950.
But the 1947 Act ended British governing responsibility and gave Indian institutions power to
make, alter or repeal constitutional law. Dominion status was transitional, not a continuation
of British legislative supremacy.

**Balanced verdict:** 1947 achieved external and constituent independence; 1950 completed the
republican constitutional settlement.

### 9. UPSC application

- Distinguish Attlee's announced deadline from the actual transfer date.
- Mountbatten Plan: 3 June 1947; independence: 15 August 1947.
- Viceroy abolished; Dominion Governor-General retained.
- Lord Mountbatten became the first Governor-General of the Indian Dominion and swore in
  Jawaharlal Nehru as the first Prime Minister of independent India.
- Constituent Assemblies could repeal British statutes.
- India's Constituent Assembly, formed in 1946, also functioned as the Dominion legislature.
- Paramountcy over princely states and treaty relations with tribal areas lapsed; accession
  was not automatic.
- The 1935 Act continued only as an adapted transitional framework.
- Topic boundary: detailed Constituent Assembly committees and drafting belong to the next
  topic.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did the lapse of British paramountcy not automatically transfer princely states to India?

**Model answer:** Paramountcy was the Crown's superior relationship with princely states. The 1947 Act terminated that relationship and associated treaty obligations rather than assigning them automatically to a successor Dominion. Accession therefore had to be secured through legal instruments, negotiation and political integration.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the lapse of paramountcy as either automatic Indian sovereignty over every princely state or proof that integration lacked any legal process.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the constitutional significance of the Indian
Independence Act, 1947 beyond the territorial partition of India.

**Directive decoding:** “Beyond” requires the answer to foreground sovereignty,
constitution-making, paramountcy and transitional continuity rather than narrating Partition.

**Model answer (146 words):**

The Indian Independence Act, 1947 was constitutionally significant not merely because it
created the Dominions of India and Pakistan, but because it ended imperial legal supremacy.
The British Government ceased to be responsible for their governance, the Viceroy's office
was abolished, and each Dominion received a Governor-General acting within a ministerial
framework. More importantly, the Constituent Assemblies could frame any constitution, repeal
British statutes and legislate until new constitutions commenced. British laws enacted after
15 August no longer extended automatically to India. The lapse of paramountcy ended Crown
relations with princely states, making accession and integration necessary rather than
automatic. At the same time, adapted provisions of the Government of India Act, 1935
temporarily maintained administrative continuity. Thus, the Act combined an immediate
transfer of external and constituent sovereignty with an orderly institutional transition.
Dominion status remained temporary; the Constitution of 1950 completed the republican
transformation.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “beyond Partition” through four constitutional
dimensions and ends by distinguishing 1947 sovereignty from 1950 republicanism.

**Scoring guidance (10):** end of imperial responsibility 2; constituent authority 2.5;
paramountcy 2; transitional continuity 1.5; Dominion/republic qualification 1; conclusion 1.

**Bridge forward:** With sovereignty transferred but much machinery temporarily retained, the
final task is to judge precisely what 1950 inherited, transformed and rejected.

### Visual: inheritance is selective, not mechanical

```text
COLONIAL CONSTITUTIONAL EXPERIENCE
                │
        ┌───────┴────────┐
        │                │
     RETAINED         REJECTED
        │                │
administrative       imperial sovereignty
continuity           restricted franchise
legislative lists    official majorities
public services      communal electorates
courts and           executive irresponsibility
provincial machinery racial/colonial purpose
        │                │
        └───────┬────────┘
                ↓
          TRANSFORMED BY
popular sovereignty + universal franchise + rights
+ responsible government + judicial review + republicanism
```

⚠️ *Inference: The Constitution's originality lies partly in the democratic transformation of inherited
institutions, not only in inventing institutions with no history.*

### 1. The continuity thesis

⚠️ **Inference grounded in institutional comparison:** The Constitution retained or adapted:

- a central executive and all-India administrative scale;
- legislative procedure and bicameral experience;
- provincial government and the office of Governor;
- division of legislative subjects;
- public-service commissions;
- the Federal Court's institutional experience;
- detailed administrative provisions;
- parliamentary-government practices derived more broadly from Westminster.

✅ **Fact base:** The **Government of India Act, 1935** supplied the largest single statutory
block of this machinery.
Earlier Acts contributed the sequence: centralisation in 1773 and 1833, legislative
specialisation in 1853, portfolio administration in 1861, legislative scrutiny in 1892,
direct election and public-service institutions in 1919.

### 2. The rupture thesis

✅ **Fact base:** The Constitution decisively changed:

- **source of authority:** “We, the People” rather than Crown and Parliament;
- **political membership:** universal adult franchise rather than a narrow property-based
  electorate;
- **rights:** justiciable Fundamental Rights;
- **accountability:** responsible governments at Union and State levels;
- **electoral structure:** joint electorate rather than separate communal electorates;
- **judiciary:** constitutional supremacy and judicial review;
- **form of state:** sovereign democratic republic rather than colonial Dominion rule;
- **citizenship:** common constitutional citizenship.

⚠️ **Inference:** Therefore, similarity of institutional form does not prove continuity of constitutional
purpose.

### 3. Act-to-1950 map

The statutory provisions in the first two columns are ✅ **facts**; the language of
“contribution,” “lesson” and later relevance is ⚠️ **analytical inference**.

| Statute | Constitutional contribution or lesson | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Regulating Act, 1773 | Central executive and apex-court beginning | Imperial supervision, not democracy |
| Pitt's India Act, 1784 | Political control over administration | Company continued |
| Charter Act, 1833 | All-India executive and legislative centralisation | No representative sovereignty |
| Charter Act, 1853 | Legislative specialisation and competitive services | Narrow local representation |
| Government of India Act, 1858 | Centrally answerable Crown administration | Direct imperial rule |
| Councils Act, 1861 | Portfolio system and provincial devolution | Nomination |
| Councils Act, 1892 | Budget discussion and questions | Official majority and no responsibility |
| Councils Act, 1909 | Expanded councils | Separate electorates rejected in 1950 |
| Government of India Act, 1919 | Subject division, bicameralism, direct election and PSC | Dyarchy failed |
| Government of India Act, 1935 | Federal lists, autonomy, courts, PSCs and administrative detail | Federation/central dyarchy did not commence; imperial safeguards |
| Independence Act, 1947 | Constituent sovereignty and transitional continuity | Republican settlement awaited 1950 |

### 4. The strongest competing claims

#### Claim A — “The Constitution is merely a colonial continuation”

⚠️ **Inference:** This claim correctly notices extensive administrative continuity. It becomes untenable when
it ignores universal franchise, rights, republican sovereignty and responsible government.

#### Claim B — “The Constitution is wholly original”

⚠️ **Inference:** This claim correctly emphasises democratic transformation. It becomes untenable when it
ignores inherited lists, offices, procedures, services, courts and provincial machinery.

#### Qualified conclusion

> The Constitution is structurally cumulative but normatively transformative: it inherited
> a developed governmental apparatus, democratised useful institutions and repudiated the
> political principles of colonial rule.

### 5. Criminal-law reform as a decolonisation illustration

✅ **Verified fact:** Official Ministry of Home Affairs and Press Information Bureau material
records that the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**, **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita,
2023**, and **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** came into force on **1 July 2024**. They
replaced, respectively, the Indian Penal Code, 1860; Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973; and
Indian Evidence Act, 1872 frameworks.

⚠️ **Analytical use:** This is a bounded illustration of continuing legal decolonisation:
independence and constitutional transformation did not automatically remove every
colonial-origin law.

**Limit:** Do not claim that these criminal codes descend directly from the Government of
India Act, 1935, or that replacing a statute by itself removes every colonial institutional
practice. The linkage is about the longer problem of inherited law, not direct constitutional
genealogy.

### 6. How to construct answers by marks

#### 10 marks

Use one thesis, two constitutional strands, the 1935 Act, one rejected feature and a qualified
verdict. Name three to four statutes.

#### 15 marks

Use centralisation, representative evolution, responsible government, 1935 inheritance and
democratic rejection. Name six to seven statutes and explain each example.

#### 20 marks

Add federal development, constituent sovereignty, colonial safeguards, indigenous
constitutional additions, the strongest counterclaim and a graded conclusion. Use eight to
ten statutes selectively rather than narrating all of them.

### 7. Answer-writing traps

- Narrating every Act without answering the directive.
- Calling every colonial reform “democratic progress.”
- Saying 1773 created the Governor-General of India.
- Saying 1853 abolished the Company.
- Exchanging 1909, 1919 and 1935 reforms.
- Saying the 1935 federation operated.
- Calling separate electorates a feature borrowed by the Constitution.
- Treating all Governors' powers in 1950 as unchanged colonial survivals.
- Claiming the Constitution was either copied or wholly unprecedented.

### 8. Objection, reply and unresolved residual

**Objection:** The continuity–rupture formula may become an easy compromise that avoids
judging the depth of colonial influence.

**Reply:** A strong answer should specify where continuity is deep. Administrative centralism,
emergency-minded safeguards, bureaucratic structures and detailed governance provisions did
not vanish. It should equally specify transformative controls: elections, rights, courts,
federal politics and constitutional amendment operate under popular sovereignty.

**Residual:** Formal democratic rules do not automatically decolonise administrative culture.
That is why the historical background remains relevant to present debates over centralisation,
policing, executive discretion and legal reform.

### Revision notes

1. Continuity is strongest in administrative machinery and institutional form.
2. Rupture is strongest in sovereignty, franchise, rights and accountability.
3. The 1935 Act is the largest statutory source, not a democratic blueprint.
4. Centralisation developed through 1773 and 1833.
5. Representation moved from nomination to indirect and direct election.
6. Responsibility lagged behind representation.
7. Separate electorates were rejected; reserved representation within joint electorates
   survived.
8. The 1947 Act made constitution-making legally autonomous.
9. Universal adult suffrage was a decisive departure from colonial franchise.
10. Judicial review and Fundamental Rights changed the use of state machinery.
11. Current legal decolonisation can illustrate persistence without proving direct genealogy.
12. A good Mains answer identifies retained, transformed and rejected elements.
13. The safest verdict is structural continuity with normative transformation.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the Constitution be deeply influenced by colonial statutes without being merely colonial in character?

**Model answer:** Institutional form and constitutional legitimacy are distinct. The Constitution retained legislative lists, provincial machinery, public services, courts and administrative procedures developed under colonial statutes. It re-grounded them in popular sovereignty, universal franchise, responsible government, rights, judicial review and republican citizenship, while rejecting official majorities and separate electorates. Influence therefore coexists with normative transformation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not prove originality merely by listing new ideals or prove colonial continuity merely by matching institutional names; explain how authority, accountability and purpose changed.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** “The Constitution of India is the culmination of colonial
constitutional development in structure and its repudiation in political morality.” Discuss.

**Directive decoding:** “Discuss” requires a reasoned examination of both parts of the
proposition. Organise around retained structure, rejected morality and a qualified verdict.

**Model answer (227 words):**

The Constitution did not begin on an institutional blank slate. Colonial statutes had already
constructed an all-India executive, legislatures, provinces, courts and public services.
The Regulating Act, 1773 initiated central administration; the Charter Act, 1833 created the
Governor-General of India and central legislative authority; the 1861 and 1892 Acts developed
portfolio government, provincial devolution and legislative scrutiny. The 1919 Act introduced
direct election, bicameralism, subject division and a public-service commission. Most
decisively, the 1935 Act supplied legislative lists, provincial autonomy, Governors,
public-service commissions, a Federal Court and extensive administrative detail. The 1947 Act
then released the Constituent Assembly from imperial legislative supremacy.

Yet the Constitution repudiated colonial political morality. Authority flowed from the
people, not Crown or Parliament. Universal adult franchise replaced restricted property-based
voting; responsible ministries replaced executive insulation; Fundamental Rights and judicial
review constrained government; republican citizenship displaced imperial subjecthood.
Separate communal electorates, official majorities and the 1935 federation's gubernatorial
safeguards were not accepted as governing principles.

The repudiation was nevertheless not total. A strong Centre, bureaucratic continuity and
emergency-oriented administrative habits reveal a deep institutional inheritance. Conversely,
retaining an office or procedure does not preserve its colonial meaning when it operates
within elections, rights and constitutional supremacy. The Constitution is therefore
structurally cumulative but normatively transformative: it democratised inherited machinery,
discarded divisive and authoritarian principles, and placed the state under a sovereign
republican charter.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly engages both halves, uses nine named developments,
acknowledges residual continuity and reaches a defensible graded verdict.

**Scoring guidance (20):** direct interpretive thesis 2; central/legislative evolution 3;
representation/responsibility evolution 3; 1935 institutional inheritance 3; sovereignty and
democratic rupture 4; counterargument/residual continuity 2; evidence-linked analysis 2;
conclusion/presentation 1.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks integrate several lessons. They supplement rather than duplicate the single
lesson-local checks.

## Check 1 — Office and authority ladder

**Question:** Reconstruct the constitutional movement from Governor-General of Bengal to
Indian constituent sovereignty in four stages.

**Model answer:** The Regulating Act, 1773 created the Governor-General of Bengal and began central administration. The Charter Act, 1833 created the Governor-General of India with all-India legislative authority. The 1858 Act added the designation Viceroy and transferred control from Company to Crown. The 1947 Act ended imperial supremacy and empowered Indian Constituent Assemblies to frame and repeal constitutional law.

**Diagnostic:** If 1773 is described as creating the Governor-General of India, revise Lessons
2 and 3.

## Check 2 — Representation is not responsibility

**Question:** Why do 1861, 1892 and 1909 show expanding representation without full responsible
government?

**Model answer:** The Acts progressively added nominated Indians, indirect electoral elements, larger councils and communal electorates. Yet official control persisted and the executive did not depend on the confidence of an elected legislature. They widened voice and scrutiny without aligning executive authority with popular accountability.

**Diagnostic:** If the answer relies only on the number of members, revise Lessons 4 and 5.

## Check 3 — The reform triad

**Question:** State the central constitutional distinction among 1909, 1919 and 1935 and
explain why the sequence matters.

**Model answer:** The 1909 Act institutionalised separate Muslim electorates; the 1919 Act introduced provincial dyarchy, dividing transferred and reserved subjects; the 1935 Act abolished provincial dyarchy and introduced provincial autonomy. The sequence tracks communal representation, divided responsibility and then wider provincial responsibility—three different constitutional problems.

**Diagnostic:** If “provincial autonomy” is assigned to 1919, revisit Lessons 5–7.

## Check 4 — Provision versus operation

**Question:** Name two central parts of the 1935 Act that were provided but did not operate,
and one provincial part that did operate.

**Model answer:** The proposed All-India Federation and central dyarchy did not operate because the federal scheme did not commence. Provincial autonomy did operate from 1937, although governors retained safeguards and discretionary authority.

**Diagnostic:** If non-commencement is treated as absence from the statute, revise Lesson 7.

## Check 5 — Continuity without colonial legitimacy

**Question:** Give one example each of an institution retained, a principle rejected and an
institution transformed in 1950.

**Model answer:** Legislative lists were retained in adapted form; separate communal electorates were rejected; and the Governor's office was transformed from an imperial safeguard into a constitutional office operating within a democratic federal charter. The examples show that inheritance may involve retention, repudiation or re-grounding.

**Diagnostic:** If the same feature is labelled both simply retained and simply rejected,
clarify its precise transformation in Lesson 9.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These final questions are distinct from the nine lesson-local questions.

## Final 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish centralisation under the Charter Act, 1833
from legislative devolution under the Indian Councils Act, 1861.

**Directive decoding:** “Distinguish” requires a common comparison basis—legislative location,
institutional purpose and political limit—not two disconnected descriptions.

**Model answer (146 words):**

The Charter Act, 1833 and Indian Councils Act, 1861 moved colonial governance in opposite
territorial directions. The 1833 Act centralised authority by converting the Governor-General
of Bengal into the Governor-General of India, giving him all-India civil-military authority
and exclusive legislative power. Bombay and Madras lost their legislative competence, and
laws made by the central authority were styled Acts. This suited a Company that had ceased
commercial activity and become an all-India administrative body.

The 1861 Act began legislative devolution. It restored legislative powers to Bombay and
Madras and enabled new provincial councils, while retaining the Viceroy's superior authority.
It also associated nominated Indian non-officials with law-making. Devolution did not create
federal democracy: provincial bodies remained subordinate and Indians were nominated rather
than elected. The contrast therefore shows a shift from building a unified colonial centre to
allowing limited provincial legislative space within that centre's continuing supremacy.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares the Acts on a shared axis, names exact provisions and
qualifies devolution.

**Scoring guidance (10):** 1833 features 3; 1861 features 3; direct comparison 2; limits 1;
conclusion 1.

## Final 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Trace the evolution of representative institutions from
the Indian Councils Act, 1861 to the Government of India Act, 1935. Did representation produce
responsible government?

**Directive decoding:** “Trace” demands chronological movement; the final question demands a
clear, qualified judgement.

**Model answer (204 words):**

Representative institutions developed through widening association, election and legislative
functions, but executive responsibility lagged behind them. The Indian Councils Act, 1861
began Indian association by permitting nominated non-official members. The 1892 Act enlarged
councils, allowed budget discussion and executive questions, and introduced an indirect
electoral element through nomination on recommendations from local and organised bodies.

The 1909 reforms expanded councils, admitted an Indian to the Viceroy's Executive Council and
introduced separate Muslim electorates. Representation therefore widened but was channelled
through communal categories, while official majority continued at the Centre. The 1919 Act
introduced direct elections and central bicameralism. In provinces, however, dyarchy divided
subjects between responsible ministers and the Governor's unaccountable Executive Council.
Limited franchise further restricted popular control.

The 1935 Act abolished provincial dyarchy and introduced provincial autonomy with ministries
responsible to legislatures from 1937. It also widened franchise and established bicameralism
in six provinces. Yet governors retained safeguards and discretionary powers, separate
electorates expanded, and the federal executive never became responsible because the proposed
federation did not commence.

Thus, representation created practices of debate, election and scrutiny, but produced only
qualified responsible government in the provinces by 1935. Full responsibility required the
post-colonial combination of universal franchise, legislative confidence and popular
sovereignty.

**Why this earns marks:** Every statutory stage changes the argument; the conclusion directly
answers whether representation yielded responsibility.

**Scoring guidance (15):** 1861/1892 3; 1909 2.5; 1919 3; 1935 3; representation–
responsibility analysis 2; conclusion/presentation 1.5.

## Final 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Assess the proposition that British constitutional reforms
in India simultaneously centralised administrative power and prepared the ground for federal
democracy.

**Directive decoding:** “Assess” requires weighing both movements and their limits. The answer
must explain coexistence, not merely narrate a centralising phase followed by a federal phase.

**Model answer (239 words):**

British constitutional development produced a strong administrative centre while gradually
creating provincial and representative institutions later adapted by federal democracy.
The Regulating Act, 1773 subordinated Bombay and Madras to the Governor-General of Bengal;
Pitt's India Act, 1784 imposed British political supervision. The Charter Act, 1833 completed
the centralising movement by creating the Governor-General of India and exclusive all-India
legislative authority. Crown rule after 1858 retained this unified executive and bureaucratic
command.

Yet centralisation generated pressures for territorial participation. The Councils Act, 1861
restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras and created provincial councils. The 1919
Act separated central and provincial subjects, introduced direct elections and experimented
with provincial dyarchy. Its divided responsibility failed, but it established that
provincial administration required a political sphere of its own. The 1935 Act then created
three legislative lists and provincial autonomy, and proposed a federation of provinces and
princely states.

The federal preparation was nevertheless incomplete and colonial. The 1935 federation and
central dyarchy never commenced; residuary powers and safeguards favoured the
Governor-General, governors retained discretion, communal electorates persisted and franchise
remained narrow. These reforms prepared institutions, not democratic sovereignty.

The Constitution inherited both strands: Union strength and a list-based federal division.
It transformed them through universal franchise, responsible ministries, constitutional
rights, judicial review and popular sovereignty. British reforms therefore supplied a
centralised administrative-federal framework, but federal democracy arose only when that
framework was released from imperial authority and made accountable to citizens.

**Why this earns marks:** It sustains both sides of the proposition, names institutional
evidence, distinguishes preparation from achievement and reaches a graded verdict.

**Scoring guidance (20):** centralisation chain 4; devolution/federal chain 5; 1919–1935
mechanism 3; colonial limitations 3; constitutional transformation 3; judgement and
presentation 2.

# REMEDIATION

## Error family 1 — Office-title collapse

**Faulty thought:** “The Regulating Act created the Governor-General of India.”

**Repair chain:**

```text
1773 → Governor-General of Bengal
1833 → Governor-General of India
1858 → Viceroy designation added

```

## Error family 2 — Company commerce versus Company rule

**Faulty thought:** “The Company ended in 1833.”

**Repair:** Its commercial activities ended in 1833; its administrative rule continued until
the 1858 transfer to the Crown.

## Error family 3 — Representation equals responsibility

**Faulty thought:** “Once elections began, government became responsible.”

**Repair test:** Ask whether ministers controlled the relevant executive field and had to
retain legislative confidence. If not, representation has widened without full responsibility.

## Error family 4 — The reform triad is swapped

```text
1909 = separate Muslim electorate
1919 = provincial dyarchy
1935 = provincial autonomy
```

**Repair:** Attach one operating mechanism—not merely one keyword—to each date.

## Error family 5 — Federation “existed” in 1935

**Faulty thought:** “The Act established a functioning federation.”

**Repair:** The Act **provided for** federation. It did not **commence** because the necessary
princely-state accessions were absent.

## Error family 6 — Separate electorate equals reservation

**Repair:** First identify the voters, then the candidate:

- separate electorate: community voters choose community representative;
- joint electorate with reserved seat: all voters choose among candidates from the protected
  group.

## Error family 7 — Colonial inheritance proves colonial character

**Repair:** Compare source, accountability and purpose. An inherited office operating under
popular sovereignty, rights and legislative confidence is not politically identical to its
colonial predecessor.

## Error family 8 — Current linkage overreach

**Faulty thought:** “The new criminal laws replace the 1935 constitutional framework.”

**Repair:** They illustrate continuing replacement of colonial-origin legal codes. They do
not form a direct statutory succession to the Government of India Act, 1935.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Starting condition and governing question

- **1600:** Queen Elizabeth I's charter granted the East India Company the exclusive right to
  trade in India.
- Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa: **1765** → territorial power.
- Company Rule: **1773–1858**; Crown Rule: **1858–1947**.
- Read the chronology through six axes: control, centralisation, representation,
  responsibility, federal design and sovereignty.

## Company government brought under law

### Regulating Act, 1773

- first parliamentary regulation of Company political-administrative functions;
- Governor-General of **Bengal**: Warren Hastings;
- four-member Council; Bombay and Madras subordinated;
- Calcutta Supreme Court, 1774: Chief Justice + three judges;
- private trade/gifts restricted; Court of Directors reporting required.

### Act of Settlement, 1781

- official acts and revenue matters excluded from Supreme Court jurisdiction;
- personal law applied to Hindu/Muslim defendants;
- provincial appeals to Governor-General-in-Council;
- regulations for provincial courts permitted.

### Pitt's India Act, 1784

- Court of Directors: commerce;
- Board of Control: politics, civil-military government and revenue;
- “double government”; “British possessions in India” terminology;
- British Government gains supreme political control.

### 1786 and 1793

- Cornwallis receives special Council override and Commander-in-Chief role;
- override later extended; Governor-General's control grows;
- Company monopoly renewed for twenty years;
- Board of Control expenses charged to Indian revenues.

## Charter transition: trader to administrator

### Charter Act, 1813

- monopoly ends except tea and China;
- Crown sovereignty asserted;
- missionaries allowed;
- standard textbook formulation: provision for the spread of western education;
- Section 43 wording: ₹1 lakh annually for revival and improvement of literature, learned Indians
  and introduction/promotion of knowledge of the sciences;
- no express English-language settlement in the Act;
- local taxation authorised.

### Charter Act, 1833

- Governor-General of **India**: William Bentinck;
- civil-military and legislative centralisation;
- Bombay/Madras legislative powers removed;
- “Acts” replace “Regulations”;
- Company commerce ends; administration continues;
- open competition attempted but frustrated.

### Charter Act, 1853

- legislative and executive Council functions separated;
- six legislative councillors; “mini-Parliament” analogy with limits;
- open civil-service competition; Macaulay Committee, 1854;
- four local representatives;
- Company rule continued without fixed term.

## Crown control and legislative participation

### Government of India Act, 1858

- Company abolished; power transferred to Crown;
- Governor-General also Viceroy: Canning;
- Court of Directors and Board of Control abolished;
- Secretary of State for India + 15-member advisory Council;
- Secretary of State chaired the Council of India;
- Secretary of State-in-Council given corporate legal capacity.

### Indian Councils Act, 1861

- Indians nominated as non-official legislative members;
- Bombay/Madras legislative powers restored;
- new provincial councils;
- portfolio system recognised;
- Viceroy's emergency ordinance: six months.

### Indian Councils Act, 1892

- councils enlarged; official majority retained;
- budget discussion and questions;
- Central non-official nominations: Viceroy acted on recommendations of provincial
  legislative councils and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce;
- provincial non-official nominations: Governors acted on recommendations of district boards,
  municipalities, universities, trade associations, zamindars and chambers;
- these recommendation routes created a limited indirect electoral element;
- statutory language retained “nomination,” not “election.”

## Representation, communal electorates and responsibility

### Indian Councils Act, 1909

- Morley–Minto reforms;
- Centre: 16 to 60; official majority retained;
- provincial non-official majorities allowed;
- S.P. Sinha first Indian in Viceroy's Executive Council;
- separate Muslim electorates; Minto associated with “Father of Communal Electorate.”

### Government of India Act, 1919

- Montagu–Chelmsford; operational from 1921;
- central/provincial subjects separated but structure centrally dominated;
- provincial dyarchy: transferred versus reserved;
- central bicameralism and direct elections;
- three of six Viceroy's Executive Council members, other than the Commander-in-Chief, were to
  be Indian;
- separate electorates extended;
- limited property/tax/education franchise;
- High Commissioner for India;
- Central PSC established 1926;
- provincial budgets separated;
- statutory review leads to Simon Commission.

### Simon-to-1935 sequence

```text
Simon Commission announced November 1927
(seven members, all British; chair: Sir John Simon)
→ report, 1930
→ three Round Table Conferences
→ White Paper
→ Joint Select Committee
→ Government of India Act, 1935
```

### Communal Award and Poona Pact, 1932

- Award extends separate electorates to Depressed Classes;
- Poona Pact substitutes reserved seats within joint electorate;
- separate electorate ≠ reserved seat.

## Government of India Act, 1935

- 321 sections; 10 schedules.
- All-India Federation proposed: provinces + princely states; never commenced.
- Lists: Federal 59; Provincial 54; Concurrent 36.
- Residuary allocation: Governor-General.
- Provincial dyarchy abolished; provincial autonomy begins in 1937.
- Central dyarchy proposed; never commenced.
- Reserved central subjects: defence, external affairs, ecclesiastical affairs and tribal areas.
- Governors retain safeguards/discretion.
- Bicameralism in Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam and United Provinces.
- Separate electorates widened; franchise about 10 per cent.
- Council of India abolished.
- separation of Burma provided; operational in 1937.
- RBI framework, Federal/Provincial/Joint PSCs and Federal Court.
- Federal Court established in 1937.
- Biggest statutory source of 1950 machinery, not its democratic legitimacy.

## Indian Independence Act, 1947

- Attlee announcement: 20 February 1947; stated deadline 30 June 1948.
- Mountbatten Plan: 3 June 1947.
- India and Pakistan become Dominions on 15 August 1947.
- Viceroy abolished; Dominion Governors-General.
- Lord Mountbatten becomes the first Governor-General of the Indian Dominion.
- Mountbatten swears in Jawaharlal Nehru as independent India's first Prime Minister.
- India's Constituent Assembly, formed in 1946, also functions as the Dominion Parliament.
- Constituent Assemblies may frame any constitution, legislate and repeal British Acts.
- Later British Acts do not automatically extend.
- Secretary of State for India abolished.
- British paramountcy over princely states and treaty relations with tribal areas lapse.
- accession of princely states is not automatic.
- 1935 Act continues temporarily with adaptation.
- constitutional heads act on ministerial advice.
- “Emperor of India” title dropped; existing civil-service interests protected.

2. 1833: Company commerce ends; 1858: Company rule ends.

7. 1935 residuary power: Governor-General.
8. Poona Pact: reserved seats plus joint electorate.
9. 1947 paramountcy lapse: no automatic accession.
10. Institutional inheritance does not equal retention of colonial legitimacy.
