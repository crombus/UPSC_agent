# Comparative Constitutional Design — Live Session Edition

A news anchor calls both India and the United Kingdom "parliamentary democracies," calls both India and the United States "federations with judicial review," and calls both India and France "republics with a President." Each label is technically true and each comparison, left there, is almost useless — because "parliamentary," "federal," "judicial review" and "President" do not mean the same thing in every system that uses the word. This topic asks a single disciplined question beneath all of that loose vocabulary: when two constitutions share a label, how do we find out whether they actually share a function, and when they do not, what does the difference actually cost or buy a citizen?

This session builds the complete answer from the ground up: what UPSC means by a "constitutional scheme" and the controlled method — context, similarity, difference, constitutional consequence — that turns a list of foreign facts into an analytical answer; the full ten-country master map; parliamentary, presidential and semi-presidential executives; constitutional supremacy against parliamentary sovereignty and the real location of judicial power; federal design and the true difference between Rajya Sabha, the US and Australian Senates and Germany's Bundesrat; rights, secularism and transformative constitutionalism across India, the USA, France and South Africa; courts, judicial review and judicial appointments; the comparative architecture of constitutional amendment; elections, representation and direct democracy; heads of state and the pardon power; emergency design and constitutional safeguards; why a borrowed institution behaves differently once it is transplanted into India; and, finally, the complete demand-routing and mark-scaled answer architecture required by the nine relevant PYQs. It closes with the reusable machinery needed to answer any comparative constitutional question UPSC has not yet asked, not only the ones it already has.

### Where this topic begins

A coaching-centre note says "India is a parliamentary democracy like the UK, a federation like the USA, and has judicial review like both." Every clause is defensible in isolation, and the sentence still teaches almost nothing, because it never asks what each label actually does once it is placed inside India's own written, supreme, transformative Constitution with graded amendment rigidity. The UK's "parliamentary" sits under parliamentary sovereignty; India's sits under a Constitution that can strike down an Act of its own Parliament. The USA's "federation" gives residuary power to the states; India's gives it to the Union. A comparative answer that stops at naming the shared label has described a coincidence of vocabulary, not a constitutional fact.

### Visual: the controlled comparison method

```text

similarity                    difference

                   |

                   |
```

*Caption: a comparative answer never stops at "similar" or "different" — it must state what the difference actually does to rights, accountability, federal balance or stability, and it must end at an India-specific qualification.*

### Simple intuition

Think of a constitution as a machine built to do seven jobs at once: say who ultimately governs, arrange the executive and legislature, divide territorial power, protect rights and handle religion, appoint and empower courts, change itself and handle emergencies, and convert votes into representation and accountability. Two countries can use an identical part-name — "President," "Senate," "federation," "secular" — for parts that do completely different jobs inside their respective machines. The comparative method's entire purpose is to stop you from assuming that a shared part-name means a shared job.

### Technical doctrine: what UPSC means by a "constitutional scheme"

A constitutional scheme is the **connected design** through which a country performs seven tasks together, not a loose list of borrowed provisions:

| Task | What it settles |
|---|---|
| 1. Sovereignty and supremacy | Who or what has the final word — a legislature, a constitution, or a shared settlement between both |
| 2. Executive-legislature relationship | Whether the executive is drawn from, and answerable to, the legislature, or elected and tenured separately |
| 3. Territorial division of power | Whether power is unitary, federal or something designed in between |
| 4. Rights and religion | What is protected, how, and how the state may or may not engage with religion |
| 5. Courts | Who appoints judges, how independent they are, and what remedy a court can actually give |
| 6. Amendment and emergency | How the constitution changes itself, and what happens to it under extraordinary stress |
| 7. Elections and accountability | How votes become seats and how representation is made answerable |

**Core method, stated as a formula:** compare the same institution across countries in the sequence **context → similarity → difference → constitutional consequence**. Never stop at the difference itself; always state what the difference does.

### The ten-country master map

| Country | Constitutional form | Executive model | Territorial form | Constitutional control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | Written, supreme Constitution; republic | Parliamentary; nominal President and responsible Council of Ministers | Holding-together federation with a strong Union | Supreme Court/High Courts; judicial review plus basic structure |
| **United Kingdom** | Uncodified, evolutionary constitution; constitutional monarchy | Westminster parliamentary government | Legally unitary with political devolution | Parliamentary sovereignty; courts generally cannot invalidate an Act of Parliament |
| **United States** | Written, rigid, supreme Constitution; republic | Presidential; separately elected fixed executive | Coming-together federation | Diffuse judicial review; federal and state court systems |
| **Canada** | Written federal constitution plus conventions; constitutional monarchy | Responsible parliamentary government | Federation designed with a comparatively strong centre | Supreme Court and constitutional review |
| **Australia** | Written federal constitution; constitutional monarchy | Federal Westminster system | Coming-together federation | High Court; constitutionally divided powers |
| **France** | Written Constitution of the Fifth Republic; republic | Semi-presidential: President plus Prime Minister | Unitary, decentralised state | Constitutional Council and other courts within a specialised system |
| **Switzerland** | Written federal constitution; republic | Plural Federal Council; collegial executive | Confederation by name, federation in law | Strong direct democracy; limited review of federal statutes |
| **South Africa** | Written, supreme and transformative Constitution; republic | Parliamentary executive; President elected by National Assembly | Constitutionally decentralised cooperative government across national, provincial and local spheres | Constitutional Court; enforceable civil-political and socio-economic rights |
| **Germany** | Written Basic Law; parliamentary federal republic | Chancellor-led parliamentary government | Federation with direct Land-government participation through Bundesrat | Separate Federal Constitutional Court; textual eternity clause |
| **Japan** | Written, supreme Constitution; constitutional monarchy | Parliamentary cabinet; symbolic Emperor | Unitary state | Supreme Court judicial review; rigid amendment plus referendum |

### Example: one noun, four different jobs

Take the single word "President." In India, the office is a nominal constitutional head who ordinarily acts on binding ministerial advice. In the United States, the President personally exercises executive power and is institutionally separated from Congress. In France, the President is the dominant half of a dual executive whose actual power depends on whether the parliamentary majority agrees with him. In Switzerland, the "President" is merely the annually rotating chair of a seven-member collegial Federal Council — not an executive at all in the US sense. **Limit of the example:** this does not mean the word is meaningless — it means the word names an office, and the office's actual power must always be read from selection, tenure, advice-structure and removal, never assumed from the title.

### Comparative treatment: why the method must end at India

The master map above is not an end in itself. UPSC never rewards a tour of ten constitutions for its own sake; every comparison must return to a stated Indian consequence. Two identical-looking facts about the United Kingdom ("uncodified," "parliamentary sovereignty") are worth marks only when the answer then states what India did differently and why — a written, supreme, judicially reviewable Constitution that places Parliament itself under legal limits the UK's Parliament does not share.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Isn't naming the shared and different features of two constitutions already a full comparison? What more does 'constitutional consequence' add?"

**Reply:** A feature list ("UK is uncodified, India is written") is a description, not an argument. The marks lie in showing what that difference *does* — here, that it lets Indian courts bind Parliament through judicial review and the basic structure, while UK courts ordinarily cannot invalidate a Westminster Act at all. Two answers can contain the same facts and earn very different marks depending on whether the facts are connected to a stated consequence.

**Qualified residual:** Some comparisons are genuinely narrower. Switzerland is most useful here for its collegial executive and direct democracy, so it should reinforce a relevant point rather than be forced into every answer.

### UPSC application

This topic's broadest-framed PYQ, 2018 GS-II Q13, asks for the "basic tenets" of two entire political systems — exactly the demand this lesson's seven-task definition and ten-country map are built to answer, because it requires touching every one of the seven constitutional tasks rather than one institution alone. **Common trap:** reducing "basic tenets" to only the executive-legislature relationship and ignoring federalism, rights and judicial review, which a 15-mark "examine" directive expects you to cover together.

### Revision notes

1. A constitutional scheme is a connected design across seven tasks: sovereignty/supremacy, executive-legislature relationship, territorial division, rights/religion, courts, amendment/emergency, and elections/accountability.
2. The core method is context → similarity → difference → constitutional consequence; never stop at the difference itself.
3. "Source is not operational identity" — borrowing identifies ancestry, not present working.
4. The ten-country master map anchors constitutional form, executive model, territorial form and constitutional control for India, UK, USA, Canada, Australia, France, Switzerland, South Africa, Germany and Japan.
5. A shared institutional title ("President," "Senate," "federation," "secular") never proves a shared function; always test selection, power, accountability and review.
6. Every comparison must end at a stated Indian consequence; foreign description alone earns no analytical marks.
7. A feature list without a stated consequence is incomplete, however factually accurate.
8. Switzerland and Japan are most useful here for specific comparisons, so deploy them as focused reinforcements rather than forcing them into every answer.
9. India's written, supreme, judicially reviewable Constitution is the baseline against which every other country's design is read.
10. The 2018 PYQ's "basic tenets" demand requires touching all seven constitutional tasks, not one institution alone.
11. UPSC rewards the India-anchored consequence of a difference, never a tour of foreign facts for its own sake.

### Concept check

**Question:** A learner writes: "India, the UK and the USA are all democracies with elected governments and courts, so their constitutional systems are fundamentally similar." Identify the flaw in this reasoning using this lesson's method.

**Model answer:** The claim stops at a shared label (democracy, elections, courts) without testing whether the underlying function is the same. It ignores that UK courts generally cannot invalidate Parliament's own Acts, while Indian and US courts can strike down unconstitutional legislation; it ignores that the UK executive is fused with the legislature while the US executive is institutionally separated from Congress. "Fundamentally similar" collapses real constitutional differences that the context → similarity → difference → consequence method is designed to surface, and it never lands on any India-specific consequence at all.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating shared democratic vocabulary (elections, courts, government) as proof of a shared constitutional design, rather than testing each institution's actual selection, power, accountability and review separately.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain, with one example, why naming a shared institutional label between two constitutions is not the same as proving a shared constitutional function.

**Model solution:** A shared label such as "President" names an office, not its actual power — the office's real content depends on selection, tenure, advice-structure and removal, which differ sharply across constitutions. India's President is a nominal head who ordinarily acts on binding ministerial advice; the US President personally exercises executive power separated from Congress; France's President shares a dual executive whose real strength depends on parliamentary alignment; Switzerland's "President" is merely a rotating chair of a collegial council. Four systems use the identical word for four structurally different jobs. A comparative answer must therefore test selection, power, accountability and review for each office rather than assume equivalence from the title, and must end by stating what the difference means for India specifically.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Correct statement that a label names an office, not its function — 3; correct four-country illustration of differing presidential power — 4; India-anchored consequence stated — 2; coherent 150-word synthesis — 1.

### Where this lesson begins

"The UK has no written constitution" is one of the most repeated sentences in Indian classrooms preparing for this exam, and it is imprecise in a way that costs marks. The UK has an enormous quantity of constitutional writing — statutes, judgments, authoritative treatises — it simply has no single codified document called "The Constitution." Confusing "no single code" with "no writing at all" is exactly the kind of imprecision a 10-mark objective-style question is built to catch.

### Visual: two independent axes, not one

```text
                 CODIFICATION AXIS
        codified <---------------------> uncodified
      (single authoritative text)      (scattered sources)

                 RIGIDITY AXIS
        rigid <-----------------------> flexible
  (special procedure needed)      (ordinary legislative process)

India                                         -> codified + graded/mixed rigidity
USA / Germany / Japan / South Africa          -> codified + rigid
UK                                            -> uncodified + politically flexible
```

*Caption: codification asks whether the constitution exists as one authoritative text; rigidity asks how hard it is to change. A constitution can combine these independently — the two axes are not synonyms, and conflating them is this lesson's single most tested trap.*

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine two different questions about a rulebook: "Is it written down in one place?" and "How hard is it to change a rule?" A company's single printed handbook that any manager can amend at a meeting is codified but flexible. A set of unwritten customs that a very powerful ethics committee refuses to touch without the agreement of every department is uncodified but, in practice, quite rigid. Constitutions work the same way — codification and rigidity answer different questions, and a constitution's position on one axis tells you nothing automatic about its position on the other.

### Technical doctrine: codification and rigidity defined

**Codification:** whether the constitutional rules exist as a single, authoritative, consolidated text.

**Rigidity:** how difficult the amendment procedure makes it to change the constitution — ranging from an ordinary legislative majority to a supermajority, federal ratification, a referendum, or a combination of these.

| Country | Codified? | Rigid or flexible? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| India | Codified | Mixed — some constitutional changes lie outside Article 368 and use ordinary legislative majorities; Article 368 amendments use a special majority, with state ratification for specified provisions | Graded rigidity by change type |
| United Kingdom | Uncodified | Politically flexible in form | Ordinary parliamentary legislation can alter constitutional rules, though political constraints and convention still operate |
| United States | Codified | Highly rigid | Article V requires a two-thirds proposal route plus three-fourths state ratification |
| Germany | Codified | Rigid, with an unamendable core | Two-thirds in Bundestag and Bundesrat for ordinary amendment; Article 79(3) places some provisions wholly beyond amendment |
| Japan | Codified | Very rigid | Two-thirds of each Diet house plus a popular referendum |

### Example: a codified constitution that is comparatively flexible

The United Kingdom is the standard *uncodified* example, but codification and rigidity can also separate the other way. India illustrates graded change: certain constitutional changes are made by ordinary legislation and lie outside Article 368; amendments under Article 368 require its special majority, and specified federal provisions additionally require ratification by at least half the states. **Limit of this example:** India should not therefore be loosely labelled "flexible" in the UK sense. Its design uses different legal routes for different kinds of change.

### Comparative treatment: form across the master map

Returning to Lesson 1's map, India, the USA, Australia, France, Switzerland, South Africa, Germany and Japan centre their orders on codified constitutional texts. Canada requires a qualification: it is a written, multi-instrument constitutional order comprising several Constitution Acts, orders and amendments, supplemented by conventions; it is not one literally single authoritative document. The United Kingdom remains uncodified, relying on statutes, judgments, authoritative works and conventions. Rigidity varies independently: the UK is comparatively flexible in form, the USA and Japan are highly rigid, while India and Germany protect constitutional identity through different mechanisms.

### Criticism and reply

**Objection:** "If the UK Constitution can be changed by ordinary legislation, isn't it simply weaker or less serious than a codified constitution?"

**Reply:** Flexibility is not automatically a weakness — it allows the UK system to adapt constitutional practice quickly without a cumbersome amendment procedure, and powerful conventions (such as the expectation that the Prime Minister commands Commons confidence) constrain behaviour even without formal legal entrenchment. The trade-off is genuine in both directions: codified rigidity protects stability and federal consent but can make necessary reform slow; uncodified flexibility supports adaptation but depends more heavily on political restraint rather than guaranteed legal limits.

### UPSC application

**Objective-style trap, future-Prelims safety:** the UK constitution is **uncodified**, not wholly unwritten — statutes, conventions, judgments and authoritative works are among its sources. A second trap sits directly beside it: do not treat "written" and "rigid" as synonyms, or "uncodified" and "flexible" as synonyms — codification and rigidity are genuinely separate axes, and a question can test either one independently. This lesson supplies the discrimination table UPSC's objective-style questions repeatedly test on this exact pair of concepts.

### Revision notes

1. Codification asks whether constitutional rules exist as one authoritative text; rigidity asks how hard the constitution is to amend.
2. The two axes are independent — a constitution's codification status does not determine its rigidity, and vice versa.
3. The UK is **uncodified**, not wholly "unwritten" — its sources include statutes, conventions, judgments and authoritative works.
4. Canada is written but multi-instrument, with conventions; do not force it into a literal single-document category.
5. The USA and Japan are highly rigid; the UK is comparatively flexible in form.
6. India's change routes are graded: ordinary-law changes outside Article 368, Article 368 special-majority amendments, and specified amendments also requiring half-state ratification.
7. Germany combines codified rigidity with an unamendable textual core under Article 79(3).
8. Flexibility is not automatically a design weakness; it trades guaranteed legal entrenchment for adaptive speed and depends more on political restraint.
9. Rigidity protects stability and federal consent but can slow necessary reform.
10. "Written" is not a synonym for "rigid," and "uncodified" is not a synonym for "flexible" — this exact confusion is UPSC's most repeated objective-style trap on this topic.

### Concept check

**Question:** A statement reads: "Since the UK constitution is unwritten, it must also be the easiest constitution in the world to change." Evaluate both halves of this claim.

**Model answer:** Both halves contain an error. The first half is imprecise: the UK constitution is not unwritten — it is uncodified, meaning its sources (statutes, conventions, judicial decisions, authoritative works) are extensive but not consolidated into one document. The second half conflates codification with rigidity: the UK is comparatively flexible in form because ordinary legislation can alter constitutional rules, but this flexibility follows from its lack of a single entrenched text and strong political convention, not automatically from being "unwritten." A codified constitution could, in principle, also be comparatively easy to amend, and an uncodified system can still be constrained by powerful convention even without formal rigidity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "uncodified" as a synonym for "unwritten," and treating codification and rigidity as though they were the same property of a constitution.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse written/uncodified and rigid/flexible constitutions without treating the pairs as synonyms, using India, the UK, the USA and Australia.

**Model solution:** Codification and rigidity are separate constitutional questions. Codification asks whether constitutional rules exist as one authoritative text; rigidity asks how difficult amendment is in practice.

India, the USA and Australia centre their systems on codified constitutional texts. Canada, by contrast, shows why "written" must not be equated with "one document": its constitutional order comprises multiple constitutional enactments and orders plus conventions. The UK is uncodified, drawing on statutes such as the Human Rights Act, judicial decisions, conventions and authoritative works — a real and extensive body of constitutional material, just not consolidated into one code.

Rigidity cuts across this split independently. The USA is highly rigid: Article V requires a two-thirds proposal route and ratification by three-fourths of the states. Australia adds a referendum with a national majority and a majority in a majority of states. In India, some constitutional changes are ordinary legislation outside Article 368; Article 368 amendments use a special majority, with half-state ratification for specified federal matters. The UK, despite being uncodified, is comparatively flexible in form, though political constraint and convention still operate.

The lesson for India is that it already demonstrates graded rigidity within one codified text rather than uniform entrenchment, and that comparing it against the UK's flexible-uncodified model or the USA's rigid-codified model requires naming both axes separately rather than collapsing them into a single "written equals rigid" assumption.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Correct definition of both axes as independent — 4; correct codification classification across all four countries — 4; correct rigidity classification across all four countries — 4; India's graded-rigidity point stated — 2; coherent 250-word conclusion — 1.

### Visual: three answers to the same executive problem

| Design question | India/UK: parliamentary | USA: presidential | France: semi-presidential |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who leads government? | Prime Minister and cabinet | President | President plus Prime Minister |
| Link to legislature | Fusion; ministers sit in legislature | Institutional separation | Dual executive; government needs Assembly confidence |
| Survival | Lower-house confidence | Fixed tenure, subject to impeachment | President fixed; government answerable to Assembly |
| Main gain | Continuous responsibility | Stability and independent mandate | Flexible allocation of authority |
| Main risk | Majority concentration | Deadlock | Rival legitimacy/cohabitation |

*Caption: executive labels become meaningful only after selection, confidence, tenure, dissolution and removal are compared.*

India's President is the constitutional head; the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister exercises ordinary governmental authority and is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3). The UK supplies the Westminster ancestry, but India constitutionalised cabinet government inside a republic, federation and supreme written Constitution. The US President combines head-of-state and head-of-government roles, cannot dissolve Congress, and does not depend on congressional confidence.

France divides executive authority. When the President and National Assembly majority align, presidential leadership is usually dominant. During **cohabitation**, an opposing parliamentary majority strengthens the Prime Minister, especially in domestic government. France must therefore never be described as permanently President-dominated.

Germany modifies parliamentary removal through the **constructive vote of no confidence**: the Bundestag removes a Chancellor only while electing a successor. Switzerland uses a seven-member collegial Federal Council; its annually rotating President chairs rather than commands it. These are different attempts to combine continuity and accountability.

### Why India's Prime Minister can be powerful without being a US President

A stable Lok Sabha majority may let the Prime Minister coordinate cabinet, party and legislative agenda. Yet that authority is relational: it depends on party support, collective responsibility, parliamentary membership, federal politics, courts and elections. A US President has an independent mandate and fixed tenure but faces a separately elected Congress. Thus "actual authority" cannot be ranked abstractly; it varies with majority control, party cohesion and constitutional veto points.

### UPSC application

**2026 GS-II Q14** belongs here. Its two limbs require: first, fusion with checks in India versus stricter institutional separation in the USA; second, the Prime Minister's majority-mediated authority versus the US President's independent but checked authority. Calling it Q15 routes the pressure-groups question incorrectly.

### Revision notes

1. Parliamentary government fuses executive and legislature but makes the cabinet continuously responsible.
2. Presidential government separates personnel and tenure; it does not eliminate checks.
3. India's President is ordinarily nominal; the Prime Minister and Council are the real executive.
4. The US President cannot dissolve Congress and does not require congressional confidence.
5. Article 75(3) constitutionalises collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha.
6. France has a dual executive, not a permanently supreme President.
7. Majority alignment usually strengthens the French President; cohabitation strengthens the Prime Minister.
8. Germany's constructive no-confidence rule combines removal with immediate replacement.
9. Switzerland's rotating presidency is not a US-style presidency.
10. Actual authority depends on party alignment and veto points, not title alone.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can an Indian Prime Minister appear more dominant than a US President even though India has a parliamentary, not presidential, executive?

**Model answer:** A disciplined Lok Sabha majority may let the Prime Minister unite cabinet, party and legislative agenda, whereas a US President confronts a separately elected Congress. The Prime Minister's strength is therefore majority-mediated and can weaken with coalition fragmentation or loss of confidence; the President's authority is independently mandated and fixed-tenure but institutionally checked.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating visible political dominance with presidential design; institutional source, tenure and accountability remain different.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine whether parliamentary fusion necessarily produces weaker checks than presidential separation.

**Model solution:** Parliamentary fusion and presidential separation distribute checks differently. In India, the executive emerges from Parliament and survives through Lok Sabha confidence. Question Hour, committees, opposition scrutiny, judicial review, federal institutions and elections check it. Fusion also makes removal politically direct. Its danger is majority concentration: a disciplined majority may convert legislative scrutiny into executive support.

The US design separates the President from Congress through distinct elections and fixed tenure. Bicameral legislation, appropriations, confirmation, oversight and judicial review multiply veto points. This can restrain unilateral action, but divided government may also create deadlock and diffuse responsibility.

The comparison therefore turns on operation, not labels. A coalition Parliament may check government intensely; a unified US party government may reduce friction. Conversely, a strong Indian majority can weaken legislative independence, while divided US government can overproduce obstruction. India should improve committee scrutiny and internal party democracy rather than copy fixed presidential tenure. Fusion offers answerability and replaceability; separation offers institutional independence. Neither guarantees effective checks without competitive parties, conventions and constitutional review.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Define both designs — 3; explain Indian checks and majority risk — 4; explain US veto points and deadlock risk — 4; operational qualification — 2; India-specific verdict — 2.

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Propose a constitutional-fit test for deciding whether India should adopt a foreign institution.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why Canada should be described as a written multi-instrument constitutional order rather than a literally single-document constitution.

**Model solution:** Canada's constitutional order is written and legally supreme, but it is not exhausted by one consolidated document. Its core law includes multiple Constitution Acts, schedules, amendments and constitutional instruments, supplemented by conventions governing responsible government and the Crown.

The distinction matters because **written** answers whether authoritative constitutional rules have textual legal form, while **codified in one document** asks whether they are consolidated in a single text. Canada satisfies the first description without fitting the second literally. The United Kingdom differs further: it is uncodified and draws constitutional rules from statutes, judgments, conventions and authoritative works without one entrenched constitutional code.

For India, the lesson is methodological. A comparison should not force every country into a written/unwritten binary. It should identify the relevant instruments, the role of conventions and the amendment hierarchy before drawing conclusions about supremacy or rigidity.

**Unique rubric (10 marks):** Written/multi-instrument distinction — 3; identify Acts/instruments/conventions — 3; UK contrast — 2; methodological consequence — 2.

## Final 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess whether constitutional review can protect rights without producing judicial supremacy.

**Model solution:** Constitutional review protects rights by requiring public power to remain within a superior legal charter. In India, the Supreme Court and High Courts may invalidate legislation, enforce writs and review amendments that damage basic structure. In the USA, courts similarly enforce a supreme Constitution. These powers differ from the UK's parliamentary-sovereignty framework, where courts ordinarily cannot annul Westminster primary legislation.

Strong review nevertheless creates a legitimacy concern when broad rights language permits judges to shape policy. Appointment opacity, weak remedial restraint or disregard of institutional competence may shift responsibility from elected bodies. Yet this does not establish unlimited judicial supremacy. Courts remain bound by constitutional text, precedent, jurisdiction, reason-giving and review procedures; legislatures retain policy choice within constitutional boundaries and can respond through compliant law.

Germany's textual eternity clause and South Africa's express supremacy show that review may protect identity or socio-economic rights through different remedial structures. The relevant question is not court versus democracy, but whether review preserves democratic preconditions: equal citizenship, fair process and limited government.

India should strengthen transparent appointments, reasoned proportionality and remedial sensitivity while preserving review. Constitutional supremacy requires an independent judiciary, but judicial legitimacy requires discipline and institutional dialogue. Rights protection and democratic government are complementary when each institution respects its constitutional role.

**Unique rubric (15 marks):** Rights-protection case — 3; India/USA/UK comparison — 4; legitimacy risks — 3; limits on courts and legislative role — 3; reform-based verdict — 2.

## Final 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Compare how India, the USA, France and Germany balance executive authority, legislative accountability and constitutional continuity.

**Model solution:** The four systems allocate mandate, removal and continuity differently.

India uses parliamentary fusion. The Prime Minister and Council emerge from the legislature and remain collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This enables continuous accountability and replacement, but a disciplined majority may concentrate power. Constitutional review and federalism add limits.

The USA separates a fixed-tenure President from Congress. Independent electoral mandates and congressional legislation, finance, oversight and confirmation produce checks and continuity. The cost is deadlock and diffuse responsibility, especially under divided government.

France divides authority between a directly elected President and a Prime Minister responsible to the National Assembly. Majority alignment generally enables presidential predominance; cohabitation shifts domestic leadership toward the Prime Minister. Dual legitimacy supplies flexibility but can generate rivalry.

Germany retains parliamentary responsibility while requiring a constructive vote of no confidence: the Bundestag can remove a Chancellor only by electing a successor. This prevents a negative majority from creating an executive vacuum and links accountability with continuity.

No model maximises all three values. India's confidence principle makes removal easier than impeachment, the USA protects tenure through separation, France adjusts authority through majority alignment, and Germany proceduralises replacement. Their operation also depends on parties and conventions.

For India, German constructive replacement can inform debate on stability, but transplantation is unnecessary without proving a specific defect. Better committee scrutiny, internal party democracy and transparent coalition responsibility more directly strengthen accountability. Design must balance governability, answerability and continuity rather than absolutise one value.

6. India has an integrated hierarchy; the USA has federal and state systems.
7. Collegium protects independence but faces opacity, delay and diversity-accountability criticism.
8. US nomination plus Senate consent adds visibility and partisan risk.
9. The reform standard is accountable independence.

## Federalism and upper houses

1. Federalism combines self-rule and shared rule.
2. India is a holding-together federation with a strong Union.
3. Union features include residuary power, single citizenship, integrated judiciary and emergency centralisation.
4. Rajya Sabha representation is unequal and generally indirectly elected.
5. US and Australian Senates give states equal seats.
6. Bundesrat represents Land governments through weighted governmental votes.
7. Canada combines federalism with a historically strong centre and federal residuary power.
8. Articles 249 and 312 are distinctive Rajya Sabha federal powers.

## Rights, secularism and transformation

1. Indian secularism joins equal citizenship, religious liberty, reform and bounded engagement.
2. US secularism combines non-establishment and free exercise.
3. Neither model is absolute separation or unlimited intervention.
4. French laïcité stresses republican neutrality in its historical context.
5. Transfer principles—proportionality, inclusion, neutrality—not entire mechanisms.
6. South Africa expressly protects socio-economic rights.
7. India uses Fundamental Rights, DPSPs, legislation and expanded Article 21.
8. *Maneka Gandhi* requires just, fair and reasonable procedure.

## Elections, mercy and emergency

1. India's indirect presidential election fits a normally nominal federal head.
2. France's direct two-round election supports an active presidency.
3. Lok Sabha FPTP produces constituency winners whose majority sustains the cabinet; the separately elected US Congress can create a dual mandate with the President.
4. Election method follows office function.
5. Swiss initiatives and referendums add direct participation and binary-choice risks.
6. Article 72 clemency is exercised on ministerial advice and is reviewable on limited grounds.
7. US pardon covers federal offences, not state offences or impeachment.
8. Pre-emptive pardon means completed conduct before charge or conviction, never future conduct.
9. Emergency comparison requires trigger, approval, duration, rights, federal effect, review and termination.
10. Post-44th safeguards include written Cabinet advice, one-month special-majority approval, six-month renewals and Lok Sabha-triggered revocation.
11. The constitutional aim is anti-normalisation of exceptional power.

## Transplantation and answer writing

1. Borrowing identifies ancestry, not operational identity.
2. Institutions pass through founding history, cleavages, parties, federalism, legal culture and capacity.
3. Outcomes may be adaptation, hybridisation or functional substitution.
4. Apply functional, structural, incentive, capacity, rights, transition and accountability tests.
5. Ten marks: one comparator, two or three contrasts, one consequence.
6. Fifteen marks: three or four axes, qualification and verdict.
7. Twenty marks: add trade-offs, context and transplantation.
8. Use claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification.
9. Depth beats a shallow country tour.
10. Conclude with a graded design trade-off.
