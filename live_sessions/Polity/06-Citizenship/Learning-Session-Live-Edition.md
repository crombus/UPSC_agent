## Lesson 1 — Who belongs to the political community?

Progress: 1/12 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Citizen, alien, nationality, domicile and residence

### 🖼️ Visual: five labels that answer five different questions

```text
PERSON
  │
  ├── CITIZENSHIP → "Am I a full legal member of India?"
  │                    rights + political membership + allegiance
  │
  ├── NATIONALITY → "Which State is my international legal bond linked to?"
  │                    often overlaps with citizenship; not a universal synonym
  │
  ├── DOMICILE → "Which legal system is my permanent home?"
  │                    residence + intention of permanence
  │
  ├── RESIDENCE → "Where do I factually or ordinarily live?"
  │                    may change without changing citizenship
  │
  └── OCI → "Am I a foreign national with specified statutory India privileges?"
                       not Indian citizenship
```

*The visual prevents the most common error: treating membership, permanent legal home,
physical presence and an overseas card as interchangeable.*

### 1. Start with the ordinary idea

A club may admit members, visitors and former members. All may enter the building, but
only full members vote on the club's governing body. A State also distinguishes between
full political members and non-members, but the consequences are far more serious:
membership affects franchise, public office, protection abroad and legal allegiance.

✅ **Citizenship** is full legal membership of a political community. It carries a
reciprocal relationship: the citizen owes allegiance and duties, while the State
recognises rights, protection and political participation.

The analogy has a limit. Constitutional rights are not private club privileges granted
at whim. Many rights protect every **person**, including foreigners; only specified
rights and political capacities are citizen-specific.

### 2. Citizen and alien

✅ A **citizen** is a full member of the Indian State. An **alien** is a citizen or
national of another State.

| Category | Meaning | Citizenship consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Friendly alien | National of a country maintaining peaceful relations with India | Receives person-based constitutional protections, but not citizen-only rights |
| Enemy alien | National of a State at war with India | Article 22(3)(a) excludes only the safeguards in Article 22(1) and 22(2) |
| Stateless person | Not recognised as a national by any State under its law | Must not be casually equated with a person breaching immigration law or with a refugee |
| Refugee/asylum seeker | Seeks protection from persecution or serious harm | Refugee status does not itself create Indian citizenship |

⚠️ **Precision:** Article 22(3)(a) does not erase the whole of Article 22 for enemy
aliens. It says that clauses **22(1) and 22(2)** shall not apply. Preventive-detention
rules in Article 22(4)–(7) form a different branch.

### 3. Which constitutional rights are citizen-specific?

| Provision or capacity | Textual/legal position |
|---|---|
| Article 15 | Protects a **citizen** against specified discrimination by the State |
| Article 16 | Equality of opportunity for **citizens** in public employment |
| Article 19 | Six freedoms available to **citizens** |
| Article 29(1) | Protects a section of **citizens** having a distinct language, script or culture |
| Article 29(2) | No **citizen** may be denied admission on the listed grounds |
| Article 30 | Text speaks of **minorities**, not expressly of citizens |
| Voting | Citizenship is required under the constitutional/statutory electoral framework |
| Contesting Parliament/State legislature | Citizenship is a statutory qualification |
| President, Vice-President, Governor, judges, AG/Advocate General | Citizenship required by the relevant constitutional provision |

✅ Articles 14, 20 and 21 are examples of protections framed for **persons**, not only
citizens.

✅ Article 58 requires the President to be a citizen of India; unlike the United States
rule for its President, the Indian Constitution does not reserve the office to a citizen
by birth. A naturalised citizen may qualify if every constitutional condition is met.

⚠️ **Article 29/30 trap:** A textbook shorthand that places both Articles 29 and 30 in a
"citizens only" list is unsafe. Article 29 uses citizen language; Article 30 speaks of
religious or linguistic minorities. Detailed minority-right doctrine belongs to the
Fundamental Rights topic.

### 4. Citizenship versus nationality

The two terms often overlap in ordinary Indian official usage, especially in passports
and international dealings. A rigid claim that they always mean different things is
therefore unsafe.

- ✅ In Indian domestic constitutional law, **citizenship** is the controlling legal
  status under Part II and the Citizenship Act, 1955.
- ⚠️ **Nationality** is commonly used for the wider international legal bond between a
  person and a State.
- ⚠️ Different legal systems may use the two words differently. In India they are often
  used interchangeably in practice, but OCI proves that an overseas connection or
  Indian origin is not the same as Indian citizenship.

> **Answer line:** Citizenship is India's domestic status of full political membership;
> nationality often describes the wider international bond, but the two terms cannot be
> separated by one universal formula across all legal systems.

### 5. Domicile and residence

✅ **Domicile** is a permanent legal home: it combines factual presence with an intention
to make a legal system one's permanent home. ✅ **Residence** is factual or ordinary
living in a place and may be temporary.

Example:

```text
Indian citizen from Kerala
      ↓ moves for employment
Ordinarily resides in Maharashtra
      ↓
Citizenship remains Indian
Residence changes
No "Maharashtra citizenship" is created
```

The example establishes that mobility does not change national membership. It does not
prove that every residence preference is invalid; later lessons show the bounded
constitutional space for such preferences.

### 6. Why this distinction matters

1. Long residence does not automatically create citizenship.
2. A State "domicile certificate" ordinarily records local status for a specified
   administrative purpose; it does not create State citizenship.
3. OCI is a foreign-national statutory status, not half-citizenship.
4. Aadhaar establishes identity under its statute, not citizenship or domicile.
5. A person may possess person-based Fundamental Rights without being a citizen.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** citizen-only versus person-based rights; OCI versus citizenship;
  citizenship versus residence.
- **Mains:** define terms before discussing domicile-based employment or the CAA.
- **Boundary:** detailed Article 19 restrictions and minority-right doctrine remain
  with Fundamental Rights; this lesson uses only the membership distinction.

### Revision notes

1. Citizenship means full legal and political membership.
2. Aliens are not deprived of every constitutional right.
3. Enemy aliens are excluded specifically from Article 22(1)–(2).
4. Articles 15, 16, 19 and 29 use citizen-specific language.
5. Article 30 speaks textually of minorities.
6. Voting and specified public offices require citizenship under their own provisions.
7. Nationality and citizenship often overlap but are not universal synonyms.
8. Domicile is permanent legal home; residence is factual presence.
9. India has no separate State citizenship.
10. OCI is a foreign-national statutory card status.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Distinguish citizenship, nationality, domicile and
residence. Why does this distinction matter in Indian constitutional law?

**Model answer (144 words):**

Citizenship is full legal membership of India under Part II of the Constitution and the
Citizenship Act, 1955; it carries political capacities such as voting eligibility and
access to specified offices. Nationality usually describes a person's international
legal bond with a State. The terms overlap in Indian practice, but they are not perfect
synonyms in every legal system. Domicile is a permanent legal home, requiring residence
plus an intention of permanence, whereas residence is factual or ordinary presence.
The distinction prevents three errors. First, a State residence or "domicile"
certificate cannot create separate State citizenship. Second, long residence does not
automatically confer Indian citizenship. Third, political rights may require both
citizenship and a residence-based electoral connection. OCI further illustrates the
boundary: it gives a foreign national specified statutory privileges but no Indian
political membership. Thus, India combines one national citizenship with
purpose-specific residence rules.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** definitions 3; qualified nationality distinction
1; two legal consequences 2; OCI or electoral illustration 1; analytical explanation 2;
qualified conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** An Indian citizen shifts permanently from Bihar to Karnataka, receives a Karnataka residence certificate and later obtains an OCI card after acquiring foreign citizenship. Which legal statuses changed at each stage?

**Model answer:** Moving and receiving a residence certificate change the person's residence/local administrative status, not Indian citizenship or create Karnataka citizenship. Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship engages Section 9 of the Citizenship Act and ends Indian citizenship according to law. OCI, if granted, is then a statutory foreign-national card status; it does not restore Indian citizenship.

**Misconception to avoid:** A State residence or "domicile" certificate is not a second citizenship, and OCI is not a revival of the citizenship that ended on acquiring foreign citizenship.

The vocabulary is now clear. The next question is historical: who counted as a citizen
on the day the Constitution commenced?

## Lesson 2 — Article 5: the domicile gateway on 26 January 1950

Progress: 2/12 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Citizenship at commencement under Article 5

Book context: Queried — Constitution Part II and citizenship chapter
CA search: "No additional current linkage — Article 5 is a commencement provision"
CA found: None used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Visual: Article 5 is an AND gate followed by an OR gate

```text
AT COMMENCEMENT: 26 JANUARY 1950
               │
               ▼
     DOMICILE IN TERRITORY OF INDIA
          (mandatory gateway)
               │
               ▼
      ANY ONE OF THREE CONDITIONS
       ┌──────────┼───────────┐
       ▼          ▼           ▼
 born in India  either parent  ordinarily resident
                born in India  in India for ≥5 years
                               immediately before
                               commencement
       └──────────┴───────────┘
               │
               ▼
       CITIZEN UNDER ARTICLE 5
```

*Domicile is compulsory; birth, parental birth and five-year ordinary residence are
alternative connecting factors.*

### 1. Why Article 5 was needed

At independence and Partition, the new Republic needed a starting register of political
membership. The Constitution therefore did not attempt to write a permanent citizenship
code. It identified citizens **at commencement** and left future acquisition and loss to
Parliament.

✅ Article 5 begins: "At the commencement of this Constitution". Its task is historical,
not a continuing birth-citizenship rule.

### 2. The two-stage test

Article 5 requires:

1. **Domicile in the territory of India**; and
2. at least one of:
   - birth in the territory of India;
   - either parent born in the territory of India; or
   - ordinary residence in the territory of India for **not less than five years
     immediately preceding commencement**.

The word **and** joins domicile to the second part. The word **or** separates the three
connecting factors.

### 3. Domicile is not mere presence

Suppose A lived in India for six years before 1950 but always intended to return
permanently to another country. Ordinary residence may be present, but domicile could
still be disputed. Conversely, birth in India alone was not enough under Article 5
without domicile in India.

⚠️ Domicile is a mixed question of fact and intention. Article 5 used it as the
membership gateway because the Constituent Assembly needed a durable connection, not
accidental presence.

### 4. What Article 5 does not do

- It does not govern a child born in India today; Section 3 of the 1955 Act does.
- It does not create State domiciles.
- It does not require all three alternatives.
- It does not make citizenship a Fundamental Right.
- It does not decide the special Partition-migration categories in Articles 6 and 7.

### 5. One domicile in the constitutional sense

✅ In *Pradeep Jain v. Union of India* (1984), the Supreme Court emphasised one
citizenship and one domicile in India while allowing that constitutionally controlled
residence-based preferences may exist.

This does not make local residence rules disappear. It means the phrase "State domicile"
should not be mistaken for a sovereign legal membership parallel to Indian citizenship.

> **Answer line:** Article 5 used domicile as the permanent-connection gateway and birth,
> parental birth or five-year residence as alternative factual links to India at
> commencement.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** identify the mandatory and alternative components.
- **Mains:** use Article 5 to distinguish domicile from residence.
- **Close-option trap:** "born in India at commencement" was not independently sufficient
  without domicile.

### Revision notes

1. Article 5 operated on 26 January 1950.
2. Domicile in India was mandatory.
3. Birth in India was one alternative link.
4. Either parent's birth in India was another.
5. Five years' ordinary residence immediately before commencement was the third.
6. Only one of the three alternatives was needed.
7. Article 5 is not today's citizenship-by-birth provision.
8. Domicile combines permanent legal home and intention.
9. State residence status is not State citizenship.
10. Articles 6 and 7 displace Article 5 for their migration categories.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain the structure and continuing significance of
Article 5 of the Constitution.

**Model answer (130 words):**

Article 5 identified citizens at the Constitution's commencement; it is not a permanent
rule for present-day births. Its structure contains one mandatory gateway and three
alternative links. A person required domicile in the territory of India and, in
addition, birth in India, either parent's birth in India, or ordinary residence for at
least five years immediately before 26 January 1950. Domicile signified a permanent
legal connection, while birth and residence supplied factual links. The provision's
continuing importance is interpretive. It explains why citizenship, domicile and
residence are distinct; why India recognises one national political membership; and why
current acquisition must be traced to the Citizenship Act, 1955 rather than Article 5.
Its limit is equally important: Partition migrants and persons of Indian origin abroad
were addressed separately by Articles 6–8.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** commencement purpose 1; mandatory domicile 2;
three alternatives 3; present-law distinction 1; significance 2; Article 6–8
qualification 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** In January 1950, B had lived in India for six years but lacked domicile in India. C had domicile in India and one parent born in India but was personally born abroad. How does Article 5 treat the two cases?

**Model answer:** B does not satisfy Article 5 merely through five years' ordinary residence because domicile in India is the mandatory gateway. C satisfies Article 5 because domicile is present and either parent's birth in India is one of the three alternative connecting factors; C's own foreign birth does not defeat that route.

**Misconception to avoid:** The three factual links are alternatives, but none replaces the separate domicile requirement.

Article 5 covered ordinary commencement membership. Partition produced exceptional
migration routes, which Articles 6 and 7 addressed with exact dates and procedures.

                                  │

         │                        │                        │

*The dates are functional: 19 July 1948 marked the permit-system divide; 1 March 1947
marked the migration-to-Pakistan exclusion in Article 7.*

### 1. Article 6: migration from Pakistan to India

Article 6 begins with ancestry. The migrant, either parent or any grandparent had to
have been born in India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935 as originally
enacted.

It then divides migrants into two cohorts:

| Migration date | Additional condition |
|---|---|
| **Before 19 July 1948** | Ordinarily resident in India since the date of migration |
| **On or after 19 July 1948** | Registered as a citizen on an application made before commencement; at least **six months' residence immediately before the application** |

Why the division? The permit system for migration was introduced on 19 July 1948.
Earlier migration was tested through continuing residence; later migration required
registration.

### 2. Article 7: migration to Pakistan

✅ A person who migrated from India to territory now included in Pakistan **after
1 March 1947** was not deemed an Indian citizen under Articles 5 and 6.

But the Constitution recognised return:

```text
migrated India → Pakistan after 1 March 1947
                    ↓
normally excluded by Article 7
                    ↓
returned to India under permit for
resettlement or permanent return
                    ↓
treated, for Article 6(b), as a migrant
to India after 19 July 1948
                    ↓
registration route + six-month residence condition
```

The permit is decisive. A casual or temporary return is not the constitutional
exception.

### 3. Article 8: persons of Indian origin residing outside India

Article 8 covered a person ordinarily residing outside India if that person, either
parent or any grandparent was born in India as defined by the 1935 Act.

Citizenship depended on registration by India's diplomatic or consular representative
in the country of residence. The application could be made **before or after**
commencement.

Article 8 therefore created an overseas registration route at commencement. It was not
the modern OCI scheme and did not create a general doctrine of dual citizenship.

### 4. The four commencement categories together

| Category | Constitutional route |
|---|---|
| Domiciled persons with one Article 5 link | Article 5 |
| Qualifying migrants from Pakistan | Article 6 |
| Returnees from Pakistan with resettlement/permanent-return permit | Article 7 proviso read with Article 6 |
| Qualifying Indian-origin persons residing abroad | Article 8 |

### 5. Criticism and constitutional response

**Objection:** The scheme appears technical and unequal because migration date,
ancestry, permits and registration produced different outcomes.

**Reply:** Partition created legally different situations: settled residents, early
migrants, regulated later migrants, persons who left India and authorised returnees.
The provisions attempted a finite commencement settlement rather than a universal
migration code.

**Residual:** These provisions cannot resolve later migration. Parliament's post-1950
statute and special provisions such as Section 6A address later periods.

> **Answer line:** Articles 6–8 transformed Partition's movement of people into a finite
> constitutional settlement by combining ancestry with date, residence, permit or
> consular-registration controls.

### UPSC application

- Memorise **19 July 1948**, **1 March 1947** and the **six-month** condition.
- Do not merge Article 7 returnees with ordinary Article 6 migrants.
- Do not call Article 8 the source of OCI.
- Use a timeline rather than prose alone in a Mains answer.

### Revision notes

1. Article 6 concerns migration from Pakistan to India.
2. It requires a qualifying birth link to undivided India.
3. Before 19 July 1948: ordinary residence since migration.
4. On/after 19 July 1948: registration plus six months' prior residence.
5. Article 7 concerns migration to Pakistan after 1 March 1947.
6. Return required a permit for resettlement or permanent return.
7. Such returnees entered the post-19 July Article 6 route.
8. Article 8 concerns Indian-origin persons ordinarily residing abroad.
9. Article 8 registration could occur before or after commencement.
10. Articles 6–8 are commencement provisions, not today's general migration law.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** How did Articles 6, 7 and 8 convert the dislocation
of Partition into a constitutional citizenship settlement?

**Model answer (136 words):**

Articles 6–8 created differentiated but finite routes for Partition-era membership.
Article 6 covered persons migrating from Pakistan who had a parental or grandparental
birth link to undivided India. Migrants before 19 July 1948 required continuous ordinary
residence from migration; later migrants required registration and six months'
residence before application. Article 7 excluded persons who migrated to Pakistan after
1 March 1947, but restored a route for those returning under a permit for resettlement
or permanent return by treating them as post-19 July Article 6 migrants. Article 8
covered persons of Indian origin ordinarily residing abroad through diplomatic or
consular registration, even after commencement. The design balanced humanitarian,
ancestral and security concerns through date, residence and official-verification
filters. Its limitation was temporal: post-commencement migration required legislation
under Article 11 rather than indefinite extension of these Partition provisions.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** Article 6 ancestry/date split 3; Article 7
exclusion and permit 2; Article 8 1.5; constitutional rationale 1.5; temporal limitation
1; coherent conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** D migrated from India to Pakistan in April 1947 and returned in 1949 with a permit for permanent return. E migrated from Pakistan to India in August 1948 and applied for registration after living in India for four months. Identify the controlling constitutional tests.

**Model answer:** D falls under Article 7's exclusion but may use its proviso because the return was under a permit for permanent return; D is then treated as a post-19 July 1948 migrant under Article 6 and must satisfy that registration route. E also falls in the post-19 July 1948 Article 6 cohort but lacks the required six months' residence immediately before the registration application.

**Misconception to avoid:** Return from Pakistan did not automatically restore citizenship, and every post-19 July 1948 migrant did not qualify merely by entering India.

Articles 5–8 identify commencement citizens. We now need the bridge from that finite
settlement to the continuing power of citizenship law.

## Lesson 4 — Articles 9–11: from constitutional settlement to parliamentary law

Book context: Queried — Constitution Part II and Citizenship Act architecture
CA search: "No additional current linkage — Articles 9–11 are structural provisions"
CA found: None used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Visual: the constitutional handover

```text
ARTICLES 5–8
Who qualified at commencement?
        │
        ├── ARTICLE 9
        │   foreign citizenship defeats the listed commencement routes
        │
        ├── ARTICLE 10
        │   citizenship continues, subject to parliamentary law
        │
        └── ARTICLE 11
            Parliament may regulate acquisition, termination
            and all other citizenship matters
                        │
                        ▼
              CITIZENSHIP ACT, 1955
        acquisition + determination + loss + OCI + special routes
```

*Article 9 closes specified commencement claims; Articles 10 and 11 make the settlement
continuing but legislatively adjustable.*

### 1. Article 9: read its words, not a slogan

Article 9 states that no person shall be a citizen **by virtue of Article 5**, or deemed
to be a citizen **by virtue of Article 6 or Article 8**, if that person voluntarily
acquired the citizenship of a foreign State.

Three points follow:

1. It addresses the listed commencement routes.
2. Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship is decisive.
3. Article 9 should not be presented as the complete post-1950 code for every dual-status
   question.

✅ Later voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship is governed principally by
**Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, 1955**, which also provides for determination of
whether, when and how foreign citizenship was acquired.

> **Safe formulation:** Article 9 controlled specified commencement claims; Section 9
> of the 1955 Act controls later voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship.

### 2. Article 10: continuity is legally qualified

Article 10 continues the citizenship of every person who is or is deemed a citizen under
Part II, but expressly makes continuance **subject to any law made by Parliament**.

It therefore prevents a vacuum after commencement without freezing citizenship forever
against legislation.

### 3. Article 11: Parliament receives the continuing field

Article 11 provides that nothing in the preceding Part II provisions derogates from
Parliament's power to make law concerning:

- acquisition of citizenship;
- termination of citizenship; and
- all other matters relating to citizenship.

Parliament used this power to enact the Citizenship Act, 1955.

⚠️ **Plenary does not mean immune:** citizenship legislation remains ordinary law
subject to the Constitution, including judicial review under Article 14 and other
applicable provisions.

### 4. Is citizenship a Fundamental Right?

No. Citizenship is a constitutional and statutory status, but it is not placed among
the Fundamental Rights in Part III.

This distinction matters:

```text
Citizenship status
      ↓ governed by Part II + statute
Citizen-specific Fundamental Rights
      ↓ become available because the person is a citizen
But citizenship itself ≠ a Part III Fundamental Right
```

A law affecting citizenship may still be challenged for violating applicable
Fundamental Rights or structural constitutional limits.

### 5. Constitution versus statute

| Question | Controlling source |
|---|---|
| Who qualified on 26 January 1950? | Articles 5–8 |
| Foreign citizenship and listed commencement routes | Article 9 |
| Continuance of Part II citizenship | Article 10 |
| Legislative competence over later citizenship | Article 11 |
| Birth, descent, registration, naturalisation | Citizenship Act, 1955 |
| Renunciation, termination, deprivation | Citizenship Act, 1955 |
| OCI and Sections 6A/6B | Citizenship Act, 1955 |

### 6. A judicial precision

✅ *Izhar Ahmad Khan v. Union of India* (1962) examined the statutory mechanism for
determining voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship under Section 9. The useful
lesson here is institutional: a disputed post-commencement termination question must be
handled through the prescribed statutory determination, not by treating Article 9 as a
self-executing answer to every later dispute.

Detailed passport-evidence doctrine is not required for a core citizenship answer.

### 7. Criticism and reply

**Objection:** Article 11 gives Parliament excessive control over a foundational status.

**Reply:** A permanent constitutional code could not anticipate migration, territorial
change, diaspora relations or later forms of acquisition. Legislative flexibility was
necessary.

**Qualification:** Flexibility must be disciplined by equality, fair procedure and
judicial review; statutory citizenship cannot become constitution-free discretion.

### UPSC application

- Start a constitutional-scheme answer with "finite commencement settlement plus
  continuing parliamentary code".
- Distinguish Article 9 from Section 9.
- Never call citizenship itself a Fundamental Right.
- Use Article 11 to explain why amendments to the 1955 Act are legally central.

### Revision notes

1. Article 9 expressly mentions Articles 5, 6 and 8.
2. It concerns voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship.
3. Section 9 governs later voluntary foreign-citizenship termination.
4. Article 10 continues Part II citizenship subject to parliamentary law.
5. Article 11 preserves Parliament's broad citizenship power.
6. The Citizenship Act was enacted in 1955.
7. Citizenship is not itself a Fundamental Right.
8. Citizen-specific rights arise after citizenship is established.
9. Parliamentary law remains subject to constitutional review.
10. A disputed Section 9 question follows the prescribed determination process.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** "Part II is a constitutional starting point, not a
complete citizenship code." Explain with reference to Articles 9–11.

**Model answer (137 words):**

Part II identifies the citizens of India at commencement but deliberately leaves future
citizenship to legislation. Article 9 prevents a person who voluntarily acquired foreign
citizenship from claiming citizenship through Articles 5, 6 or 8. It should not be
expanded into the entire later law: post-commencement voluntary acquisition is governed
principally by Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, 1955. Article 10 continues the status of
Part II citizens, expressly subject to parliamentary law. Article 11 then preserves
Parliament's power over acquisition, termination and all other citizenship matters.
This architecture combines constitutional certainty in 1950 with flexibility for
birth, descent, migration, territorial incorporation and diaspora policy. Citizenship
is consequently a constitutional-statutorial status rather than a Fundamental Right.
However, Parliament's breadth is not constitutional immunity; citizenship legislation
remains reviewable against Article 14, procedural fairness and other applicable
constitutional limits.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** starting-point thesis 1; Article 9 precision 2;
Articles 10 and 11 2; Section 9 distinction 1.5; flexibility rationale 1.5;
constitutional-review qualification 1; conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** A person qualified under Article 5 in 1950. Another Indian citizen voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship in 1995. Which provisions explain the two situations and why should Article 9 not be used alone for both?

**Model answer:** Article 5 establishes the first person's commencement citizenship, continued under Article 10 subject to law. Article 9 could defeat a listed commencement claim where foreign citizenship had been voluntarily acquired. The 1995 event is a post-commencement statutory matter governed principally by Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, including its prescribed determination mechanism.

**Misconception to avoid:** Article 9 is not a free-standing, exhaustive code for every acquisition of foreign citizenship after 1950.

The constitutional handover is complete. The next two lessons examine how the 1955 Act
actually confers citizenship.

*The first panel defines the statutory migration gateway; the next two show how
Section 3 links citizenship to Indian territory and Section 4 links it to a citizen
parent, with increasing documentary control.*

The definition has two alternative limbs, but each limb is internally conjunctive. The
first concerns entry without the required travel document **and** other prescribed
document or authority; the second concerns lawful documented entry followed by
overstay. "Illegal migrant" is therefore a defined statutory category, not a loose label
for every foreigner or every person whose citizenship is disputed.

### 1. Citizenship by birth — Section 3

| Birth period in India | Rule |
|---|---|
| 26 January 1950 to before 1 July 1987 | Citizen by birth irrespective of parents' nationality, subject to Section 3(2) |
| 1 July 1987 to before 3 December 2004 | Either parent must be an Indian citizen at birth |
| On or after 3 December 2004 | Both parents citizens, **or** one citizen and the other not an illegal migrant |

The chronology shows a movement from a broad territorial rule toward a qualified
parentage-and-migration rule.

### 2. Two statutory exclusions from citizenship by birth

A person is not a citizen by birth under Section 3 where, at birth:

1. either parent has diplomatic immunity as an accredited envoy and is not an Indian
   citizen; or
2. either parent is an enemy alien and the birth occurs in a place under enemy
   occupation.

Do not replace these precise conditions with "all children of foreigners are excluded".

### 3. Citizenship by descent — Section 4

For a person born outside India:

| Birth period | Parent link |
|---|---|
| 26 January 1950 to before 10 December 1992 | Father was an Indian citizen at birth |
| On or after 10 December 1992 | Either parent was an Indian citizen at birth |

The 1992 change removed the father-only formulation.

### 4. Registration controls for descent

The descent route contains qualifications, especially where the citizen parent was a
citizen by descent. Under the earlier provisos, where the relevant citizen parent was a
citizen by descent only, the child's citizenship generally required consular
registration within one year or the parent's service under a Government in India.

For births on or after 3 December 2004:

- the birth must be registered at an Indian consulate within **one year**; or
- registration may occur later with Central Government permission; and
- the parents must declare that the minor does not hold another country's passport.

Further, a minor who is an Indian citizen by descent and also a citizen of another
country ceases to be an Indian citizen if the other citizenship or nationality is not
renounced within **six months of attaining full age**.

### 5. *Jus soli* and *jus sanguinis*

- **Jus soli** means a citizenship connection through place of birth.
- **Jus sanguinis** means a citizenship connection through descent or parentage.

India does not fit a pure label. Section 3 retains birth in India but progressively adds
parental and migration controls; Section 4 separately recognises descent abroad.

### 6. Worked scenarios

**Scenario A:** Born in India in 1985 to two foreign parents who were not diplomats or
enemy aliens. The first period applies; parentage did not control.

**Scenario B:** Born in India in 1995. One parent was an Indian citizen. The second
period applies; the citizenship of either parent is sufficient.

**Scenario C:** Born in India in 2010. One parent was Indian and the other an illegal
migrant. Section 3's post-2004 requirement is not met.

**Scenario D:** Born abroad in 2008 to an Indian citizen parent. Parentage alone is not
the whole answer; consular-registration requirements must also be checked.

### 7. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** The tightening of citizenship by birth burdens children for their
parents' migration status.

**Reply:** Parliament sought to connect birthright citizenship with lawful and durable
membership in the context of irregular migration.

**Residual:** The policy trade-off remains between prevention of irregular membership
and the risk of exclusion or statelessness. UPSC answers should identify the trade-off
without inventing a constitutional outcome not supplied by a judgment.

> **Answer line:** Indian birth citizenship evolved from broad territorial inclusion to
> a qualified model in which the applicable date determines the required parental link.

### UPSC application

- Draw the three-period birth timeline.
- Remember the separate 10 December 1992 descent transition.
- Do not say every person born in India is a citizen.
- Do not omit consular registration for post-2004 births abroad.

### Revision notes

1. Section 3 governs birth in India.
2. 1950–before July 1987: broad territorial rule.
3. July 1987–before December 2004: either parent citizen.
4. Post-3 December 2004: both parents, or one citizen plus one non-illegal migrant.
5. Diplomatic-immunity and enemy-occupation exclusions are precise.
6. Section 4 governs birth outside India.
7. Before 10 December 1992, the father link applied.
8. From 10 December 1992, either parent may supply the link.
9. Post-2004 descent normally requires timely consular registration.
10. Dual-status minors by descent face a six-month post-majority renunciation rule.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Trace the movement from broad birthright
citizenship to qualified citizenship by birth in India.

**Model answer (147 words):**

Section 3 of the Citizenship Act reveals a deliberate tightening of India's territorial
birth rule. A person born in India from 26 January 1950 to before 1 July 1987 was a
citizen irrespective of parental nationality, subject to the diplomatic and
enemy-occupation exclusions. From 1 July 1987 to before 3 December 2004, either parent
had to be an Indian citizen. For births on or after 3 December 2004, both parents must
be citizens, or one must be a citizen while the other is not an illegal migrant. The
shift therefore moves from predominantly *jus soli* toward a mixed territorial and
parentage model. Its policy rationale is to connect membership with a durable lawful
link amid concerns over irregular migration. Its cost is that a child's status may
depend on parental classification, creating exclusion risks. India consequently retains
birth citizenship but no longer recognises an unconditional birthplace rule.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** three statutory periods 4; exclusions 1; conceptual
shift 1.5; policy rationale 1; critical cost 1.5; precise conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** F was born in India in June 1987 to non-citizen parents; G was born in India in August 1987 with one Indian parent; H was born in India in 2005 with one Indian parent and one illegal-migrant parent. Apply Section 3.

**Model answer:** F falls in the first period and qualifies by birth, assuming neither statutory exclusion applies. G falls in the second period and qualifies because either parent was a citizen. H falls in the post-3 December 2004 period and does not qualify through Section 3 because the non-citizen parent was an illegal migrant.

**Misconception to avoid:** The year of birth is not background information; it selects the legal rule and changes the parental requirement.

Birth and descent operate automatically when statutory conditions are met. Registration
and naturalisation instead require an application and governmental decision.

### 1. Registration under Section 5

The Central Government may register a person who is not an illegal migrant and belongs
to a listed category:

1. person of Indian origin ordinarily resident in India for seven years;
2. person of Indian origin ordinarily resident outside undivided India;
3. person married to an Indian citizen and ordinarily resident in India for seven years;
4. minor child of Indian citizens;
5. adult whose parents are registered citizens under the specified route;
6. adult who, or either parent, was earlier a citizen of independent India and who is
   ordinarily resident in India for twelve months before application;
7. adult OCI cardholder for five years who is ordinarily resident in India for twelve
   months before application.

#### Do not merge four different Indian-origin tests

The phrase **person of Indian origin** does not carry one ancestry test across the
Constitution and the Act:

| Provision | Whose birth supplies the link? | Geographic and functional boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Section 5(1), Explanation 2 | The person **or either parent** | Born in undivided India **or** territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947; used only for Section 5 registration |
| Article 6 | Person, either parent or any grandparent | Born in India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935; additionally requires migration from Pakistan and the Article 6 date/residence or registration test |
| Article 8 | Person, either parent or any grandparent | Same 1935-Act birth link, plus ordinary residence outside India and diplomatic/consular registration |
| Section 6A(1)(d) | Person, either parent or any grandparent | Born in undivided India; operates only inside the Assam Accord scheme |

Section 5 does **not** import the grandparent test from Articles 6/8 or Section 6A, and
the constitutional migration or overseas-residence conditions do not migrate into
Section 5. Similar labels therefore require provision-specific reading.

For the seven-year routes in clauses (a) and (c), ordinary residence means:

```text
12 months immediately before application
                 +
at least 6 years in the preceding 8 years
                 =
the statutory seven-year residence structure
```

An oath of allegiance applies to the relevant adult registration routes.

### 2. Naturalisation under Section 6 and the Third Schedule

The ordinary naturalisation route is available to a person who is not an illegal
migrant and satisfies the Third Schedule. The central qualifications include:

- reciprocity: the applicant is not a citizen of a country that prevents Indians from
  naturalising there;
- where the applicant is already a citizen of another country, the statutory
  renunciation undertaking/foreign-citizenship requirement must be satisfied;
- residence or government service throughout the **twelve months immediately before**
  application, subject to the limited statutory relaxation;
- aggregate residence or government service for **eleven years in the preceding
  fourteen years**;
- good character;
- adequate knowledge of an Eighth Schedule language;
- intention to reside in India or continue the specified Indian/international service.

The Central Government may waive qualifications for a person who rendered distinguished
service to science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace or human progress.

Section 6(2) supplies the final legal step. A person to whom the Central Government has
granted a naturalisation certificate under Section 6(1) becomes a citizen by
naturalisation **on taking the oath of allegiance in the Second Schedule**, with
citizenship taking effect from the certificate's date. The oath requires true faith and
allegiance to the Constitution, faithful observance of India's laws and fulfilment of
the duties of a citizen. A granted certificate and the prescribed oath must therefore
be stated together.

### 3. Registration versus naturalisation

| Registration | Naturalisation |
|---|---|
| Requires a listed connection to India | General qualification route |
| Categories include PIO, spouse, minor, former citizen or OCI cardholder | Focuses on residence, character, language and intended connection |
| Section 5 | Section 6 + Third Schedule |
| Not an automatic entitlement merely because a category is named | Certificate follows governmental decision |
| Full-age registration ordinarily requires the Section 5(2) oath | Section 6(2) requires the Second Schedule oath after certificate grant |

Marriage to a citizen does not automatically create citizenship. It places the person
within a registration category, subject to application and statutory residence.

### 4. Incorporation of territory — Section 7

If territory becomes part of India, the Central Government may specify by order which
persons connected with that territory become citizens and from what date.

✅ The Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962 is the standard illustration.

This is different from birth or naturalisation: the legal trigger is territorial
incorporation and a governmental specification.

### 5. Why discretion and conditions coexist

Citizenship affects political membership, so an application route cannot be reduced to
a residence stopwatch. Character, allegiance, identity and legal eligibility matter.
At the same time, discretion should remain connected to statutory categories and
constitutional equality.

**Objection:** Governmental discretion may create opacity.

**Reply:** Security and allegiance require scrutiny.

**Qualification:** Statutory discretion is not a licence for irrelevant or
constitutionally discriminatory decision-making.

> **Answer line:** Registration asks whether the applicant belongs to a listed
> India-linked category; naturalisation asks whether a general applicant satisfies the
> Third Schedule's residence, character, language and future-connection requirements.

### UPSC application

- Registration: learn the category logic, not merely "seven years".
- Naturalisation: write **12 months + 11 of preceding 14 years** for the ordinary rule.
- Do not apply the CAA five-year substitution to every naturalisation applicant.
- Incorporation of territory is a fifth acquisition mode, not a form of registration.

2. An illegal migrant is ordinarily excluded from Sections 5 and 6.
3. PIO and spouse routes use a structured seven-year residence test.
4. Marriage does not automatically confer citizenship.
5. OCI cardholders have a five-year status plus twelve-month residence route.
6. Section 6 governs naturalisation.
7. The ordinary aggregate is eleven of the preceding fourteen years.
8. The immediately preceding period is twelve months.
9. Good character and Eighth Schedule language knowledge matter.
10. Distinguished service may justify waiver.
11. Section 7 covers incorporation of territory.
12. Application routes remain subject to statutory scrutiny.

    grandparent-based tests in Articles 6/8 and Section 6A.

    certificate.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Compare citizenship by registration and
naturalisation under the Citizenship Act, 1955.

**Model answer (147 words):**

Registration and naturalisation are application routes resting on different legal
connections. Section 5 is confined to listed India-linked categories, including
specified persons of Indian origin, spouses, minors, former citizens and eligible OCI
cardholders. Its PIO definition counts the applicant or either parent born in the
specified territory; it does not import the distinct grandparent tests in Articles 6/8
or Section 6A. The principal seven-year routes combine twelve months immediately before
application with six years in the preceding eight, and a full-age registrant ordinarily
takes the Section 5(2) oath. Section 6 is a general integration route for a non-illegal
migrant satisfying the Third Schedule: immediate residence, the eleven-of-fourteen-year
aggregate, character, language and future connection. Distinguished service may justify
waiver. After certificate grant, Section 6(2) requires the Second Schedule oath; the
person then becomes a naturalised citizen from the certificate date. Neither category,
marriage nor residence creates automatic citizenship.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** organising distinction 2; registration categories
2; residence calculation 1.5; naturalisation qualifications 2.5; non-automatic caution
1; comparative conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** J is married to an Indian citizen and has lived in India for three years. K has no Indian family link but satisfies twelve months' immediate residence and eleven years in the preceding fourteen, has good character and knows an Eighth Schedule language. Which routes are relevant?

**Model answer:** J belongs conceptually to the spouse registration category, but three years does not satisfy the seven-year ordinary-residence structure. K's facts point to naturalisation under Section 6 and the Third Schedule, subject to every remaining qualification, certificate grant and the Section 6(2) Second Schedule oath.

**Misconception to avoid:** A listed relationship such as marriage opens an application category; it does not itself confer citizenship.

*The mnemonic "R–T–D" is useful only after the mechanism is understood: declaration,
foreign acquisition and reasoned governmental order are different legal events.*

### 1. Renunciation — Section 8

An Indian citizen of full age and capacity may make a prescribed declaration
renouncing citizenship. Citizenship ends when the declaration is registered.

If India is engaged in war, registration of the declaration shall be withheld until the
Central Government otherwise directs.

When a person renounces citizenship, every minor child of that person also ceases to be
an Indian citizen. The child may, **within one year after attaining full age**, make the
prescribed declaration to resume Indian citizenship.

### 2. Termination — Section 9

A citizen who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country by
naturalisation, registration or otherwise ceases to be an Indian citizen.

The war proviso delays this consequence until the Central Government otherwise directs
where foreign citizenship is voluntarily acquired during a war in which India is
engaged.

If a question arises about **whether, when or how** foreign citizenship was acquired,
the prescribed authority determines it under the prescribed procedure and rules of
evidence.

This is the post-commencement rule that must be distinguished from Article 9.

### 3. Deprivation — Section 10 is category-limited

Deprivation is not a general power to cancel the citizenship of any person.

Section 10(1) identifies the categories exposed to deprivation, principally specified
citizens:

- by naturalisation;
- by virtue only of Article 5(c), the five-year-residence commencement route; or
- by registration, other than registration under Article 6(b)(ii) or Section 5(1)(a).

This exact category limitation matters: Section 10 is not drafted as a deprivation
power over every citizen by birth, descent or constitutional route.

The Central Government may act on one of five grounds:

1. fraud, false representation or concealment in registration/naturalisation;
2. disloyalty or disaffection towards the Constitution by act or speech;
3. unlawful trade or communication with the enemy during war;
4. imprisonment for at least two years within five years after registration or
   naturalisation;
5. continuous ordinary residence outside India for seven years without falling within
   the listed student, Indian-government/international-organisation service or annual
   consular-registration protections.

### 4. Procedure prevents a free-floating cancellation power

Even where a ground exists:

- the Central Government must be satisfied that continuation of citizenship is not
  conducive to the public good;
- written notice of the proposed ground is required;
- except for the seven-year-residence ground, the person may seek reference to a
  Committee of Inquiry;
- the committee has a chair with at least ten years' judicial office and two other
  members;
- the Central Government is ordinarily guided by its report.

```text
eligible category
      ↓
listed statutory ground
      ↓
public-good satisfaction
      ↓
notice and opportunity
      ↓
committee route where applicable
      ↓
reasoned deprivation order
```

### 5. Comparison

| Feature | Renunciation | Termination | Deprivation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Section 8 | Section 9 | Section 10 |
| Trigger | Citizen's declaration | Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship | Governmental order |
| Category | Adult citizen of full age/capacity | Any citizen satisfying Section 9 | Only Section 10's specified categories |
| Procedure | Registration; war withholding | Determination if facts disputed | Grounds, public good, notice, possible inquiry |
| Minor child | Consequential loss with resumption route | Not the defining feature | Not a universal derivative rule |

### 6. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Terms such as "disaffection" or "public good" may appear broad.

**Reply:** Section 10 confines the affected categories and grounds and supplies notice
and inquiry protections.

**Qualification:** Because citizenship loss is severe, statutory language should be
read strictly and executive satisfaction remains open to constitutional review.

> **Answer line:** Deprivation is a category-limited, ground-bound and procedurally
> controlled power; it is not a general executive authority to cancel any citizen's
> membership.

### UPSC application

- **2021 Prelims GS-I, Q89** tests one citizenship/domicile and deprivation.
- Separate the voluntary act in renunciation from the separate voluntary acquisition
  that triggers termination.
- In deprivation, write category → ground → public good → procedure.
- In an objective question, test each statement separately against the applicable
  provision before combining the response code.

1. Renunciation is a registered declaration under Section 8.
2. War may postpone registration.
3. Minor children are affected but have a resumption route.
4. Resumption must be sought within one year after attaining full age.
5. Section 9 termination follows voluntary foreign-citizenship acquisition.
6. Disputed acquisition facts require prescribed determination.
7. Article 9 and Section 9 occupy different temporal/legal roles.
8. Section 10 deprivation applies only to specified categories.
9. It contains five grounds.
10. Seven-year foreign residence has statutory exceptions.
11. Public-good satisfaction is additionally required.
12. Notice and a Committee of Inquiry may protect the person.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Why is it inaccurate to describe deprivation under
Section 10 as a general power to cancel Indian citizenship?

**Model answer (145 words):**

Section 10 creates a narrow deprivation power, not an unrestricted executive authority.
First, it applies only to specified categories, principally citizens linked to
naturalisation, registration and stated commencement routes; it is not directed at
every citizen. Second, the Central Government must establish one of five grounds:
fraud, constitutional disloyalty, enemy assistance during war, a qualifying
post-registration or post-naturalisation imprisonment, or seven years' continuous
residence abroad without a protected connection. Third, a listed ground is insufficient
by itself: continuation must also be considered contrary to the public good. Finally,
the Act requires written notice and, for most grounds, permits a Committee of Inquiry
whose chair has judicial experience and whose report ordinarily guides the Government.
These category, ground and procedure controls distinguish deprivation from
renunciation and automatic termination. Given the gravity of citizenship loss, the
power also remains subject to strict statutory construction and constitutional review.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** category limitation 2; five-ground architecture 2;
public-good test 1.5; notice/inquiry safeguards 2; comparison with other modes 1;
constitutional qualification 1.5.

### Concept check

**Question:** L, a naturalised citizen, is alleged to have obtained citizenship by fraud. M, a citizen by birth, has lived abroad for eight years while annually registering an intention to retain citizenship at an Indian consulate. Can both simply be deprived by an executive announcement?

**Model answer:** No. L belongs to a category potentially covered by Section 10 and fraud is a listed ground, but deprivation still requires the public-good satisfaction, notice and applicable inquiry procedure. M cannot be treated through a bare seven-year-residence slogan: category limits and the annual-consular-registration protection must be examined. Neither case permits deprivation by an unsupported announcement.

**Misconception to avoid:** A listed ground does not erase Section 10's category and procedural safeguards.

citizenship operates inside a federal and regionally diverse country.

### 1. What single citizenship means

India has a federal distribution of power but no separate citizenship of each State. An
Indian does not become a citizen of Karnataka, Assam or Tamil Nadu in addition to being
an Indian citizen.

The model differs from federations such as the United States, where federal and State
citizenship are constitutionally recognised.

India's design aims to support:

- fraternity and national integration;
- interstate mobility;
- equal political membership;
- avoidance of State-created political outsiders.

### 2. Comparative control: Canada, the United States and Switzerland

| Federation | Citizenship structure | Qualification against oversimplification |
|---|---|---|
| Canada | One Canadian citizenship; provinces do not confer a separate provincial citizenship | Canada is a useful single-citizenship comparison, not proof that Indian and Canadian federal powers, institutions or rights are identical |
| United States | The Fourteenth Amendment recognises citizenship of the United States and of the State of residence | State citizenship is nested within one federation, not a separate international nationality; federal constitutional guarantees limit discriminatory State treatment |
| Switzerland | Citizenship is connected at commune, canton and Confederation levels | "Dual citizenship" is an exam shorthand that understates Switzerland's three-level, locally rooted structure |

India resembles Canada only on the narrow point that citizenship is national rather
than State-conferred. The United States and Switzerland illustrate subnational
citizenship links, but in different constitutional forms. Standard Indian polity
comparison therefore groups the USA and Switzerland as **dual-citizenship
federations**; the label is shorthand, because the US model is federal-plus-State while
the Swiss model is commune–canton–Confederation. The comparison tests the level at which
legal membership is constituted; it does not rank the federations or imply that every
subnational preference is valid.

> **Comparative answer line:** Single versus dual citizenship is a membership
> classification, not a complete description of a federation's distribution of power
> or equality guarantees.

### 3. One citizenship and one domicile

✅ *Pradeep Jain v. Union of India* (1984) stressed one citizenship and one domicile in
the territory of India. Administrative usage of "State domicile" generally describes a
residence or local-status rule; it does not create State citizenship or a separate
sovereign domicile.

This is why the following sentence is useful:

> **Citizenship is membership, domicile is permanent legal home, and residence is a fact
> of living in a place.**

### 4. Article 15: place of birth is not residence

Article 15(1) prohibits State discrimination against a citizen on grounds only of
religion, race, caste, sex or **place of birth**. It does not list residence.

That textual difference explains why some residence-based concessions, such as a
bounded educational fee preference, are not automatically prohibited by Article 15.
They remain subject to Article 14 and other applicable provisions.

### 5. Article 16(3): only Parliament receives the residence power

Article 16(2) includes residence among prohibited discrimination grounds in public
employment. Article 16(3), however, authorises **Parliament** to prescribe residence
requirements for specified classes of employment or appointment under a State, Union
territory, local authority or other authority.

⚠️ A State legislature cannot invoke Article 16(3) as though it independently received
that power.

Parliament used this power in the Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act,
1957 for specified areas. Those temporary authorisations expired in 1974. The surviving
Andhra Pradesh/Telangana local-opportunity framework rests on the distinct constitutional
route in Article 371D and its implementing orders, not on a general power of every State
to impose residence conditions.

### 6. Article 19(5): mobility has protective limits

Citizens possess movement and residence freedoms under Articles 19(1)(d) and 19(1)(e).
Article 19(5) permits reasonable restrictions:

- in the interests of the general public; or
- for the protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.

The provision demonstrates that equal citizenship can coexist with protection of
vulnerable communities and tribal land/culture.

### 7. Region-specific constitutional arrangements

Only the citizenship-facing edge belongs here:

- Articles 371A and 371G protect specified customary-law and land matters through State
  Assembly consent mechanisms.
- Article 371D supports local cadres/opportunity in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.
- These devices do not create Nagaland, Mizoram, Andhra Pradesh or Telangana
  citizenship.

Detailed Article 371 design belongs to the Special Provisions topic.

### 8. Article 35A is historical, not current

Until 2019, Article 35A, inserted through the 1954 Presidential Order under Article 370,
supported J&K permanent-resident privileges concerning employment, property,
settlement and aid. It ceased to operate with the 2019 constitutional reconfiguration.
*In re Article 370* (11 December 2023) upheld the constitutional result.

Do not use former Article 35A as a current exception.

### 9. Does differentiation weaken single citizenship?

**Criticism:** Residence preferences can make interstate migrants second-class members.

**Reply:** Bounded protective differentiation may make national membership credible in
regions facing cultural, tribal or opportunity vulnerabilities.

**Test:** Ask whether the distinction has constitutional or statutory authority, a
legitimate purpose, proportionate design and no claim to separate political membership.

> **Answer line:** India's Union is held together not by uniformity at every level but
> by one political citizenship combined with constitutionally bounded regional
> protection.

### Revision notes

1. India has one national citizenship and no State citizenship.
2. Single citizenship supports fraternity and interstate mobility.
3. *Pradeep Jain* emphasised one Indian domicile.
4. A State domicile certificate usually records a local residence status.
5. Article 15 prohibits place-of-birth discrimination, not residence by name.
6. Article 16(3) gives the residence-prescription power to Parliament.
7. Article 19(5) permits ST-protection restrictions.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Does the existence of residence-based and regional
protections contradict India's single citizenship?

**Model answer (146 words):**

Single citizenship means that Indians possess one national political membership and no
separate State citizenship. It does not require identical treatment for every regional
purpose. Article 16(3) allows Parliament to prescribe residence requirements for
specified public employment; Article 19(5) permits reasonable movement and residence
restrictions to protect Scheduled Tribes; and the Article 371 family contains tailored
customary-law, land or local-opportunity safeguards. These provisions protect vulnerable
regions while keeping their beneficiaries Indian citizens. However, residence
preferences can contradict equal citizenship when they lack legal authority, become
hereditary exclusion, or disproportionately burden interstate migrants. *Pradeep Jain*
therefore remains important: India has one citizenship and one constitutional domicile,
although reasonable residence classifications may survive. The proper conclusion is
conditional. Regional differentiation strengthens the Union when it enables fair
participation within common citizenship; it weakens the Union when local status is used
as a substitute for a separate political membership.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** meaning of single citizenship 1.5; three
constitutional safeguards 3; *Pradeep Jain* 1; conflict test 2; balanced evaluation 1.5;
conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State legislature reserves every State government post permanently for persons holding its own "domicile certificate", claiming Article 16(3). What citizenship and equality questions arise?

**Model answer:** The certificate does not create State citizenship. Article 16(3) assigns to Parliament, not a State legislature acting alone, the power to prescribe residence for specified public employment. The blanket and permanent design must also face Articles 14 and 16, including proportionality and the distinction between legitimate local protection and exclusion of equal Indian citizens.

**Misconception to avoid:** Single citizenship does not invalidate every residence rule, but it also does not permit a State to invent a separate citizenship or appropriate Parliament's Article 16(3) power.

Single citizenship deals with internal membership. OCI addresses a different problem:
how India can maintain deep diaspora connections without conferring a second
citizenship.

### 1. NRI, PIO and OCI are not the same

| Status | Citizenship position |
|---|---|
| NRI | An Indian citizen ordinarily residing outside India |
| PIO | Historical origin-based administrative/card category; the PIO card scheme was merged into OCI |
| OCI cardholder | A foreign national registered under Section 7A; not an Indian citizen |

The word "Overseas Citizen" is misleading if read literally. The legal provisions and
official 2021 notification expressly treat an OCI cardholder as a foreign national
holding a foreign passport and not an Indian citizen.

The original PIO Card Scheme dates to **1999**. A revised scheme was notified on
**19 August 2002** and took effect on **15 September 2002**. The OCI card scheme became
operational on 2 December 2005. Running the revised PIO and OCI schemes in parallel
caused duplication and confusion, which the 2015 merger sought to remove.

### 2. Eligibility under Section 7A

Subject to statutory conditions, OCI registration may cover:

- a foreign citizen who was an Indian citizen at or after commencement;
- a foreign citizen eligible to become an Indian citizen at commencement;
- a foreign citizen connected to territory that became part of India after
  15 August 1947;
- a child, grandchild or great-grandchild of such a person;
- specified minor children;
- a foreign-origin spouse of an Indian citizen or OCI cardholder where the registered
  marriage subsisted continuously for at least two years, with security clearance.

A person is ineligible where that person, a parent, grandparent or great-grandparent is
or was a citizen of Pakistan, Bangladesh or another country notified by the Central
Government.

### 3. Rights have two layers

**Statutory denials in Section 7B(2):**

- no Article 16 equality of opportunity in public employment;
- no eligibility for President or Vice-President;
- no appointment as a Supreme Court or High Court judge;
- no voter registration;
- no membership of Parliament or a State legislature;
- no general entitlement to public services/posts, except as specially specified.

**Rights specified by the 4 March 2021 notification under Section 7B(1):**

- multiple-entry lifelong visa, subject to special permission for listed activities;
- exemption from FRRO/FRO registration for any length of stay, with address/occupation
  intimation for normally resident cardholders;
- parity with Indian nationals for domestic airfares and entry fees at specified parks,
  sanctuaries, monuments, historical sites and museums;
- parity with NRIs for inter-country adoption, specified entrance-test access against
  NRI/supernumerary seats, purchase/sale of non-agricultural immovable property and
  listed professions;
- for unspecified economic, financial and educational fields, the same position as a
  foreigner unless another notification or FEMA rule provides otherwise.

Special permission is required for research, missionary/Tabligh, mountaineering,
journalistic activities, specified diplomatic internships/employment and protected or
restricted areas.

### 4. Why OCI is not dual citizenship

OCI holders:

- hold a foreign passport;
- lack Indian political rights;
- cannot hold the listed constitutional offices;
- receive only rights conferred by statute and notification;
- remain subject to registration cancellation.

The no-dual-citizenship conclusion rests on the constitutional commencement rule and,
for later voluntary foreign citizenship, especially Section 9 of the Citizenship Act.
OCI does not displace either.

### 5. Renunciation and cancellation

An OCI cardholder may renounce the card under Section 7C.

Section 7D permits cancellation on listed grounds including fraud, disaffection towards
the Constitution, enemy assistance during war, qualifying imprisonment, notified legal
violations, sovereignty/security/public-interest grounds and specified breakdown or
abuse of a spouse-based registration. A reasonable opportunity of being heard is
required.

This is cancellation of OCI registration, not deprivation of Indian citizenship,
because the cardholder is not an Indian citizen.

### 6. Policy debate

**Criticism:** OCI may appear to create a second-class or incomplete membership.

**Reply:** A person who remains a foreign national cannot simultaneously exercise
India's democratic authorship merely because of ancestry or mobility privileges.

**Balanced view:** OCI is deliberately calibrated: stronger than an ordinary visa,
weaker than citizenship. Its value lies in diaspora connection without divided
political allegiance.

> **Answer line:** OCI is notified statutory access for a foreign national, not a
> constitutional citizenship status; its economic and mobility benefits stop at the
> boundary of political membership.

### UPSC application

- Do not equate NRI with OCI.
- Do not say OCI rights are constitutionally guaranteed.
- Separate Section 7B statutory exclusions from notification-based benefits.
- Mention the 2015 PIO–OCI merger.
- Use "foreign national holding a foreign passport" for precision.

### Revision notes

1. Singhvi Committee was established in 2000 and reported in 2002.
2. The original PIO Card Scheme began in 1999.
3. The revised PIO scheme was notified on 19 August 2002 and effective
   15 September 2002.
4. The 2003 amendment created the OCI framework.
5. The 2015 amendment merged the PIO card scheme into OCI.
6. NRI remains an Indian citizen; OCI does not.
7. Section 7A contains eligibility and ancestry rules.
8. Pakistan/Bangladesh ancestry exclusions apply as stated.
9. Section 7B(2) withholds political and specified public-office rights.
10. Section 7B(1) benefits depend on notification.
11. The 2021 notification regulates present principal parity.
12. Agricultural, farmhouse and plantation property are excluded from the notified parity.
13. Some activities require special permission.
14. OCI registration may be renounced or cancelled with statutory safeguards.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain why OCI is a form of calibrated diaspora
engagement rather than dual citizenship.

**Model answer (145 words):**

OCI is a statutory registration of a foreign national under Sections 7A–7D, not Indian
citizenship. Its design encourages diaspora mobility and connection through a
multiple-entry lifelong visa, exemption from ordinary foreigner registration and
notified parity in specified fees, adoption, education, non-agricultural property and
professional fields. Yet Section 7B withholds the vote, legislative membership,
Presidency, Vice-Presidency, constitutional judgeship, Article 16 public-employment
equality and a general claim to public service. The 2021 notification further confirms
that an OCI cardholder holds a foreign passport and remains a foreign national.
Benefits are therefore notification-dependent rather than constitutionally entrenched,
and registration may be renounced or cancelled on listed grounds after an opportunity
of hearing. The arrangement is calibrated because it separates cultural and economic
connection from democratic authorship. It is deeper than an ordinary visa, but
deliberately below citizenship and does not override Section 9's rule on voluntary
foreign citizenship.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** legal status/sections 2; benefits 2; political
denials 2; notification/cancellation limits 1.5; policy logic 1.5; conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** An OCI cardholder claims a constitutional right to vote, an unrestricted right to buy agricultural land and immunity from future changes to OCI benefits. Assess the claims.

**Model answer:** All three claims fail. Section 7B expressly withholds voter registration; the 2021 notification excludes agricultural land, farmhouses and plantation property from the notified property parity; and OCI benefits arise through statute and notification rather than an immutable constitutional status. Applicable law and hearing safeguards still control any adverse action.

**Misconception to avoid:** Lifelong visa language does not convert every OCI benefit into a permanent constitutional right.

OCI concerns the diaspora. Section 6A addresses a territorially and historically bounded
settlement within Assam and must not be converted into a general migration rule.

## Lesson 10 — Assam Accord and Section 6A: a time-banded settlement

Progress: 10/12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Section 6A cohorts, electoral consequence and constitutional validity

                              after expiry of ten years

*The operative boundary is Assam-specific and date-specific. "Before 25 March 1971"
means the last included entry date is 24 March 1971.*

### 1. Origin and definitions

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1985 inserted Section 6A to implement the citizenship
dimension of the Assam Accord.

For Section 6A:

- "Assam" uses the statutory territorial definition linked to the 1985 amendment;
- "specified territory" means territory included in Bangladesh immediately before the
  amendment;
- Indian origin exists where the person, either parent or any grandparent was born in
  undivided India;
- Section 6A's text ties "detected to be a foreigner" to the Foreigners Act, 1946 and
  the Foreigners (Tribunals) Order, 1964.

The Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 came into force on 1 September 2025 and
repealed the Foreigners Act, 1946 along with three other immigration enactments.
Section 36 saves prior rules, orders, directions and proceedings, insofar as consistent,
by deeming them under corresponding provisions of the 2025 Act. The statutory
Section 6A cohorts and cut-off were not rewritten by that immigration consolidation.

### 2. Cohort one: before 1 January 1966

Persons of Indian origin who came to Assam from the specified territory before
1 January 1966 and were ordinarily resident in Assam since entry are deemed citizens
from **1 January 1966**.

This deeming date should not be replaced by the date of entry.

### 3. Cohort two: 1 January 1966 to 24 March 1971

The person must:

1. be of Indian origin;
2. have entered Assam on or after 1 January 1966 but before 25 March 1971;
3. have been ordinarily resident in Assam since entry;
4. have been detected to be a foreigner; and
5. register under the statutory rules.

If the person's name appears on an electoral roll at detection, it is deleted.

For ten years from detection, the registered person has citizen-like rights and
obligations, including the passport-related statutory position, but cannot be included
in an Assembly or parliamentary electoral roll. After ten years, the person is deemed a
citizen for all purposes.

Section 6A also contained time-bound declaration routes for a qualifying person who did
not wish to become a citizen or be governed by the middle-cohort mechanism. Its cohort
benefits do not apply to a person who was already an Indian citizen immediately before
the 1985 amendment or who had been expelled before its commencement. These savings
prevent the provision from displacing an existing citizenship or undoing a completed
expulsion.

### 4. The cut-off distinction

```text
ASSAM SECTION 6A / NRC REFERENCE DATE → 24 March 1971
CAA ENTRY CUT-OFF                     → 31 December 2014
```

The dates belong to different statutory schemes and must never be swapped.

This lesson does not teach the entire NRC procedure, Foreigners Tribunal system or
border-management policy. Those are separate specialist fields.

### 5. Supreme Court judgment of 17 October 2024

In *In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, 1955*, the Supreme Court upheld Section 6A
by a **4:1 majority**.

The majority's controlling conclusions included:

- Assam and the 24 March 1971 cut-off form a reasonable classification connected to the
  Accord's humanitarian, economic and cultural balance;
- Section 6A does not violate Article 29(1), because the petitioners did not establish
  that Assamese citizens were prevented from taking steps to conserve their language,
  script or culture;
- Article 355 is not a standard for invalidating Section 6A;
- the provision was not invalid merely because implementation had been deficient.

The sole dissent would have declared Section 6A invalid prospectively, while protecting
important completed or pending categories. A complete answer should acknowledge the
dissent without presenting it as law.

### 6. Validity and implementation are different questions

The majority sharply distinguished:

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL VALIDITY OF SECTION 6A
                  ≠
ADEQUACY OF DETECTION, REGISTRATION AND DEPORTATION IMPLEMENTATION
```

On 6 February 2025, the Court sought status reports to monitor compliance with the
majority's implementation directions. The provision's validity therefore survives,
while administrative performance remains reviewable.

### 7. Main constitutional arguments

**Challenge:**

- Assam-specific treatment allegedly violated Article 14.
- Migration allegedly threatened culture protected by Article 29(1).
- Indefinite or delayed detection allegedly made the mechanism arbitrary.

**Majority response:**

- Assam's migration impact and Accord history supplied an intelligible, purpose-linked
  classification.
- Article 29(1) protects citizens' capacity to conserve culture; demographic change was
  not by itself proof that Section 6A destroyed that right.
- implementation failure did not automatically make the statutory settlement invalid.

**Balanced assessment:** Section 6A constitutionalises neither a general open border nor
a separate Assam citizenship. It produces Indian citizenship through a historically
bounded asymmetrical settlement.

> **Answer line:** Section 6A is an Assam-specific temporal compromise: the Supreme
> Court upheld its constitutional design while keeping its implementation under
> scrutiny.

### UPSC application

- Write the exact two cohorts and ten-year electoral consequence.
- Use "before 25 March 1971", not "up to 25 March" without explanation.
- State the 4:1 outcome and separate majority from dissent.
- Do not import the CAA 2014 cut-off.
- Keep NRC procedure outside unless the question expressly demands it.

### Revision notes

1. Section 6A was inserted in 1985 after the Assam Accord.
2. It is territorially confined to Assam as statutorily defined.
3. Specified territory refers to Bangladesh.
4. Pre-1 January 1966 entrants satisfying conditions are deemed citizens from that date.
5. The middle cohort entered from 1 January 1966 to 24 March 1971.
6. Detection as a foreigner and registration matter for the middle cohort.
7. The ten-year interval excludes electoral-roll inclusion.
8. Full deemed citizenship follows expiry of ten years from detection.
9. The Section 6A/NRC reference date is not the CAA cut-off.
10. The Supreme Court upheld Section 6A by 4:1 on 17 October 2024.
11. The dissent favoured prospective invalidity.
12. Implementation monitoring does not reverse the validity holding.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain the constitutional logic of Section 6A and
the significance of the Supreme Court's 2024 judgment.

**Model answer (148 words):**

Section 6A implements the Assam Accord through a territorially and temporally bounded
citizenship settlement. Indian-origin entrants before 1 January 1966 who remained
ordinarily resident in Assam are deemed citizens from that date. Those entering from
1 January 1966 to 24 March 1971 must be detected and registered; for ten years they
carry citizen-like rights and obligations but remain outside electoral rolls, after
which they become citizens for all purposes. In *In Re: Section 6A* (17 October 2024),
the Supreme Court upheld the provision 4:1. The majority found the Assam classification
and 1971 cut-off reasonably connected to the Accord's humanitarian, cultural and
resource balance and rejected the Article 29(1) challenge. It also separated statutory
validity from deficient implementation, which remains monitored. The dissent preferred
prospective invalidity, but is not controlling law. Section 6A thus represents
asymmetric accommodation within Indian citizenship, not separate Assam citizenship or
a general migration rule.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** origin/purpose 1; both cohorts 3; electoral
consequence 1; majority reasoning 2; dissent/status 1; asymmetry analysis 1; conclusion
1.

### Concept check

**Question:** N entered Assam from the specified territory in 1964 and remained ordinarily resident. O entered in February 1970, was detected as a foreigner and registered. Compare their statutory positions.

**Model answer:** N belongs to the pre-1 January 1966 cohort and, if the Indian-origin and residence conditions are satisfied, is deemed a citizen from 1 January 1966. O belongs to the 1966–24 March 1971 cohort: registration follows detection, electoral-roll inclusion is barred for ten years from detection, and citizenship for all purposes is deemed after that period expires.

**Misconception to avoid:** The middle cohort does not receive immediate full electoral citizenship on registration, and the pre-1966 cohort's deeming date is not simply the date of entry.

Section 6A arose from the Assam Accord. Section 6B, inserted by the CAA 2019, uses a
different class, different countries, a different cut-off and different exclusions.

### 1. What the 2019 amendment changed

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 added a proviso to the definition of "illegal
migrant" in Section 2(1)(b). It covers a person:

- belonging to the Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi or Christian community;
- from Afghanistan, Bangladesh or Pakistan;
- who entered India on or before 31 December 2014; and
- who was exempted by the Central Government under the relevant Passport (Entry into
  India) Act or Foreigners Act framework.

Such a person is not treated as an illegal migrant for the purposes of the Citizenship
Act.

⚠️ The statutory class does not expressly require each applicant to prove individual
religious persecution as a separate element. Persecution is central to the Government's
policy justification, but UPSC answers should not insert an additional textual
qualification into Section 2 or Section 6B.

### 2. Section 6B: application, not automatic citizenship

Section 6B authorises the Central Government or specified authority, on application and
subject to prescribed conditions, to grant:

- a certificate of registration under the relevant Section 5 route; or
- a certificate of naturalisation under Section 6 and the Third Schedule.

Only after the certificate is granted does Section 6B(2) deem the person to be a citizen
from the date of entry into India.

Pending illegal-migration or citizenship proceedings abate **on conferment of
citizenship**, not merely on filing an application. A pending proceeding cannot by
itself disqualify an otherwise qualified applicant, and application does not strip the
person of existing rights and privileges.

### 3. The naturalisation relaxation

For the six-community, three-country class, the Third Schedule substitutes **five
years** for the ordinary requirement of eleven years' aggregate residence/service in
the preceding fourteen years.

The immediately preceding twelve-month requirement and other applicable qualifications
do not disappear merely because the aggregate is reduced.

> **UPSC trap:** "CAA requires five years" is incomplete. It changes the specified
> aggregate from eleven to five for the protected class; application, scrutiny and other
> applicable statutory requirements remain.

### 4. Territorial exclusions

Section 6B does not apply to:

- tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura included in the Sixth Schedule;
  and
- areas covered by the Inner Line under the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873.

These exclusions protect constitutionally or administratively safeguarded regions. They
do not convert Section 6B into Section 6A, and Section 6A's 1971 date remains separate.

### 5. The 2024 Rules

The Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024 operationalised the Section 6B application
process through electronic applications, documentary schedules, scrutiny, enquiry and
the oath of allegiance.

The Rules are machinery. They do not enlarge the statutory community, country or entry
date written into the Act.

### 6. Immigration-law currency: the 2024 and 2014 cut-offs do different work

The Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 came into force on 1 September 2025 and
repealed the Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920 and the Foreigners Act, 1946, while
saving consistent prior actions, rules and orders under corresponding provisions.

The Immigration and Foreigners (Exemption) Order, 2025 exempts the same six named
minority communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan from specified
passport/visa requirements where they entered India on or before **31 December 2024**
because of religious persecution or fear of it.

This immigration exemption does not expand Section 6B citizenship:

```text
IMMIGRATION EXEMPTION ORDER CUT-OFF → 31 December 2024
CAA CITIZENSHIP-ROUTE CUT-OFF       → 31 December 2014
```

A person entering after 31 December 2014 may fall within the immigration exemption but
does not thereby enter the CAA citizenship class. The 2025 regime concerns entry,
stay, exit and document liability; Section 6B continues to govern citizenship.

### 7. Exactly one current linkage — 19 August 2026 procedural redirection

✅ **Official fact:** MHA notified the Citizenship (Third Amendment) Rules, 2026 as
**G.S.R. 742(E)** on 19 August 2026. For Section 6B applicants ordinarily residing in
Gujarat, Rajasthan, Punjab, West Bengal, Assam except tribal areas, Tripura except
tribal areas, and the Union territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, electronic
applications are submitted to the jurisdictional **Collector**, who receives,
scrutinises and disposes of them, verifies documents, conducts necessary enquiry,
administers the oath and decides the application.

✅ **Official fact:** **S.O. 4583(E)** of the same date disapplied the 11 March 2024
order to those listed jurisdictions, rescinded specified February/March 2026 orders
subject to completed acts and transferred pending Section 6B applications from the
Empowered and District Level Committees to the concerned Collector.

**Decisive qualification:** These instruments changed the competent procedural channel.
They did **not** alter the six communities, three countries, 31 December 2014 cut-off,
Section 6B exclusions or the need for application and scrutiny.

No other current-affairs linkage is used in this learning session.

### 8. The Article 14 debate

Article 14 permits classification where:

1. an intelligible differentia distinguishes the included class; and
2. the differentia has a rational nexus with the law's objective.

**Challenge:**

- religion is used as an express classification;
- Muslims and other persecuted groups are excluded;
- only three countries are selected;
- the scheme is alleged to conflict with secular equality and to be under-inclusive.

**Defence:**

- Parliament selected six non-Muslim minority communities from three neighbouring
  States with an asserted history of religious persecution;
- the measure is a limited facilitation, not a general refugee code;
- it takes away no existing Indian citizen's citizenship;
- territorial exclusions protect vulnerable north-eastern regions.

**Qualification:** The defence is a constitutional argument, not a judicial holding on
the final merits.

### 9. Litigation status through 30 September 2026

Official Supreme Court orders dated 19 February and 20 March 2026 organised the
challenges for final hearing and extended the time for additional material. No final
judgment on the constitutional validity of the CAA was located in the official judgment
record checked through **30 September 2026**.

Therefore:

- do not say the CAA has been struck down;
- do not say it has been conclusively upheld;
- state that the constitutional challenge remains pending on the verified record used
  here.

> **Answer line:** The CAA creates a religion-, country- and date-defined facilitated
> application route; its operation is statutory, its grant is not automatic and its
> constitutional validity remains under Supreme Court consideration.

### UPSC application

- Write the six communities, three countries and exact cut-off.
- Distinguish statutory eligibility from actual grant.
- Mention excluded Sixth Schedule/Inner Line areas.
- Frame both Article 14 positions before a status-controlled conclusion.
- Treat the August 2026 change as procedural only.

2. The six communities are Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian.
3. The three countries are Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan.
4. Entry must be on or before 31 December 2014.
5. The 1920 and 1946 immigration Acts were repealed in 2025 with savings for consistent

6. The 2025 immigration exemption's 31 December 2024 date does not change the CAA's

7. The route requires an application and certificate.
8. Citizenship is deemed from entry only after grant.
9. Proceedings abate on conferment, not merely application.
10. The Third Schedule aggregate is reduced from eleven years to five for the class.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** The CAA 2019 is neither an automatic citizenship
grant nor a complete refugee law. Explain.

**Model answer (148 words):**

The CAA creates a facilitated statutory route for six named communities from
Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014
and satisfy the relevant exemption framework. It removes their treatment as illegal
migrants for Citizenship Act purposes and permits an application under Section 6B for
registration or naturalisation. Citizenship is not automatic: documents, eligibility,
enquiry, oath and a certificate remain necessary; pending migration proceedings abate
only on conferment. The naturalisation concession substitutes five years for the
ordinary eleven-year aggregate but does not erase all other qualifications. Sixth
Schedule tribal areas and Inner Line areas are excluded. Nor is the Act a general
refugee law: it selects communities, countries and a date rather than creating a
universal asylum framework. Its humanitarian justification is defended as reasonable
classification, while critics challenge religion-based under-inclusion under Article
14. That constitutional question remained pending through 30 September 2026.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** statutory class 2; application mechanism 2;
naturalisation precision 1; exclusions 1; refugee-law distinction 1.5; Article 14
balance 1.5; status-controlled conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** P belongs to one of the six communities, came from one of the three countries before the cut-off and files an electronic application in Punjab after 19 August 2026. Has citizenship already been acquired, and what did the 2026 instruments change?

**Model answer:** Eligibility facts and filing do not themselves confer citizenship. The application must be scrutinised and a registration or naturalisation certificate granted; only then does Section 6B deem citizenship from the date of entry. The 19 August 2026 instruments made the jurisdictional Collector the competent authority for specified applicants in Punjab and transferred pending files; they did not change the community, country, cut-off or excluded-area rules.

**Misconception to avoid:** A procedural change in the receiving authority is not an amendment of substantive CAA eligibility and an application is not a citizenship certificate.

### 🖼️ Visual: document, proposition and decision-maker

| Document/status | What it ordinarily establishes | What it does **not** automatically establish |
|---|---|---|
| Aadhaar | Identity/residence-linked enrolment under the Aadhaar framework | Citizenship or domicile |
| Birth certificate | Recorded place/date/parent details | Citizenship without applying the correct Section 3 period |

| Residence/"domicile" certificate | Local residence/status for its issuing purpose | Indian citizenship or State citizenship |
| OCI card | Section 7A foreign-national registration and notified privileges | Indian citizenship or voting rights |
| Citizenship certificate | Registration/naturalisation under the Citizenship Act | Immunity from fraud-based statutory consequences |

*Evidence must be matched to the legal proposition for which it is offered.*

### 1. Aadhaar: an express statutory warning

Section 9 of the Aadhaar Act states that an Aadhaar number or its authentication shall
not, by itself, confer any right of or be proof of citizenship or domicile.

Aadhaar is issued within a resident-oriented identity framework. Treating it as
citizenship proof confuses identification with political membership.

### 2. Birth certificates require a second legal step

A birth certificate may prove birth date, place and recorded parent details. Citizenship
still depends on the law applicable on that date:

- pre-1 July 1987;
- 1 July 1987 to before 3 December 2004; or
- post-3 December 2004.

The document supplies facts; Section 3 supplies the legal consequence.

### 3. Passport and citizenship certificate

An Indian passport is powerful ordinary evidence of Indian nationality and travel
status, but an exam answer should avoid calling any single document irrebuttable in
every possible citizenship dispute.

Section 13 of the Citizenship Act permits the Central Government, in appropriate cases
of doubt, to issue a certificate of citizenship. Unless obtained by fraud, false
representation or concealment, it is conclusive evidence of citizenship on the date
specified, without excluding proof of an earlier date.

### 4. Voter eligibility: constitutional framework, statutory right

Article 326 establishes elections on adult suffrage subject to citizenship, age and
statutory disqualifications. The operational right to be enrolled and vote is governed
by election statutes; it is not itself a Fundamental Right.

The freedom to make an electoral choice and receive necessary electoral information may
engage Article 19(1)(a), but that does not convert the statutory franchise itself into a
Fundamental Right.

### 5. Electoral-roll citizenship examination

Section 16(1)(a) of the Representation of the People Act, 1950 disqualifies a person
from electoral registration if the person is not an Indian citizen. Section 19 requires
the prescribed age and ordinary residence in the constituency.

Therefore, the electoral registration machinery must examine whether citizenship is
present when deciding inclusion or deletion. It must provide the prescribed notice,
hearing and appeal safeguards.

But its decision serves the electoral roll:

```text
ERO / ECI SIDE
decide eligibility for inclusion or deletion in electoral roll
including whether citizenship requirement is met
                         │
                         ≠
                         │
CITIZENSHIP-ACT SIDE
grant registration/naturalisation, issue Section 13 certificate,
determine Section 9 foreign-citizenship question or order Section 10 deprivation
```

The Election Commission does not acquire a general power to grant citizenship or
deprive a person of citizenship for every legal purpose.

### 9. Institutional map

| Institution | Citizenship-facing function | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Parliament | Law under Article 11 | Subject to Constitution |

### 10. The final examiner traps

- Citizenship is not proved by collecting the largest number of identity documents.
- Electoral registration requires citizenship but is not an irreversible citizenship
  certificate.
- Ordinary residence is necessary for constituency enrolment but does not itself confer
  citizenship.
- Article 9, Aadhaar Act Section 9 and Citizenship Act Section 9 are three different
  provisions.
- A State "domicile certificate" does not create State citizenship.

> **Answer line:** Identity documents supply evidence for defined purposes; citizenship
> arises from constitutional or statutory law, and each authority may decide only the
> question entrusted to it.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Distinguish the Election Commission's power to
examine citizenship for electoral rolls from the power to grant or terminate
citizenship.

**Model answer (146 words):**

Citizenship is a threshold for electoral registration because Section 16 of the
Representation of the People Act, 1950 disqualifies a non-citizen, while Section 19
requires age and ordinary residence. Consequently, the ECI's electoral registration
machinery may examine citizenship documents and objections when revising rolls and may
include or delete names through the prescribed hearing and appeal process. This is an
incidental determination for electoral eligibility. It is not a general power to create
or extinguish citizenship. Registration and naturalisation certificates, Section 9
foreign-citizenship determinations, Section 10 deprivation orders and Section 13
citizenship certificates belong to authorities under the Citizenship Act. The
distinction protects roll integrity and institutional competence. ECI cannot ignore
citizenship because only citizens vote, but neither can an electoral inquiry replace
the statutory citizenship code. Fair procedure is essential because wrongful deletion
impairs democratic participation, although voting remains statutory rather than a
Fundamental Right.

**Unique scoring rubric — 10 marks:** RPA eligibility provisions 2; ECI/ERO examination
power 2; Citizenship Act functions 2; institutional-limit analysis 1.5; due process 1.5;
conclusion 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** Q possesses Aadhaar and an EPIC but faces an objection during electoral-roll revision that Q is not an Indian citizen. What may the electoral authority decide, and what do the two documents prove?

**Model answer:** Aadhaar proves identity within its statutory framework and is expressly not citizenship or domicile proof. EPIC reflects electoral registration but is not immune from lawful correction. The electoral authority may examine citizenship as an eligibility condition for inclusion or deletion, following notice and hearing, but it cannot use that process to grant citizenship or make a Section 10 deprivation order for all legal purposes.

**Misconception to avoid:** Possession of two government-issued identity documents does not displace the distinct legal rules governing citizenship and electoral eligibility.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks recombine the twelve lesson-level distinctions through short factual and
applied problems.

## Cumulative check A — commencement architecture

**Question:** Arrange the legal reasoning for a person claiming citizenship in 1950
through domicile, migration from Pakistan, authorised return from Pakistan or Indian
origin abroad.

**Model answer:** Begin by identifying the factual category. Article 5 requires domicile
plus one of birth, parental birth or five-year residence. Article 6 requires the
undivided-India ancestry link and then applies the 19 July 1948 residence/registration
split. Article 7 normally excludes post-1 March 1947 migrants to Pakistan but routes an
authorised resettlement/permanent-return permit holder into the post-19 July Article 6
path. Article 8 requires the ancestry link, ordinary residence abroad and diplomatic or
consular registration.

**Misconception to avoid:** Articles 5–8 are not four interchangeable methods; each
begins with a distinct factual category.

## Cumulative check B — statutory acquisition and loss

**Question:** Why must an answer on the Citizenship Act use both chronology and actor?

**Model answer:** Chronology selects the applicable birth or descent rule: 1987, 1992
and 2004 transitions change parentage and registration conditions. Actor identifies the
mechanism: birth/descent arise on statutory facts; registration and naturalisation
require application and governmental decision; renunciation begins with the citizen's
declaration; termination follows voluntary foreign citizenship; deprivation requires a
category-limited Central Government order with grounds and safeguards.

**Misconception to avoid:** Memorising five acquisition and three loss labels without
dates, decision-makers or conditions cannot resolve a factual problem.

## Cumulative check C — equal citizenship and differentiated protection

**Question:** Formulate a legal test for a residence-based preference within India.

**Model answer:** First confirm that the preference does not claim to create separate
State citizenship. Identify the legal source: Article 16(3) requires parliamentary
authority for specified public-employment residence rules; Article 19(5) permits
reasonable ST/general-public restrictions; Article 371 may supply a tailored regional
mechanism. Then test legitimate purpose, fit, breadth, duration and impact under
Articles 14 and 16. Place of birth and residence must not be conflated.

**Misconception to avoid:** Neither "single citizenship invalidates every local
preference" nor "regional protection validates every preference" is constitutionally
sound.

## Cumulative check D — three special statutory arrangements

**Question:** Distinguish OCI, Section 6A and Section 6B by person, territory, time and
legal effect.

**Model answer:** OCI registers an eligible foreign national with Indian connections
for notified privileges but not political citizenship. Section 6A is an Assam-specific
Accord settlement for specified-territory entrants divided into pre-1966 and
1966–24 March 1971 cohorts, including a ten-year electoral consequence. Section 6B is
an application route for six communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan
entering by 31 December 2014, with excluded Sixth Schedule and Inner Line areas.

**Misconception to avoid:** Similar vocabulary about origin or migration does not merge
the schemes: OCI is not citizenship, Section 6A is not the CAA and the 1971 and 2014
dates are not alternatives.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question — 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish Article 9 of the Constitution from Section 9 of the
Citizenship Act, 1955.

**Model answer (146 words):**

Article 9 belongs to the Constitution's commencement settlement. It denies citizenship
by virtue of Article 5, or deemed citizenship under Articles 6 and 8, to a person who
voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State. Its text should therefore not
be expanded into a complete permanent code for every later dual-status dispute.
Section 9 of the Citizenship Act governs the post-commencement consequence of a citizen
voluntarily acquiring another country's citizenship by naturalisation, registration or
otherwise. It provides that Indian citizenship ceases on such acquisition, subject to
the war proviso. Section 9(2) also supplies an institutional mechanism: disputes about
whether, when or how foreign citizenship was acquired are determined by the prescribed
authority under prescribed rules of evidence. Thus, Article 9 closes specified
constitutional commencement routes, whereas Section 9 operationalises later statutory
termination. Confusing them obscures both their temporal fields and the need for lawful
determination.

**Scoring rubric — 10 marks:** Article 9 scope 2.5; Section 9 rule 2.5; Section 9(2)
procedure 1.5; temporal comparison 2; conclusion 1.5.

## Final 15-mark question — 250 words

**Question:** "Single citizenship in India is compatible with, but places limits upon,
regional differentiation." Discuss.

**Model answer (245 words):**

India's federal Constitution distributes legislative and executive power but creates
one national citizenship. Citizens owe political allegiance to the Union and do not
possess a separate citizenship of the State in which they reside. This design advances
fraternity, interstate mobility and equal participation in national and State
democracy.

Single citizenship, however, is not mechanical uniformity. Article 16(3) permits
Parliament to prescribe residence requirements for specified public employment.
Article 19(5) allows reasonable restrictions on movement and residence in the general
public interest or to protect Scheduled Tribes. The Article 371 family contains
purpose-specific arrangements protecting customary law, land or local opportunity.
These provisions recognise that equal national membership may require safeguards
against regional displacement or structural disadvantage.

The compatibility has limits. Article 15 prohibits discrimination on place of birth,
while Article 16 controls residence discrimination in public employment. A State cannot
convert an administrative "domicile" certificate into separate political membership or
invoke Article 16(3) without parliamentary authority. Under Articles 14 and 16,
preferences must pursue a legitimate objective and remain proportionate rather than
hereditary, blanket or permanently exclusionary. *Pradeep Jain* captures the balance:
India has one citizenship and one constitutional domicile, although reasonable
residence classifications may exist.

Former Article 35A privileges illustrate why historical and current law must be
separated; they ceased with the 2019 reconfiguration upheld in 2023.

Therefore, regional differentiation strengthens single citizenship when it enables
substantive participation within one Union, but contradicts it when local status becomes
a substitute for equal political membership.

**Scoring rubric — 15 marks:** constitutional meaning 2; rationale 2; Articles 16(3),
19(5) and 371 evidence 4; equality limits 2.5; case/historical qualification 2;
balanced analysis 1.5; conclusion 1.

## Final 20-mark question — 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate India's citizenship architecture as a balance among historical
settlement, national integration, migration control and diaspora engagement.

**Model answer (249 words):**

India's citizenship architecture is layered rather than contained in one rule. Articles
5–8 produced a finite commencement settlement: domicile-based membership, Partition
migration routes, authorised return from Pakistan and consular registration of persons
of Indian origin abroad. Articles 10–11 then ensured continuity while empowering
Parliament to regulate future acquisition and termination.

The Citizenship Act, 1955 combines inclusion with increasing control. Birth citizenship
moved from a broad territorial rule before July 1987 to parentage and illegal-migrant
conditions after December 2004. Descent, registration and naturalisation preserve
family, residence and integration routes, while renunciation, Section 9 termination and
category-limited Section 10 deprivation regulate exit with different triggers and
safeguards.

National integration appears in single citizenship: no State citizenship fragments
political membership. Yet Articles 16(3), 19(5) and 371 allow bounded local and tribal
protection. Section 6A represents deeper historical asymmetry. Its Assam-specific
pre-1966 and 1966–24 March 1971 cohorts were upheld 4:1 in 2024, although implementation
remains monitored.

Diaspora engagement is separated from democratic membership through OCI. Foreign
nationals receive notified mobility and economic privileges but no vote, legislature,
constitutional office or general public-employment equality.

CAA Section 6B adds a facilitated application route for six communities from three
countries entering by 31 December 2014. It is not automatic, excludes protected areas
and remains contested under Article 14. The 19 August 2026 Collector redirection changed
procedure, not eligibility.

The architecture's strength is adaptive differentiation. Its constitutional risk lies
in opaque discretion, exclusion or religion-based classification. Legitimacy depends
on statutory limits, equality, fair procedure and judicial review.

**Scoring rubric — 20 marks:** commencement architecture 3; ordinary acquisition/loss
4; single citizenship and safeguards 3; Section 6A 2.5; OCI 2; CAA/current qualification
3; critical evaluation 1.5; conclusion 1.

# REMEDIATION

## Common error 1 — treating domicile as one more Article 5 alternative

**Faulty thought:** domicile, birth, parental birth and five-year residence are four
equal alternatives.

**Repair:**

```text
domicile in India
       AND
one of three alternatives
```

**Retrieval cue:** **Domicile opens the gate; one factual link completes the route.**

## Common error 2 — losing the two Partition dates

| Date | Function |
|---|---|
| 1 March 1947 | Article 7 migration-to-Pakistan exclusion begins after this date |
| 19 July 1948 | Article 6 divides residence-based and registration-based migrants |

**Repair question:** Did the person move **from** Pakistan, move **to** Pakistan or
reside abroad? Only then apply the date.

## Common error 3 — saying Article 9 permanently prohibits all dual status

**Repair:** Article 9 defeats claims under Articles 5, 6 and 8 where foreign citizenship
was voluntarily acquired. Section 9 of the Citizenship Act controls later voluntary
foreign-citizenship termination and factual determination.

## Common error 4 — using one birth rule for every year

```text
1950–before 1 Jul 1987 → birthplace-oriented
1 Jul 1987–before 3 Dec 2004 → either parent citizen
on/after 3 Dec 2004 → both, or one citizen + other not illegal migrant
```

**Repair question:** What is the exact date of birth? Without it, no Section 3 answer is
complete. For the post-2004 rule, also apply Section 2(1)(b)'s two-limb, internally
conjunctive definition rather than using "illegal migrant" as an undefined label.

## Common error 5 — converting marriage or residence into automatic citizenship

Marriage, PIO connection, former citizenship and OCI status create possible
**registration categories**. Naturalisation residence creates eligibility for an
**application**. Neither is an automatic grant; naturalisation additionally requires a
certificate and the Section 6(2) Second Schedule oath.

## Common error 5A — importing one Indian-origin test everywhere

Section 5 counts the applicant or either parent and includes post-1947 incorporated
territory. Articles 6/8 and Section 6A contain distinct grandparent language and attach
different migration, overseas-residence or Assam conditions. Always name the provision
before applying an ancestry test.

## Common error 6 — describing deprivation as universal cancellation

Use the sequence:

```text
covered citizenship category
      → listed ground
      → public-good satisfaction
      → notice
      → inquiry where applicable
      → order
```

If an answer starts directly with five grounds, it has skipped the category limitation.

## Common error 7 — confusing one citizenship with identical local rules

Single citizenship prohibits separate State political membership. It does not erase
Articles 16(3), 19(5) or purpose-specific Article 371 safeguards. The correct test is
authority, purpose and proportionality.

Canada supplies a narrow national-only citizenship comparison. The United States
recognises federal and State citizenship, while Switzerland has commune, canton and
Confederation links. These labels do not erase the different equality guarantees and
federal structures in each country.

## Common error 8 — calling OCI dual citizenship

Ask three questions:

1. Does the person hold an Indian passport?
2. Can the person vote or contest?
3. Are benefits constitutional or notification-based?

OCI answers point to foreign nationality, no political rights and notified statutory
benefits.

**Chronology repair:** original PIO Card Scheme 1999 → revised scheme notified
19 August 2002 and effective 15 September 2002 → OCI operational in December 2005 →
PIO rescinded/merged in January 2015.

## Common error 9 — swapping the Assam and CAA dates

| Scheme | Date |
|---|---|
| Section 6A middle-cohort outer boundary | Entry before 25 March 1971, meaning through 24 March |
| CAA class | Entry on or before 31 December 2014 |

**Mnemonic:** **Assam Accord → 1971; Amendment route → 2014.**

## Common error 10 — saying CAA automatically grants citizenship

Eligibility is only the first gate. Application, documents, enquiry, oath, statutory
qualification and certificate follow. Section 6B's retrospective deeming operates
after grant.

## Common error 11 — stating that CAA requires individual persecution proof

The statute names communities, countries, date and exemption status. Persecution is the
policy justification advanced for the classification, not an additional express
individual-proof element in Section 2 or Section 6B.

## Common error 12 — treating identity documents as interchangeable

Use the sentence:

> A document proves only the proposition assigned to it by law; citizenship is the
> legal consequence of constitutional or statutory rules, not the visual authority of
> the card.

## Short remedial applications

1. **Born in India in 1984:** start with the first Section 3 period, not the 2004 rule.
2. **Born abroad in 2006:** check citizen parent and consular registration.
3. **OCI wants to vote:** Section 7B denies voter registration.
4. **State claims Article 16(3):** identify Parliament as the authorised legislature.
5. **CAA applicant files in 2026:** filing is not grant; competent authority depends on
   the applicable procedural order.
6. **EPIC holder faces citizenship objection:** electoral authority may examine roll
   eligibility with due process; EPIC is not a universal citizenship certificate.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Membership vocabulary and citizen-specific consequences

- **Citizenship:** full legal membership involving allegiance, protection and political
  participation.
- **Alien:** national/citizen of another State; friendly and enemy alien are textbook
  categories.
- **Enemy alien precision:** Article 22(3)(a) excludes Article 22(1)–(2), not the whole
  article.
- **Nationality:** usually the international legal bond with a State; overlaps with
  citizenship in Indian practice but admits no universal cross-system distinction.
- **Domicile:** permanent legal home, involving residence plus intention.
- **Residence:** factual/ordinary living; can change without changing citizenship.
- **Citizen-specific text:** Articles 15, 16, 19 and 29.
- **Article 30 nuance:** text speaks of religious or linguistic minorities, not
  expressly citizens.
- **Political capacities:** voting, legislative membership and specified constitutional
  offices require citizenship under their respective provisions.
- **Person-based rights:** Articles 14, 20 and 21 are not confined to citizens.

## Part II at commencement — Articles 5–8

### Article 5

```text
domicile in India
      AND
birth in India OR either parent born in India
OR ordinary residence ≥5 years immediately before commencement
```

- Commencement date: **26 January 1950**.
- Domicile is mandatory; the three factual links are alternatives.
- It is not today's citizenship-by-birth provision.

### Article 6 — Pakistan to India

- Qualifying link: person, parent or grandparent born in undivided India.
- Migrated **before 19 July 1948**: ordinarily resident since migration.
- Migrated **on/after 19 July 1948**: registration on pre-commencement application plus
  at least six months' residence immediately before application.

### Article 7 — India to Pakistan

- Migration to Pakistan **after 1 March 1947** normally defeats Articles 5 and 6.
- Exception: return under a permit for **resettlement or permanent return**.
- Returnee is treated as a post-19 July 1948 Article 6 migrant.

### Article 8 — Indian origin abroad

- Person, parent or grandparent born in undivided India.
- Person ordinarily residing outside India.
- Registration by diplomatic/consular representative.
- Application could be before or after commencement.
- Article 8 is not OCI.

## Articles 9–11 — the continuing framework

- **Article 9:** voluntary foreign citizenship defeats claims under Articles 5, 6 and 8.
- **Section 9 Citizenship Act:** later voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship
  terminates Indian citizenship; prescribed authority decides whether, when and how.
- **Article 10:** Part II citizenship continues subject to parliamentary law.
- **Article 11:** Parliament may regulate acquisition, termination and all other
  citizenship matters.
- Citizenship is not itself a Fundamental Right.
- Citizenship law remains subject to constitutional review.

## Acquisition chronology under the Citizenship Act

### Defined gateway — "illegal migrant", Section 2(1)(b)

A foreigner is an illegal migrant where the person:

1. entered India without a valid passport or other travel documents **and** without such
   other document or authority as may be prescribed by or under law; **or**
2. entered with those required documents/authority but remained beyond the permitted
   period.

The two limbs are alternatives; the document conditions inside each limb must be read
conjunctively. The CAA proviso creates a bounded statutory exception for its specified
class; it does not erase the definition generally.

### Birth — Section 3

| Period | Requirement |
|---|---|
| 26 January 1950–before 1 July 1987 | Birth in India, subject to exclusions |
| 1 July 1987–before 3 December 2004 | Either parent citizen |
| On/after 3 December 2004 | Both parents citizens, or one citizen and other not illegal migrant |

Exclusions:

- non-citizen parent enjoying accredited-envoy immunity;
- enemy-alien parent where birth occurs in enemy-occupied territory.

### Descent — Section 4

| Period | Requirement |
|---|---|
| 26 January 1950–before 10 December 1992 | Father citizen |
| On/after 10 December 1992 | Either parent citizen |
| On/after 3 December 2004 | Consular registration within one year or later permission; prescribed declaration |

A dual-status minor citizen by descent must renounce the other citizenship/nationality
within six months after attaining full age or cease to be an Indian citizen.

### Registration — Section 5

Main categories:

- PIO ordinarily resident in India for seven years;
- PIO ordinarily resident outside undivided India;
- spouse of citizen with seven-year ordinary residence;
- minor children of citizens;
- adult child of specified registered citizens;
- specified former citizen/parent route with twelve-month residence;
- OCI cardholder for five years plus twelve-month ordinary residence.

Section 5(1), Explanation 2 defines PIO for registration through the applicant or either
parent born in undivided India or territory becoming part of India after
15 August 1947. It does not include a grandparent. Articles 6/8 instead use the person,
parent or grandparent born in India as defined by the 1935 Act and add their migration
or overseas-registration conditions. Section 6A separately uses the person, parent or
grandparent born in undivided India within the Assam scheme.

Seven-year structure for principal PIO/spouse routes:

```text
12 months immediately before application
      +
6 years within the preceding 8 years
```

Marriage does not automatically confer citizenship.

### Naturalisation — Section 6 and Third Schedule

- not an illegal migrant;
- reciprocity condition;
- twelve months immediately before application, with limited statutory relaxation;
- eleven years' aggregate residence/service in preceding fourteen years;
- good character;
- adequate Eighth Schedule language knowledge;
- intention to reside or continue specified service.
- after certificate grant, Section 6(2) requires the Second Schedule oath; citizenship
  operates from the certificate date on taking that oath.

CAA class: five years replaces the eleven-year aggregate; all other applicable
requirements do not automatically vanish.

Distinguished service in science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace or human
progress may justify waiver.

### Incorporation of territory — Section 7

Central Government specifies the connected persons and effective date. Standard example:
Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962.

## Loss — actor, category, ground and procedure

### Renunciation — Section 8

- declaration by citizen of full age/capacity;
- takes effect on registration;
- withheld during war until Central Government direction;
- minor child also ceases;
- child may resume within one year after attaining full age.

### Termination — Section 9

- voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship;
- war proviso;
- prescribed determination for whether, when and how acquisition occurred.

### Deprivation — Section 10

- applies only to a citizen by naturalisation, by virtue only of Article 5(c), or by
  registration other than Article 6(b)(ii)/Section 5(1)(a);
- grounds: fraud; disloyalty/disaffection; enemy trade/communication; two-year
  imprisonment within five years of registration/naturalisation; seven years'
  continuous foreign residence without a listed protected connection;
- continuation must not be conducive to public good;
- written notice;
- Committee of Inquiry route for applicable grounds;
- strict statutory and constitutional review.

## One citizenship and bounded regional difference

- India has no separate State citizenship.
- Canada is the controlled single-citizenship comparator: Canadian citizenship is
  national and provinces do not confer provincial citizenship, but Canada's wider
  federal architecture is not identical to India's.
- The US Fourteenth Amendment recognises citizenship of the United States and the State
  of residence; State citizenship is nested within federal supremacy and equality
  guarantees, not a separate international nationality.
- Swiss citizenship is rooted at commune, canton and Confederation levels; calling it
  merely "dual citizenship" is an exam shorthand for a more complex three-level model.
- In standard comparative shorthand, the USA and Switzerland are dual-citizenship
  federations, while Canada and India use one national citizenship; the internal
  structures behind those labels remain different.
- *Pradeep Jain* (1984): one citizenship and one domicile in India; reasonable residence
  classifications may exist.
- Article 15 lists **place of birth**, not residence.
- Article 16(3): Parliament may prescribe residence for specified public employment.
- Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act, 1957 authorisations expired in
  1974; Article 371D separately supports the AP/Telangana framework.
- Article 19(5): reasonable movement/residence restrictions for general public or ST
  interests.
- Article 371A/371G: bounded customary-law/land consent protections.
- Article 371D: local opportunity/cadre mechanism in Andhra Pradesh/Telangana.
- These are safeguards, not separate citizenships.
- Article 35A is historical; it ceased with the 2019 reconfiguration upheld in 2023.

## OCI/PIO/NRI rapid register

- **NRI:** Indian citizen ordinarily abroad.
- **PIO:** historical origin/card category; PIO card merged into OCI.
- Original PIO Card Scheme: 1999.
- Revised PIO scheme: notified 19 August 2002; effective 15 September 2002.
- Singhvi Committee: established 2000, report 2002.
- 2003 amendment: OCI for PIOs of 16 specified countries.
- OCI card scheme: operational from 2 December 2005; eligibility widened beyond the
  original 16-country model subject to statutory exclusions.
- 9 January 2015: PIO scheme rescinded and existing PIO cards deemed OCI.
- 2015 amendment: unified OCI Cardholder scheme.
- OCI is a foreign national holding a foreign passport.
- Section 7A: eligibility, ancestry and spouse routes.
- Section 7B(2): no voter registration, legislature, Presidency/VP, SC/HC judgeship,
  Article 16 equality or general public-service right.
- 4 March 2021 notification: lifelong multiple-entry visa; registration exemption;
  specified parity; special permissions; non-agricultural-property boundary.
- Unspecified fields default to foreigner treatment unless separately notified.
- Sections 7C/7D: renunciation/cancellation with listed grounds and hearing.
- OCI is not dual citizenship.

## Assam Section 6A rapid register

- Inserted by Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1985 after Assam Accord.
- Applies to Assam and "specified territory" as defined.
- Pre-1 January 1966 cohort: Indian origin + residence since entry → deemed citizen from
  1 January 1966.
- 1 January 1966–24 March 1971 cohort: Indian origin + residence + foreigner detection +
  registration.
- Ten years from detection: citizen-like rights/obligations, no electoral-roll entry.
- After ten years: citizen for all purposes.
- Time-bound opt-out declarations and statutory exclusions for existing citizens and
  persons expelled before the 1985 amendment remain part of the complete mechanism.
- Section 6A's 1946-Act/Tribunal references must now be read with the 2025 repeal and
  savings framework; the cohorts and cut-off themselves were not altered.
- Section 6A date is not CAA date.
- 17 October 2024: upheld **4:1**.
- Majority: reasonable Assam/date classification; no established Article 29(1)
  violation; implementation failure not automatic invalidity.
- Dissent: prospective invalidity, not controlling.
- 6 February 2025: implementation status reporting/monitoring.

## CAA Section 6B rapid register

Statutory class:

```text
Hindu / Sikh / Buddhist / Jain / Parsi / Christian
from Afghanistan / Bangladesh / Pakistan
entered on or before 31 December 2014
and covered by relevant exemption framework
```

- Not treated as illegal migrant for Citizenship Act purposes.
- Application for registration or naturalisation required.
- Certificate required; no automatic grant.
- After grant, deemed citizen from date of entry.
- Pending migration/citizenship proceeding abates on conferment.
- Five-year aggregate substitutes for eleven-year aggregate in Third Schedule.
- Excluded: Sixth Schedule tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Tripura and Inner
  Line areas.
- 2024 Rules operationalised electronic application and scrutiny.
- Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 repealed the 1920/1946 Acts from 1 September
  2025 with a savings clause for consistent prior instruments/actions.
- Immigration and Foreigners (Exemption) Order, 2025 uses **31 December 2024** for
  specified immigration-document relief; it does not change the CAA citizenship
  cut-off of **31 December 2014**.
- **Sole current linkage:** G.S.R. 742(E) and S.O. 4583(E), 19 August 2026, redirected
  specified applications/pending files to jurisdictional Collectors.
- Redirection was procedural; substantive eligibility did not change.
- Article 14 challenge: humanitarian minority-classification defence versus
  religion/country under-inclusion and secular-equality challenge.
- No final merits judgment located through 30 September 2026.

## Verification qualifications and remaining uncertainty

1. The 2018 and 2021 question texts were verified from local official papers. Local
   official answer-key files were unavailable; this learning session deliberately
   displays no key or inferred answer.
2. The 2026 GS-II question was OCR-verified directly from the official local scan.
3. No final Supreme Court CAA merits judgment was located in the official judgment
   record checked through 30 September 2026; later use must refresh this status.
4. The 4 March 2021 notification is the controlling OCI Section 7B notification located
   for this generation; later use should check for supersession.
5. "Nationality" is used in a qualified comparative sense because Indian law principally
   defines and regulates citizenship rather than providing one universal citizenship–
   nationality distinction.
6. Detailed refugee law, NRC procedure, Foreigners Tribunal procedure, Article 19
   doctrine, Article 371 machinery and electoral-roll administration remain with their
   specialist topics beyond the bounded interfaces taught here.
