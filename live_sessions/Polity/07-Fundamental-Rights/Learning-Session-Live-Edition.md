# Fundamental Rights - Live Learning Session

> Fundamental Rights are best understood as a constitutional control system, not as a list to memorise. Part III first identifies who is bound, then determines what kinds of law and action are reviewable, states the protected interests, supplies remedies, and finally defines exceptional limits. The session therefore moves from architecture to gateways, equality, freedom, dignity, exploitation, religion, minority rights, remedies and emergency control.

*Part III works only when its gateway, right, restriction and remedy are read together.*

### 1. Why these rights are called fundamental

The Constitution protects many interests, but a Fundamental Right has a special legal position. It is placed in Part III, can invalidate inconsistent State action, and carries a constitutional route to judicial enforcement. Fundamental does not mean unlimited. It means that ordinary political majorities must justify interference within constitutional limits.

Part III is often described as the **“Magna Carta of India”** because it gives an extensive, enforceable charter of liberty and equality. The phrase is a memory aid, not a claim that the Indian text simply reproduces the English Magna Carta or the United States Bill of Rights.

The rights promote **political democracy** by placing legal limits on public power. They protect individual choice, equal status, dignity and pluralism while allowing specified regulation for public order, social reform, security and competing rights.

### 2. The article map

| Function | Articles | Core subject |
|---|---:|---|
| Gateways | 12-13 | State and inconsistent law |
| Equality | 14-18 | equal status, non-discrimination, employment, untouchability, titles |
| Freedom | 19-22 | civil freedoms, criminal safeguards, life, education, arrest and detention |
| Exploitation | 23-24 | trafficking, forced labour and child labour |
| Religion | 25-28 | conscience, denomination, taxation and instruction |
| Culture and education | 29-30 | cultural conservation and minority institutions |
| Remedy | 32 | Supreme Court enforcement |
| Saving and special competence | 31A-31C, 33-35 | protected laws, disciplined forces, martial-law indemnity and Parliament's exclusive fields |

Originally, the Constitution contained a Fundamental Right to property in Article 31 and Article 19(1)(f). The 44th Amendment removed those provisions from Part III. Property is now protected by Article 300A: deprivation requires authority of law, but the right is not a Fundamental Right.

### 3. Right-holders: never use one blanket list

| Formulation in the text | Principal examples |
|---|---|
| **Any person / no person / all persons** | Arts 14, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27 and 28 |
| **Citizen / all citizens / section of citizens** | Arts 15, 16, 19 and 29 |
| **All children aged six to fourteen** | Art 21A |
| **Religious denomination or section** | Art 26 |
| **Religious or linguistic minorities** | Art 30 |

Article 30 does not use the word “citizen”; it speaks of minorities based on religion or language. The safest exam method is to reproduce the right-holder stated in the Article rather than depend on an oversimplified foreigners table.

### 4. Who can violate a right?

Most Fundamental Rights restrain the **State**, whose Part III meaning is defined by Article 12. Some provisions expressly reach private action:

- Article 15(2): access to specified public places and facilities;
- Article 17: disabilities arising from untouchability;
- Article 23: trafficking, begar and forced labour;
- Article 24: prohibited child employment.

Other rights create or support positive State obligations. Article 21 jurisprudence requires fair criminal procedure, emergency medical care in public facilities and protection against custodial abuse. This is different from saying that every social need becomes an unlimited judicial entitlement.

### 5. Four recurring distinctions

| Distinction | Correct meaning |
|---|---|
| Negative / positive | restraint on State interference / duty to secure conditions or protection |
| Vertical / horizontal | claim against State / claim affecting private conduct |
| Citizen / person | nationality-limited / available regardless of citizenship, subject to textual exceptions |
| Right / remedy | protected interest / judicial means of enforcement |

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Because Fundamental Rights are restricted and amendable, they are not truly fundamental.

**Reply:** Their fundamentality lies in constitutional priority, judicial enforceability and the State's burden of justification, not in absoluteness. Even amendment power cannot damage the Constitution's basic structure.

**Residual:** Judicial enforceability can protect a constitutional minimum, but implementation still depends on legislation, administration and access to justice.

### UPSC application

Begin every rights answer with four questions:

```text
Who holds the right?
        |
Who is the respondent?
        |
What is the protected interest and permitted restriction?
        |
What remedy and standard of review apply?
```

The 2021 Prelims demand on property status and the repeated privacy questions test this architecture: an interest may be constitutionally protected without being a separately numbered Fundamental Right.

### Revision notes

1. Part III runs from Articles 12 to 35.
2. India presently recognises six groups of Fundamental Rights.
3. Property moved outside Part III through the 44th Amendment.
4. Fundamental does not mean absolute.
5. Articles use different right-holders; quote the textual category.
6. Most rights are vertical, but Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24 have direct horizontal reach.
7. Positive obligations are not identical to unlimited welfare entitlements.
8. Article 32 supplies a guaranteed enforcement route for Part III rights.
9. Saving and Emergency provisions qualify enforcement but do not erase the constitutional structure.
10. A good answer identifies holder, respondent, right, limit, review test and remedy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to describe Fundamental Rights either as absolute individual freedoms or merely as restrictions on the State?

**Model answer:** They are qualified constitutional guarantees. Most restrain State interference, but each operates within textually defined limits, and some also reach private conduct or require protective State action. Their constitutional importance lies in enforceability and justified limitation, not absoluteness.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating “fundamental” as “unrestricted”, or assuming that every Part III provision has the same beneficiary and respondent.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Explain how Part III combines limited government with an enforceable constitutional order. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Part III converts constitutional ideals into legal limits on constituted power. Articles 12 and 13 first identify the public authorities and laws subject to review. Articles 14-30 then protect equality, civil freedom, dignity, freedom from exploitation, conscience and cultural choice. These guarantees are qualified rather than absolute: Article 19 contains specified restriction clauses, religious freedom yields to public order, morality, health and reform, and special provisions permit substantive equality.

The architecture is enforceable because Article 32 guarantees access to the Supreme Court for Part III violations, while Article 226 gives High Courts a wider public-law jurisdiction. Some provisions also reach private domination, especially Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24.

Part III therefore establishes limited government without disabling governance. Its constitutional method is not freedom without regulation, but power subjected to text, reason, review and remedy.

**Unique scoring note:** **10 marks = direct thesis 2 + architecture of Articles 12-35 3 + vertical/horizontal and qualified-right analysis 2 + remedy linkage 2 + conclusion 1.**

*Article 12 and Article 226 answer different questions: constitutional identity and public-law amenability.*

### 1. The four textual limbs

Article 12 includes:

1. the Government and Parliament of India;
2. the Government and Legislature of every State;
3. all local authorities;
4. other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

The definition is inclusive, not exhaustive. It ensures that government cannot avoid Part III merely by acting through a corporation, society or formally separate agency.

### 2. “Other authorities” and instrumentality

The Supreme Court's instrumentality analysis developed through cases such as *Sukhdev Singh*, *R.D. Shetty*, *Ajay Hasia* and *Pradeep Kumar Biswas*. No single factor is mechanically decisive. The inquiry asks whether government domination is financially, functionally and administratively deep enough that the body is effectively an agency of the State.

| Factor | What it indicates | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership or share capital | structural government link | ownership alone is not always decisive |
| Financial assistance | dependence | grants do not automatically convert every recipient |
| Monopoly protected by State | special public position | must be assessed with other factors |
| Deep and pervasive control | loss of independent character | ordinary regulation is insufficient |
| Public function closely related to government | governmental character | public importance alone is not enough |
| Transfer of a government department | functional continuity | strong but still part of the overall inquiry |

### 3. Statutory origin is not the test

A body need not be created by statute to fall within Article 12; *Ajay Hasia* involved a registered society. Conversely, statutory creation does not automatically settle every case. The constitutional concern is real governmental character, not legal form.

### 4. Article 12 versus public duty under Article 226

*Zee Telefilms* illustrates the distinction. A body may fall outside Article 12 yet perform a public duty that can be reviewed by a High Court under Article 226. This does not enlarge Article 12 by another name.

```text
ARTICLE 12                         ARTICLE 226
Is the body constitutionally      Is there a public-law duty or
part of "State"?                  public function fit for writ review?
       |                                      |
Part III applies directly         High Court may review the duty
```

A purely private contractual dispute does not become public law merely because one party is influential or provides a useful service.

### 5. Why the distinction matters

If the body is State, its actions must satisfy the full range of applicable Fundamental Rights, including Article 14. If it is not State but owes a public duty, the High Court may control that duty without constitutionalising its entire private sphere.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A broad Article 12 test lets courts convert every regulated or subsidised body into government.

**Reply:** The controlling inquiry requires cumulative evidence of domination or agency; ordinary regulation, public importance or financial assistance alone is insufficient.

**Residual:** Hybrid bodies and privatised public services remain difficult because public power may be exercised through networks rather than a single government-controlled entity.

### UPSC application

In an unfamiliar problem, do not begin by naming a case. First classify the body, test instrumentality factors, and only then ask whether Article 226 provides a narrower public-duty route.

### Revision notes

1. Article 12 is an inclusive definition for Part III.
2. Government, legislatures and local authorities are express limbs.
3. “Other authorities” prevents evasion through separate legal personality.
4. Statutory creation is relevant but not conclusive.
5. *Ajay Hasia* uses an instrumentality/agency analysis.
6. *Pradeep Kumar Biswas* stresses cumulative financial, functional and administrative domination.
7. Regulation is not the same as deep control.
8. Public importance alone does not create Article 12 State status.
9. Article 226 may reach a public duty even when Article 12 does not.
10. A public-duty writ does not constitutionalise every private action of the body.

### Concept check

**Question:** A privately incorporated body receives government grants, is heavily regulated and performs an important public service. Is it necessarily “State” under Article 12?

**Model answer:** No. Grants, regulation and public importance are relevant but not individually conclusive. The court examines cumulative government domination and whether the body functions as an instrumentality or agency. Even if Article 12 is not satisfied, a specific public duty may remain reviewable under Article 226.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating “performs a public service” with “is the State”, or treating Article 226's public-duty reach as part of the Article 12 definition.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Distinguish the instrumentality test under Article 12 from the public-duty jurisdiction of High Courts under Article 226. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 12 asks an identity question: whether a body is sufficiently governmental to be included within “State” for Part III. Cases such as *Ajay Hasia* and *Pradeep Kumar Biswas* examine cumulative financial, functional and administrative domination, government ownership, protected monopoly and transfer of governmental functions. Neither statutory origin nor public importance alone is conclusive.

Article 226 asks a remedial question. A High Court may issue a writ to a non-State body when the challenged act concerns a public duty or public-law function. *Zee Telefilms* therefore permits a distinction between failure of Article 12 status and possible writ amenability.

The consequence also differs. Article 12 status subjects applicable conduct to Fundamental Rights generally; Article 226 review controls the particular public duty. The distinction prevents both governmental evasion and indiscriminate constitutionalisation of private relations.

**Unique scoring note:** **10 marks = accurate two-question distinction 3 + Article 12 factors and cases 3 + Article 226 consequence 2 + limitation and conclusion 2.**

*The doctrines answer different legal problems; they are not interchangeable slogans.*

### 1. Four clauses of Article 13

- **Article 13(1):** pre-Constitution laws are void to the extent of inconsistency with Part III.
- **Article 13(2):** the State shall not make a law taking away or abridging Part III rights; contravening law is void to that extent.
- **Article 13(3)(a):** “law” includes Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law.
- **Article 13(3)(b):** “laws in force” includes unrepealed pre-Constitution laws even if not then operating everywhere.
- **Article 13(4):** inserted by the 24th Amendment, it excludes constitutional amendments under Article 368 from Article 13.

The exclusion in clause (4) does not create unlimited amendment power. *Kesavananda Bharati* permits amendment of Part III but prohibits damage to the Constitution's basic structure.

### 2. Severability

Under severability, the court removes only the unconstitutional portion if:

1. valid and invalid parts are textually separable;
2. the legislature would have enacted the valid remainder independently;
3. the remainder is complete and workable;
4. severance does not rewrite the legislative scheme.

*R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala* is the standard anchor. Judicial restraint supports preservation, but legislative intent limits it.

### 3. Eclipse

Classically, an inconsistent pre-Constitution law is not repealed. It becomes unenforceable against the relevant Fundamental Right and may revive if the constitutional obstacle disappears. *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras* illustrates this dormant or eclipsed condition.

| Eclipse | Repeal |
|---|---|
| law remains on statute book but is unenforceable to the inconsistency | law is legally removed |
| classical operation concerns pre-Constitution law | may occur through legislative repeal |
| revival may follow removal of constitutional shadow | fresh legislation is ordinarily required after repeal |

Do not casually extend eclipse to every post-Constitution law. Article 13(2) prohibits the State from making the contravening law in the first place.

### 4. Non-waiver

*Basheshar Nath* rejected the idea that individual consent can validate State action contrary to a Fundamental Right. Part III expresses public constitutional policy, not merely a private benefit. This does not mean every statutory, contractual or procedural right is non-waivable.

### 5. Prospective overruling

Prospective overruling controls the temporal effect of a newly declared constitutional rule. *I.C. Golaknath* introduced the device into Indian constitutional adjudication. It does not arise automatically whenever precedent changes; the court must deliberately and reasonedly shape the future effect.

### 6. The amendment sequence

```text
Shankari Prasad / Sajjan Singh
amendment not "law" for Art 13
          |
Golaknath, 1967
Parliament cannot amend FRs; prospective overruling
          |
24th Amendment, 1971 -> Art 13(4)
          |
Kesavananda, 1973
amendment power survives, basic structure limits it
```

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Severability, reading down and prospective overruling let judges preserve laws they should simply invalidate.

**Reply:** Each doctrine has a bounded trigger. Severability respects a workable legislative remainder; reading down requires a meaning the text can bear; prospective overruling addresses temporal disruption.

**Residual:** These remedial choices still involve judicial judgement about legislative intent, institutional consequences and fairness to settled transactions.

### UPSC application

The official 2026 Prelims question on Article 13 tested whether custom or usage having the force of law falls within “law”. It does. The available 2026 key remains provisional, so the constitutional text is stated without inferring or disclosing an option letter.

### Revision notes

1. Article 13(1) controls pre-Constitution laws.
2. Article 13(2) prohibits new rights-abridging State law.
3. “Law” expressly includes custom and usage with force of law.
4. Invalidity operates to the extent of inconsistency or contravention.
5. Article 13(4) excludes Article 368 amendments from Article 13.
6. Basic-structure review nevertheless controls amendments.
7. Severability preserves an intended, workable valid remainder.
8. Eclipse classically makes inconsistent pre-Constitution law dormant.
9. A Fundamental Right cannot generally be waived to validate unconstitutional State action.
10. Prospective overruling must be expressly fashioned; it is not automatic.

### Concept check

**Question:** A pre-1950 statute contains one separable provision inconsistent with a Fundamental Right. Which two doctrines may operate, and what distinct work does each perform?

**Model answer:** Eclipse explains why the inconsistent pre-Constitution provision becomes unenforceable rather than automatically erased. Severability determines whether that provision can be removed while the intended and workable remainder continues. Eclipse concerns enforceability; severability concerns the law's divisible structure.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating eclipse, severability and repeal as synonyms, or assuming that every invalid law automatically revives after a later constitutional change.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Article 13 moderates invalidity rather than always destroying the entire law.” Analyse. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 13 constitutionalises judicial review by rendering inconsistent or contravening law void only “to the extent” of the defect. Its operation is therefore structured rather than mechanically destructive.

For pre-Constitution laws, Article 13(1) supports the classical doctrine of eclipse. In *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras*, inconsistency made the law dormant against the Fundamental Right; it was not treated as repealed. Severability, illustrated by *R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala*, preserves valid provisions where they are separable, independently intended and workable. Non-waiver under *Basheshar Nath* prevents individual consent from curing unconstitutional State action.

Temporal moderation is possible through prospective overruling, introduced in *Golaknath*, but only where the court expressly shapes the new rule's effect. Constitutional amendments fall outside Article 13 after clause (4), yet *Kesavananda Bharati* subjects them to basic-structure review.

These doctrines respect legislation and settled relations while preserving constitutional supremacy. Their limit is equally important: a court cannot rewrite an inseparable scheme, casually revive a post-Constitution void law, or invoke prospectivity without reasons. Article 13 moderates consequences, not the supremacy of Part III.

### Visual: three layers of Article 14 review

```text
ARTICLE 14
   |
   +--> FORMAL STATUS
   |    equality before law -> no special privilege; ordinary legal subjection
   |
   +--> COMPARATIVE TREATMENT
   |    equal protection -> like cases treated alike
   |          |
   |          +--> intelligible differentia
   |          +--> rational nexus to statutory object
   |
   +--> NON-ARBITRARINESS
        unguided or capricious State action violates equality
        legislation may also face manifest-arbitrariness review
```

*Classification asks whether a distinction is constitutionally connected to its object; arbitrariness asks whether power has a reasoned constitutional basis at all.*

### 1. Two expressions in one guarantee

| Expression | Origin | Character | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equality before law | British | negative | absence of special privilege; equal subjection to ordinary law |
| Equal protection of laws | American | positive | equal treatment of similarly situated persons |

Article 14 protects every **person**, including citizens, foreigners and legal persons where the nature of the right permits. It does not demand identical treatment regardless of relevant difference.

### 2. Reasonable classification

A classification is valid when:

1. the class is defined by an **intelligible differentia** distinguishing included from excluded persons or things; and
2. the differentia has a **rational nexus** with the law's object.

The law may classify on geography, occupation, need or another relevant basis. It may not select a group through an artificial distinction unrelated to its purpose.

**Example:** A public-health rule applying stricter controls to a demonstrably higher-risk activity may classify rationally. A rule excluding an unrelated community without evidence would fail the nexus test.

The example establishes the need for relevant differentiation; it does not mean that courts replace legislative policy with their preferred epidemiology.

### 3. From classification to arbitrariness

*E.P. Royappa* linked equality with non-arbitrariness: equality and arbitrariness are constitutional opposites. *Maneka Gandhi* integrated Articles 14, 19 and 21, requiring fair and non-arbitrary procedure.

**Manifest arbitrariness** is a demanding standard used against legislation where a measure is capricious, irrational or lacks an adequate determining principle. It is not a general judicial power to invalidate policy merely because another choice appears better.

### 4. Rule of law

Dicey's first two propositions are useful in India:

- absence of arbitrary power;
- equal subjection to ordinary law.

His third proposition—that rights precede and produce the constitution—does not fit India, where the written Constitution is the source and superior legal authority. Rule of law forms part of the basic structure.

### 5. Constitutional exceptions do not destroy the rule

Examples include:

- immunities of the President and Governors under Article 361;
- legislative speech and vote protections under Articles 105 and 194;
- substantially true reports under Article 361A;
- diplomatic immunities;
- valid saving under Article 31C within its present scope.

These are legally defined immunities, not a licence for arbitrary action.

### 6. Bounded India-United States comparison

The United States Fourteenth Amendment contains an **Equal Protection Clause** prohibiting a State from denying any person equal protection of the laws. United States courts developed different intensities of review rather than writing them into the constitutional text:

| United States approach | General field | Indian contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Strict scrutiny | race and certain fundamental-right classifications | India does not use one universal US-style tier; it begins with Article 14 classification/non-arbitrariness and applies proportionality where the right requires it |
| Intermediate scrutiny | principally sex-based classification | India combines Article 14 with express sex non-discrimination and Article 15(3) special provisions |
| Rational-basis review | ordinary social/economic classification | India's reasonable-classification test similarly asks for relevant differentiation and nexus, but its doctrinal vocabulary is distinct |

India's equality code is textually broader and more group-conscious. Articles 15(3)-(6) and 16(4), 16(4A), 16(4B) and 16(6) expressly permit specified advancement or representation measures. The United States Constitution contains no direct counterparts to these detailed protective-discrimination clauses; affirmative-action doctrine developed through legislation, policy and judicial interpretation. The Fourteenth Amendment has no direct counterpart to Article 17's abolition of untouchability or a separately codified equality provision equivalent to Article 18. The US Constitution does, however, contain distinct federal and State **Titles of Nobility Clauses**, which must not be overlooked.

Both systems protect formal equality and permit constitutionally justified differentiation, but their legal architecture differs. India's Article 32 additionally guarantees a direct Supreme Court enforcement route for Part III rights; United States equal-protection claims proceed through its general constitutional adjudication structure.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The anti-arbitrariness doctrine is so open-ended that judges can invalidate any policy they dislike.

**Reply:** Constitutional review still requires a legal defect: irrelevant classification, unguided discretion, caprice, lack of a determining principle or disproportionate treatment.

**Residual:** The boundary between constitutional irrationality and ordinary policy disagreement remains institutionally sensitive.

### UPSC application

The 2021 Prelims demand on uncontrolled executive discretion routes to Article 14. The 2021 GS-II comparison with the United States can now be answered from this bounded structure without turning the lesson into a general survey of American constitutional law. In Mains, write classification and arbitrariness as connected but distinct tests rather than two labels for the same inquiry.

1. Article 14 protects every person.
2. Equality before law is negative; equal protection is positive.
3. Equality does not require identical treatment of unequal situations.
4. Reasonable classification needs intelligible differentia and rational nexus.
5. Class legislation is prohibited.
6. *E.P. Royappa* connects equality and non-arbitrariness.

### Concept check

**Question:** A law creates a clearly identifiable class but the distinction has no connection with the law's stated objective. Does it satisfy Article 14?

**Model answer:** No. Intelligibility is only the first limb. The differentia must also bear a rational nexus to the law's object. A clear but irrelevant classification remains unconstitutional.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that any precise classification is valid, or that Article 14 forbids every legal distinction.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Trace the movement of Article 14 doctrine from reasonable classification to non-arbitrariness. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 14 contains both formal and substantive equality. “Equality before law” rejects legal privilege, while “equal protection of laws” requires like treatment of similarly situated persons. Early doctrine operationalised the second expression through reasonable classification: a law must employ an intelligible differentia and connect it rationally to its objective. This permits relevant differentiation but forbids class legislation.

The doctrine later moved beyond comparison between classes. In *E.P. Royappa*, the Supreme Court treated arbitrariness as antithetical to equality. *Maneka Gandhi* integrated Articles 14, 19 and 21, so that unfair and capricious procedure could not survive merely because a formal law existed. Review of legislation may now include manifest arbitrariness, but the standard requires caprice, irrationality or absence of a determining principle—not judicial disagreement with policy.

The expansion makes Article 14 responsive to modern discretionary government, where injustice may arise without an explicit classification. Its institutional limit is essential: courts must identify constitutional arbitrariness, not conduct an unrestricted merits appeal. Article 14 has thus evolved from “compare the classes” to “justify public power”, while retaining structured tests.


*Article 15 treats targeted advancement as a constitutional technique of equality, not automatically as its violation.*

### 1. Clause (1): discrimination on named grounds

Article 15(1) protects a **citizen** against State discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth or any of them. “Only” matters. A decision connected to a legitimate qualification is not invalid merely because a protected characteristic is present in the factual background; the court asks whether the prohibited ground supplied the adverse basis.

The list does not include **descent**, **residence** or **language**. Descent and residence appear in Article 16(2); language appears in Article 29(2). Close-option questions often exploit these differences.

### 2. Clause (2): direct horizontal reach

No citizen may, on the named grounds, face disability, liability, restriction or condition concerning:

- access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment;
- use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained from State funds or dedicated to public use.

The provision reaches private exclusion because its language is not confined to State action. Its field is specified public access, not every private relationship.

### 3. Clauses (3)-(5): advancement

- **15(3):** special provisions for women and children.
- **15(4):** advancement of socially and educationally backward classes of citizens, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- **15(5):** admission-related provisions for those groups in public and private educational institutions, aided or unaided, excluding Article 30(1) minority institutions.

The constitutional clauses are enabling. A particular percentage, beneficiary list or administrative criterion comes from legislation or policy and must not be confused with the Article itself.

### 4. Clause (6): EWS

The 103rd Amendment inserted Article 15(6). It allows special provisions for economically weaker sections other than the classes mentioned in clauses (4) and (5), including additional reservation up to ten per cent in each category of educational seats, while excluding Article 30(1) institutions.

*Janhit Abhiyan* upheld the 103rd Amendment by a 3:2 majority. State-notified income and asset criteria are dynamic administrative rules; they are not permanent constitutional thresholds and are not frozen in this lesson.

### 5. Formal and substantive equality

```text
FORMAL EQUALITY
"Do not discriminate on prohibited grounds"
                   +
SUBSTANTIVE EQUALITY
"Permit targeted measures against entrenched disadvantage"
                   =
ARTICLE 15'S COMPLETE DESIGN
```

The strongest defence of special provisions is that equal legal treatment may reproduce unequal social starting points. The strongest caution is that beneficiary identification, exclusion and implementation require evidence and constitutional discipline.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Special provisions classify by caste or sex and therefore contradict non-discrimination.

**Reply:** The Constitution expressly authorises specified advancement clauses. Their aim is substantive equality: dismantling conditions that make formally identical treatment unequal in effect.

**Residual:** Constitutional permission does not settle every design question. Evidence, beneficiary definition, institutional impact and competing claims remain reviewable.

### UPSC application

Use a clause map before discussing reservation. Article 15(3), 15(4), 15(5) and 15(6) authorise different measures and beneficiaries. Do not import employment doctrine from Article 16 or legislative-seat reservation from other Parts.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a current income threshold for EWS not part of the permanent constitutional meaning of Article 15(6)?

**Model answer:** Article 15(6) supplies enabling constitutional authority and a textual reservation ceiling. It leaves identification of economically weaker sections to State notification using income and other indicators. Those administrative criteria may change and must be verified separately.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating policy thresholds, quota implementation rules and constitutional clauses as the same legal source.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Article 15 moves from anti-classification to anti-subordination.” Explain with constitutional provisions. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 15 begins with an anti-discrimination command. Clause (1) prevents the State from disadvantaging a citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Clause (2) extends equality into specified public spaces, reaching private as well as State exclusion.

The Article then recognises that formal neutrality may preserve entrenched disadvantage. Clause (3) permits special provisions for women and children; clause (4) permits advancement of SEBCs, SCs and STs; clause (5) enables admission measures in aided and unaided institutions while protecting Article 30(1) minority institutions. Clause (6), inserted by the 103rd Amendment, permits EWS measures and an additional educational reservation up to ten per cent. *Janhit Abhiyan* upheld that design by a 3:2 majority.

Thus Article 15 does not simply prohibit classification. It distinguishes hostile exclusion from constitutionally authorised advancement. The qualification is that every enabling clause has its own beneficiaries and field; dynamic thresholds and policy percentages cannot be read into the Constitution, and implementation remains subject to equality review.

*Each clause answers a different employment question; “reservation” is not one undifferentiated power.*

### 1. Clauses (1)-(3): opportunity and prohibited grounds

Article 16(1) guarantees equality of opportunity to **citizens** in employment or appointment to an office under the State. Clause (2) adds **descent** and **residence** to the grounds familiar from Article 15 and does not include “language”.

Article 16(3) authorises **Parliament**, not a State legislature or executive order, to prescribe residence requirements for specified public employment. Current State-specific administrative arrangements should not be guessed from an old textbook illustration.

### 2. Clause (4): an enabling power

Article 16(4) allows the State to reserve appointments or posts for a backward class of citizens that, in the State's opinion, is not adequately represented in State services. It is enabling, not an individual right to demand a fixed quota.

*Indra Sawhney*:

- upheld OBC reservation in appointments;
- required exclusion of the creamy layer for OBCs;
- treated fifty per cent as the ordinary ceiling, with exceptional situations requiring justification;
- rejected reservation in promotion under Article 16(4);
- distinguished backward-class identification from poverty alone.

### 3. Promotion amendments and judicial discipline

| Provision | Function | Main constitutional control |
|---|---|---|
| 16(4A) | promotion reservation for SCs/STs, with consequential seniority | inadequacy of representation and Article 335 efficiency considerations |
| 16(4B) | carry-forward of unfilled reserved vacancies as a separate class | text separates backlog from current-year ceiling calculation |
| 16(6) | EWS reservation in appointments/posts, additional maximum ten per cent | excludes classes covered by 16(4) |

Nagaraj* upheld the promotion-enabling amendments subject to constitutional conditions. *Jarnail Singh* removed the requirement that the State re-prove the backwardness of SCs/STs, but retained the need for quantifiable data concerning inadequacy of representation and constitutional attention to efficiency. It did not overrule *Nagaraj* wholesale.

Article 335 is a counterweight, not a veto. It asks that SC/ST claims be considered consistently with administrative efficiency and contains an amendment-based relaxation route.

### 4. EWS and sub-classification

*Janhit Abhiyan* upheld Article 16(6) by a 3:2 majority. *State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* permits evidence-based sub-classification within Scheduled Castes for fair distribution of benefits; it does not compel every State to sub-classify or transfer the Article 341 list-alteration power from Parliament.

### 5. Why stale thresholds are unsafe

Creamy-layer income limits, EWS income and asset criteria, service rosters and State lists are administrative or statutory matters capable of change. This session teaches the constitutional authority, cases and tests. A current numerical threshold should be used only after official verification.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Reservation sacrifices merit and therefore contradicts equality of opportunity.

**Reply:** Article 16 treats representation and removal of structural exclusion as dimensions of substantive equality. Article 335, evidence requirements and judicial ceilings discipline the design rather than prohibit it.

**Residual:** “Merit”, representation and efficiency are institutionally produced concepts. Poor design or weak data can undermine both equality and administrative legitimacy.

### UPSC application

The 2023 Prelims demand linked Article 16 reservation with Article 335 efficiency. The safe answer route is provision -> enabling character -> data and case-law condition -> efficiency qualification.

The 2021 GS-II equality-comparison PYQ uses Article 16 as evidence that Indian equality is expressly group-conscious. Its full comparison is recorded answer-neutrally in the final PYQ section.

## Lesson 7 - Articles 17 and 18: dismantling status hierarchy

Progress: 7/25 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: untouchability, private enforcement, offences, titles and national awards

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: two constitutional attacks on inherited rank

```text
INHERITED CASTE DISABILITY -- Article 17 --> abolished + punishable
STATE/FOREIGN TITULAR RANK -- Article 18 --> prohibited + consent-controlled
```

| Article 17 | Article 18 |
|---|---|
| abolishes “Untouchability” and forbids its practice in any form | prohibits State titles other than military or academic distinctions |
| enforcement of resulting disability is an offence | controls acceptance of foreign titles, presents, offices and emoluments |
| reaches public and private conduct | primarily prevents legally recognised ranks and foreign influence |
| requires penal and protective State action | permits national awards if not used as hereditary or titular prefixes |

*Article 17 attacks caste disability; Article 18 attacks State-created or foreign-derived titular rank.*

### 1. Article 17

The Constitution leaves “Untouchability” undefined and places it in quotation marks. Courts read it as the historically developed caste-based practice, not every social refusal or literal act of avoiding contact.

Three elements must be kept together:

1. untouchability is abolished;
2. its practice in any form is forbidden;
3. enforcement of a disability arising from it is an offence punishable by law.

The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 provide statutory machinery. Article 17 itself directly reaches private persons, while the State has a positive duty to prevent, investigate and punish violations.

### 2. Why Article 17 is not merely symbolic

*State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale* demonstrates that caste exclusion from common resources is a constitutional injury, not only a private social wrong. Legal abolition must be paired with enforcement because entrenched hierarchy can survive formal equality.

The limit is conceptual precision: not every caste injustice is automatically an Article 17 violation. Articles 14-16 and statutory law may govern adjacent discrimination.

### 3. Article 18's four clauses

1. The State cannot confer a title except a military or academic distinction.
2. A citizen cannot accept a title from a foreign State.
3. A non-citizen holding an office of profit or trust under the State needs presidential consent to accept a foreign title.
4. A person holding an office of profit or trust under the State needs presidential consent for a foreign present, emolument or office.

### 4. National awards

In *Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India*, the Supreme Court upheld Bharat Ratna and Padma awards because they recognise merit and are not titles of nobility. They cannot be used as prefixes or suffixes. Academic and military distinctions remain textually permitted.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Constitutional abolition cannot remove deeply embedded social hierarchy.

**Reply:** Article 17 changes legal status, criminalises enforcement of caste disability and creates a foundation for protective legislation and public duty. Law is necessary even when not sufficient.

**Residual:** Under-enforcement, social power and barriers to reporting can leave a gap between constitutional command and lived equality.

### UPSC application

The 2020 Prelims demand asks which rights category contains abolition of untouchability: it belongs to the **Right to Equality**, not the Right against Exploitation. The 2021 national-awards question tests the difference between an award and a prohibited title; no answer option or key is disclosed here.

### Revision notes

1. Article 17 is absolute in its constitutional command.
2. “Untouchability” refers to historical caste-based disability.
3. The provision reaches private conduct.
4. Enforcement of a disability is constitutionally made punishable.
5. Protective legislation gives the command institutional force.
6. Article 18 has four distinct clauses.
7. Military and academic distinctions are permitted.
8. Citizens cannot accept foreign titles.
9. Office-holders face additional presidential-consent controls.
10. National awards are valid but cannot be used as titles.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a valid national award not violate Article 18, while attaching it permanently before a person's name would create a constitutional problem?

**Model answer:** The award recognises achievement without creating a legal rank. Using it as a permanent prefix or suffix converts recognition into a titular distinction, which Article 18 seeks to prevent.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every honour as a prohibited title, or limiting Article 17 only to State action.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Assess the constitutional significance of Articles 17 and 18 in creating equal civic status. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Articles 17 and 18 address two forms of inherited or conferred hierarchy that ordinary non-discrimination rules alone could not dismantle. Article 17 abolishes caste-based untouchability, forbids its practice in any form and makes enforcement of resulting disability punishable. Its direct horizontal reach and the Protection of Civil Rights Act demonstrate that private social power can violate constitutional status. *Appa Balu Ingale* illustrates the need to treat exclusion from common facilities as a constitutional injury.

Article 18 prevents the State from creating titular ranks, bars citizens from accepting foreign titles and subjects foreign benefits to consent rules for public office-holders. *Balaji Raghavan* nevertheless permits national awards where they remain recognition rather than prefixes or hereditary rank.

Together, the Articles constitutionalise republican citizenship: birth, caste and State favour cannot create superior civic status. Their continuing weakness lies in implementation, not in the clarity of their command.

### Visual: never transfer one restriction clause to another freedom

| Freedom | Right | Permitted restriction grounds |
|---|---|---|
| Speech and expression | 19(1)(a) | 19(2): sovereignty and integrity; security of State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence |
| Peaceful, unarmed assembly | 19(1)(b) | 19(3): sovereignty and integrity; public order |

| Residence and settlement | 19(1)(e) | 19(5): same two grounds |
| Profession, occupation, trade or business | 19(1)(g) | 19(6): general public; qualifications; State monopoly |

*Article 19 is six rights with distinct constitutional defences, not one general liberty clause.*

### 1. Beneficiary and respondent

Article 19 protects **citizens**. Companies do not possess citizenship, although citizen shareholders or editors may assert their own rights where State action affects them. The Article principally operates against State-imposed legal restrictions.

The former property freedom in Article 19(1)(f) was omitted by the 44th Amendment. The remaining six are clauses (a)-(e) and (g).

### 2. A restriction requires more than a desirable objective

A valid restriction must:

1. be imposed by law;
2. fit the relevant clause's permitted ground;
3. be reasonable;
4. satisfy a proportionate relationship between means and end;
5. preserve procedural safeguards where the power risks abuse.

An executive preference without legal authority fails before proportionality begins. A law directed to “offensiveness” cannot be saved under Article 19(2) unless its actual operation fits an enumerated ground.

### 3. Reasonableness and proportionality

```text
LEGAL AUTHORITY
      |
LEGITIMATE CONSTITUTIONAL GROUND
      |
RATIONAL CONNECTION
      |
NECESSITY / LESS RESTRICTIVE MEANS
      |
BALANCE BETWEEN SOCIAL BENEFIT AND RIGHTS COST
      |
PROCEDURAL REVIEW, REASONS AND DURATION
```

Proportionality does not make courts the primary policy-maker. It requires the State to show that a rights cost is justified rather than excessive.

### 4. Close distinctions

- Assembly must be **peaceable and without arms**; Article 19 does not guarantee a strike.
- Association includes a negative freedom not to be compelled into an association, but not an unconditional right to recognition.
- Movement is internal; travel abroad is protected through Article 21.
- Residence includes temporary residence and settlement.
- Profession permits qualifications and State monopoly.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The broad grounds “public order” and “general public” allow the State to justify almost any restriction.

**Reply:** Grounds are only the first gate. The restriction must be lawful, connected, necessary, proportionate and procedurally reviewable.

**Residual:** Deferential review or delayed remedies may allow temporary restrictions to achieve their practical purpose before invalidation.

### UPSC application

When a question names a freedom, quote its exact restriction clause. Using Article 19(2)'s speech grounds to justify a residence restriction is a basic structural error.

### Revision notes

1. Article 19 protects citizens.
2. Six freedoms remain after deletion of property.
3. Each freedom has a separate restriction clause.
4. Article 19(2) contains eight exhaustive speech grounds.
5. Assembly and association have different morality coverage.
6. Movement and residence share Article 19(5).
7. Travel abroad is an Article 21 issue.
8. Article 19(6) permits qualifications and State monopoly.
9. Restrictions require law, a permitted ground and reasonableness.
10. Proportionality tests connection, necessity and balance.
11. Procedure, reasons, publication, review and duration can determine constitutionality.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the State not justify a restriction on movement by citing “security of the State” directly under Article 19(5)?

**Model answer:** Article 19(5) permits movement and residence restrictions only in the interests of the general public or for protection of Scheduled Tribe interests. Security concerns must be legally connected to one of those grounds; Article 19(2)'s wording cannot be transferred mechanically.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating all restriction clauses as a common list or beginning proportionality without first establishing legal authority and the correct constitutional ground.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Explain why Article 19 protects liberty through a structured test rather than an absolute guarantee. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 19 gives citizens six specific freedoms, but each carries its own restriction structure. Speech may be restricted only on the eight grounds in Article 19(2); assembly and association have narrower grounds; movement and residence are governed by general-public and Scheduled Tribe interests; profession permits qualifications and State monopoly.

This clause-specific design prevents a vague appeal to public interest. The State must identify legal authority, the correct enumerated ground and a reasonable connection between measure and objective. Modern review also asks whether the measure is suitable, necessary, proportionate and procedurally controlled.

Thus Article 19 neither constitutionalises licence nor permits unrestricted government. It protects liberty by forcing public power through successive gates of text, legality, reason, necessity and review. The main implementation risk is that temporary restrictions may impose their cost before effective judicial relief.

*The doctrine protects speech by controlling legal grounds, statutory precision and executive procedure.*

### 1. Press and prior restraint

There is no separate press Article. Freedom of the press derives from Article 19(1)(a) and carries the same Article 19(2) limits.

*Romesh Thappar* protected circulation; *Brij Bhushan* invalidated pre-censorship under the original Article 19(2). Both preceded the First Amendment. They establish strong suspicion of prior restraint, not an absolute prohibition: film pre-certification under the **Cinematograph Act, 1952** operates through a distinct statutory regime and has been constitutionally permitted.

### 2. Right to know and the constitutional foundation of information access

Freedom of speech includes the ability to **receive information needed to form, express and act upon an opinion**. The right to know is therefore a facet of Article 19(1)(a), especially where information concerns public power or democratic choice.

- In *State of Uttar Pradesh v. Raj Narain* (1975), the Supreme Court connected responsible government with the people's right to know public acts of public functionaries. The proposition supports openness, but it does not abolish every lawful claim of secrecy, privilege or national security.
- *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981) strengthened the presumption of open government and treated disclosure concerning public institutions as the norm, subject to legally justified confidentiality.
- In *Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms* (2002), the Court applied Article 19(1)(a) to a voter's right to know material information about electoral candidates so that political choice could be informed.

The **Right to Information Act, 2005** codifies and gives an institutional procedure to this constitutional principle; it did not create the Article 19(1)(a) foundation. The statutory right operates through defined public authorities, requests, exemptions, appeals and Information Commissions. It is not a constitutional command to disclose every document. National security, privacy, privileged material and other legally protected interests require clause-specific and proportionate balancing. Detailed RTI exemptions, commission powers and later statutory amendments remain in the information-commission topic.

### 3. Eight exhaustive grounds

> sovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence.

“Offensiveness”, “annoyance”, “fake news” and hurt sentiment are not free-standing grounds. The State must connect the law to the constitutional list.

### 4. Incitement and reading down

*Kedar Nath Singh* upheld former IPC section 124A only by reading it down to speech involving incitement to violence or public disorder. It did not strike the provision down. The IPC has since been repealed; BNS section 152 contains different ingredients concerning secession, armed rebellion, subversive activity, separatist feeling and threats to sovereignty, unity and integrity. It should not be described either as identical renaming or as proof that every constitutional controversy disappeared. Section 152 is itself under constitutional challenge and remained undecided as of **30 September 2026**.

In ***S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India***, the Supreme Court's interim order of **11 May 2022** kept IPC section 124A in abeyance while the Union reconsidered it: governments were expected to refrain from fresh use, and pending trials, appeals and proceedings concerning a section 124A charge were held in suspension, while proceedings on other charges could continue where no prejudice resulted. The constitutional challenge remains undecided.

A later **21 May 2026** clarification was reported as allowing a pending section 124A trial or appeal to proceed where the accused raises no objection, protecting an accused from indefinite delay. The 2022 order is official; the later relaxation is supported by contemporaneous reporting, but no official PDF of the clarification was available for verification. It must therefore be labelled a **reported partial relaxation**, not a final decision on section 124A's constitutionality.

### 5. Vagueness, overbreadth and chilling effect

*Shreya Singhal* struck down IT Act section 66A because undefined expressions such as “grossly offensive” and “annoyance” swept protected discussion and advocacy into criminal liability.

```text
DISCUSSION -------- protected
ADVOCACY ---------- protected, even if unpopular
INCITEMENT -------- may be restricted under Article 19(2)
```

The judgment upheld section 69A blocking with safeguards and read down intermediary liability under section 79. It did not invalidate every blocking power.

### 6. Hate speech: no universal statutory definition

India has no single enacted definition covering every expression described politically or socially as “hate speech”. Constitutional and statutory analysis must identify the actual words, harm, legal provision and Article 19(2) ground.

- The principal Article 19(2) route is **public order**, with **incitement to an offence** relevant where the statutory ingredients fit.
- *Shreya Singhal* protects discussion and advocacy, even when unpopular, until the constitutionally relevant threshold of incitement is reached. Mere offence or hurt sentiment is not an independent Article 19(2) ground.
- **BNS section 196** addresses promoting enmity between groups and acts prejudicial to harmony; it succeeds the field historically covered by IPC section 153A.
- **BNS section 353** concerns statements conducing to public mischief; IPC section 505 is the repealed historical reference.
- **Representation of the People Act, 1951, sections 123(3) and 123(3A)** treat specified religious, racial, caste, community or linguistic electoral appeals and promotion of enmity or hatred as corrupt practices within their precise electoral field.

These provisions are related but not interchangeable. Do not invent a general “hate-speech law”, import the RPA provisions outside elections, or attribute one universal definition to a judgment.

### 7. Internet as medium

*Anuradha Bhasin* protected speech and trade **through** the internet. It did not declare the internet a standalone Fundamental Right. The case examined orders under **section 144 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973** and internet-suspension orders under the **Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services (Public Emergency or Public Safety) Rules, 2017**. The Court required publication so that orders could be challenged, periodic review, attention to duration and proportionality, and held indefinite suspension impermissible. It did **not** quash the restrictions before it; it directed review under the governing framework.

### 8. Competing rights

*Subramanian Swamy* upheld the criminal-defamation scheme then contained in **IPC sections 499-500**, treating reputation as part of Article 21. After repeal of the IPC, the current offence is in **BNS section 356**. The criticism is that criminal process may be disproportionate where civil remedies exist. Balance cannot bypass Article 19(2): defamation is expressly listed, but restrictions must still be lawful and reasonable.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Incitement and proportionality are too weak because arrests and shutdowns chill speech before a final judgment.

**Reply:** Doctrine requires precision, published orders, review and narrow connection to an enumerated ground, enabling early constitutional challenge.

**Residual:** Enforcement gaps remain: a formally strong standard cannot prevent every unlawful prosecution or opaque restriction in real time.

### UPSC application

```text

FORM: vague penal law / blocking order / prior restraint
PROCESS: authority / reasons / publication / review / duration
```

### Concept check

**Model answer:** “Annoyance” is not an Article 19(2) ground, and its undefined breadth creates vagueness and a chilling effect by sweeping protected discussion and advocacy into criminal liability. *Shreya Singhal* supplies both objections.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying that all online regulation is unconstitutional, or that *Shreya Singhal* struck down section 69A and created a Fundamental Right to internet access.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Analyse how vagueness and procedural opacity threaten freedom of speech in India. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 19 protects speech through substantive and procedural controls. Every restriction must fit one of Article 19(2)'s eight grounds; “annoyance” or “offensiveness” cannot become a new legislative ground.

In *Shreya Singhal*, IT Act section 66A failed because vague, overbroad language lacked a manageable standard, captured discussion and advocacy, and chilled speech. The Court upheld section 69A with safeguards and read down intermediary liability, showing that precision and procedure are decisive.

“Hate speech” is not a universal statutory formula. The State must prove BNS section 196 or 353, or RPA sections 123(3)/(3A) within elections, and maintain the required public-order or incitement nexus.

Procedural opacity creates a parallel injury. *Anuradha Bhasin* required publication and periodic review of orders under section 144 CrPC and the Telecom Suspension Rules, applied proportionality, rejected indefinite suspension, but did not itself quash the restrictions before the Court. Secret or unreasoned orders prevent affected citizens from challenging authority, duration and necessity.

Opacity also burdens the Article 19(1)(a) right to know. *Raj Narain* and *S.P. Gupta* connect accountable government with public access, while *Association for Democratic Reforms* applies the principle to informed voting. The RTI Act supplies a statutory procedure subject to defined exemptions rather than an unlimited disclosure rule.

The strongest doctrine therefore joins content and process: identify the Article 19(2) ground, test precision and incitement, then examine reasons, publication, review and less restrictive alternatives. Its limitation is enforcement delay; speech may be chilled before courts correct unlawful administration.

### 1. Assembly

Article 19(1)(b) protects peaceful, unarmed assembly. Public streets and parks may be regulated for competing use and public order. Regulation of time, place and manner differs from an arbitrary prohibition.

The Article does not create a Fundamental Right to strike. Industrial action may receive statutory protection, but that source must be identified separately.

### 2. Association

Article 19(1)(c) protects formation of associations, unions and co-operative societies. The freedom includes a negative dimension against compelled membership and protects the association's basic voluntary composition.

It does not guarantee State recognition, a preferred legal status or achievement of the association's objects. Morality appears in Article 19(4), unlike Article 19(3)'s assembly clause.

### 3. Movement and residence

Movement in Article 19(1)(d) is throughout the territory of India. Article 19(1)(e) protects residence and settlement. Article 19(5) permits reasonable restrictions:

- in the interests of the general public;
- for protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.

Foreign travel belongs to personal liberty under Article 21. Restricted-area and tribal-protection measures must still satisfy legality and reasonableness.

### 4. Profession, occupation, trade and business

Article 19(1)(g) protects economic choice. Article 19(6) expressly permits:

- reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public;
- professional or technical qualifications;
- State monopoly, complete or partial.

There is no Fundamental Right to carry on an inherently harmful activity free from prohibition. The State must still classify and regulate consistently with Articles 14 and 19.

### 5. The 2022 Mains demand

The official question states:

> "Right of movement and residence throughout the territory of India are freely available to the Indian citizens, but these rights are not absolute." Comment. (Answer in 150 words)

This is directly owned by the Fundamental Rights topic. It is displayed without an answer. The required concepts are Article 19(1)(d)-(e), Article 19(5), internal versus foreign travel, legality, reasonableness and proportionality.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Broad general-public restrictions reduce the five freedoms to legislative grace.

**Reply:** The State must establish authority, a clause-specific ground, rational connection and proportionality; Article 14 additionally guards against arbitrary selection.

**Residual:** Restrictions often affect vulnerable groups unevenly, so formally neutral regulation still requires evidence-sensitive review.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Comment on the constitutional relationship between mobility, tribal protection and national integration. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Articles 19(1)(d) and 19(1)(e) allow citizens to move freely, reside and settle throughout India. They support a common citizenship and economic and social integration by preventing internal territorial barriers.

The freedoms are not absolute. Article 19(5) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public and for protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe. Protected-area rules may therefore preserve land, culture and community autonomy against disruptive settlement or exploitation. Public-health and narrowly tailored public-order measures may also fall within general-public interests.

The constitutional balance is not mobility versus tribal rights. It is integrated citizenship through differentiated protection. Restrictions require authority, evidence, rational connection and proportionality; they cannot become arbitrary exclusion. Foreign travel must be analysed separately under Article 21.

*The clauses are narrow but powerful. Each requires its own trigger.*

### 1. Article 20(1): ex-post-facto criminal punishment

No person may be:

- convicted for an act that was not an offence when committed; or
- subjected to a penalty greater than the penalty then applicable.

The prohibition concerns disadvantageous retrospective criminal law. A later beneficial criminal provision may be applied where law permits. The clause does not prohibit retrospective civil liability or taxation and does not bar the trial itself where the charged act was already an offence.

Preventive detention is preventive rather than conviction for an offence, so Article 20(1) does not apply merely because earlier conduct informs the detention decision.

### 2. Article 20(2): double jeopardy

The constitutional phrase is **“prosecuted and punished for the same offence”**. Both elements matter.

| Situation | Article 20(2) position |
|---|---|
| prior judicial prosecution and punishment for same offence | protected |
| departmental inquiry or administrative penalty | not itself prosecution by a court or judicial tribunal |
| customs confiscation followed by criminal prosecution | constitutional bar does not automatically arise |
| distinct offence arising from related facts | “same offence” must be established |

*Maqbool Hussain* and *S.A. Venkataraman* distinguish judicial prosecution from departmental or administrative proceedings. Statutory protections may be wider, but Article 20(2)'s text remains exact.

### 3. Article 20(3): testimonial compulsion

The protection requires:

1. a person accused of an offence;
2. compulsion;
3. being made a witness against oneself.

*State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* distinguishes testimonial communication based on personal knowledge from physical identification. Fingerprints, specimen signatures, handwriting, blood or bodily material are generally not testimony merely because compelled. *Nandini Satpathy* extends protection to compelled answers during investigation where they have a reasonable tendency to incriminate.

In *Selvi*, involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph examination and brain-mapping were treated as violating testimonial autonomy and personal liberty. The constitutional problem lies in compelled extraction of personal mental responses, not in every scientific investigative technique.

### 4. Documentary nuance

It is unsafe to say either “all documents are protected” or “documents are never protected”. The inquiry asks whether the accused is compelled to make a testimonial communication based on personal knowledge, as distinct from production or collection of physical material under valid law.

### 5. Non-derogable status

After the 44th Amendment, an Article 359 order cannot suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of Article 20. This is separate from the operation of Article 358, which concerns Article 19.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Excluding fingerprints and bodily samples from Article 20(3) undermines the protection.

**Reply:** The clause protects against compelled testimonial self-accusation, not every use of the body as physical evidence. Article 21, statutory procedure and privacy still regulate collection methods.

**Residual:** New neurotechnology may blur the distinction between physical measurement and extraction of mental content, requiring close constitutional scrutiny.

### UPSC application

Write each clause as **trigger -> protection -> exclusion -> case**. Do not use the broad common-law expression “double jeopardy” without reproducing “prosecuted and punished”.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Explain the constitutional boundaries of the privilege against self-incrimination under Article 20(3). (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 20(3) protects an accused person from being compelled to be a witness against oneself. Three elements must coexist: accused status, compulsion and testimonial incrimination.

The protection covers compelled oral answers and testimonial communication based on personal knowledge. *Nandini Satpathy* applies it during investigation where answers have a reasonable tendency to expose the accused to criminal liability. It is not confined to testimony at trial.

The Constitution distinguishes testimony from physical identification. In *Kathi Kalu Oghad*, fingerprints, specimen handwriting or signatures and comparable physical material were not treated as testimonial communication merely because compelled. *Selvi* drew the line differently for involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping because they extract personal mental responses and also implicate Article 21.

The doctrine therefore does not create immunity from investigation. It preserves decisional and testimonial autonomy while permitting lawfully obtained physical evidence. Collection remains controlled by fair procedure, privacy and statutory safeguards. The key is what the person is compelled to communicate, not simply whether the State uses compulsion.

```text
"No person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty
```

*Maneka did not replace the constitutional words; it changed what qualifies as constitutionally valid procedure.*

### 1. Text and beneficiary

Article 21 protects **every person**. Deprivation requires:

- law;
- a procedure created by law;
- a procedure that satisfies constitutional fairness after *Maneka Gandhi*.

Life is not limited to continued biological existence, and personal liberty is not limited to freedom from physical imprisonment.

### 2. The narrow beginning

In *A.K. Gopalan*, the Supreme Court treated Fundamental Rights as relatively separate compartments and read “procedure established by law” formally. If a competent legislature enacted a procedure, Article 21 review was limited.

This did not mean that no other Article could ever apply, but the controlling approach was substantially narrower than modern doctrine.

### 3. The shift through effect-based review

*R.C. Cooper* weakened the compartmental approach by examining the effect of State action across rights. *Maneka Gandhi* completed the transition:

- procedure cannot be arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive;
- it must be fair, just and reasonable;
- Articles 14, 19 and 21 form an interlocking framework;
- principles of natural justice may be required where liberty is affected.

### 4. Due process: use precise language

India did not simply insert the United States phrase “due process of law” into the text or adopt American substantive due process wholesale. The safe formulation is:

> Article 21's “procedure established by law” must be **fair, just and reasonable**, with the law and procedure tested through Articles 14, 19 and 21.

This imports a due-process standard without erasing textual and institutional differences.

### 5. The review sequence

```text
Is there authority of law?
        |
Is the procedure non-arbitrary under Article 14?
        |
Does it burden an Article 19 freedom, where applicable?
        |
Is the deprivation fair, just, reasonable and proportionate?
        |
Are hearing, reasons, review and safeguards adequate?
```

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** *Maneka* allowed courts to rewrite an intentionally narrower constitutional phrase.

**Reply:** A procedure that is arbitrary cannot satisfy a Constitution simultaneously guaranteeing equality and liberty. Integrated review prevents formal legislation from becoming sufficient authority for oppression.

**Residual:** Open-ended fairness review increases judicial power and requires discipline concerning text, precedent and institutional competence.

### UPSC application

The 2023 Prelims due-process demand tests the contrast between narrow formal procedure and the post-*Maneka* fair-procedure standard. The 2023 GS-II “living instrument” PYQ depends on this transition and is reproduced answer-neutrally later.

1. Article 21 protects every person.
2. Deprivation must follow procedure established by law.
3. *Gopalan* adopted a narrow and compartmental approach.
4. *R.C. Cooper* emphasised effects and overlapping rights.
5. *Maneka Gandhi* requires fair, just and reasonable procedure.
6. Articles 14, 19 and 21 operate as a golden triangle.
7. Natural justice may form part of fair procedure.
8. Legislative action as well as executive action is reviewable.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the existence of a duly enacted procedure necessary but no longer sufficient for a deprivation under Article 21?

**Model answer:** Article 21 requires legal procedure, but *Maneka Gandhi* also requires that procedure to be fair, just and reasonable. Articles 14 and, where applicable, 19 prevent a formally enacted but arbitrary or disproportionate process from validating deprivation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying that Article 21 contained the modern due-process standard unchanged from 1950, or that courts may ignore enacted text whenever a procedure seems harsh.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Maneka Gandhi transformed Article 21 without amending its text.” Analyse. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 21 states that no person may be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. In *A.K. Gopalan*, this was read formally: a validly enacted procedure substantially satisfied the Article, and Fundamental Rights were treated as separate compartments.

The transformation occurred through effects-based and integrated review. *R.C. Cooper* weakened the compartment theory. *Maneka Gandhi* then held that procedure must be fair, just and reasonable rather than arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. Articles 14, 19 and 21 became an interlocking “golden triangle”: legality alone was insufficient if the law or procedure was unequal or unreasonably burdened a protected freedom. Natural-justice safeguards could therefore be constitutionally required.

The case did not textually substitute American substantive due process. It read a due-process standard into “procedure established by law” through the Constitution's equality and liberty structure.

This made Article 21 capable of protecting dignity, privacy and fair criminal process. Its institutional cost is expanded judicial judgement over legislative reasonableness; disciplined use therefore requires text, precedent, proportionality and attention to democratic competence.

*Article 21 makes the State answerable for deprivation; it does not automatically make courts the allocator of every social good.*

### 1. Dignity as the organising idea

*Francis Coralie Mullin* states that life means more than animal existence and includes living with human dignity. The decision involved a detenu's ability to meet family and counsel, showing that lawful custody does not erase personhood.

Dignity supplies a constitutional standard. It does not itself specify a universal quantity of housing, healthcare or income.

### 2. Livelihood and fair deprivation

*Olga Tellis* recognises livelihood as part of life because survival depends on means of living. Yet the petitioners did not receive immunity from eviction. The Court upheld removal subject to authority and fair procedure.

This dual holding is exam-important:

```text
livelihood protected
        !=
permanent right to occupy any chosen public land
```

The case also rejects waiver of Fundamental Rights through a concession or undertaking.

### 3. Custody and criminal process

- *Hussainara Khatoon*: speedy trial is part of Article 21; no universal numerical deadline was created.
- *M.H. Hoskot*: free legal assistance may be required where justice demands it; Article 39A and the Legal Services Authorities Act support the institutional route.
- *D.K. Basu*: recorded officers, arrest memo, information to relatives, medical examination, diary record and access to counsel are constitutional safeguards later reflected in criminal-procedure legislation.
- *Sunil Batra*: a prisoner is not a non-person; solitary confinement and bar fetters require lawful, narrow and reviewable use.

### 4. Health and environment

*Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity* imposes a State obligation to provide timely emergency medical treatment in public hospitals. It does not create an unlimited judicially enforceable entitlement to every treatment.

*Subhash Kumar*, read with the *M.C. Mehta* line, places enjoyment of pollution-free water and air within Article 21. The environmental doctrines of absolute liability, precaution and polluter-pays belong to their specialist field; this lesson retains the rights hook.

### 5. End-of-life dignity

In ***Common Cause (A Registered Society) v. Union of India* (2018)**, a five-judge Bench recognised passive euthanasia—withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment in defined circumstances—and the validity of advance medical directives or “living wills” as part of the Article 21 right to die with dignity, subject to safeguards. Active euthanasia and assisted suicide remain legally distinct and must not be described as constitutionally authorised.

Because the 2018 procedure proved difficult to operate, a Constitution Bench order dated **24 January 2023** modified and simplified the safeguards: it streamlined the medical-board process and removed the earlier requirement of countersignature by a Judicial Magistrate of First Class. The constitutional holding remained intact while its implementation procedure changed. The 2026 GS-II question therefore requires both the 2018 recognition and the 2023 procedural modification, while its wider judicial-legislation dimension belongs in a study of the Supreme Court.

### 6. Expansion and institutional restraint

The strongest defence of expansion is that dignity would be hollow if State custody, emergency healthcare or livelihood deprivation escaped effective protection. The strongest criticism is that transforming policy needs into rights may strain budgets and separation of powers.

The principled limit is **remedial rather than distributive** enforcement: fair procedure, minimum protection, emergency response, compensation and accountable decision-making, not judicial administration of every welfare sector.

However, the holdings are usually remedial, not distributive. *Olga Tellis* did not create immunity from eviction; *Paschim Banga* did not guarantee every health service; dignity does not specify housing or expenditure levels. Statute, policy and budgets remain necessary.

Article 21 is therefore a constitutional floor of fair procedure, minimum protection, emergency response and accountability—not a judicially administered welfare budget. Its legitimacy depends on named limits, institutional restraint and complementary legislation.

### 1. Puttaswamy

In *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017), a nine-judge Bench recognised privacy as intrinsic to life and personal liberty and connected it with the freedoms and dignity of Part III. Privacy is not a new Article and is not absolute.

A restriction requires:

1. legality;
2. a legitimate State aim;
3. proportionality;
4. procedural safeguards against abuse.

### 2. Autonomy and identity

*NALSA v. Union of India* protects gender identity through dignity, equality and freedom. *Navtej Singh Johar* protects consensual adult same-sex intimacy through equality, dignity, privacy and autonomy. Choice concerning marriage and intimate association similarly draws strength from Articles 19 and 21.

Autonomy does not exclude regulation. It changes the burden: the State must justify why an intimate decision or identity classification may be overridden.

### 3. Horizontality

Direct horizontality is textually clear in Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24. Privacy and dignity often operate against private power indirectly:

- the State enacts protective law;
- courts interpret private law consistently with rights;
- public authorities must prevent or remedy private violence;
- *Vishaka* used Articles 14, 19 and 21 to create temporary workplace safeguards until legislation.

*Kaushal Kishor* contains a broader majority proposition concerning enforcement of Articles 19 and 21 against persons other than the State. It must be used cautiously, not as a claim that every private dispute is now a direct constitutional action.

### 4. Current statutory change: privacy and self-identification under Article 21

The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026 is **Act No. 3 of 2026**. Official Gazette notification **S.O. 2620(E), dated 22 May 2026**, appointed **25 May 2026** as its commencement date.

- deletion of the express statutory self-perceived-identity clause;

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Examine how privacy and autonomy should discipline State verification of personal identity. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Privacy under *Puttaswamy* protects bodily integrity, decisional autonomy and personal information. Identity verification is therefore not constitutionally neutral: it may compel disclosure, transfer decisional authority or exclude persons from legal protection.

The 2026 Transgender Amendment is operative from 25 May 2026, while the Supreme Court has issued notice and no merits determination was located by 30 September. Its constitutionality should therefore be analysed, not predicted.

The constitutional position is neither “no verification” nor “administrative convenience prevails”, but justified and safeguarded verification.

### 1. Exact text

Article 21A requires the State to provide free and compulsory education to **all children aged six to fourteen years** in the manner the State may, by law, determine.

It does not textually guarantee higher or professional education. Children below six are addressed through the recast Article 45, not Article 21A.

### 2. Judicial origin

*Mohini Jain* used dignity and equal access to describe a broad right to education. *Unni Krishnan* refined the rule: free education up to age fourteen was part of Article 21; beyond that, the claim depended on State economic capacity and development.

The 86th Amendment transformed the six-to-fourteen guarantee into express text rather than leaving it entirely to Article 21 interpretation.

### 3. The three-part amendment design

| Provision | Effect |
|---|---|
| Article 21A | free and compulsory education, ages 6-14 |
| Article 45 | early childhood care and education below age six |
| Article 51A(k) | parent/guardian duty to provide educational opportunity for child/ward aged 6-14 |

### 4. RTE Act and institutional boundaries

The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 supplies admission, school and State obligations. In *Society for Unaided Private Schools*, the Supreme Court upheld the Act's application to non-minority private unaided schools. *Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust* protected Article 30(1) minority institutions from an application that would abrogate their minority character.

This is a rights-collision problem: children's educational access and minority institutional autonomy must both be given constitutional effect.

### 5. Positive obligation and practical limit

Article 21A is expressly positive: the State must provide education. Yet quality, teacher capacity, inclusion and implementation depend on legislation, finance and administration. Courts can enforce legal duties but cannot replace the entire education system.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Limiting Article 21A to ages six to fourteen narrows the dignity-based importance of education.

**Reply:** The text defines the directly enforceable guarantee while Articles 14, 21, Directive Principles and legislation may protect adjacent educational interests.

**Residual:** Access without quality or inclusion can satisfy formal enrolment while failing the constitutional purpose of equal citizenship.

### UPSC application

Article 21A is the cleanest example of:

```text
judicial recognition -> constitutional amendment -> implementing statute
```

Do not continue citing Article 21 alone as the principal textual source for six-to-fourteen education after the 86th Amendment.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should a post-2002 answer cite Article 21A rather than rely only on Article 21 for free education between six and fourteen?

**Model answer:** The 86th Amendment converted the judicially recognised interest into an express Fundamental Right with a defined age group and a law-based implementation route. Article 21 remains the historical and dignity background, but Article 21A is the direct textual source.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Article 21A as a Directive Principle, extending it automatically to all levels of education or ignoring Article 30 minority-institution limits.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Analyse the constitutional journey of the right to education from Article 21 interpretation to Article 21A. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

The original Constitution placed free and compulsory education in Article 45 as a Directive Principle. Judicial interpretation later connected education with dignity and effective liberty. *Mohini Jain* stated the right broadly; *Unni Krishnan* refined it by recognising free education up to age fourteen under Article 21 and treating later education as dependent on State capacity.

Article 45 was recast for early childhood care below six, and Article 51A(k) added a parental duty.

The RTE Act, 2009 supplied the implementing framework. *Society for Unaided Private Schools* upheld its application to non-minority unaided schools, while *Pramati* protected Article 30(1) minority institutions from rights-abrogating application.

The journey shows interaction among adjudication, amendment and legislation. Its remaining challenge is substantive: enrolment must be converted into quality, inclusion and equal educational capability without erasing minority autonomy.

### 1. Article 22(1)

An arrested person must:

- be informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds of arrest;
- not be denied the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of choice.

Information enables challenge; a bare arrest without intelligible grounds prevents meaningful legal defence.

### 2. Article 22(2)

Every arrested and detained person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within twenty-four hours, **excluding the time necessary for the journey** from the place of arrest to the court. Further detention requires magistrate authority.

The magistrate is not a clerical relay. Judicial scrutiny must examine legal grounds and the need for custody.

### 3. Constitutional exclusions

Clauses (1) and (2) do not apply to:

- an enemy alien;
- a person arrested or detained under preventive-detention law.

Preventive detention has its own safeguards in clauses (4)-(7). Exclusion from clauses (1)-(2) does not place the person outside Articles 14 and 21 or outside statutory procedure.

### 4. Police and judicial custody

| Police custody | Judicial custody |
|---|---|
| physical custody of police for authorised investigation | detention under magistrate's authority, ordinarily in jail |
| direct interrogation subject to law and safeguards | police require lawful permission for further custodial interrogation |
| not identical to arrest itself | not proof of guilt |

The 2021 Prelims demand tested this distinction. No official answer key has been verified for this historical question, so no answer letter is inferred.

### 5. D.K. Basu safeguards

*D.K. Basu* treated custodial violence and secret arrest as Article 21 injuries. Its requirements include identifiable arresting officers, an arrest memo, information to a friend or relative, diary entry, medical examination and access to counsel during interrogation at permitted stages. They were judicial safeguards later reflected in criminal-procedure legislation, now including the BNSS framework.

Do not quote an uncertain fixed number of guidelines or describe the judgment itself as a statute.

### 6. Parole boundary

Parole is conditional release governed by prison law, rules and State policy. It is not, merely by name, a Fundamental Right. A decision may still be reviewed for arbitrariness, irrelevant considerations or breach of law. Parole differs from furlough and from bail.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Judicial production within twenty-four hours is too weak where police can create paperwork after abuse.

**Reply:** Article 22 works with Article 21, *D.K. Basu*, statutory arrest records, medical examination, counsel, judicial scrutiny and public-law compensation.

**Residual:** Safeguards fail when magistrate review becomes routine or detainees lack practical access to counsel and family.

### UPSC application

Separate constitutional text, judicial safeguards and statutory custody rules. A precise answer should not place every arrest rule inside Article 22 itself.

### 1. The constitutional anomaly

Preventive detention permits deprivation of liberty without criminal trial. India places it within the Fundamental Rights chapter but surrounds it with procedural limits. The structure reflects a security power accepted by the Constitution and a simultaneous distrust of uncontrolled executive custody.

### 2. The operative three-month rule

Current Article 22(4) permits detention beyond **three months** only where:

- an Advisory Board reports sufficient cause before expiry of that period; or
- the person is detained under a parliamentary law made within Article 22(7)'s specified field.

The Advisory Board consists of persons who are, have been, or are qualified to be appointed as High Court judges. It is inaccurate to reduce the text to “a board of serving High Court judges”.

### 3. The uncommenced 44th-Amendment substitution

The 44th Amendment enacted a substitute two-month design with a differently constituted Board. The official constitutional text records that the substitution's commencement date remains to be notified. Therefore:

```text
OPERATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL RULE: three months
```

Do not teach the two-month rule as current law.

### 4. Grounds and representation

The detaining authority must:

- communicate grounds as soon as may be;
- afford the earliest opportunity to make a representation.

Facts considered contrary to public interest may be withheld under Article 22(6). This exception cannot justify non-communication so broad that representation becomes meaningless.

### 5. Parliament's Article 22(7) role

Parliament may prescribe:

- circumstances and classes in which detention beyond three months may occur without the ordinary Board opinion;
- maximum detention periods;
- Advisory Board procedure.

Legislative competence is distributed separately:

- Parliament: defence, foreign affairs and security of India;
- Parliament and States: security of a State, public order and essential supplies/services.

### 6. Statutory precision

Safe direct examples include the National Security Act, 1980 and COFEPOSA, 1974, alongside historical preventive-detention enactments such as MISA. Do **not** classify UAPA, TADA or POTA as interchangeable preventive-detention statutes merely because they concern security or permit stringent custody.

### 7. Criticism and defence

**Criticism:** The power reverses ordinary criminal justice by permitting custody on suspicion, encourages vague grounds and weakens open adjudication.

**Defence:** Exceptional threats may require prevention where disclosure or waiting for an offence would defeat public protection; the Constitution therefore provides legislative and procedural controls.

**Residual:** Advisory review and representation are weaker than trial, so strict scrutiny of grounds, delay, purpose and statutory compliance remains essential.

### UPSC application

Answer in the sequence **purpose -> operative text -> safeguards -> uncommenced amendment -> federal competence -> critique**. Do not place preventive detention under Emergency provisions; it exists in ordinary constitutional time.

### Revision notes

1. Preventive detention aims at anticipated conduct, not punishment.
2. Article 22(1)-(2) do not apply to preventive detention.
3. Articles 14 and 21 remain relevant.
4. The operative no-Board period is three months.
5. Board members may be current, former or qualified High Court judges.
6. The two-month 44th-Amendment substitution is uncommenced.
7. Grounds must be communicated as soon as may be.
8. Earliest representation opportunity is required.
9. Public-interest facts may be withheld, not every intelligible ground.
10. Article 22(7) grants Parliament specified powers.
11. Legislative competence is split by subject.
12. Do not misclassify UAPA, TADA or POTA as ordinary examples of Article 22 preventive-detention law.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “the 44th Amendment reduced preventive detention without Advisory Board review to two months” legally incomplete?

**Model answer:** The Amendment enacted that substitution, but the relevant provision was never brought into force. The operative constitutional text still uses three months. Enactment and commencement must be distinguished.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading an uncommenced amendment clause as current law or placing preventive detention only within a declared Emergency.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Preventive detention is constitutionally authorised but constitutionally distrusted.” Discuss. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 22 authorises preventive detention to avert anticipated prejudicial conduct rather than punish a proved past offence. Its inclusion recognises security, public-order and essential-supply concerns that may not fit ordinary criminal prosecution.

The same Article displays constitutional distrust. The operative rule permits detention without Advisory Board approval for only three months. Beyond that, a Board of persons who are, have been or are qualified to be High Court judges must find sufficient cause, subject to Parliament's Article 22(7) powers. Grounds must be communicated as soon as may be and the detainee must receive the earliest opportunity to represent, although public-interest facts may be withheld.

The 44th Amendment's proposed two-month substitution never commenced; three months remains the current rule. Legislative competence is also divided by security subject.

The safeguards remain weaker than trial: detention rests on executive satisfaction, disclosure may be limited and Advisory review is not open adjudication. Strict control of purpose, relevant material, delay and statutory compliance is therefore indispensable. The Constitution accepts prevention as an exception, not as a substitute criminal-justice system.

### Visual: coercion is wider than physical force

```text

traffic in human beings
        +
begar: compelled work without payment
        +

        |
        +--> physical force
```

### 1. Beneficiary and respondent

Article 23 protects **all persons** and applies against the State and private actors. Trafficking and forced labour frequently arise through private economic and social power, so a State-only interpretation would defeat the provision.

Contravention is constitutionally declared an offence punishable by law.

### 2. Trafficking and begar

Traffic in human beings covers treating persons as objects of sale, purchase or exploitation. Begar is compelled labour without remuneration. “Other similar forms” ensures that constitutional protection is not defeated by changing the label of coercion.

### 3. Economic compulsion

In *People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India*, payment below the statutory minimum wage was treated as forced labour where economic necessity deprived workers of meaningful choice. *Sanjit Roy* rejected payment below minimum wage even in famine-relief work.

This does not mean that every poorly paid or unequal bargain automatically violates Article 23. The constitutional question concerns compulsion and the legally protected minimum.

### 4. Bonded labour and positive duty

*Bandhua Mukti Morcha* demonstrates the State's positive duty to identify, release and rehabilitate bonded labourers. Non-enforcement of protective legislation can itself leave Article 23 ineffective.

Relevant statutes include the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 and anti-trafficking and wage legislation. Detailed labour administration remains with its specialist field.

### 5. Compulsory service

Article 23(2) permits compulsory service for public purposes. The State is not constitutionally required by this clause to pay for every such service, but it cannot discriminate only on religion, race, caste or class.

The exception should be read narrowly: ordinary exploitation cannot be relabelled “public service”.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Extending “force” to economic compulsion converts every unequal labour market into a constitutional case.

**Reply:** The doctrine uses objective legal anchors such as statutory minimum wage, bonded obligation and absence of meaningful choice; it does not invalidate every hard bargain.

**Residual:** Informality, migrant vulnerability and weak inspection make proof and enforcement difficult even where the constitutional rule is clear.

### UPSC application

Article 23 is a classic example of both **horizontal effect** and **positive obligation**. Use the private exploiter, the worker and the State's enforcement duty as three separate analytical actors.

### Revision notes

1. Article 23 protects all persons.
2. It reaches State and private conduct.
3. Trafficking treats persons as objects of exploitation.
4. Begar is compelled unpaid labour.
5. Forced labour includes more than physical force.
6. *PUDR* recognises economic compulsion and minimum-wage relevance.
7. *Sanjit Roy* applies the principle to relief work.
8. *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* emphasises identification, release and rehabilitation.
9. Contravention is an offence.
10. Compulsory public service is permitted.
11. Such service cannot discriminate only on religion, race, caste or class.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can labour accepted because of extreme economic necessity still be constitutionally “forced”?

**Model answer:** Where a worker has no meaningful choice and is paid below a legally guaranteed minimum, economic compulsion may function as force. Article 23 examines real coercion, not only formal consent.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every unequal wage as forced labour or assuming that private employers fall outside Fundamental Rights entirely.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Explain the horizontal and positive-obligation dimensions of Article 23. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 23 prohibits trafficking, begar and similar forced labour against all persons. Its horizontal dimension is explicit: private employers, traffickers or dominant social actors can violate it; the claimant need not identify State action as the immediate coercion.

Its positive dimension follows from enforceability. In *PUDR*, economic compulsion and payment below the statutory minimum were treated as forced labour. *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* required identification, release and rehabilitation of bonded labourers. The State must therefore enact, inspect and enforce protective law rather than remain neutral toward private exploitation.

Article 23(2) permits compulsory service for public purposes but prohibits discrimination only on religion, race, caste or class. This exception cannot disguise ordinary exploitation.

The provision transforms freedom from coercion into a duty to control private power. Its persistent weakness lies in informal work, weak inspection and barriers to reporting.

The constitutional rights perform different tasks:

- Article 24: prohibited employment;
- Article 21A: education;
- Article 23: coercion and forced labour;
- Articles 14-15: equal protection and non-discrimination.

**Objection:** Family-work exceptions may preserve exploitation behind household or cultural language.

**Reply:** Statutory exceptions remain subject to strict interpretation, education requirements, hazardous-work prohibitions and constitutional review.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Distinguish the constitutional and statutory approaches to child labour in India. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 24 creates a constitutional floor: no child below fourteen shall be “employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment”. It applies against public and private employers but does not itself contain the entire contemporary labour code.

The Child and Adolescent Labour legislation goes further. It uses statutory age categories, broadly prohibits child employment subject to defined exceptions, and separately regulates hazardous work by adolescents. Constitutional silence on a work category therefore does not establish its legality.

Judicial protection also extends beyond prohibition. *M.C. Mehta* connected hazardous-child-labour enforcement with compensation, rehabilitation, education and family support. Article 21A reinforces this design by guaranteeing education from six to fourteen, while Article 23 addresses forced or bonded labour.

The effective approach is integrated: prohibition, schooling, rehabilitation and household livelihood support. Enforcement fails when removal merely shifts children into less visible work.

### Visual: three constitutional zones


### 1. Article 25 right-holders and content

Article 25 protects **all persons**. It includes:

- freedom of conscience;
- profession of religion;
- practice of religion;
- propagation of religion.

Conscience protects the inner freedom to hold, change or reject belief. Profession concerns open declaration; practice concerns observance; propagation concerns transmission of belief.

### 2. Propagation and conversion

In *Rev. Stainislaus*, the Supreme Court held that propagation does not include a right to convert another person through force, fraud or improper inducement, because the other person's freedom of conscience is equally protected.

This does not turn every voluntary conversion into a constitutional wrong. The legal question is the nature of consent and the precision of regulation.

### 3. Article 25 limits and reform

Article 25 is expressly subject to:

- public order;
- morality;
- health;
- other provisions of Part III.

Article 25(2) allows regulation of economic, financial, political and other secular activity associated with religion, as well as social welfare and reform. It also permits opening public Hindu religious institutions to all classes and sections; the constitutional explanation includes Sikh, Jain and Buddhist persons for this purpose. Carrying the kirpan is included in profession of the Sikh religion.

### 4. Article 26 and denomination

A religious denomination or section has rights to:

1. establish and maintain religious and charitable institutions;
2. manage its own affairs in matters of religion;
3. own and acquire property;
4. administer property in accordance with law.

The commonly used denominational indicators are a common faith, common organisation and distinctive name. *S.P. Mittal* illustrates the need to establish genuinely religious denominational character rather than merely philosophical or organisational identity.

### 5. Article 26 regulation and the Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025

The Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025 supplies a bounded example of statutory regulation of religious-property administration under Article 26 read with equality. Relevant changes included:

- removal of the statutory category commonly described as **“waqf by user”**;
- a requirement that a person creating a waqf must have practised Islam for at least five years;
- a designated **Collector-level officer** role in deciding whether disputed waqf property was government land;
- provision for non-Muslim members on the Central Waqf Council and State Waqf Boards.

Petitions challenged provisions under **Articles 14, 15, 25 and 26**, raising equality, non-discrimination, conscience, denominational-administration and separation-of-powers concerns.

By its **interim order of 15 September 2025**, the Supreme Court declined to stay the Act as a whole, but:

1. stayed the five-year practising-Muslim requirement pending the framing of rules;
2. stayed the Collector's adjudicatory sub-provisions, leaving property-status adjudication to the statutory tribunal/courts;
3. capped non-Muslim membership rather than invalidating it: not more than **4 of 22** members of the Central Waqf Council and **3 of 11** members of a State Waqf Board;
4. extended specified registration timelines.

No final judgment on the Act's constitutional validity was located as of **30 September 2026**. The interim order must not be described as either upholding or striking down the Act, and no conclusion should be drawn about a particular property.

### 6. Essential Religious Practices doctrine

*Shirur Mutt* distinguished protected religious affairs from secular activity and connected essentiality to the religion's own doctrines. Later adjudication has often required courts to determine whether a claimed practice is essential.

**Criticism:** Secular judges may become theological authorities and freeze internal religious diversity.

**Reply:** Without a threshold, any practice could be immunised from regulation merely by religious assertion.

**Residual:** Rights-conflict and proportionality analysis may sometimes be clearer than a single essentiality inquiry.

### 7. Sabarimala: equality, religious freedom and constitutional morality

In *Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (2018), the majority tested the exclusion of women aged ten to fifty from the Sabarimala temple against Articles 14, 25 and 26. It held the exclusion unconstitutional and did not accept the devotees as a separate religious denomination entitled to sustain the exclusion under Article 26. The case therefore applies the Essential Religious Practices inquiry within a wider conflict among individual religious freedom, equality and claimed denominational autonomy.

**Constitutional morality** supplied a competing standard: public power and legally protected institutions must remain faithful to equal citizenship, dignity and the Constitution rather than rely only on social or religious majoritarian morality. The dissent cautioned that courts should ordinarily respect questions of religious belief unless a practice is socially pernicious or constitutionally prohibited.

**Status discipline:** Review proceedings produced a reference of broader questions concerning religious freedom to a nine-judge Bench. Official Supreme Court cause lists showed further arguments in April-May 2026; no final nine-judge opinion disposing of the reference was located as of 30 September 2026. The 2018 majority judgment must not be described as overruled, and the eventual outcome must not be predicted.

| all persons | religious denomination or section |
| individual conscience and religious activity | collective institutional autonomy |

**Question:** Why can a practice accepted as religious still be regulated under Article 25?

**Model answer:** Article 25 is qualified by public order, morality, health, other Part III rights and Article 25(2)'s secular-regulation and social-reform powers. Religious character is relevant but not an automatic immunity.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Critically examine the constitutional role of the Essential Religious Practices doctrine. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Articles 25 and 26 protect individual conscience and denominational autonomy while permitting public-order, health, morality, secular-regulation and reform measures. The Essential Religious Practices doctrine developed to separate protected religious affairs from regulable secular activity.

In *Shirur Mutt*, the Court stated that essentiality should be understood with reference to the religion's doctrines. The test prevents every asserted custom from acquiring constitutional immunity and preserves a domain of denominational self-government. In *Indian Young Lawyers Association* (2018), the Sabarimala exclusion was also tested against Articles 14 and 25 and constitutional morality, showing that essentiality does not exhaust a rights conflict.

The Waqf interim order illustrates a neighbouring point: Article 26 administration may be regulated by statute, but equality, institutional competence and separation-of-powers objections remain independently reviewable without converting an interim order into a merits holding.

Its difficulty is institutional. Courts may become arbiters of theology, privilege formal texts over lived diversity and decide which version of a faith is authentic. Essentiality may also obscure the actual constitutional issue: whether equality, dignity, public order or reform justifies a proportionate restriction.

The doctrine remains useful as a threshold but should not become the entire analysis. A sound approach identifies the claimant, distinguishes religious from secular activity, states the Article's exact limitations, considers other Fundamental Rights and applies proportionality. Religious autonomy deserves protection, but neither assertion nor judicial theology should replace constitutional reasoning.

### 1. Article 27

No person may be compelled to pay a tax whose proceeds are **specifically appropriated** for promotion or maintenance of a particular religion or denomination.

Three qualifications matter:

1. the Article concerns a **tax**, not every fee;
2. the appropriation must be for a **particular** religion or denomination;
3. general public expenditure affecting religious sites, security or services is not automatically religious promotion.

A regulatory fee may fund administration or a service where a reasonable relationship exists. Calling a levy a “fee” is not conclusive; constitutional analysis examines its character.

### 2. Article 28

Article 28 distinguishes institutions by their relation to the State.

- Wholly State-funded institutions cannot provide religious instruction.
- State-administered institutions established under a trust or endowment requiring instruction are exempt from that prohibition.
- In recognised or aided institutions, no person may be compelled to attend religious instruction or worship without consent; for a minor, guardian consent is needed.

The text regulates **instruction and compelled attendance**. Academic study of religion, history or philosophy is not necessarily religious instruction.

### 3. Indian secularism

The constitutional model does not require complete public invisibility of religion. It combines:

- freedom of conscience;
- denominational autonomy;
- no earmarked religious taxation;
- differentiated rules for education;
- State regulation of secular activity;
- social reform and equal access.

This is often described as **principled distance**: the State may engage, regulate, reform or accommodate for constitutional reasons rather than maintain one mechanically uniform distance.

### 4. Static statutory illustration: Places of Worship Act, 1991

The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991 gives statutory form to a secular commitment to prevent present legal conflict from repeatedly reopening the religious character of places of worship.

- **Section 3:** no person may convert a place of worship of one religious denomination or section into a place of worship of a different section of the same denomination, a different denomination, or a section of a different denomination.
- **Section 4(1):** the religious character of a place of worship as it existed on **15 August 1947** shall continue to be the same.
- **Section 4(2):** pending suits, appeals and other proceedings concerning conversion of religious character abate, and no new proceeding lies, subject to the statutory proviso for a post-cut-off conversion requiring disposal according to Section 4(1).
- **Section 4(3):** Sections 4(1)-(2) do not apply to specified categories, including places covered by the **Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958 or any other law for the time being in force** governing ancient and historical monuments or archaeological sites/remains; matters finally decided before commencement; disputes settled before commencement; pre-commencement conversions accepted by acquiescence; and pre-commencement conversions no longer challengeable because limitation had expired.
- **Section 5:** the Act does not apply to the **Ram Janmabhoomi-Babri Masjid** place of worship at Ayodhya or to a suit, appeal or other proceeding relating to it.

Sections 3 and 4 perform different work: Section 3 prohibits conversion, while Section 4 preserves the cut-off character and controls proceedings. The freeze concerns **religious character**; it should not be overstated as deciding every ownership, title or property question. Ayodhya is an express statutory exclusion, so the 2019 Ayodhya outcome cannot be generalised to other places of worship through Section 5.

**Status discipline:** Constitutional challenges remain pending. The Supreme Court's order dated **12 December 2024** directed that no fresh religious-character suits be registered and restricted effective interim or final orders, including survey orders, in pending matters until further orders. No final merits judgment on the Act's validity was located as of **30 September 2026**. The Act must therefore not be described as upheld, struck down or diluted.

In the **Ayodhya judgment (2019)**, the Constitution Bench referred approvingly to the Act's constitutional purpose as a legislative affirmation of the equality of all faiths and of secularism, a basic feature. That discussion supports the Act's secular purpose; it was **not a holding adjudicating the Act's constitutional validity**. Purpose approval and validity review must remain separate.

### 5. Comparative boundary

The 2019 GS-II question asks:

> What can France learn from the Indian Constitution's approach to secularism?

This is Question 5 for 10 marks. Its 150-word limit comes from the paper's general instruction block rather than the printed English question. The complete India-France comparison is treated in Comparative Constitutional Schemes. This lesson supplies the Articles 25-28 constitutional engine only. France's laicite and its institutional history must not be reduced to a slogan of hostility to religion.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** State engagement with religion inevitably creates favouritism.

**Reply:** Articles 25-28 subject engagement to equality, conscience, reform, non-compulsion and no particular-religion tax appropriation. Constitutional purpose, not contact alone, is decisive.

**Residual:** Selective intervention or inconsistent accommodation can still appear partisan and must be justified transparently.

### UPSC application

Do not answer secularism only through the Preamble. Use the operational code: Articles 14-17 and 25-28, then explain how each controls a different State-religion relationship.

### Revision notes

1. Article 27 protects every person.
2. It concerns taxes specifically appropriated for a particular religion.
3. It does not automatically prohibit regulatory fees.
4. General public expenditure is not necessarily religious promotion.
5. Article 28(1) governs wholly State-maintained institutions.
6. Article 28(2) creates the trust/endowment exception.
7. Article 28(3) requires consent in recognised or aided institutions.
8. Guardian consent applies for minors.
9. Academic teaching about religion is not automatically religious instruction.
10. Indian secularism combines protection, regulation and reform.
11. Section 3 bars conversion between religious denominations or their sections.
12. Section 4(1) preserves religious character as on 15 August 1947; Section 4(2) abates and bars proceedings subject to its proviso.
13. Section 4(3) contains exclusions, including ancient monuments/sites governed by the 1958 Act and specified settled, accepted or time-barred matters.
14. Section 5 expressly excludes the Ram Janmabhoomi-Babri Masjid place at Ayodhya; the exclusion cannot be generalised.
15. Ayodhya (2019) approved the Act's equality-of-faiths/secular purpose, not its validity; challenges remain pending under the 12 December 2024 interim regime.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a regulatory fee for services at a pilgrimage site survive Article 27 when an earmarked tax promoting that religion may not?

**Model answer:** Article 27 targets compulsory taxes specifically appropriated for promotion or maintenance of a particular religion. A genuine fee finances regulation or a service and has a different legal character, though its label is not conclusive.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying Article 27 prohibits every public rupee connected with religion or that every State-aided institution must prohibit religious instruction.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Explain how Articles 27 and 28 operationalise Indian secularism without imposing an absolute wall of separation. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 27 prevents compulsory taxation specifically appropriated to promote or maintain a particular religion. It thereby checks fiscal establishment without prohibiting genuine regulatory fees or general public expenditure for safety and services.

Article 28 adopts a differentiated educational design. Religious instruction is prohibited in institutions wholly maintained by the State, permitted in State-administered trust or endowment institutions, and allowed on a voluntary basis in recognised or aided institutions. Consent protects individual conscience.

Together, the Articles avoid both State-sponsored religion and mechanical exclusion of religion from every public setting. Read with Articles 25-26 and the reform power in Article 25(2), they support principled distance: engagement is constitutional when justified by equality, conscience, regulation or reform.

The model's risk is inconsistent or partisan engagement. Transparency, equal treatment and non-compulsion therefore determine whether accommodation remains secular.

The right includes meaningful choice over institution, governing body, staff, admissions and educational character, subject to valid regulation.

**Reply:** Standards, transparency and non-exploitative regulation remain valid. Article 30 protects plural educational agency, not maladministration.

**Residual:** The boundary between regulatory standards and administrative takeover remains fact-sensitive, especially in professional education.

**Question:** Can a linguistic majority in India invoke Article 29(1) to conserve a distinct script used by only one regional section?

**Model answer:** Yes, if the claim is made by a section of citizens possessing a distinct language, script or culture. Article 29(1)'s text is not confined to numerical minorities.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Article 30 protects autonomy, not isolation from regulation.” Discuss. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 30 allows religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. The guarantee preserves pluralism by enabling communities to shape institutional character, governing structures, staff and educational priorities.

Autonomy is not immunity. *St. Xavier's* and *T.M.A. Pai* permit regulation directed to academic standards, teacher qualifications, sanitation, financial transparency and educational excellence. *Malankara Syrian Catholic College* emphasises that there is no right to maladminister. In professional education, *P.A. Inamdar* protects unaided institutional autonomy while accepting fair, transparent and non-exploitative regulation.

The constitutional line is functional: regulation may improve education and prevent abuse, but cannot transfer core administration or erase minority character. Articles 15(5) and 15(6) expressly exclude Article 30(1) institutions, while *Pramati* protects them from an RTE application that would abrogate the right.

Article 30 therefore creates regulated autonomy. The difficult task is to distinguish genuine standards from disguised takeover through close examination of a measure's effect.

## Lesson 23 - Article 32 and the five constitutional writs

Progress: 23/25 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: guaranteed access, appropriate relief, writ scope and Article 226 comparison

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried - Article 32 text, local books and bounded Supreme/High Court material
CA search: "Not required - static constitutional doctrine"
CA found: None required
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: choose the remedy by the legal wrong

```text
UNLAWFUL DETENTION? ---------------- HABEAS CORPUS
PUBLIC DUTY NOT PERFORMED? ---------- MANDAMUS
LOWER COURT ABOUT TO EXCEED POWER? -- PROHIBITION
ORDER ALREADY MADE WITHOUT POWER? --- CERTIORARI
UNLAWFUL OCCUPATION OF PUBLIC OFFICE? QUO WARRANTO
```

*The writ follows the defect; it is not chosen merely because its Latin name is remembered.*

### 1. Article 32's four clauses

Article 32:

1. guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court through appropriate proceedings for enforcement of Part III rights;
2. empowers the Court to issue directions, orders and writs;
3. allows Parliament to empower other courts within local limits without prejudicing Supreme Court power;
4. protects the right from suspension except as otherwise provided by the Constitution.

Ambedkar called Article 32 the Constitution's heart and soul. Judicial review and effective rights enforcement form part of the basic structure.

### 2. Guaranteed access does not mean automatic requested relief

Where a genuine Part III violation is shown, Article 32 supplies guaranteed constitutional access. The Court still determines:

- whether the claimant has established a violation;
- which proceeding and remedy are appropriate;
- whether disputed facts require another procedural route;
- the scope of declaration, compensation, injunction or continuing supervision.

It is therefore too categorical to say the Supreme Court must grant every writ or every form of relief requested.

### 3. Five writs

| Writ | Principal function | Core limits |
|---|---|---|
| Habeas corpus | release from unlawful detention | may reach public or private detention; fails where custody is presently lawful under competent authority |
| Mandamus | compel performance of public duty | not ordinarily for purely private duty, discretionary choice or private contract |
| Prohibition | prevent lower judicial/quasi-judicial body exceeding jurisdiction | preventive; issued before impugned proceeding concludes |
| Certiorari | quash jurisdictionally defective or procedurally unlawful public-law decision | curative; modern reach includes administrative decisions affecting rights |
| Quo warranto | test legal authority to hold substantive public office | office must be public and legally constituted; relator need not show personal injury |

### 4. Article 32 versus Article 226

| Article 32 | Article 226 |
|---|---|
| Supreme Court | High Court |
| enforcement of Fundamental Rights | Fundamental Rights and “any other purpose” |
| right to approach guaranteed by Part III | discretionary public-law jurisdiction |
| nationwide constitutional court | territorial/cause-of-action limits |
| public and private detention can attract habeas corpus | may also reach non-State public duty |

Article 226 is wider in subject matter; Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right.

### 5. Standing and public interest

Relaxed standing allowed letters and representative petitions where prisoners, bonded labourers or marginalised persons could not realistically approach the Court. That access should not erase evidentiary discipline or turn PIL into private, political or publicity litigation.

Institutional PIL history and court administration remain with the judiciary topic; this lesson retains the enforcement principle.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Expansive writ jurisdiction converts constitutional courts into administrators.

**Reply:** Effective relief may require guidelines, compensation or monitoring where ordinary remedies fail and continuing State violation persists.

**Residual:** Courts must distinguish rights enforcement from permanent policy management and preserve reasons, evidence and institutional exit.

### UPSC application

The 2022 Prelims demand on mandamus and quo warranto tests that both Supreme Court and High Courts can issue them, while Article 226 is wider in subject matter. No historical key is inferred.

### Revision notes

1. Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for Part III enforcement.
2. The Court may issue directions, orders and writs.
3. Parliament may empower other courts under Article 32(3).
4. Article 32 concerns Fundamental Rights.
5. Appropriate access does not guarantee every requested relief.
6. Habeas corpus can reach private detention.
7. Mandamus enforces public duty.
8. Prohibition is preventive.
9. Certiorari is curative and can reach administrative public-law decisions.
10. Quo warranto tests a public office claim.
11. Article 226 also protects ordinary legal rights.
12. High Court territorial limits differ from Supreme Court reach.
13. Relaxed standing improves access but does not remove proof and good faith.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can Article 32 be a guaranteed Fundamental Right while the Supreme Court still refuses the particular writ requested?

**Model answer:** The guarantee is access to appropriate proceedings for Part III enforcement. The Court must still determine whether a violation exists and which remedy is legally appropriate. Guaranteed access is not automatic entitlement to a chosen writ.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying Article 32 covers every legal right, or that Article 226 applies only when a Fundamental Right is violated.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Evaluate Article 32 as both a Fundamental Right and an institutional power. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 32 is unusual because it constitutionalises both access and judicial power. Clause (1) guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Part III; clause (2) authorises directions, orders and writs. Rights therefore do not depend entirely on legislative willingness or appellate delay.

The writs match distinct wrongs: habeas corpus addresses unlawful detention; mandamus public duty; prohibition threatened jurisdictional excess; certiorari a completed defective decision; quo warranto unlawful occupation of public office. Relaxed standing has allowed prisoners and bonded labourers meaningful access.

The guarantee is not overcategorical. A claimant must establish a Part III violation, and the Court selects appropriate proceedings and relief. Article 226 remains wider in subject matter because High Courts may act for Fundamental Rights and any other purpose, including certain public duties of non-State bodies.

Article 32 is consequently both the enforcement engine and a source of judicial responsibility. Effective relief may require compensation or monitoring, but courts must distinguish constitutional correction from permanent administration.

## Lesson 24 - Saving clauses and special Part III competence

### Visual: four different constitutional techniques

```text
SAVING SPECIFIED LAWS
Art 31A -> named categories, principally estate/property restructuring
Art 31B -> Ninth Schedule protection, subject to basic-structure review
Art 31C -> laws genuinely implementing Article 39(b)-(c), present valid scope

MODIFYING RIGHTS FOR DISCIPLINE

```

### 1. Article 31A

Article 31A protects specified categories of laws—historically centred on agrarian reform and restructuring of property or corporate management—from invalidity under Articles 14 and 19. A State law seeking Article 31A protection must have been reserved for the President's consideration and must have received presidential assent.

It is not a general immunity for every economic law.

### 2. Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule

Article 31B protects Acts and Regulations placed in the Ninth Schedule from Part III invalidity. Yet *I.R. Coelho* holds that insertions made after **24 April 1973**, the date of *Kesavananda Bharati*, remain open to basic-structure review where their impact damages protected constitutional principles.

The correct proposition is neither “Ninth Schedule is fully immune” nor “Article 31B has disappeared”.

### 3. Article 31C

Article 31C was inserted for laws implementing Article 39(b)-(c). The no-review declaration clause was struck down in *Kesavananda Bharati*. The 42nd Amendment attempted to extend protection to all Directive Principles; *Minerva Mills* invalidated that extension. The surviving scope is tied to Article 39(b)-(c).

*Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* confirms that not every private asset automatically becomes a “material resource of the community”; context and statutory purpose matter.

### 4. Article 33

Parliament may determine the extent to which Part III rights are restricted or abrogated for:

- members of the Armed Forces;
- members of forces charged with maintaining public order;
- persons in State intelligence or counter-intelligence organisations;
- persons employed in connected telecommunications systems.

The purpose is proper discharge of duty and discipline. Parliament, not State legislatures, exercises this power.

Parliament has used enabling statutes including the **Army Act, 1950**, **Navy Act, 1957**, **Air Force Act, 1950** and **Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1966**. Restrictions depend on the applicable law and service relationship; Article 33 does not itself erase every right of every person connected with security work.

Service legislation may extend disciplined-force rules to specified non-combatant personnel attached to or accompanying a force, including support staff such as cooks or barbers where the statute makes them subject to service law. This bounded reach does not convert unrelated civilian employees into members of the Armed Forces.

Court-martial decisions are subject to a limited constitutional review for jurisdictional error, mala fides, natural-justice failure or fundamental illegality. Article 33 does not create a complete zone beyond judicial review.

### 5. Article 34

**Martial law** is an exceptional form of temporary military authority used to maintain or restore order in a particular area when ordinary civil authority has broken down. The Constitution does not define it or provide a general proclamation procedure. It is distinct from a National Emergency under Article 352 and does not, merely by existing, extinguish every judicial remedy.

Article 34 does **not** proclaim martial law and does not itself impose a blanket suspension of Fundamental Rights. It allows Parliament to:

- indemnify public servants or other persons for acts connected with maintenance or restoration of order in an area where martial law was in force;
- validate sentences, punishments, forfeitures or other acts done under martial law.

The Article is therefore an indemnity and validation provision operating after or in connection with acts under martial law, not an independent source of military government.

### 6. Article 35

Parliament has exclusive legislative power concerning:

- residence requirements under Article 16(3);
- empowerment of other courts under Article 32(3);
- Articles 33 and 34;
- punishment for acts declared offences under Part III.

Pre-Constitution law in those fields continues subject to parliamentary alteration and constitutional adaptation. Article 35 ensures national uniformity in specified rights matters; it is not a general claim that Parliament alone implements every Fundamental Right.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Saving clauses contradict judicial review and place selected laws above rights.

**Reply:** They are themselves constitutional provisions with defined fields, and basic-structure review prevents their use to destroy constitutional identity.

**Residual:** The breadth of protected categories and judicial evaluation of purpose can generate difficult separation-of-powers disputes.

### UPSC application

Keep the techniques separate:

```text
31A/31B/31C = saving laws
33 = modifying application to specified personnel
34 = indemnity/validation connected with martial law
35 = exclusive parliamentary competence
```

### Revision notes

1. Article 31A protects specified law categories, not all economic legislation.
2. Article 31B operates through the Ninth Schedule.
3. Post-24 April 1973 insertions face *I.R. Coelho* basic-structure review.
4. Article 31C presently protects Article 39(b)-(c) implementing laws.
5. *Minerva Mills* invalidated extension to all Directive Principles.
6. Not every private asset is automatically an Article 39(b) community resource.
7. Article 33 lists four personnel categories.
8. Parliament alone uses Article 33.
9. Army Act 1950, Navy Act 1957, Air Force Act 1950 and the 1966 Police-Forces Act are enabling statutes.
10. Statutory discipline may reach specified attached non-combatants, but not unrelated civilians.
11. Court-martial review is limited, not wholly extinguished.
12. Martial law is temporary military authority to restore order where ordinary civil authority has broken down; it differs from National Emergency.
13. Article 34 is indemnity and validation, not proclamation or blanket suspension.
14. Article 35 lists exact exclusive parliamentary fields.
15. Article 35 does not centralise every Part III implementing law.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the statement “Article 34 authorises the President to declare martial law and suspend Fundamental Rights” incorrect?

**Model answer:** Article 34 contains no declaration procedure and grants no presidential suspension power. It authorises Parliament to indemnify or validate specified acts in an area where martial law was in force.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Articles 31A-31C as one immunity, Article 33 as complete judicial exclusion or Article 35 as Parliament's monopoly over every rights-related law.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Analyse how Articles 31A-31C and 33-35 create bounded exceptions within Part III. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

The provisions use different constitutional techniques and must not be fused. Article 31A protects specified restructuring laws, especially estate-related measures, from Articles 14 and 19. Article 31B validates Ninth Schedule laws, but *I.R. Coelho* subjects post-*Kesavananda* insertions to basic-structure review. Article 31C protects laws genuinely implementing Article 39(b)-(c); *Minerva Mills* invalidated its extension to all Directive Principles.

Article 33 permits Parliament to restrict or abrogate rights of listed armed, public-order, intelligence and connected telecommunications personnel for discipline and duty. The Army Act 1950, Navy Act 1957, Air Force Act 1950 and 1966 Police-Forces Act operationalise the power, including for specified attached non-combatants where service law applies. Limited constitutional review of court-martial legality survives.

Martial law means exceptional temporary military authority after breakdown of ordinary civil order in an area. Article 34 permits parliamentary indemnity or validation of connected acts; it neither proclaims martial law nor automatically suspends rights.

Article 35 gives Parliament exclusive power over Article 16(3), Article 32(3), Articles 33-34 and punishment for Part III offences.

These are bounded constitutional qualifications, not extra-constitutional immunities. Textual fields and basic-structure review preserve the rule that exception cannot destroy constitutional identity.

## Lesson 25 - Fundamental Rights during National Emergency

### Visual: the post-44th Amendment distinction

| Article 358 | Article 359 |
|---|---|
| concerns Article 19 only | concerns enforcement of rights specified in presidential order |
| operates automatically during Emergency based on war or external aggression | requires presidential order during a National Emergency |
| does not operate for armed-rebellion Emergency | may operate for named rights in either type, but never Articles 20 and 21 |
| protects only emergency-related law/action satisfying constitutional conditions | suspends right to move specified courts and may suspend pending enforcement proceedings |
| Article 19 revives after Emergency; protected emergency law does not continue as ordinary immunity | rights are not textually repealed; enforcement is suspended to order's extent and duration |

### 1. Article 358

Article 358 removes Article 19's constraint automatically only when the National Emergency is declared on grounds of **war or external aggression**. After the 44th Amendment it does not operate during an armed-rebellion Emergency.

The protection is limited to laws related to the Emergency and carrying the constitutionally required recital, and to executive action under such law. It is not a general licence for unrelated State action.

### 2. Article 359

The President may order suspension of the right to move a court for enforcement of specified Part III rights, and pending proceedings may remain suspended as stated in the order.

The constitutional distinction is:

```text
right continues in constitutional text
          |
right to judicial enforcement is suspended to order's extent
```

Articles **20 and 21 cannot be included** after the 44th Amendment.

### 3. ADM Jabalpur

During the 1975 Emergency, the majority in *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* denied judicial enforcement of liberty against unlawful detention under the prevailing suspension order. Justice H.R. Khanna dissented.

*K.S. Puttaswamy* later held the majority decision wrong and overruled it. The 44th Amendment's textual protection of Articles 20 and 21 ensures that criminal safeguards and life/personal liberty enforcement survive an Article 359 order.

### 4. Rights and Emergency topic boundary

This lesson owns only the effect on Fundamental Rights. Article 352's proclamation grounds, Cabinet advice, parliamentary approval, duration, revocation and federal effects belong to Emergency Provisions.

Preventive detention is not created by a National Emergency and should not be placed under Articles 358-359. It exists under Article 22 and statute even outside Emergency.

### 5. Constitutional lesson

Emergency law tests whether the Constitution continues to bind the State when claims of necessity are strongest. The post-1978 design makes suspension asymmetric:

- Article 19 may yield under strictly defined external Emergency conditions;
- enforcement of other named rights may be suspended by order;
- Articles 20 and 21 remain judicially enforceable.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** If other rights can become unenforceable, the Constitution still permits authoritarian government.

**Reply:** Narrow grounds, parliamentary controls, judicial review of proclamation and action, emergency relation requirements and the non-derogable Articles 20-21 core significantly constrain the power.

**Residual:** Legal safeguards require institutional courage; the history of *ADM Jabalpur* shows that text and courts can fail under political pressure.

### UPSC application

Use a four-column answer: trigger, operation, protected rights and aftermath. Never say “all Fundamental Rights are suspended”.

### Revision notes

1. Article 358 concerns Article 19 only.
2. It is automatic only during war/external-aggression Emergency.
3. It does not operate for armed rebellion.
4. Protected law/action must be Emergency-related and satisfy textual conditions.
5. Article 359 requires a presidential order.
6. It suspends enforcement to the order's extent, not textual existence.
7. Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included.
8. *ADM Jabalpur* denied liberty enforcement during the 1975 Emergency.
9. Justice H.R. Khanna dissented.
10. *Puttaswamy* overruled the majority position.
11. Preventive detention belongs to Article 22, not only Emergency.
12. Broader proclamation and federal effects belong to the Emergency topic.

### Concept check

**Question:** During an armed-rebellion National Emergency, does Article 19 automatically cease to constrain the State under Article 358?

**Model answer:** No. After the 44th Amendment, Article 358 operates only for an Emergency based on war or external aggression. An armed-rebellion Emergency does not automatically suspend Article 19.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying Article 359 repeals the named rights, that Articles 20-21 may be included, or that every Emergency automatically suspends Article 19.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Compare Articles 358 and 359 and assess the constitutional significance of the 44th Amendment. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Articles 358 and 359 use different mechanisms. Article 358 automatically relaxes Article 19 during a National Emergency based on war or external aggression. After the 44th Amendment it does not operate during armed rebellion, and its protection is confined to emergency-related laws carrying the required recital and action under them.

Article 359 requires a presidential order. It suspends the right to move specified courts for enforcement of named Fundamental Rights, and may suspend pending proceedings to the order's extent. It does not erase the rights from the Constitution. Most importantly, Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included after the 44th Amendment.

The reform responds directly to the 1975 Emergency and *ADM Jabalpur*, where the majority denied judicial enforcement of liberty and Justice H.R. Khanna dissented. *Puttaswamy* later overruled that majority position.

The 44th Amendment therefore created a non-derogable criminal-liberty core and narrowed automatic suspension. It did not abolish emergency power, but made its rights consequences ground-specific, order-specific and more judicially controllable.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Block A - Architecture and equality, Lessons 1-7

### Cumulative check A1 - the complete rights question

**Question:** A private association denies access to a publicly dedicated facility on caste grounds. Identify the holder, respondent, right, horizontal basis and possible State duty without assuming that every private act falls under Article 12.

**Model answer:** The claimant is a citizen protected by Article 15(2), and potentially by Article 17 if the denial enforces an untouchability disability. The private association is directly reached by those textual horizontal provisions even if it is not Article 12 State. Public authorities also have positive duties to enforce penal and civil-rights legislation. Article 12 status and direct horizontal application are separate routes.

**Misconception to avoid:** Trying to prove that every private discriminator is “State” before using a right whose text already reaches private conduct.

### Cumulative check A2 - classification, advancement and employment

**Question:** Explain why Articles 14, 15(4) and 16(4) do not form a contradiction.

**Model answer:** Article 14 permits relevant differentiation and rejects arbitrariness. Articles 15(4) and 16(4) expressly enable advancement and representation measures for defined disadvantaged classes. They operationalise substantive equality but remain clause-specific and reviewable for evidence, beneficiary fit and constitutional limits.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every classification as discrimination or every enabling clause as an unconditional individual quota.

## Block B - Freedom and liberty, Lessons 8-17

### Cumulative check B1 - restriction sequence

**Question:** A government blocks an online platform for “public welfare” without publishing the order. State the constitutional sequence without deciding facts not supplied.

**Model answer:** Identify the affected speech and trade freedoms; locate a valid Article 19(2) or 19(6) ground rather than rely on “welfare” as a slogan; verify legal authority; apply rational connection, necessity and balance; then test reasons, publication, duration, review and challenge. Non-publication also obstructs the Article 19(1)(a) right to know and an effective judicial challenge. *Shreya Singhal* and *Anuradha Bhasin* supply different parts of the inquiry.

**Misconception to avoid:** Declaring either that all blocking is invalid or that a desirable purpose cures secrecy and overbreadth.

### Cumulative check B2 - physical evidence and privacy

**Question:** How should compelled fingerprints, involuntary narco-analysis and a mandatory medical-data disclosure be placed within Articles 20 and 21?

**Model answer:** Fingerprints are generally physical identification rather than testimonial compulsion under *Kathi Kalu Oghad*, though collection must remain lawful and fair. Involuntary narco-analysis extracts mental responses and violates testimonial autonomy and Article 21 under *Selvi*. Medical-data disclosure principally raises bodily and informational privacy, legality, necessity and safeguards under *Puttaswamy*.

**Misconception to avoid:** Using Article 20(3) for every bodily sample or ignoring Article 21 whenever Article 20 does not apply.

### Cumulative check B3 - custody sequence

**Question:** Separate ordinary arrest, police custody, judicial custody and preventive detention in one constitutional chain.

**Model answer:** Ordinary arrest triggers Article 22(1)-(2): grounds, counsel and magistrate production within twenty-four hours excluding journey time. Police custody permits authorised investigative control; judicial custody rests under magistrate authority. Preventive detention is not punishment or ordinary remand and is governed by Article 22(4)-(7), the operative three-month rule, representation and statutory purpose.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every form of custody as preventive detention or including journey time within the twenty-four-hour limit.

## Block C - Exploitation, pluralism and remedies, Lessons 18-25

### Cumulative check C1 - horizontality and positive duty

**Question:** Compare the constitutional routes against bonded labour, child labour and workplace sexual harassment.

**Model answer:** Bonded and economically compelled labour is directly prohibited by Article 23 against private as well as State actors, with positive duties of identification and rehabilitation. Article 24 directly prohibits specified child employment, while statute extends protection and Article 21A adds education. Workplace harassment was addressed through State positive obligation and temporary *Vishaka* safeguards derived from Articles 14, 19 and 21 until legislation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that all horizontality must be express in identical language or that every positive duty creates an unlimited welfare entitlement.

### Cumulative check C2 - religion and minority education

**Question:** A minority institution claims that religious autonomy makes it immune from teacher-qualification and financial-transparency rules. How should the claim be analysed?

**Model answer:** Identify whether the measure concerns religious affairs under Article 26 or educational administration under Article 30. Minority character and core administration are protected, but academic standards, qualifications, transparency and prevention of maladministration may be regulated if they do not transfer control or destroy institutional choice.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating denomination, minority and institution as interchangeable labels or converting autonomy into immunity from standards.

### Cumulative check C3 - exceptional powers

**Question:** Distinguish Article 33, Article 34, Article 358 and Article 359 in four sentences.

**Model answer:** Article 33 lets Parliament restrict or abrogate rights of listed disciplined personnel for duty and discipline. Article 34 lets Parliament indemnify or validate specified acts connected with martial law. Article 358 automatically affects Article 19 only during war/external-aggression Emergency within its post-44th limits. Article 359 requires a presidential enforcement-suspension order and cannot include Articles 20 or 21.

**Misconception to avoid:** Describing all four as declarations or blanket suspensions of Fundamental Rights.

### Cumulative check C4 - remedy selection

**Question:** An unlawfully detained person, an ignored statutory public duty and an ineligible public-office holder present three disputes. Select the writ for each and explain why Article 226 may be available beyond Article 32.

**Model answer:** Habeas corpus addresses unlawful detention; mandamus compels performance of public duty; quo warranto tests legal authority to hold public office. Article 32 is confined to Part III enforcement, while Article 226 extends to Fundamental Rights and any other public-law purpose within territorial rules.

**Misconception to avoid:** Choosing writs by respondent identity alone or saying High Courts issue writs only for Fundamental Rights.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark model

**Question:** Explain the constitutional distinction between direct horizontality and positive State obligations under Fundamental Rights. (10 marks, 150 words)

### Directive decoding

**Explain** requires a clear distinction, constitutional examples and their relationship. It does not require an exhaustive private-law theory.

### Model answer

Direct horizontality means that a Fundamental Right itself reaches specified private conduct. Article 15(2) prohibits caste, religion, race, sex or birthplace exclusion from listed public facilities; Article 17 forbids untouchability; Article 23 reaches private trafficking and forced labour; Article 24 reaches prohibited child employment.

Positive obligation is different. The immediate wrong may be private, but the Constitution requires the State to prevent, regulate, investigate or remedy it. *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* required identification and rehabilitation of bonded labourers; *Vishaka* imposed temporary workplace safeguards through Articles 14, 19 and 21 until legislation; custodial and emergency-care cases require protective public systems.

Neither route constitutionalises every private dispute. Direct reach depends on text and doctrine; positive duties require a sufficiently close rights connection and lawful institutional remedy. Together they prevent the State from avoiding responsibility merely because domination is privately exercised.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both concepts, uses four textual provisions and two cases, and states the limiting principle.

**Quantified rubric:** **10 = distinction/thesis 2 + direct-horizontal provisions 3 + positive-obligation cases 3 + limit 1 + conclusion/presentation 1.**

## Final 15-mark model

**Question:** “The true content of a Fundamental Right is revealed by its restriction and remedy.” Analyse with reference to Articles 19, 21 and 32. (15 marks, 250 words)

### Directive decoding

**Analyse** requires breaking the claim into right, restriction, review and remedy, then judging whether the proposition is sufficient.

### Model answer

A constitutional right is not understood fully by naming its protected interest. Its practical content emerges from the grounds on which the State may interfere, the standard used to review interference and the remedy available.

Article 19 demonstrates clause-specific restriction. Speech is limited only by Article 19(2)'s eight grounds; movement and residence by general-public and Scheduled Tribe interests; profession by general-public regulation, qualifications and State monopoly. Every measure additionally requires legal authority, reasonableness and proportionality. *Shreya Singhal* invalidated vague speech law, while *Anuradha Bhasin* added publication, review and duration controls. The same freedom includes a right to know public acts and electoral information under *Raj Narain* and *Association for Democratic Reforms*, with the RTI Act supplying a statutory procedure rather than universal disclosure.

Article 21's sparse phrase “procedure established by law” acquired substantive force through remedy-oriented review. *Maneka Gandhi* required fair, just and reasonable procedure; later cases protected dignity, speedy trial, livelihood, privacy and emergency care. These remain qualified: *Olga Tellis* protected livelihood but not immunity from lawful eviction.

Article 32 completes the structure by guaranteeing access to the Supreme Court for Part III enforcement and authorising appropriate directions, orders and writs. Yet guaranteed access does not mean automatic requested relief.

The claim is therefore substantially correct, but incomplete: text identifies the protected interest; restrictions, review and remedy determine its operational constitutional meaning.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates three Articles, named cases, exact limits and a qualified verdict rather than producing a rights list.

**Quantified rubric:** **15 = qualified thesis 2 + Article 19 structure 4 + Article 21 development/limit 4 + Article 32 nuance 3 + synthesis and conclusion 2.**

## Final 20-mark model

**Question:** Fundamental Rights have evolved from a shield against State action into a constitutional framework for substantive equality, dignity and control of private power. Critically evaluate. (20 marks, 250 words)

### Directive decoding

**Critically evaluate** requires a defensible thesis, evidence for the transformation, counter-limits and a graded verdict. A purely celebratory list is insufficient.

### Model answer

Part III began primarily as a restraint on public power, but its text and interpretation now address structural inequality, dignitary harm and some private domination.

The vertical shield remains central. Articles 12-13 identify State instrumentalities and invalidate inconsistent law; Article 19 subjects restrictions to enumerated grounds and proportionality; *Maneka Gandhi* requires fair, just and reasonable deprivation under Article 21; Article 32 guarantees an enforcement route.

Substantive equality is express rather than merely judicial. Articles 15(3)-(6) and 16(4), 16(4A), 16(4B) and 16(6) authorise advancement and representation. *Indra Sawhney*, *Nagaraj*, *Jarnail Singh* and *Janhit Abhiyan* add creamy-layer, data, efficiency and clause-specific controls.

Private power is reached directly by Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24. *PUDR* treats economic compulsion as forced labour; *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* requires rehabilitation. Positive obligations also emerge through *Vishaka*, custodial safeguards and emergency medical care.

Expansion has limits. Article 21 is generally remedial rather than an unlimited distributive charter; horizontality cannot constitutionalise every private dispute; open-ended arbitrariness or dignity review may strain institutional competence. Saving clauses and Emergency provisions also remain part of the text.

Thus Part III is no longer only a negative shield, but its legitimacy depends on exact clauses, evidence, proportionality, enforceable remedies and judicial restraint.

**Why this earns marks:** It covers gateways, equality, liberty, horizontal reach, positive duties, counterarguments and a graded conclusion within the word ceiling.

**Quantified rubric:** **20 = evaluative thesis 2 + vertical architecture 4 + substantive-equality evidence 4 + private-power/positive-duty evidence 4 + three critical limits 3 + integrated verdict and presentation 3.**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Part III architecture

- Part III covers Articles 12-35 and presently contains six right groups.
- “Magna Carta of India” is a conventional description of Part III's extensive enforceable rights charter, not a claim of textual identity with foreign charters.
- Article 31 and Article 19(1)(f) were removed by the 44th Amendment; Article 300A now protects property from deprivation except by authority of law.
- Fundamental means constitutionally prioritised and enforceable, not absolute.
- Always identify right-holder, respondent, protected interest, restriction, review test and remedy.
- Citizen rights: Articles 15, 16, 19 and 29.
- Person formulations: Articles 14, 20-25, 27-28, subject to each Article's exact wording.
- Direct horizontal reach: Articles 15(2), 17, 23 and 24.
- Positive duties may require protection and enforcement without creating unlimited distributive entitlements.

## Articles 12-13

- Article 12: Union and State governments/legislatures, local authorities and other authorities.
- Instrumentality analysis is cumulative: ownership, finance, function, domination, monopoly and transfer of governmental work.
- Statutory origin and public importance are not individually conclusive.
- A non-State body may still owe a public duty reviewable under Article 226.
- Article 13(1): pre-Constitution law; 13(2): prohibited new State law.
- “Law” includes Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom and usage with force of law.
- Article 13(4) excludes Article 368 amendments; basic-structure review remains.
- Severability preserves an intended workable remainder.
- Eclipse classically makes inconsistent pre-Constitution law dormant.
- Fundamental Rights cannot generally be waived to validate unconstitutional State action.
- Prospective overruling requires express judicial design.

## Equality code

- Article 14 protects persons: equality before law plus equal protection.
- Classification: intelligible differentia plus rational nexus.
- *E.P. Royappa*: arbitrariness opposes equality.
- Manifest arbitrariness is not a general merits appeal.
- The US Fourteenth Amendment contains the Equal Protection Clause; US strict, intermediate and rational-basis scrutiny are judicially developed.
- India combines classification and non-arbitrariness with express protective-discrimination clauses and distinctive Articles 17-18.
- Article 15(1): citizens; religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth; word “only”.
- Article 15(2): specified horizontal public-access protection.
- Article 15(3): women/children; 15(4): SEBC/SC/ST; 15(5): admissions, minority institutions excluded; 15(6): EWS.
- Article 16(2) additionally names descent and residence.
- Parliament alone exercises Article 16(3).
- Article 16(4) is enabling and concerns appointments.
- Articles 16(4A)/(4B): SC/ST promotion and backlog.
- *Indra Sawhney*: creamy layer for OBCs, appointments and ordinary fifty-per-cent framework.
- *Nagaraj* and *Jarnail Singh*: promotion data and efficiency discipline.
- Article 335 is a counterweight, not a veto.
- *Janhit Abhiyan*: 103rd Amendment upheld by 3:2.
- *Davinder Singh*: evidence-based SC sub-classification permitted, not mandated.
- Article 17 abolishes caste-based untouchability and reaches private persons.
- Article 18 bars titles but permits military/academic distinctions; national awards cannot be used as titles.

## Article 19

- Six citizen freedoms: speech, peaceful unarmed assembly, association/unions/co-operatives, movement, residence/settlement, profession/trade.
- Restrictions require law, correct clause ground, reasonableness and proportionality.
- Article 19(2) has eight exhaustive grounds.
- *Romesh Thappar*: circulation; *Brij Bhushan*: pre-censorship under original text.
- First Amendment added grounds and “reasonable”; 16th Amendment added sovereignty/integrity.
- Article 19(1)(a) includes the right to know information necessary for accountable government and informed democratic choice.
- *Raj Narain* and *S.P. Gupta* ground open government; *Association for Democratic Reforms* applies the right to electoral-candidate information.
- The RTI Act, 2005 codifies a statutory request, exemption and appeal system; it did not create the constitutional foundation or mandate disclosure of every record.
- *Kedar Nath*: former sedition law upheld through incitement/public-disorder reading.
- *S.G. Vombatkere*: 11 May 2022 interim abeyance; challenge undecided; a reported 21 May 2026 relaxation allows a pending matter to proceed where the accused does not object.
- BNS section 152 has different ingredients; it was under constitutional challenge and remained undecided as of 30 September 2026, so do not call it mere renaming, complete abolition or settled law.
- India has no single statutory hate-speech definition; use *Shreya Singhal*'s incitement distinction, BNS sections 196/353 and RPA sections 123(3)/(3A) in their precise fields.
- *Shreya Singhal*: section 66A struck for vagueness/overbreadth; discussion/advocacy/incitement; section 69A upheld, section 79 read down.
- *Anuradha Bhasin*: section 144 CrPC and Telecom Suspension Rules routes; freedoms through internet, publication, periodic review, duration and proportionality; no indefinite suspension, no standalone internet FR, and no quashing of the restrictions before the Court.
- *Subramanian Swamy*: reputation under Article 21 balances speech through the defamation ground; the judgment considered IPC sections 499-500, while BNS section 356 is the current offence.
- No Fundamental Right to strike follows from assembly or association.
- Movement/residence: Article 19(5), general public and Scheduled Tribe interests.
- Foreign travel: Article 21.
- Article 19(6): qualifications and State monopoly.

## Articles 20-22

- Article 20(1): no disadvantageous retrospective criminal offence or enhanced penalty.
- Article 20(2): prosecuted and punished for same offence.
- Departmental action is not automatically prosecution.
- Article 20(3): accused + compulsion + testimonial self-incrimination.
- Physical identification differs from compelled mental/testimonial content.
- *Kathi Kalu Oghad*, *Nandini Satpathy*, *Selvi* are the principal distinctions.
- Article 21: life/personal liberty only through procedure established by law.
- *Gopalan*: narrow; *R.C. Cooper*: effects; *Maneka*: fair, just, reasonable procedure and golden triangle.
- Do not say India imported American substantive due process wholesale.
- Dignity: *Francis Coralie*.
- Livelihood: *Olga Tellis*, with lawful-eviction limit.
- Speedy trial/legal aid: *Hussainara*, *M.H. Hoskot*.
- Custody: *D.K. Basu*, *Sunil Batra*.
- Emergency care/environment: *Paschim Banga*, *Subhash Kumar*.
- Passive euthanasia/living will: *Common Cause* (2018); the 24 January 2023 Constitution Bench order simplified procedural safeguards; active euthanasia and assisted suicide remain distinct.
- Privacy: *Puttaswamy*; legality, legitimate aim, proportionality, safeguards.
- Identity/autonomy: *NALSA*, *Navtej*.
- 2026 transgender amendment: operative 25 May; Supreme Court notice 4 May; no merits determination located by 30 September.
- Article 21A: all children six-fourteen; 86th Amendment; RTE Act; minority-institution boundary.
- Article 22 ordinary arrest: grounds, counsel, magistrate within twenty-four hours excluding journey time.
- Preventive detention: operative three months; Board members current/former/qualified HC judges; grounds and earliest representation.
- Two-month 44th-Amendment substitution is uncommenced.
- Do not classify every stringent security statute as preventive-detention law.

## Exploitation

- Article 23 protects all persons against State and private trafficking, begar and forced labour.
- Force includes economic compulsion tied to lawful minimum protection: *PUDR*, *Sanjit Roy*.
- *Bandhua Mukti Morcha*: identification, release and rehabilitation.
- Compulsory public service permitted without discrimination only on religion, race, caste or class.
- Article 24 says no child below fourteen shall be “employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment”.
- Amended statute creates a broader child/adolescent framework.
- *M.C. Mehta*: compensation and rehabilitation.
- Article 21A links protection with schooling.

## Religion and minority rights

- Article 25: all persons; conscience, profess, practise, propagate.
- Limits: public order, morality, health, other Part III rights.
- Propagation does not protect forced/fraudulent conversion: *Rev. Stainislaus*.
- Article 25(2): secular regulation and social reform.
- Article 26: denomination or section; institutions, religious affairs, property.
- *Shirur Mutt*: religious/secular distinction and ERP origin.
- ERP criticism: courts become theological arbiters.
- *Indian Young Lawyers Association* (2018): Sabarimala exclusion tested under Articles 14, 25 and 26, with constitutional morality competing against the claimed practice and denominational autonomy.
- The broader nine-judge reference remained unresolved; no final opinion was located as of 30 September 2026, and the 2018 judgment must not be described as overruled.
- Waqf Amendment Act, 2025 changes included “waqf by user”, five-year dedicator eligibility, Collector-level property review and non-Muslim council/board membership.
- The 15 September 2025 interim order did not stay the Act as a whole; it stayed the five-year and Collector provisions, capped membership at 4/22 and 3/11, and left final validity undecided.
- Article 27: no tax specifically appropriated to particular religion; fee distinction.
- Article 28: four institution categories; consent in aided/recognised institutions.
- Places of Worship Act, Section 3: conversion between denominations or their sections is barred.
- Section 4(1) preserves religious character as on 15 August 1947; Section 4(2) abates and bars proceedings subject to its proviso.
- Section 4(3) excludes ancient monuments/sites governed by the 1958 Act and specified finally decided, settled, accepted or time-barred matters.
- Section 5 expressly excludes Ram Janmabhoomi-Babri Masjid at Ayodhya and related proceedings; the exclusion cannot be generalised.
- Challenges remain pending under the 12 December 2024 interim regime; no final merits judgment was located as of 30 September 2026.
- Ayodhya (2019) approved the Places Act's equality-of-faiths and secular purpose, but did not adjudicate the Act's validity.
- Article 29(1): any section of citizens, not only minorities.
- Article 29(2): admission; religion, race, caste, language.
- Article 30: religious/linguistic minorities; establish/administer institution.
- State ordinarily supplies minority unit under *T.M.A. Pai*.
- No right to maladminister; standards may be regulated without takeover.
- *P.A. Inamdar*, *Malankara*, *Pramati* define professional, administrative and RTE boundaries.

## Remedies and exceptional controls

- Article 32 guarantees approach to Supreme Court for Part III enforcement.
- Guaranteed access is not automatic entitlement to every requested relief.
- Habeas corpus: unlawful detention, including private detention.
- Mandamus: public duty.
- Prohibition: preventive jurisdictional control.
- Certiorari: curative quashing of defective public-law decision.
- Quo warranto: unlawful claim to public office.
- Article 226 is wider: FRs plus any other purpose; public-duty reach; territorial limits.
- Article 31A: specified protected law categories.
- Article 31B: Ninth Schedule; post-24 April 1973 insertions face basic-structure review.
- Article 31C: present scope tied to Article 39(b)-(c); *Minerva Mills* rejected all-DPSP extension.
- Article 33: Parliament modifies rights of listed disciplined personnel; limited court-martial review survives.
- Enabling laws include the Army Act 1950, Navy Act 1957, Air Force Act 1950 and Police-Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act 1966; specified attached non-combatants may be covered by service law.
- Article 34: parliamentary indemnity/validation; not declaration or blanket suspension.
- Martial law means exceptional temporary military authority to restore order in an area after breakdown of ordinary civil authority; it is distinct from National Emergency.
- Article 35 exact fields: 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34 and Part III offences.
- Article 358: Article 19 only; automatic only for war/external aggression; emergency-related law/action conditions.
- Article 359: presidential enforcement order; Articles 20-21 excluded.
- *ADM Jabalpur* majority overruled by *Puttaswamy*; H.R. Khanna dissent remains the constitutional warning.
