# Directive Principles of State Policy - Live Learning Session

> Part IV is the Constitution's programme for converting political democracy into social and economic democracy. It does not create directly enforceable claims merely by stating an objective; instead, it directs public power, shapes legislation, informs constitutional interpretation and supplies standards by which democratic performance can be judged.

*The Constitution does not ask the State merely to avoid oppression; it also asks the State to build conditions in which freedom can be meaningfully enjoyed.*

### 1. The problem Part IV addresses

A Constitution may guarantee speech, equality and liberty while leaving citizens without education, health, livelihood or access to justice. Formal liberty then exists, but the capacity to use it is radically unequal. Part IV addresses this second problem. It directs the State towards a social order in which political citizenship is supported by material security, equal opportunity and public institutions.

The Directive Principles of State Policy are therefore not a miscellaneous policy list. They are a constitutional programme for a **welfare state**: a State that accepts responsibility for promoting minimum conditions of dignified life rather than limiting itself to policing, defence and contract enforcement.

### 2. Historical source and Indian adaptation

The framers drew the idea from the **Irish Constitution of 1937**, which had been influenced by the Spanish constitutional tradition. B.R. Ambedkar described the Directive Principles as a **novel feature**. He also compared them to the **Instrument of Instructions** under the Government of India Act, 1935, but with a decisive democratic change: the instructions now address constitutional legislatures and executives that are answerable to the electorate.

Granville Austin described Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles together as the **conscience of the Constitution**. The phrase matters because neither part by itself completes the constitutional project:

- rights without social reform may preserve formal freedom amid severe inequality;
- welfare without rights may become paternalistic or authoritarian;
- constitutional democracy requires liberty and social transformation together.

### 3. Political democracy and economic democracy

Fundamental Rights principally secure the legal conditions of **political democracy**: equal citizenship, civil freedom, personal liberty and judicial remedies. Directive Principles principally pursue **social and economic democracy**: livelihood, fair distribution, public assistance, humane work, education, health and protection from exploitation.

This is a functional distinction, not an absolute wall. Some Fundamental Rights impose positive obligations, and some Directive Principles restrain harmful policy choices. The point is one of emphasis: Part III supplies enforceable constitutional limits; Part IV supplies governing objectives.

### 4. Welfare state, not a police state

```text
POLICE-STATE IDEA                 WELFARE-STATE IDEA
maintain order                    secure justice
protect borders                   reduce disabling inequality
punish offences                   support health and education
enforce contracts                 protect workers and weaker sections
                                  create equal access to institutions
```

The contrast does not mean that the State must directly provide every good or abolish markets. It means public power must be assessed against the constitutional objective of social welfare, distributive justice and human dignity.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Broad social goals do not belong in a Constitution because elected governments should choose their own policies.

**Reply:** Part IV generally leaves the instrument, timing and institutional design to democratic choice while fixing constitutional ends. It creates direction without prescribing one permanent economic model.

**Residual:** Flexibility can become an excuse for indefinite delay. The democratic value of Part IV therefore depends on legislation, budgets, administration and electoral accountability.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why would enforceable civil and political rights alone be insufficient to realise the Preamble's promise of justice?

**Model answer:** Civil and political rights restrain arbitrary power, but severe deprivation can prevent citizens from using those freedoms in practice. Part IV directs the State to create livelihood, education, health, social security and distributive conditions that make equal citizenship materially meaningful.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating welfare as a charitable addition to rights rather than as the constitutional condition that can make formal freedom effective.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

“Directive Principles transform the Constitution from a charter of limited government into a programme of social transformation.” Explain. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Fundamental Rights make government limited by protecting equality, liberty and remedies against unconstitutional power. Directive Principles add a second constitutional function: they direct that power towards social and economic transformation.

Articles 38-39 seek a just social order, livelihood, distributive fairness and prevention of wealth concentration. Articles 41-47 address work, public assistance, humane labour, education, nutrition and public health, while other directives support local self-government, environmental protection and international peace. Their non-justiciability preserves policy choice, but Article 37 makes them fundamental in governance and imposes a duty to apply them in law-making.

Part IV therefore does not replace limited government. It completes it. The Constitution seeks welfare through lawful democratic institutions, not social reform at the cost of rights. Rights and directives together combine political democracy with social-economic democracy.

*Non-enforceability answers who may compel; it does not erase constitutional importance.*

### 1. Article 36: who is directed?

Article 36 says that, unless the context otherwise requires, **“the State” has the same meaning as in Part III**. The direction therefore reaches:

- the Union and State governments;
- Parliament and State legislatures;
- local authorities;
- other public authorities falling within the constitutional meaning of State.

This matters for Article 40. Village panchayats are not merely beneficiaries of Part IV; once constituted as public authorities, they can also be part of the State expected to act consistently with constitutional governance.

### 2. Article 37: non-justiciability

The first limb says that the provisions in Part IV **“shall not be enforceable by any court.”** A person ordinarily cannot obtain a writ merely because the State has not fully realised a directive. Article 41, for example, does not by itself permit every unemployed person to demand a judicially ordered job.

Non-justiciability must be separated from three other ideas:

| Claim | Correct position |
|---|---|
| DPSP are unconstitutional | False; they are part of the Constitution |
| DPSP are legally enforceable exactly like FR | False; Article 37 denies direct enforcement |
| DPSP are optional advice | False; Article 37 calls them fundamental and imposes a State duty |
| Every implementing policy is valid | False; legislation still faces competence, rights and other constitutional limits |

### 3. “Fundamental in the governance of the country”

The second limb gives Part IV constitutional weight. “Fundamental” does not mean that every directive becomes an individual cause of action. It means that governments must treat these principles as governing standards when designing laws and policies.

Their force appears in four forms:

1. **legislative direction** - statutes may convert a directive into an enforceable entitlement;
2. **political accountability** - governments can be judged by the electorate and opposition;
3. **interpretive use** - courts read laws and rights in light of constitutional objectives;
4. **reasonableness support** - a genuine DPSP purpose may help justify a proportionate law under Articles 14 and 19.

### 4. “Duty of the State to apply”

The third limb uses mandatory constitutional language: it **shall be the duty** of the State to apply the principles in making laws. The duty is institutionally real even though its performance is not directly compelled in the same way as a Fundamental Right.

The Constitution thus separates:

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL DUTY
        ≠
DIRECTLY ENFORCEABLE INDIVIDUAL REMEDY
```

Legislatures retain choices about sequence, resources and instruments. They do not receive permission to ignore Part IV as though it were a political manifesto outside the Constitution.

### 5. Interpretive force has limits

Courts may use Directive Principles to resolve ambiguity, support a rights-consistent social purpose or understand the content of dignity and fair procedure. But interpretation cannot simply rewrite an unambiguous constitutional or statutory rule. Nor can a court create an unlimited budgetary entitlement without a legal foundation.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** If courts cannot enforce Part IV, the word “duty” has no legal meaning.

**Reply:** Constitutional duties may guide institutions without creating a direct remedy. Legislative competence, statutory enactment, rights interpretation and electoral accountability give Part IV practical legal and political effects.

**Residual:** The absence of a direct remedy makes implementation uneven and permits governments to prioritise some directives while postponing others.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can Article 37 impose a constitutional duty while denying court enforcement?

**Model answer:** Article 37 distinguishes institutional obligation from individual remedy. The State must apply Part IV in law-making, but a citizen cannot ordinarily obtain judicial enforcement of the directive itself. The duty operates through legislation, interpretation, political accountability and statutory remedies once enacted.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that every constitutional duty must be directly enforceable or that non-enforceability makes a provision optional.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Directive Principles are “non-justiciable but not non-binding.” Discuss with reference to Article 37. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 37 deliberately combines restraint and obligation. It first provides that Directive Principles are not enforceable by any court; therefore, non-implementation does not by itself create a writ claim comparable to Article 32. Yet the same Article declares them fundamental in governance and makes it the State's duty to apply them in making laws.

Their binding force is consequently institutional and political. Legislatures translate them into statutes such as the Legal Services Authorities Act and the Right to Education Act. Courts use Part IV to interpret rights and assess whether welfare-oriented restrictions pursue legitimate constitutional purposes. Governments are also accountable to legislatures and electorates for ignoring the constitutional programme.

However, implementing measures remain subject to legislative competence, Fundamental Rights and judicial review. Article 37 thus creates constitutional direction without converting every socio-economic objective into an immediately enforceable individual entitlement.

*Article 38 states the social order; Article 39 identifies major distributive mechanisms and protected interests.*

### 1. Article 38(1): justice must inform institutions

Article 38(1) directs the State to promote welfare by securing and protecting, as effectively as it may, a social order in which **social, economic and political justice** informs all institutions of national life.

The phrase “all institutions” prevents justice from being confined to welfare departments. Taxation, labour regulation, education, local government, public finance and access to courts all affect the distribution of opportunity and power.

### 2. Article 38(2): the exact inequality command

Article 38(2), added by the **44th Amendment**, directs the State to:

- minimise inequalities in **income**; and
- endeavour to eliminate inequalities in **status, facilities and opportunities**;
- not only among individuals, but also among groups residing in different areas or engaged in different vocations.

This is broader than income redistribution. Regional inequality, occupational disadvantage, unequal public services and status hierarchy all fall within its constitutional field.

### 3. Article 39(a)-(f): read every clause separately

| Clause | Exact constitutional direction | Core distinction |
|---|---|---|
| 39(a) | citizens, men and women equally, should have an adequate means of livelihood | livelihood, not a guaranteed identical occupation |
| 39(b) | ownership and control of material resources of the community should be distributed to subserve the common good | distribution of control/resources |
| 39(c) | economic operation should not concentrate wealth and means of production to the common detriment | prevention of harmful concentration |
| 39(d) | equal pay for equal work for men and women | sex equality in remuneration for equal work |
| 39(e) | health and strength of workers and tender age of children must not be abused; economic necessity should not force unsuitable avocations | protection against exploitative work |
| 39(f) | children should receive opportunities and facilities for healthy development in freedom and dignity and protection from exploitation and moral and material abandonment | child development and protection |

### 4. Article 39(b) is not Article 39(c)

Article 39(b) asks **how material resources are owned, controlled and distributed for the common good**. Article 39(c) asks whether the economic system produces **concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment**.

```text
39(b): distribution and control of resources
39(c): structure and harmful concentration of economic power
```

They may support the same law, but they test different mechanisms.

### 5. Property Owners Association and the meaning of resources

In *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (2024 INSC 835), a **nine-judge Constitution Bench** held that the original Article 31C protection survives and examined Article 39(b). The Court rejected the proposition that **every privately owned asset automatically becomes a “material resource of the community.”**

Some privately owned resources may fall within Article 39(b), but the inquiry is contextual. Relevant considerations include the nature and characteristics of the resource, its impact on community well-being, scarcity and the consequences of concentration. The decision does not erase Article 39(b); it rejects an automatic all-private-property rule.

### 6. Example and limit

Suppose a law regulates a scarce natural resource essential to community survival. Article 39(b) may support distribution of access for the common good, while Article 39(c) may support limits on control becoming concentrated. But Part IV alone does not decide whether a particular acquisition, licence or restriction is constitutionally valid. Text, competence, nexus and applicable rights remain relevant.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Articles 38-39 constitutionalise one rigid socialist economic model.

**Reply:** They establish distributive ends—justice, livelihood, common good and anti-concentration—without prescribing exclusive State ownership or one permanent policy instrument. *Property Owners Association* expressly resists reading Article 39(b) as an automatic collectivisation clause.

**Residual:** Terms such as “common good” require institutional judgment and can be invoked rhetorically; judicial review must therefore test the genuine connection between law and directive.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it incorrect to treat Articles 39(b) and 39(c) as interchangeable?

**Model answer:** Article 39(b) concerns distribution of ownership and control of material resources to serve the common good. Article 39(c) concerns preventing the economic system from concentrating wealth and means of production to common detriment. A policy may engage both, but their constitutional mechanisms differ.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that every anti-concentration measure redistributes a material resource, or that every resource regulation automatically proves harmful concentration.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Articles 38 and 39 create an economic constitution without prescribing a single economic system. Analyse. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Articles 38 and 39 constitutionalise distributive objectives rather than one immutable economic model. Article 38 seeks a social order informed by social, economic and political justice. Its second clause extends the inquiry beyond income to inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities among individuals, regions and vocations.

Article 39 translates this objective into distinct policy directions: adequate livelihood; distribution of material resources for the common good; prevention of concentration of wealth and productive power; equal pay; and protection of workers and children. These provisions can support land reform, regulation, public services, social protection or competition-oriented measures. They do not require every resource to be State-owned.

*Property Owners Association* reinforces this openness. It held that some private resources may enter Article 39(b), but rejected the automatic inclusion of all private property and required contextual analysis.

The Articles therefore establish constitutional ends and evaluative standards while leaving democratic institutions a range of instruments, subject to rights, competence and judicial review.

*The cluster moves from formal opportunity to the institutions and material conditions needed to use that opportunity.*

### 1. Article 39A: equal justice and free legal aid

Article 39A directs the State to ensure that the legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity and, in particular, to provide free legal aid by legislation, schemes or other means so that opportunities for securing justice are not denied **to any citizen** by reason of **economic or other disabilities**.

The last phrase is crucial. The Article is not confined to “the poor.” Disability may arise from social position, custody, age, sex, trafficking, disability or another condition recognised by law.

The **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**, which came into force in 1995, created a nationwide legal-services network. It includes:

- National and State Legal Services Authorities;
- District Legal Services Authorities and Taluk Legal Services Committees;
- High Court and Supreme Court Legal Services Committees;
- front offices, panel lawyers and para-legal volunteers;
- Lok Adalats and legal-awareness programmes.

NALSA is **statutory**, not constitutional. The Chief Justice of India is its Patron-in-Chief and the senior-most Supreme Court judge is its Executive Chairman.

Section 12 of the Act identifies several eligibility routes. They include members of Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes; victims of trafficking or begar; women and children; persons with disability; persons facing undeserved want arising from circumstances such as mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster; industrial workmen; persons in specified forms of custody; and persons below the income ceiling prescribed by the appropriate authority. There is no single universal income figure that should be quoted for every jurisdiction.

### 2. Lok Adalats: what they do and do not do

Ordinary Lok Adalats are statutory conciliatory forums for pending or pre-litigation disputes. Their members facilitate settlement; they do not coerce parties or decide the merits at their own instance. A settlement award is deemed a civil-court decree, final and binding, with no appeal under the Act. Non-compoundable offences cannot be settled there.

Permanent Lok Adalats for specified public utility services have a different statutory design and may decide the dispute if conciliation fails within their lawful jurisdiction. Do not merge the two mechanisms.

### 3. Article 41: capacity-qualified provision

Article 41 directs the State, **within the limits of its economic capacity and development**, to make effective provision for:

- the right to work;
- education;
- public assistance in unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement;
- other cases of undeserved want.

The capacity clause is textual. It recognises fiscal scarcity, but does not convert the provision into empty discretion. The constitutional demand is progressive and reasoned provision within available capacity.

### 4. Articles 42, 43 and 43A: the quality of work

- **Article 42:** just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
- **Article 43:** by suitable legislation, economic organisation or another method, a living wage, decent standard of life, full enjoyment of leisure and social-cultural opportunities for all workers—agricultural, industrial or otherwise; it also specifically promotes cottage industries on an individual or cooperative basis in rural areas.
- **Article 43A:** by suitable legislation or another method, participation of workers in the management of undertakings, establishments or other organisations engaged in industry.

A **living wage** is wider than a bare subsistence or minimum wage. It aspires to conditions supporting education, health, security, leisure and social participation.

| Wage concept | Exam-safe distinction |
|---|---|
| Minimum wage | protective legal/economic floor aimed at basic subsistence and prevention of exploitation |
| Fair wage | an intermediate standard above the minimum but below the full living-wage ideal, shaped by industry capacity and prevailing conditions |
| Living wage | decent life including health, education, security, leisure and social-cultural participation |

Implementation evidence must be status-controlled. Several older labour statutes listed in textbooks were reorganised through later labour-code legislation. An answer should not call an historical statute currently operative without checking commencement and repeal. The enduring constitutional point is the progression:

```text
minimum protection -> humane work -> living wage -> voice in management
```

### 5. Article 47: primary duties of the State

Article 47 makes raising nutrition, standard of living and public health among the State's **primary duties**. It separately directs an endeavour towards prohibition, except for medicinal purposes, of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health.

The two limbs must not be collapsed. Public health is broader than prohibition, and prohibition is not the whole of Article 47.

### 6. Rights conversion without doctrinal confusion

The Supreme Court read free legal aid into fair procedure under Article 21 in *M.H. Hoskot* and *Hussainara Khatoon*. The enforceable route is Article 21 and statute; Article 39A itself remains non-justiciable. This is a model of harmonisation, not a judicial conversion of every DPSP into an unlimited Fundamental Right.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** These directives promise socio-economic rights without guaranteeing delivery.

**Reply:** Their constitutional function is to require institutionalisation. Legal services authorities, public-assistance laws, labour standards and health systems convert general directions into defined legal mechanisms.

**Residual:** Formal institutions may still suffer from low awareness, uneven quality, inadequate funding and geographic exclusion. Existence must never be equated with effective access.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

#### Model answer

The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 operationalises this direction through NALSA, State and District Authorities, Taluk Committees, High Court and Supreme Court Committees, panel lawyers and para-legal volunteers. NALSA frames policy, coordinates schemes, promotes legal literacy and supports Lok Adalats. Ordinary Lok Adalats provide consensual settlement with binding awards; they cannot coerce compromise or settle non-compoundable offences.

The architecture nevertheless faces uneven counsel quality, low awareness and weak last-mile access. Disposal volume alone does not prove justice quality.

### 1. Classification is analytical, not authoritative

The Constitution contains no formal threefold classification. Textbooks commonly group the directives as **socialistic**, **Gandhian** and **liberal-intellectual**. This helps recall, but it must not be treated like a Schedule or constitutional definition.

The same Article may contain more than one idea. Overlap is not a defect in the constitutional text; it reflects the fact that welfare, decentralisation, science, community and social reform interact.

### 2. Article 40: village panchayats

Article 40 directs the State to take steps to organise **village panchayats** and endow them with powers and authority necessary to function as units of self-government.

The exact boundary matters:

- Article 40 speaks of village panchayats;
- the **73rd Amendment Act, 1992** later inserted Part IX and constitutionally structured Panchayati Raj;
- the **74th Amendment Act, 1992** separately constitutionalised municipalities under Part IXA;
- municipalities are important local government, but they are not the textual object of Article 40.

The 73rd Amendment Act, 1992 gave panchayats constitutional status, regular elections, reservations, State Election Commissions and State Finance Commissions. Yet detailed devolution remains substantially dependent on State law, so constitutional status does not automatically produce functional self-government.

### 3. Articles 43 and 43B: village production and cooperation

Article 43 combines:

- a socialistic commitment to a living wage and decent life; and
- a Gandhian commitment to cottage industries on an individual or cooperative basis in rural areas.

Article 43B, inserted by the 97th Amendment, directs promotion of:

- voluntary formation;
- autonomous functioning;
- democratic control;
- professional management of cooperative societies.

Article 43B remains part of Part IV. In *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (2021), the Supreme Court struck down Part IXB **insofar as it applies to cooperative societies operating within a single State**, because the 97th Amendment had not received the State ratification required by the proviso to Article 368(2). Part IXB survives for **multi-State cooperative societies**. The ruling did not invalidate Article 43B.

### 4. Article 46: weaker sections

Article 46 directs the State to promote with **special care** the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation.

This is both distributive and protective:

```text
education + economic advancement
               |
               v
capability and representation
               +
protection from injustice and exploitation
```

Detailed reservation doctrine, atrocities law, commissions and welfare delivery belong to their specialist topics. This lesson owns the constitutional directive.

### 5. Articles 47 and 48

The prohibition limb of Article 47 is commonly placed in the Gandhian category. But the Article's nutrition and public-health limb is also socialistic. Article 48 directs modern and scientific organisation of agriculture and animal husbandry and specifically calls for preserving and improving breeds and prohibiting slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle.

Article 48 therefore combines scientific modernisation with a cattle-protection directive. It is inaccurate to reduce it to “cow slaughter” alone.

### 6. Why Gandhian does not mean legally superior

The category describes intellectual affinity with Gandhi's reconstruction programme: village republics, local production, vulnerable-group uplift, temperance and cattle protection. It does not give these directives a higher constitutional rank or a unique enforcement method.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The Gandhian category mixes rural development with culturally contested directives and therefore lacks conceptual unity.

**Reply:** Its unity lies in a reconstruction programme centred on village self-government, decentralised production, social protection and moral restraint. The classification remains analytical rather than constitutional.

**Residual:** Some directives can support competing policies. Scientific animal husbandry, livelihood, food systems and cattle protection require careful legislative balancing rather than slogan-based application.

### UPSC application

Use a two-step method in classification questions:

1. identify the conventional textbook category;
2. add that the categories overlap and have no constitutional status.

**Prelims traps:**

- Article 40 concerns village panchayats, not municipalities generally.
- Article 44 is liberal-intellectual, not Gandhian.
- Article 43 appears in both socialistic and Gandhian explanations.
- Article 48 includes scientific agriculture and animal husbandry as well as cattle protection.

### Revision notes

1. The threefold classification is not in the Constitution.
2. It is based on content and direction.
3. Article 40 concerns village panchayats as units of self-government.
4. The 73rd Amendment Act, 1992 is the direct constitutional implementation of Article 40.
5. The 74th Amendment Act, 1992 concerns municipalities separately.
6. Article 43 combines living wage and cottage industries.
7. Article 43B lists voluntary, autonomous, democratic and professional cooperatives.
8. Article 46 combines educational-economic promotion and protection from exploitation.
9. Article 47 overlaps between public health and prohibition.
10. Article 48 combines scientific agriculture/animal husbandry and cattle protection.
11. Classification does not determine enforceability or constitutional rank.
12. *Rajendra N. Shah* invalidated Part IXB for single-State cooperatives for want of Article 368(2) ratification, while preserving it for multi-State cooperatives.
13. Overlap should be acknowledged, not “corrected” by forcing each Article into one box.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can Article 43 legitimately appear in both socialistic and Gandhian classifications?

**Model answer:** Article 43 contains two substantive directions. Its living-wage and decent-life limb reflects social-economic welfare, while its rural cottage-industry limb reflects Gandhi's decentralised production programme. The overlap follows the text and confirms that the classification is only analytical.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that every Article must belong exclusively to one constitutionally fixed category.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

#### Model answer

The Gandhian directives seek to restructure power, production and social relations from below. Article 40 envisages village panchayats as units of self-government, later constitutionalised through the 73rd Amendment Act, 1992 and Part IX. Article 43 promotes rural cottage industries, while Article 43B protects voluntary, autonomous and democratically controlled cooperatives. Article 46 directs special educational and economic advancement of weaker sections and protection from exploitation.

Articles 47 and 48 add temperance, public health, scientific animal husbandry and cattle protection. These provisions are not conceptually identical, but together reflect village-centred production, social protection, moral restraint and decentralised participation.

The category remains a textbook aid, not a constitutional label, and several Articles overlap with socialistic or liberal objectives. Its continuing significance lies in asking whether local bodies and cooperative institutions possess real power, resources and autonomy rather than merely formal recognition.

*Article 44 states a constitutional objective; it does not itself prescribe the content of one code or erase the need to satisfy Fundamental Rights.*

### 1. Exact constitutional direction

Article 44 says that the State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a **uniform civil code throughout the territory of India**.

The provision concerns personal civil relations such as marriage, divorce, maintenance, succession, adoption and guardianship. It is not a directive to create one criminal code or contract law; those fields already operate through general legislation.

### 2. Non-justiciability and judicial observations

Article 44 is subject to Article 37. A court cannot compel Parliament to enact a UCC merely because the directive remains incompletely implemented.

In *Shah Bano*, *Sarla Mudgal* and *John Vallamattom*, the Supreme Court decided the disputes under the applicable legal provisions and also observed that Article 44 remained unfulfilled. These observations encouraged legislative action; they were not writs commanding enactment of a UCC.

### 3. Equality and religious freedom

The constitutional debate cannot be reduced to “Article 44 versus Article 25.”

- Articles 14 and 15 support equality and non-discrimination.
- Articles 25 and 26 protect conscience and religious affairs.
- Article 25 is expressly subject to public order, morality, health and other Part III provisions.
- Article 25(2)(b) permits laws for social welfare and reform.
- Articles 29-30 protect cultural and educational interests but do not constitutionalise every personal-law rule.

The strongest equality case for reform concerns rules that impose unequal civil consequences based on sex. The strongest pluralist objection concerns identity, trust, internal diversity and the risk that “uniformity” becomes majoritarian assimilation.

### 4. Reform routes short of a single national code

India has used several constitutional routes:

| Route | Example | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| community-specific codification | Hindu law statutes of the 1950s | not a UCC |
| optional secular route | Special Marriage Act, 1954 | opt-in and not a complete common civil code |
| targeted reform | *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) invalidated the practice of instant triple talaq; the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 followed | the case invalidated a practice, not personal-law systems wholesale |
| State legislation | Goa's family-law regime; Uttarakhand legislation | models contain their own scope and exceptions |

The 21st Law Commission's 2018 consultation paper favoured reform of discriminatory practices rather than a UCC at that stage. The 22nd Law Commission reopened consultation through an official public notice dated 14 June 2023 inviting views from the public and recognised organisations. A consultation notice is advisory process, not a final recommendation or law.

### 5. Legislative competence

Personal law falls in **Concurrent List Entry 5**. Parliament and State legislatures may legislate, subject to constitutional competence, repugnancy rules, presidential-assent questions where applicable and Fundamental Rights.

This federal position explains how a State can legislate in the field without converting its model into the constitutionally mandatory national design.

### 6. The single current linkage

The Uttarakhand UCC Act, 2024 commenced on 27 January 2025. An Amendment Ordinance was promulgated on 27 January 2026. The State's official UCC portal now publishes **The Uniform Civil Code, Uttarakhand (Amendment) Act, 2026**, Government Order No. 101/XXXVI(3)/2026/32(1)/2026, dated **7 April 2026**.

The exam-safe use is narrow:

```text
Article 44 objective
        |
Concurrent List legislative action
        |
enactment -> commencement -> later amendment
        |
constitutional implementation is a process,
not proof that one State design is nationally required
```

Do not cite the superseded Ordinance as the current amending instrument. Do not invent litigation outcomes, implementation statistics or unverified clause details.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Uniformity threatens religious and cultural pluralism.

**Reply:** Equality-oriented civil reform need not erase belief or worship, and Article 25 itself permits social reform. Consultation, neutral drafting and protection of genuinely diverse choices can distinguish equality from assimilation.

**Residual:** The legitimacy of a code depends on content and process. A formally uniform rule may still be substantively unequal or insensitive to diversity.

### UPSC application

For “Discuss” or “Critically examine,” separate four registers:

1. what Article 44 directs;
2. what courts held in the actual dispute;
3. what courts merely observed about UCC;
4. what reform designs and constitutional objections remain.

### Revision notes

1. Article 44 directs the State to endeavour to secure a UCC for citizens throughout India.
2. It concerns personal civil relations, not criminal law generally.
3. Article 44 is non-justiciable.
4. Courts have urged legislative attention but have not ordered Parliament to enact a UCC.
5. Equality and religious freedom must both be analysed.
6. Article 25 is subject to other Part III provisions and social reform.
7. Community codification and the Special Marriage Act are not themselves a UCC.
8. *Shayara Bano* (2017) invalidated instant triple talaq as a practice, and the 2019 Act followed; neither event abolished personal-law systems wholesale.
9. Personal law is in Concurrent List Entry 5.
10. A State model does not settle the required national design.
11. The 2026 Uttarakhand Amendment Act superseded the January Ordinance as the current amending instrument.
12. Constitutional legitimacy depends on content, consultation and rights compliance.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the existence of Article 44 not permit a court simply to order Parliament to enact a uniform civil code?

**Model answer:** Article 44 states a non-justiciable legislative objective under Article 37. Courts may decide rights disputes and comment on legislative inaction, but code design involves policy choices, consultation, competence and balancing of equality and pluralism that remain primarily legislative.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating judicial observations favouring reform as a mandamus to enact one specified code.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Article 44 is an equality-oriented constitutional objective whose legitimacy depends on pluralist and consultative implementation. Critically examine. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 44 directs the State to endeavour to secure a uniform civil code across India. Its equality rationale arises because personal-law rules may impose unequal consequences in marriage, divorce, maintenance or succession, especially on women. Articles 14 and 15 therefore reinforce the case for reform.

Yet Article 44 is non-justiciable, and religious and cultural pluralism cannot be dismissed. Articles 25-26 protect conscience and religious affairs, although Article 25 remains subject to other Fundamental Rights and expressly permits social reform. Courts in *Shah Bano*, *Sarla Mudgal* and *John Vallamattom* urged legislative attention but did not order a UCC.

India has also pursued targeted reform: *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) invalidated the practice of instant triple talaq, and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 followed. The judgment did not invalidate personal-law systems wholesale. Community codification and optional secular marriage provide other reform routes. Personal law lies in Concurrent List Entry 5, allowing State as well as Union legislation within federal and rights limits.

Uniformity is therefore not self-justifying. A legitimate code must be rights-compliant, neutrally drafted and consultative, protecting equal citizenship without turning reform into cultural assimilation.

### 1. Article 45: early childhood care and education

The present Article 45 directs the State to endeavour to provide **early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of six years**.

The wording “until they complete six” should be preferred over the shorthand “below six.” The provision addresses the period before the Article 21A right to free and compulsory education for ages six to fourteen.

The constitutional education sequence is:

```text
birth to completion of six: Article 45 DPSP
six to fourteen: Article 21A Fundamental Right
parent/guardian opportunity duty: Article 51A(k)
```

### 2. Article 48: science and husbandry

Article 48 directs the State to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on **modern and scientific lines**. It then specifies breed preservation and improvement and prohibition of slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle.

Its two limbs must be kept together. Scientific organisation may involve productivity, animal health, breeding, sustainability and farming systems. The cattle-protection limb does not exhaust the Article.

### 3. Article 48A: environment, forests and wildlife

Article 48A directs the State to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife. It operates with:

- Article 21's judicially recognised environmental dimension;
- Article 51A(g), the citizen's environmental duty;
- environmental statutes such as the Water Act, Air Act, Environment (Protection) Act and wildlife/forest legislation.

The constitutional triangle is:

```text
Art 21: enforceable life/dignity hook
           /                 \
          /                   \
Art 48A: State direction ---- Art 51A(g): citizen duty
```

Detailed principles such as polluter pays and precaution belong to Environment. Here the focus is constitutional interaction.

### 4. Article 49: nationally important heritage

Article 49 makes it the State's obligation to protect every monument, place or object of artistic or historic interest **declared by or under law made by Parliament to be of national importance** from spoliation, disfigurement, destruction, removal, disposal or export. The **Seventh Amendment Act, 1956** substituted this formulation for the narrower words “declared by Parliament by law.”

The parliamentary declaration qualifier is essential. Not every old structure automatically falls within Article 49's exact wording. The Ancient and Historical Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains (Declaration of National Importance) Act, 1951 (**AHMASR Declaration Act, 1951**) served as a declaration/listing law under the earlier regime. The Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958 (**AMASR Act, 1958**) created the broader protective framework; under its statutory transition, the 1951 Act ceased to have effect for monuments and sites covered by the 1958 Act, subject to preservation of earlier acts or omissions. Cite the 1951 law for its historical declaratory role and the 1958 Act for the consolidated protection regime.

### 5. Article 50: judicial separation

Article 50 directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive **in the public services of the State**. Its immediate focus is the subordinate judicial and magistracy structure, not a claim that India has a rigid presidential separation among all organs.

The directive supports judicial independence by reducing executive control over judicial functions at the State level. The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 was the major historical procedural vehicle for separating judicial and executive magistracy. It has since been replaced by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, in force from 1 July 2024; therefore, cite the CrPC historically and the BNSS as the current criminal-procedure framework. Detailed appointment, control and court administration belong to the judiciary topics.

### 6. Article 51: four international directions

The State shall endeavour to:

1. promote international peace and security;
2. maintain just and honourable relations between nations;
3. foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in dealings of organised peoples;
4. encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration.

Article 51 does not make every treaty automatically enforceable in domestic courts. Domestic effect depends on constitutional structure, legislation and the nature of the obligation. The Article supplies a foreign-policy and interpretive direction.

### 7. UDHR resonance without textual overclaim

The Preamble, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties share several ideals with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948: dignity, equality, social security, education, humane conditions and responsible social participation. The safe formulation is **constitutional resonance**, not article-by-article incorporation. The UDHR did not amend Part IV, and every Indian constitutional claim must still be anchored in the actual Article.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The liberal-intellectual category is too broad to have analytical value.

**Reply:** Its common thread is a modern constitutional State organised around education, scientific governance, institutional separation, heritage, ecological stewardship and international legal order.

**Residual:** The common label should not hide distinct legal mechanisms. Article 49 has a parliamentary-declaration qualifier; Article 50 has a public-services scope; Article 51 does not itself incorporate treaties.

- **inserted Article 43A** on worker participation in management;

Textbooks sometimes say it “added four new Directive Principles.” That is useful shorthand but legally imprecise: Article 39(f) already existed and was replaced; the other three Articles were newly inserted.

The same Amendment separately inserted Fundamental Duties in Part IVA and attempted to extend Article 31C to all Directive Principles. The latter extension was later invalidated in *Minerva Mills*.

### 2. The 44th Amendment: Article 38(2), not a new Article 38

The 44th Amendment renumbered the existing Article 38 as clause (1) and added clause (2), directing the State to minimise income inequalities and endeavour to eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities among individuals and groups across areas and vocations.

Write **“added Article 38(2)”**, not “added Article 38.”

The 44th Amendment also removed the Fundamental Right to property and altered the text of Article 31C by deleting the reference to Article 31, leaving the present operative rights shield concerned with Articles 14 and 19.

### 3. The 86th Amendment: the education triangle

The 86th Amendment made three connected changes:

| Provision | Change |
|---|---|
| Article 21A | inserted a Fundamental Right to free and compulsory education for ages six to fourteen |
| Article 45 | substituted with early childhood care and education until completion of six |
| Article 51A(k) | inserted a parent/guardian duty to provide educational opportunity to a child or ward aged six to fourteen |

This is not merely a transfer of Article 45 into Part III. It creates a differentiated age-based architecture involving State right/duty and citizen duty.

### 4. The 97th Amendment: Article 43B

The 97th Amendment inserted Article 43B on voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies. It also made other cooperative-related changes.

In *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (2021), the Supreme Court struck down Part IXB insofar as it applies to single-State cooperative societies for want of ratification by at least half the States under the proviso to Article 368(2). Part IXB survives for multi-State cooperative societies. Article 43B was not invalidated.

### 5. Amendment chronology and doctrinal chronology differ

An Amendment Act's year, enactment and commencement may differ. For standard answers, identify the Amendment and legal operation first. Add commencement dates only where the question requires them and the official text has been checked.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Later amendments converted Part IV into an incoherent accumulation of policy preferences.

**Reply:** The additions respond to identifiable constitutional developments: access to justice, workplace voice, environmental protection, inequality, education and cooperative governance.

**Residual:** Expansion increases the need for precise ownership and status control. A new Article does not guarantee implementation, and a judicial ruling on a related Part does not necessarily invalidate the DPSP.

### 1. Why the framers divided rights

Constitutional Adviser B.N. Rau recommended dividing rights into:

- justiciable rights, which became Part III; and
- non-justiciable principles, which became Part IV.

The framers' reasons included:

1. inadequate financial resources at independence;
2. severe social and regional diversity and backwardness;
3. the need to let elected governments choose order, time, place and method;
4. concern that courts could not design or supervise every distributive policy.

This was a pragmatic sequencing decision, not a declaration that socio-economic justice was unimportant.

### 2. The real sanction: democratic accountability

Article 37 supplies constitutional duty, but public opinion supplies much of the practical sanction. Ambedkar argued that a government resting on popular vote could not lightly ignore Part IV and would answer to the electorate.

The opposition, legislatures, audit institutions, media and civil society can use Part IV as a standard for evaluating whether governments pursue nutrition, education, equality, local self-government and access to justice.

### 5. Why judicial enforcement is not a complete solution

Making every directive directly enforceable would not automatically secure welfare. Courts cannot by themselves raise revenue, build administrative capacity or decide all trade-offs among health, housing, education and employment. Judicial enforceability can secure minimums and fair procedure; durable socio-economic delivery requires institutions and budgets.

**Objection:** Political sanction is too weak where deprived groups possess little electoral influence.

**Reply:** Statutory entitlements, judicial interpretation, decentralisation, commissions and transparent budgeting can strengthen the political model without transforming every directive into an unlimited judicial claim.

**Residual:** Unequal voice and weak implementation remain serious. The constitutional design explains non-justiciability; it does not excuse persistent governmental failure.

*The conflict was never simply “rights versus welfare”; it was also a dispute over amendment power and judicial review.*

### 1. Champakam Dorairajan

In *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), the Supreme Court held that an inconsistent Directive Principle could not override a Fundamental Right. Part IV had to operate within Part III. The ruling established early priority for enforceable rights in ordinary-law conflict.

The First Amendment followed. It inserted Article 15(4), enabling special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes and Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The amendment history shows that Parliament often pursued Directive-Principle objectives by changing constitutional rights architecture rather than ignoring the Court.

### 2. Land reform and property conflict

Redistributive laws repeatedly faced property and equality challenges. The First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments expanded constitutional protection for land-reform measures through Articles 31A, 31B and the Ninth Schedule.

These measures have distinct doctrines. Article 31C is not a synonym for the Ninth Schedule. The full land-reform and Ninth-Schedule review structure belongs to Fundamental Rights and Basic Structure; this lesson tracks the DPSP conflict.

### 3. Golak Nath

In *I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab* (1967), the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights to abridge them. This threatened Parliament's ability to constitutionally protect redistributive legislation designed to implement Part IV.

The ruling shifted the controversy:

```text
ordinary law conflict
        became
scope of constituent/amending power
```

### 4. The 24th Amendment

The 24th Amendment affirmed Parliament's power under Article 368 to amend any constitutional provision, including Fundamental Rights, and made presidential assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill mandatory.

This did not give Parliament unlimited power. The basic-structure limitation emerged in the next stage.

### 5. The 25th Amendment and original Article 31C

The 25th Amendment inserted Article 31C. Its original design contained:

1. a shield preventing laws giving effect to Article 39(b) or 39(c) from being void for inconsistency with Articles 14, 19 or then-Article 31; and
2. a clause seeking to bar courts from questioning whether a declaratory law genuinely gave effect to that policy; and
3. a surviving proviso under which a law made by a State Legislature receives Article 31C protection only if it was reserved for the President's consideration and received presidential assent.

Article 31C was therefore not general DPSP supremacy. It was a specific protection for two distributive clauses and an attempted restriction of judicial review.

### 6. Institutional stakes

| Position | Constitutional concern |
|---|---|
| unrestricted rights priority | may block redistribution and social reform |
| unrestricted DPSP priority | may extinguish enforceable liberty and equality |
| uncontrolled amendment power | may allow destruction of constitutional limits |
| complete judicial finality | may freeze social reform against democratic correction |

The later doctrine sought balance among these concerns.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Parliament used amendments merely to evade judicial decisions.

**Reply:** Constitutional amendment is a legitimate democratic response where ordinary law encounters constitutional limits. The decisive question is whether the amendment preserves the Constitution's basic structure.

**Residual:** Repeated amendment can also entrench majoritarian power. Judicial review of amendment limits is therefore essential.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Trace the constitutional movement from *Champakam Dorairajan* to the creation of Article 31C. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Parliament responded through the First Amendment, including Article 15(4), and through property and land-reform protections developed by the First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments.

The 24th Amendment restored Parliament's asserted constituent power to amend Fundamental Rights. The 25th Amendment then inserted Article 31C. Its first limb shielded laws giving effect to Article 39(b)-(c) from Articles 14, 19 and then-Article 31; its second sought to prevent courts from testing whether the law genuinely served that policy. For a State law, the surviving proviso additionally requires reservation for the President's consideration and presidential assent before the Article 31C shield can apply.

The chronology thus moved from ordinary-law priority to amendment power and judicial-review limits, setting the stage for *Kesavananda Bharati*.

*Nexus review of an ordinary law and basic-structure review of a constitutional amendment are distinct.*

### 1. Kesavananda Bharati

In *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973), the Supreme Court:

- upheld the first limb of original Article 31C protecting genuine Article 39(b)-(c) laws; and
- invalidated the clause that attempted to make a legislative declaration conclusive and exclude judicial examination.

Judicial review could not be eliminated. Courts retained power to test whether a law genuinely had the required connection with Article 39(b) or 39(c).

### 2. The 42nd Amendment's extension

The 42nd Amendment replaced the specific reference to Article 39(b)-(c) with language extending Article 31C protection to laws implementing **any Directive Principle**. This would have placed all Part IV objectives above Articles 14 and 19 within the shield.

### 3. The 44th Amendment's textual effect

The 44th Amendment removed the Fundamental Right to property and amended Article 31C's rights reference from Articles 14, 19 and 31 to **Articles 14 and 19**.

The official Constitution may print the 42nd Amendment's “all or any principles in Part IV” words in the main text with a footnote recording their invalidation. The operative legal scope must therefore be reconstructed through the original text and case law: **Article 39(b) and Article 39(c)**.

### 4. The surviving State-law proviso

Where a law is made by a State Legislature, Article 31C does not apply unless the law has been reserved for the President's consideration and has received presidential assent. Reservation and assent are **necessary conditions for the State law to claim the shield**, not a conclusive certificate of constitutionality. They do not prove a genuine Article 39(b)/(c) nexus, cure lack of legislative competence, remove review under other applicable constitutional provisions or transform a welfare recital into operative protection.

### 5. Minerva Mills

In *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980), the Court invalidated the 42nd Amendment's all-DPSP extension. It held that harmony and balance between Parts III and IV form part of the Constitution's basic structure.

The Court's attributed metaphor is exam-useful: Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are **“two wheels of a chariot, one no less than the other.”** The goals in Part IV must be pursued without destroying the rights-based constitutional means in Part III.

The judgment's core proposition is not that Part III always defeats Part IV. It is that neither may be given absolute primacy:

```text
rights provide constitutional means
directives provide constitutional goals
destroy either -> damage constitutional harmony
```

The original 39(b)-(c) shield survives.

### 6. Property Owners Association

In *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024; 2024 INSC 835), a **nine-judge Constitution Bench** confirmed that the unamended Article 31C continues to exist after *Minerva Mills*.

It also held:

- Article 31C protects a law only after a genuine nexus with Article 39(b) or 39(c) is established;
- not every private resource automatically qualifies as a material resource of the community;
- some private resources may qualify after contextual examination;
- Article 31C then protects the law against Articles 14 and 19.

### 7. Ordinary-law review versus amendment review

This distinction is essential:

| Question | Review |
|---|---|
| Does an ordinary law genuinely implement 39(b)/(c)? | courts review the nexus and applicable constitutional limits |
| Can a legislative declaration conclusively exclude that inquiry? | no; *Kesavananda* rejected the ouster |
| Can a State law claim the shield without reservation and presidential assent? | no; the surviving proviso makes both prerequisites |
| Does presidential assent establish nexus or cure every defect? | no; nexus, competence and other applicable constitutional limits remain reviewable |
| Can a constitutional amendment expand or redesign the shield? | Parliament may amend, but basic-structure review applies |
| Is every ordinary law independently invalid merely because it is said to offend “basic structure”? | do not state this; ordinary-law review must use the applicable constitutional provisions and doctrines |

### 8. Present position

The safest formulation is:

> Parts III and IV are constitutionally complementary; their harmony is basic structure. Genuine laws implementing Article 39(b) or 39(c) receive the specific Article 31C shield against Articles 14 and 19, subject to judicial examination of the required nexus. A State law additionally requires reservation for the President's consideration and presidential assent, neither of which cures other constitutional defects. Constitutional amendments remain limited by the basic structure.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Article 31C permits the State to evade equality and freedom merely by citing redistribution.

**Reply:** The shield is narrow, limited to 39(b)-(c), and depends on a genuine connection that courts may examine. It is not a blanket immunity for every welfare-labelled law.

**Residual:** Once the shield applies, Articles 14 and 19 cannot invalidate the law. Precision in defining resources, common good and nexus therefore remains constitutionally important.

### Concept check

**Model answer:** A court reviewing an ordinary law under Article 31C asks whether it genuinely implements Article 39(b) or 39(c) and whether other applicable constitutional limits remain satisfied. A State law must also satisfy the reservation-and-presidential-assent proviso, which does not itself cure other defects. Basic-structure review asks whether a constitutional amendment damages foundational features.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying that every ordinary law is directly tested against basic structure or that an Article 31C declaration ends judicial review.

The 42nd Amendment extended protection to all DPSP, but *Minerva Mills* invalidated that extension and held that harmony between Parts III and IV is basic structure. The 44th Amendment removed the former Article 31 reference.

Thus courts may verify the 39(b)/(c) connection, while constitutional amendments remain basic-structure constrained. Article 31C balances redistribution with review; it does not constitutionalise any welfare label.

### 1. The standard comparison—qualified

| General tendency | Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|---|
| legal form | directly enforceable constitutional rights | non-justiciable State directions |
| principal function | protect liberty, equality and political democracy | pursue welfare and social-economic democracy |
| typical orientation | restrain unconstitutional action | require public action and institution-building |
| sanction | judicial remedy and invalidation | political, legislative and interpretive |
| implementation | some operate directly; many still need institutions/law | generally require law, policy and administration |

The words “negative” and “positive” are useful only as tendencies. Fundamental Rights may impose positive duties, as with legal aid, custodial protection and emergency care. Directive Principles can support restraints on harmful concentration, exploitation or intoxicants.

### 2. Fundamental Duties differ from State duties

Directive Principles primarily direct the **State**. Fundamental Duties in Article 51A apply to **citizens**. Both are non-justiciable, but their subjects and functions differ.

A legislature may enact precise laws supporting either. The law, not the bare non-justiciable clause, creates enforceable consequences and remains subject to rights, competence and proportionality.

### 3. Education as constitutional conversion

The education sequence illustrates interaction:

1. original Article 45 directed free and compulsory education up to fourteen;
2. *Unni Krishnan* read a bounded education right through Article 21 informed by Part IV;
3. the 86th Amendment inserted Article 21A for ages six to fourteen;
4. Article 45 was recast for early childhood;
5. Article 51A(k) added the parent/guardian duty;
6. the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 supplied statutory detail.

This is constitutional conversion through interpretation, amendment and statute—not proof that every DPSP automatically becomes a Fundamental Right.

### 4. Environment as constitutional convergence

```text
STATE DIRECTION: Article 48A
             +
CITIZEN DUTY: Article 51A(g)
             +
ENFORCEABLE HOOK: Article 21
             +
ENVIRONMENTAL STATUTES
             =
multi-source environmental constitutionalism
```

Each component does different work. Article 21 supplies an enforceable rights route; Articles 48A and 51A(g) inform constitutional purpose; statutes define institutions, standards and offences.

### 5. Legal aid as another bridge

Article 39A directs equal justice. *M.H. Hoskot* and *Hussainara Khatoon* connect free legal aid with fair procedure under Article 21, while the 1987 Act establishes delivery. The directive does not vanish; it supplies the constitutional objective that rights and statute operationalise.

### 6. Harmony does not erase boundaries

Courts cannot use harmony to:

- amend clear constitutional text judicially;
- create unlimited distributive entitlements without legal standards;
- treat duties as independent offences;
- disregard express Fundamental Rights;
- declare every welfare policy reasonable without proportionality.

### Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Judicial harmonisation lets courts convert policy into rights without democratic authorisation.

**Reply:** The strongest examples connect Part IV to existing rights text such as fair procedure and dignity, while legislation and amendment supply durable institutional form.

**Residual:** Boundary disputes remain. Courts should protect constitutional minimums without assuming continuous control of budgetary policy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the formula “FR are negative; DPSP are positive” useful but incomplete?

**Model answer:** It captures the dominant orientation—rights restrain unconstitutional State action while DPSP direct welfare action. But rights can require positive protection and services, while directives can support restraints on exploitation or concentration. Exact text and doctrine must qualify the shorthand.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a textbook comparison as an exceptionless constitutional rule.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties are distinct constitutional instruments, not competing lists. Discuss. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

The three Parts allocate different constitutional roles. Fundamental Rights in Part III create enforceable claims protecting equality, liberty and political democracy. Directive Principles in Part IV guide the State towards social-economic democracy but are not directly enforceable. Fundamental Duties in Part IVA address responsible citizenship and likewise lack direct enforcement.

Their distinction does not imply isolation. Article 39A informs the Article 21 guarantee of fair legal procedure and is institutionalised through legal-services legislation. Article 48A and Article 51A(g) reinforce the environmental dimension of Article 21. Education moved from the original Article 45 through *Unni Krishnan* to Article 21A, recast Article 45, Article 51A(k) and the RTE Act.

Yet boundaries matter. A duty is not an offence without law; a DPSP does not automatically become a right; and welfare purpose does not defeat rights review.

The Constitution therefore combines enforceable freedom, governing objectives and civic responsibility. Harmony means coordinated operation with distinct legal consequences.

### 2. Historical law lists require current-status discipline

Older textbooks correctly show the historical movement from directive to legislation, but several labour enactments have later been consolidated, replaced or made subject to commencement arrangements. The safe exam method is:

1. identify the constitutional directive;
2. name a statute only if its current status is known;
3. distinguish enactment, commencement, repeal and continuing rules;
4. avoid presenting a historical vehicle as current merely because it appears in a standard list.

This status discipline is especially important for wages, industrial relations, occupational safety and social-security laws.

The bounded examples used here have stable legal identities: the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987; the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009; the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974; the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981; the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972; the 1951 national-importance declaration law; and the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958. Their existence should still be distinguished from rules, institutional capacity, compliance and outcomes.

### 4. Article 40 and the local-government boundary

The 73rd Amendment is the clearest institutional implementation of Article 40. But constitutional status did not automatically transfer functions, functionaries and funds. Article 243G is enabling and devolution varies by State.

The 74th Amendment created the urban local-government system, important to devolution but not the textual implementation of the village-panchayat directive. This distinction prevents attractive but inaccurate answers.

### 5. Social Justice applies what Polity defines

Part IV owns the constitutional norms. Social Justice topics examine whether food, health, education, labour and vulnerable-section systems convert those norms into capabilities.

The analytical chain is:

```text
constitutional objective
-> statutory entitlement or policy
-> institution and finance
-> access
-> service quality
-> human outcome
```

An answer that lists schemes without this mechanism is descriptive rather than analytical.

### 6. Directives outside Part IV

Three commonly identified non-justiciable directions outside Part IV are:

- Article 335: SC/ST claims in services consistently with administrative efficiency;
- Article 350A: facilities for mother-tongue instruction at the primary stage for linguistic-minority children;
- Article 351: Union duty to promote and develop Hindi.

They are not Articles 36-51 and should not be inserted into the Part IV article count. They are useful boundary examples showing that constitutional direction is not confined to one Part.

### 1. What Part IV has achieved

Part IV has changed the constitutional baseline in at least five ways:

1. **Policy legitimacy:** redistribution, public services and social protection are constitutional purposes rather than discretionary charity.
2. **Institution-building:** legal-services authorities, Panchayati Raj and welfare systems connect directives to administration.
3. **Rights development:** legal aid, education, health and environment have influenced Article 21 doctrine.
4. **Amendment:** the Constitution has expanded access to justice, environmental responsibility, education and cooperation.
5. **Democratic evaluation:** governments can be judged against enduring standards beyond one manifesto.

### 2. Why implementation remains uneven

| Constraint | Mechanism of failure |
|---|---|
| fiscal capacity | Article 41 itself recognises economic limits; weak revenue narrows provision |
| federal variation | health, education, prohibition, local government and welfare depend heavily on State institutions |
| incomplete devolution | local bodies may lack functions, staff and funds |
| administrative capacity | vacancies, weak frontline systems and procedural complexity obstruct access |
| social hierarchy | caste, gender, disability and location can block formally available services |
| policy fragmentation | separate schemes fail to converge around the person |
| accountability deficit | output counts replace quality, remedy and outcome assessment |

### 3. Rights-based welfare and public investment

Rights-based welfare requires more than using the word “right.” It needs:

- a defined right-holder and duty-bearer;
- adequate public investment;
- service standards;
- accessible grievance and review;
- coordination across departments and levels;
- data that measures outcome rather than announcement.

This is why non-integrated governance can defeat even a statutory entitlement.

### 4. Federalism is both constraint and laboratory

Many directives are implemented through fields in which States possess substantial responsibility. Variation may produce unequal outcomes, but it also permits experimentation in local government, health, education and social reform.

The constitutional response is not automatic centralisation. It is clearer minimum standards, predictable finance, cooperative federalism, transparent comparison and respect for legislative competence.

### 5. Economic democracy

Ambedkar's phrase **economic democracy** captures the final purpose: citizenship should not be hollowed out by deprivation, domination or exclusion. Economic democracy does not require equal outcomes in every respect. It requires institutions that prevent extreme concentration, secure fair opportunity and protect a dignified social minimum.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Block A - Text, nature and classification

### Cumulative check A1 - Article 37

**Question:** A petitioner asks the Supreme Court to order the government to provide a job solely because Article 41 mentions the right to work. What constitutional distinctions must be made?

**Model answer:** Article 41 is a non-justiciable Directive Principle and is expressly conditioned by economic capacity and development. It creates a State law-making and policy duty, not by itself an Article 32 employment remedy. An enforceable claim would require a relevant Fundamental Right, statute or binding legal duty.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the phrase “right to work” in Part IV as identical to a directly enforceable Fundamental Right.

### Cumulative check A2 - classification

**Question:** Explain why Articles 43, 47 and 48 cannot be reliably remembered through an exclusive one-Article-one-category method.

**Model answer:** Article 43 combines socialistic living-wage objectives with Gandhian cottage industry. Article 47 combines public health and nutrition with prohibition. Article 48 combines modern scientific agriculture and husbandry with cattle protection. The three categories are overlapping textbook aids, not constitutional compartments.

**Misconception to avoid:** Correcting an overlap by deleting one textual limb or assigning constitutional status to the classification.

**Question:** State the four major Part IV amendment families without describing every change as an insertion.

**Model answer:** The 42nd Amendment substituted Article 39(f) and inserted Articles 39A, 43A and 48A. The 44th inserted Article 38(2). The 86th substituted Article 45 while inserting Articles 21A and 51A(k). The 97th inserted Article 43B.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying that the 44th inserted Article 38 or that the 42nd newly inserted Article 39(f).

### Cumulative check B2 - Article 31C

**Question:** A State law merely declares that it serves Article 39(b) and claims total immunity from constitutional review. Identify the errors.

**Model answer:** A declaration is not conclusive. Courts may examine whether the law genuinely has a nexus with Article 39(b) or 39(c). Because it is a State law, reservation for the President's consideration and presidential assent are additional prerequisites. Even assent does not prove nexus or cure competence and other constitutional defects. Basic-structure review separately governs constitutional amendments.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a welfare recital as self-proving nexus or applying basic structure to ordinary law as an undefined free-standing test.

## Block C - Institutional conversion

### Cumulative check C1 - legal aid network

**Question:** Distinguish Article 39A, NALSA and a Lok Adalat in one chain.

**Model answer:** Article 39A is the non-justiciable constitutional direction for equal justice and free legal aid. NALSA is a statutory national authority under the 1987 Act coordinating legal services. An ordinary Lok Adalat is a statutory consensual settlement forum whose award follows compromise and is final and binding under the Act.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling NALSA constitutional or describing ordinary Lok Adalat members as judges deciding merits without settlement.

### Cumulative check C2 - local government boundary

**Question:** Why is the statement “Articles 40 and the 74th Amendment constitutionalised local government” imprecise?

**Model answer:** Article 40 specifically directs organisation of village panchayats, and the 73rd Amendment's Part IX is its direct institutional expression. The 74th Amendment separately constitutionalises urban municipalities under Part IXA; it is part of local-government reform but not the textual implementation of Article 40.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating rural and urban local government as one undifferentiated constitutional route.

## Block D - Final synthesis

### Cumulative check D1 - welfare and rights

**Question:** Why does constitutional harmony reject both unlimited Fundamental Rights priority and unlimited Directive-Principles priority?

**Model answer:** Rights provide enforceable means protecting liberty and equality; directives state constitutional goals of social-economic justice. Absolute rights priority can obstruct transformation, while absolute DPSP priority can destroy protected freedom. *Minerva Mills* therefore treats them as “two wheels of a chariot, one no less than the other,” whose harmony and balance form part of basic structure.

**Misconception to avoid:** Concluding that harmony means every conflict produces an equal compromise or that neither Part ever prevails under a specific constitutional rule.

### Cumulative check D2 - performance

**Question:** Give a one-sentence test for whether a DPSP has been effectively implemented.

**Model answer:** A directive is effectively implemented only when valid law, adequate finance, competent institutions, accessible eligibility, accountable delivery and grievance remedies produce the intended human capability rather than merely an announcement or registration count.

**Misconception to avoid:** Measuring implementation solely by the existence of a scheme, statute or institution.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark model

**Question:** Why must the socialistic, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual classification of Directive Principles be used with constitutional caution? (10 marks, 150 words)

### Directive decoding

**Why** requires the purpose of the classification, its limits and examples proving overlap.

### Model answer

The threefold classification is a textbook method based on the content and direction of the provisions; the Constitution itself creates no such categories. It is useful because it groups social-economic justice, Gandhian reconstruction and modern institutional objectives for recall.

However, exclusive classification can distort the text. Article 43 combines a socialistic living-wage direction with Gandhian cottage industries. Article 47 combines nutrition and public health with prohibition. Article 48 combines modern scientific agriculture and animal husbandry with cattle protection. Nor does a category determine legal rank: every Part IV provision remains subject to Article 37's common non-justiciable character.

The correct exam method is therefore to state the conventional category, reproduce the exact Article and acknowledge overlap. Classification should organise the Constitution, not replace it.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies source, function, three overlaps and the common legal character.

**Quantified rubric:** **10 = non-authoritative classification 2 + usefulness 2 + three accurate overlap examples 4 + Article 37/common-rank conclusion 2.**

## Final 15-mark model

**Question:** Trace the constitutional journey from conflict to harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. (15 marks, 250 words)

### Directive decoding

**Trace** requires chronology, causal transitions and the present legal position rather than an isolated case list.

### Model answer

The initial position favoured enforceable rights. In *Champakam Dorairajan* (1951), the Supreme Court held that a Directive Principle could not override a Fundamental Right. Parliament responded through the First and later land-reform amendments.

In *Golak Nath* (1967), the Court restricted Parliament's power to abridge Fundamental Rights by amendment. The 24th Amendment reaffirmed constituent power, while the 25th inserted Article 31C to protect laws implementing Article 39(b)-(c) and attempted to exclude nexus review.

*Kesavananda Bharati* upheld the original shield but invalidated the judicial-review ouster. The 42nd Amendment then extended Article 31C to every DPSP. *Minerva Mills* struck that extension and held harmony between Parts III and IV to be basic structure. The 44th Amendment removed the former Article 31 reference.

*Property Owners Association*, decided by a nine-judge Constitution Bench, confirmed that unamended Article 31C survives: genuine 39(b)/(c) laws are protected from Articles 14 and 19, subject to judicial nexus review. A State law additionally requires reservation for presidential consideration and presidential assent, which do not cure other constitutional defects.

The settled position is therefore harmony, not abstract hierarchy, with a narrow textual exception under Article 31C.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives causal chronology, exact holdings and the current operative rule.

**Quantified rubric:** **15 = Champakam/amendment opening 3 + Golak Nath/24th/25th 4 + Kesavananda/42nd/Minerva 5 + current Article 31C position 2 + conclusion 1.**

## Final 20-mark model

**Question:** Directive Principles are simultaneously a constitutional compass, an institutional programme and a democratic performance standard. Critically evaluate. (20 marks, 250 words)

### Directive decoding

**Critically evaluate** requires proof of all three functions, counter-evidence, institutional limits and a graded verdict.

### Model answer

Directive Principles are a constitutional compass because Article 37 makes them fundamental in governance and imposes a State duty to apply them in law-making. Articles 38-39 direct justice, livelihood, distributive fairness and anti-concentration; Articles 41-47 add work, education, humane labour, nutrition and health.

They are an institutional programme because amendments and statutes translate direction into machinery. Article 39A supports the legal-services network; Article 40 informed Part IX Panchayats; Article 45 evolved with Article 21A and the RTE Act; Article 48A reinforces environmental legislation and Article 21 interpretation.

They are also a performance standard. Legislatures, opposition, courts and citizens can evaluate whether budgets and administration convert welfare promises into capability.

Criticism remains substantial. Non-justiciability permits delay; fiscal and federal variation produces unequal outcomes; institutions may record coverage without quality; and contested directives require rights-sensitive implementation. Courts cannot replace finance or administration.

Part IV has therefore not delivered complete economic democracy, but it has permanently constitutionalised welfare. Its next challenge is not adding aspirations but creating financed duties, local capacity, convergence, transparent outcomes and effective remedies.

**Why this earns marks:** It develops three distinct claims, supplies named evidence and preserves institutional limits.

**Quantified rubric:** **20 = three-part thesis 3 + constitutional compass 4 + institutional programme 5 + performance standard 3 + critical limits 3 + reform verdict 2.**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional identity and operating rule

- Part IV covers Articles 36-51.
- Source: Irish Constitution of 1937, influenced by Spain.
- Ambedkar: novel feature; Austin: Rights and DPSP together form the conscience of the Constitution.
- Purpose: welfare state and social-economic democracy, complementing political democracy.
- Article 36 imports the Part III meaning of State.
- Article 37: no court enforcement; nevertheless fundamental in governance; State duty to apply in making laws.
- Sanctions: constitutional duty, legislation, interpretation, legislative scrutiny, opposition and electoral accountability.
- Non-justiciable does not mean unconstitutional, advisory or irrelevant.

## Complete article recall

- **38(1):** welfare through a social order informed by social, economic and political justice.
- **38(2):** minimise income inequality; eliminate inequality in status, facilities and opportunities among individuals and groups across areas and vocations.
- **39(a):** adequate livelihood for citizens, men and women equally.
- **39(b):** material resources of the community distributed to subserve common good.
- **39(c):** no concentration of wealth and means of production to common detriment.
- **39(d):** equal pay for equal work for men and women.
- **39(e):** protect workers' health/strength and children's tender age; no unsuitable work through economic necessity.
- **39(f):** healthy child development in freedom and dignity; protection from exploitation and abandonment.
- **39A:** equal justice and legal aid so opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by economic or other disabilities.
- **40:** village panchayats with powers necessary for self-government.
- **41:** work, education and public assistance within economic capacity and development.
- **42:** humane work and maternity relief.
- **43:** living wage, decent life, leisure, social-cultural opportunities and rural cottage industries.
- **43A:** worker participation in industrial management.
- **43B:** voluntary, autonomous, democratic and professionally managed cooperatives; *Rajendra N. Shah* invalidated Part IXB for single-State cooperatives for want of Article 368(2) ratification, while it survives for multi-State cooperatives.
- **44:** UCC for citizens throughout India.
- **45:** early childhood care and education until completion of six.
- **46:** educational-economic interests of weaker sections, especially SC/ST, plus protection from exploitation.
- **47:** nutrition, living standard and public health as primary duties; prohibition limb.
- **48:** modern scientific agriculture/animal husbandry; breeds and specified cattle protection.
- **48A:** environment, forests and wildlife.
- **49:** nationally important monuments, places and objects declared by/under parliamentary law; the Seventh Amendment, 1956 introduced the present qualifier, while the AHMASR Declaration Act, 1951 and AMASR Act, 1958 have distinct historical/consolidated roles.
- **50:** separation of judiciary from executive in State public services; CrPC 1973 was the historical procedural vehicle and BNSS 2023 is the current criminal-procedure framework.
- **51:** peace/security; just and honourable relations; international law and treaty respect; arbitration.

- **Liberal-intellectual:** 44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51.

- First/Fourth/Seventeenth Amendments: reform and property protections.

- *Property Owners Association* (2024 INSC 835), nine-judge Constitution Bench: original Article 31C survives; nexus is reviewable; not every private asset is automatically a 39(b) resource.
