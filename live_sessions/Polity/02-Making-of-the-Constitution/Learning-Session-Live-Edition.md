# Making of the Constitution — Live Session Edition

The Constitution did not appear fully formed on 26 January 1950. It emerged through a
political demand, an indirectly constituted Assembly, specialised committees, expert
drafting, public comments, prolonged debate, adoption, signing and commencement. The
central learning problem is therefore:

> **How did a body that was not elected by universal adult franchise create a
> Constitution that claimed authority from the people of India?**

The answer requires both history and constitutional reasoning. Dates tell us what
happened; the institutional sequence explains why the resulting Constitution acquired
authority.

**Evidence convention**

- ✅ **Fact:** directly supported chronology, constitutional text or verified records.
- ⚠️ **Analysis / inference:** an evaluative conclusion drawn from those facts.
- ⚠️ **Limit:** a qualification that prevents an overbroad claim.

## Roadmap

| Lesson | Learner-facing problem | Main exam use |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Why did Indians demand a Constituent Assembly? | Demand chronology and political control over constitution-making |
| 2 | Who constituted the Assembly, and how representative was it? | Composition, election method and legitimacy |
| 3 | How did the Assembly become sovereign and perform two roles? | Office-bearers, Independence Act and institutional transition |
| 4 | What values were fixed before the clauses were drafted? | Objectives Resolution and Preamble lineage |
| 5 | Who did what in the constitution-making machinery? | Committees, subcommittees and institutional division of labour |
| 6 | How did expert advice become a constitutional draft? | B.N. Rau, Drafting Committee, Ambedkar and consultation |
| 7 | How did the draft become the adopted and signed Constitution? | Readings, amendments, deliberation and other functions |
| 8 | Why are 26 November and 26 January constitutionally different? | Articles 393–395, commencement, Republic and PYQs |
| 9 | Was the Assembly legitimate, and how should its debates be used today? | Criticism, reply, borrowing, interpretation and balanced verdict |

```text
Political demand
      ↓
Cabinet Mission scheme
      ↓
Constituent Assembly
      ↓
Objectives + committees + expert advice
      ↓
Drafting Committee and public consultation
      ↓
Three readings and adoption
      ↓
Signing and commencement
      ↓
Continuing constitutional authority
```

The sequence matters. If composition is studied without the earlier demand, the
Assembly looks like a gift from Britain. If drafting is studied without committees and
public debate, the Constitution looks like the work of one person. If adoption and
commencement are merged, several recurring UPSC questions become impossible to answer
accurately.

*The issue moved from “India needs a constitution” to “Indians must control the body
that frames it.”*

### 1. The problem before the demand

Under colonial rule, major constitutional changes came through British statutes. Indians
could negotiate, protest or participate in legislatures, but ultimate constituent power
remained outside India.

**Constituent power** means the authority to create or replace the fundamental legal
order. It is different from ordinary legislative power:

| Constituent power | Ordinary legislative power |
|---|---|
| Creates the constitutional framework | Makes law within that framework |
| Decides institutions and their limits | Exercises powers already granted |
| Claims foundational authority | Derives authority from the Constitution |

⚠️ **Analysis:** The demand for a Constituent Assembly was therefore not merely a request for another
committee. It was a demand that the people of India, through their representatives,
become authors of the basic rules under which they would be governed.

### 2. The demand develops in stages

#### 1934 — M.N. Roy

✅ **Fact:** M.N. Roy first put forward the idea of a Constituent Assembly for India in 1934. This
is a frequent factual distinction: the idea did not originate with the Cabinet Mission,
and the Mission did not create the political demand.

#### 1935 — the Congress adopts the demand

In 1935, the Indian National Congress officially demanded a Constituent Assembly to
frame India's Constitution. The movement from an individual's proposal to the demand
of the principal nationalist organisation gave it political force.

#### 1938 — Nehru defines democratic authorship

Jawaharlal Nehru stated that free India's Constitution must be framed:

- without outside interference;
- by a Constituent Assembly; and
- on the basis of adult franchise.

The democratic ideal was thus stronger than the arrangement eventually created in
1946. The eventual Assembly was indirectly elected by provincial legislatures elected
on a restricted franchise. Keep the **ideal** and the **implemented mechanism**
separate.

### 3. British responses: acceptance without immediate surrender of control

#### August Offer, 1940

The British Government accepted the demand in principle through the August Offer.
Acceptance “in principle” did not immediately create a sovereign Indian Assembly.
Britain still controlled the timing and framework.

#### Cripps proposals, 1942

Sir Stafford Cripps brought proposals for constitution-making after the Second World
War. The Muslim League rejected the arrangement because it wanted the possibility of
two autonomous states with separate Constituent Assemblies.

This reveals the emerging conflict:

```text
Nationalist demand: one free India frames its Constitution
                         │
                         versus
                         │
League demand: political separation must shape constitution-making
```

#### Cabinet Mission, 1946

The Cabinet Mission rejected the proposal for two Constituent Assemblies and supplied
the scheme under which India's Constituent Assembly was constituted in November 1946.

✅ **Fact:** The Mission consisted of Lord Pethick-Lawrence, Sir Stafford Cripps and
A.V. Alexander. It arrived in India on **24 March 1946** and published its plan on
**16 May 1946**.

### 4. What the Cabinet Mission did—and did not do

It did:

1. convert a long-standing political demand into an institutional scheme;
2. provide rules for allocating and filling seats;
3. enable constitution-making to begin before the transfer of power.

It did not:

1. fulfil Nehru's 1938 demand for direct election on adult franchise;
2. initially make the Assembly fully sovereign;
3. settle the conflict over Partition.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the Assembly arose from a British plan rather than a direct popular
vote, it could not embody Indian constituent power.

**Reply:** This objection correctly identifies the Assembly's limited origin, but origin
was not its entire constitutional history. The Assembly later became fully sovereign
under the Indian Independence Act 1947, deliberated through representative and expert
institutions, and created universal adult franchise in the Constitution itself.

**Residual limitation:** Later sovereignty does not retroactively turn the 1946
selection process into a universal election. A balanced answer should concede the
democratic deficit before explaining the later sources of legitimacy.

### Example and its limit

Think of the Cabinet Mission scheme as a bridge. The Indian demand stood on one bank;
an operational Assembly stood on the other.

The analogy explains institutional transition, but it has a limit: the British did not
merely build a neutral bridge. They designed its initial rules, while Indian political
forces disputed who could cross it and towards what national future.

### UPSC application

**Prelims focus**

- M.N. Roy: first proposal, 1934.
- INC: official demand, 1935.
- Nehru: adult-franchise formulation, 1938.
- August Offer: acceptance in principle, 1940.
- Cripps: post-war proposal, 1942.
- Cabinet Mission: operative Assembly scheme, 1946.

**Mains use**

Do not narrate the dates alone. Explain the changing location of constitutional
authority:

```text
British-made constitutional reform
            ↓
Indian demand for constituent power
            ↓
British-created initial mechanism
            ↓
Indian sovereign constitution-making
```

### Revision notes

1. A Constituent Assembly exercises foundational, not ordinary legislative, power.
2. M.N. Roy first proposed the idea in 1934.
3. The INC officially demanded it in 1935.
4. Nehru's 1938 formulation required adult franchise and freedom from outside
   interference.
5. The August Offer accepted the demand only in principle.
6. The Cripps proposals contemplated post-war constitution-making.
7. The Muslim League sought two Constituent Assemblies.
8. The Cabinet Mission arrived on 24 March 1946 and published its one-Assembly scheme
   on 16 May 1946.
9. The 1946 mechanism did not match the ideal of direct adult-franchise election.
10. Limited origin and later sovereignty must both appear in a balanced answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to say that the Cabinet Mission “gave Indians the
idea” of a Constituent Assembly?

**Model answer:** The demand predated the Mission: M.N. Roy proposed it in 1934, the
Congress adopted it in 1935, and Nehru supplied an adult-franchise formulation in 1938.
The Cabinet Mission's role was institutional, not intellectual: it converted the
existing demand into the 1946 scheme for constituting an Assembly.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate the origin of an idea with the later plan that
made it operational.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Trace the evolution of the demand for a Constituent
Assembly between 1934 and 1946. What central political principle did the demand express?

**Complete model response:**

The demand for a Constituent Assembly expressed the principle that Indians, rather than
the British Parliament, should exercise constituent power. M.N. Roy first proposed the
idea in 1934, and the Congress made it an official demand in 1935. In 1938, Jawaharlal
Nehru sharpened its democratic content by insisting on an Assembly elected through
adult franchise and free from outside interference.

The British accepted the demand only in principle through the August Offer of 1940.
The Cripps proposals of 1942 promised post-war constitution-making but failed amid,
among other differences, the Muslim League's demand for two Assemblies. The Cabinet
Mission of 1946 rejected that demand and provided the scheme under which one Assembly
was constituted.

Thus, the institutional route remained imperfectly democratic, but the underlying
claim was clear: legitimate constitutional authority had to move from imperial grant
to Indian self-authorship.

*“Indirectly elected” explains who elected the provincial representatives; “partly
nominated” explains how princely-state representatives were to enter.*

### 1. Allocation principle

✅ **Fact:** The Cabinet Mission scheme fixed a total strength of **389**:

- **296** for British India:
  - 292 from the eleven Governors' Provinces;
  - 4 from the Chief Commissioners' Provinces;
- **93** for the princely states.

The eleven Governors' Provinces were Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, Central
Provinces, Orissa, Punjab, North-West Frontier Province, Sindh, Bengal and Assam. The
four Chief Commissioners' Provinces were Delhi, Ajmer-Merwara, Coorg and British
Baluchistan.

Seats were broadly allocated in proportion to population, at roughly **one seat per
million persons**.

Within each British Indian province, seats were divided among three principal
categories—**Muslims, Sikhs and General**—in proportion to their population.

### 2. Election was indirect

Members of each community in a provincial legislative assembly elected that
community's representatives to the Constituent Assembly. Voting used:

> **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote**

This method aimed to reflect more than a simple winner-takes-all majority inside each
electoral category.

However, the provincial assemblies themselves had been elected on the limited franchise
of the colonial period, based substantially on qualifications such as property, tax and
education. The Constituent Assembly was therefore not the outcome of universal adult
franchise.

### 3. Nomination was a separate feature

Representatives of the princely states were to be nominated by their rulers. The
Assembly was consequently:

| Description | Why it applies |
|---|---|
| Indirectly elected | Provincial voters did not directly elect Assembly members |
| Partly elected | British Indian provincial seats used an electoral process |
| Partly nominated | Princely-state representatives were chosen by rulers |
| Not elected on adult franchise | The underlying provincial electorate was restricted |

These descriptions overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

### 4. The 1946 result

Elections for the 296 British Indian seats were held in July–August 1946:

| Group | Seats |
|---|---:|
| Indian National Congress | 208 |
| Muslim League | 73 |
| Smaller groups and independents | 15 |
| **Total** | **296** |

The 93 princely-state seats were initially unfilled because the states stayed away.
They joined gradually later.

The election result created two simultaneous realities:

1. the Congress had an overwhelming numerical advantage;
2. the League possessed a substantial, concentrated mandate among Muslim seats before
   its boycott and Partition changed participation.

### 5. Social breadth and its limits

The Assembly contained Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Parsis, Anglo-Indians, Indian
Christians, Scheduled Castes, tribal representatives and women. Most major public
figures were present, but **Mahatma Gandhi was not a member**.

A contemporary community table for the initial 296 British Indian seats recorded:

| Community | Seats |
|---|---:|
| Hindus | 163 |
| Muslims | 80 |
| Scheduled Castes | 31 |
| Indian Christians | 6 |
| Backward Tribes | 6 |
| Sikhs | 4 |
| Anglo-Indians | 3 |
| Parsis | 3 |

**Qualification:** This table totals 296 and should not be misread as the composition
of the later 299-member post-Partition Assembly.

### 6. Representation is not one single test

⚠️ **Analysis:** Three tests must be separated:

| Test | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Electoral representativeness | Weak: no direct universal-adult-franchise election |
| Social presence | Broader: many religious and social groups were present |
| Deliberative representation | Significant but unequal: committees and debate created channels, though participation deficits remained |

The phrase “representative Assembly” is therefore incomplete unless the test being used
is identified.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A body elected through restricted provincial legislatures and nominated
by princes could not truthfully speak in the name of “We, the People.”

**Reply:** The electoral criticism is valid. Yet a fresh all-India election in 1946
would have faced missing universal rolls, communal conflict, unsettled princely-state
relations and impending Partition. The Assembly partially compensated through social
breadth, committee participation, open debate and the decision to constitutionalise
universal adult franchise immediately.

**Residual limitation:** Administrative difficulty is not proof that direct election
was logically impossible. It explains the constraint; it does not erase the deficit.

### Example and its limit

Imagine a university constitution drafted by representatives elected by departmental
councils rather than every student. The process is indirect, but it may still include
many interests and produce a generally accepted charter.

The analogy helps distinguish electoral method from substantive acceptance. Its limit
is scale and power: India's Constitution founded a sovereign political order, not a
voluntary association.

### UPSC application

**Prelims traps**

- 389 was the planned strength; 299 was the post-Partition strength.
- 296 meant British India, not only Governors' Provinces.
- Provincial representatives were elected; princely-state representatives were
  nominated.
- PR–STV was used by community members in provincial legislatures.
- Gandhi was not a member.

**Mains argument**

Use a concession–reply structure:

```text
Concede restricted and indirect election
                 ↓
Show social and ideological breadth
                 ↓
Explain committees and deliberation
                 ↓
Add post-1947 sovereignty
                 ↓
Conclude: imperfect mandate, substantial deliberative legitimacy
```

### Revision notes

1. The planned strength was 389.
2. British India received 296 seats; princely states received 93.
3. The 296 included 292 provincial and 4 Chief Commissioners' seats.
4. Allocation was broadly one seat per million population.
5. Provincial seats were divided among Muslim, Sikh and General categories.
6. Provincial representatives were indirectly elected through PR–STV.
7. Princely-state representatives were nominated by rulers.
8. The Congress won 208, the League 73 and others 15 of the 296 elected seats.
9. Princely states initially stayed away.
10. The Assembly included many communities and women, but Gandhi was not a member.
11. Electoral, social and deliberative representation are different tests.
12. Restricted franchise is a real democratic limitation, not a fact to conceal.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why must “indirectly elected” and “partly nominated” be stated separately
when describing the Constituent Assembly?

**Model answer:** “Indirectly elected” refers to provincial representatives being
chosen by members of provincial legislatures rather than directly by the people.
“Partly nominated” refers to the separate method for princely-state representatives,
who were chosen by their rulers. One describes an electoral chain; the other describes
a non-electoral route.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say all members were nominated merely because the
Assembly was not directly elected.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** “The Constituent Assembly was deficient in electoral
representativeness but not devoid of representative legitimacy.” Examine.

**Complete model response:**

The Constituent Assembly did not satisfy the modern electoral test of representation.
Its provincial members were indirectly elected by legislatures based on a restricted
colonial franchise, while princely-state representatives were nominated by rulers.
The Congress also held 208 of the 296 elected seats, and the League's boycott later
weakened Muslim participation.

Yet electoral form is not the only dimension of representation. The Assembly included
Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Parsis, Anglo-Indians, Indian Christians, Scheduled Castes,
tribal representatives and women. Its committee system created structured channels for
fundamental-rights, minority and tribal concerns. Congress dominance also contained
substantial ideological and provincial diversity rather than perfect unanimity.
Hansa Mehta, Dakshayani Velayudhan and Begum Aizaz Rasul illustrate substantive gender,
Dalit and minority interventions.

The strongest defence is institutional and consequential. After the Indian
Independence Act 1947, the Assembly became sovereign. It debated the draft extensively
and created universal adult franchise, thereby giving the future electorate a right
the Assembly's own electorate lacked.

Nevertheless, social presence and deliberation cannot erase indirect election,
nomination or unequal participation. The Assembly's legitimacy was therefore earned
less through a universal founding mandate than through inclusive deliberation,
sovereign decision and broad subsequent acceptance.

```text
        ↓

        ↓

Two Vice-Presidents: H.C. Mookherjee and V.T. Krishnamachari

        ↓
26 Nov 1949 — constitution-making task completed
        ↓
26 Jan 1950–17 Apr 1952 — Provisional Parliament
        ↓
May 1952 — first elected Parliament
```

*The Assembly's origin remained historical fact, but its legal power did not remain
frozen in December 1946.*

### 1. The first sitting

✅ **Fact:** The Assembly first met on **9 December 1946**. The Muslim League boycotted the sitting
and continued to insist on Pakistan. Only **211 members** attended.

Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha, the oldest member, served as the temporary or provisional
President, following the French practice. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was later elected
President.

The Assembly also had two Vice-Presidents:

- H.C. Mookherjee;
- V.T. Krishnamachari.

The temporary Presidency and the elected permanent Presidency are different offices
in the opening chronology. UPSC has directly tested this distinction.

### 2. Princely states and changing participation

The princely states did not fill their 93 seats at the beginning. Their representatives
joined gradually. Representatives of Baroda, Bikaner, Jaipur, Patiala, Rewa and Udaipur
participated by 28 April 1947, and most others entered after the Mountbatten Plan of
3 June 1947 clarified the path to Partition and transfer of power.

Muslim League members from territories remaining in India also entered, while members
from West Punjab, East Bengal, North-West Frontier Province, Sindh, Baluchistan and the
Sylhet district of Assam withdrew as those areas formed part of Pakistan.

Participation therefore changed over time; a single static membership figure cannot
describe every phase.

### 3. The Indian Independence Act changed the Assembly's legal position

The Act produced three fundamental changes.

#### Change 1 — full sovereignty

The Assembly could frame any Constitution it considered appropriate and could alter or
abrogate British parliamentary law applicable to India.

Before Independence:

```text
Assembly created within a British constitutional scheme
```

After Independence:

```text
Assembly exercises constituent authority for an independent Dominion
```

This is why “British-created” and “never sovereign” are not equivalent claims.

#### Change 2 — a legislative role

The same membership also functioned as the Dominion Legislature, the first Parliament
of free India. The two roles were performed on separate sitting days:

| Capacity | Presiding officer | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Constituent Assembly | Dr. Rajendra Prasad | Framed the Constitution |
| Dominion Legislature | G.V. Mavlankar | Enacted ordinary law |

It first met as the Dominion Legislature on **17 November 1947** and elected Mavlankar
as Speaker.

The distinction is functional, not merely ceremonial. Constituent power created the
framework; legislative power governed within the transitional framework.

#### Change 3 — reduced strength after Partition

The withdrawal of members from Pakistan territories reduced the Assembly:

| Component | Original | Post-Partition |
|---|---:|---:|
| Provinces | 296 | 229 |
| Princely states | 93 | 70 |
| **Total** | **389** | **299** |

### 4. What happened after the Constitution was made?

The Assembly's constitution-making function ended on 26 November 1949. It held a final
session on 24 January 1950. From 26 January 1950, it continued as the Provisional
Parliament until 17 April 1952. The first elected Parliament with two Houses came into
being in May 1952 after the 1951–52 general elections.

⚠️ **Analysis:** This continuity prevented a legal vacuum:

```text
Constituent Assembly / Dominion Legislature
                  ↓
           Provisional Parliament
                  ↓
         First elected Parliament
```

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A body cannot become democratically legitimate merely because a colonial
statute labels it sovereign.

**Reply:** Legal sovereignty and democratic legitimacy are distinct. The Independence
Act removed external British legal control; it did not cure every representative
deficit. Democratic legitimacy additionally rested on deliberation, inclusion, the
future commitment to universal franchise and public acceptance.

**Balanced conclusion:** The Act answers the sovereignty objection more fully than the
representation objection.

### Example and its limit

A person may enter a meeting as a delegate with instructions and later receive full
authority to settle the issue. The person's origin has not changed, but the scope of
authority has.

The analogy clarifies legal transformation. Its limit is that constituent sovereignty
concerns an entire people's political order, not a private delegation.

```text

```

1. The first meeting occurred on 9 December 1946.
2. The League boycotted it; 211 members attended.
3. Sachchidananda Sinha was temporary President.
4. Rajendra Prasad became President.
5. H.C. Mookherjee and V.T. Krishnamachari were Vice-Presidents.
6. Princely-state participation grew gradually.
7. The Independence Act made the Assembly fully sovereign.
8. The Assembly also became the Dominion Legislature.

### Concept check

**Question:** How could the same body act as both constitution-maker and legislature
without collapsing the two powers into one?

**Model answer:** It met in two legally distinct capacities on separate sitting days.
As the Constituent Assembly, chaired by Rajendra Prasad, it framed the foundational
legal order. As the Dominion Legislature, chaired by G.V. Mavlankar, it enacted
ordinary laws. Identity of membership did not erase difference of function and
presiding authority.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that the Assembly exercised constituent and
ordinary legislative power simultaneously in every sitting.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** How did the Indian Independence Act 1947 transform
the constitutional position and functions of the Constituent Assembly?

**Complete model response:**

The Indian Independence Act transformed the Constituent Assembly from a body created
under the Cabinet Mission scheme into a fully sovereign constitution-making authority.
It could frame any Constitution and alter or repeal British parliamentary law
applicable to India, thereby removing external legal control.

The Act also gave the Assembly a second institutional capacity: it functioned as the
Dominion Legislature and enacted ordinary laws on separate sitting days. Rajendra
Prasad presided over constituent sittings, while G.V. Mavlankar presided over
legislative sittings. Partition simultaneously reduced its planned strength from 389
to 299 as representatives from Pakistan territories withdrew.

The Act therefore answered the criticism that the Assembly permanently lacked legal
sovereignty. It did not, however, convert its indirect and partly nominated composition
into a universal popular mandate. Its later democratic legitimacy had to rest on
deliberation, inclusion and the Constitution it produced.

## Lesson 4 — Objectives Resolution: Values Before Clauses

Progress: 4 / 9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Constitutional purpose and Preamble lineage

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Resolution text and preparatory committee history checked
CA search: "Not required for this historical statement of constitutional purpose"
CA found: No separate current-affairs linkage used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: from political aspiration to constitutional value

```text
CONGRESS EXPERTS COMMITTEE, JULY 1946
Preparatory ideas and draft resolution
                    ↓
NEHRU MOVES OBJECTIVES RESOLUTION
13 December 1946
                    ↓
ASSEMBLY ADOPTS IT
22 January 1947
                    ↓
Guides committees and constitutional design
                    ↓
Modified constitutional expression
in the Preamble
```

*The Resolution did not draft every institution. It fixed the normative destination
towards which institutional drafting had to move.*

### 1. Why values had to precede detailed drafting

A constitution cannot be designed intelligently by beginning with isolated articles.
Before deciding how a legislature, executive or court should work, the framers needed
answers to prior questions:

- Where does public authority come from?
- What kind of political community is India?
- What freedoms and equalities must institutions protect?
- How should minorities and tribal areas be safeguarded?
- What should India contribute to the wider world?

The Objectives Resolution supplied this value framework.

### 2. Movement and adoption

✅ **Fact:** Jawaharlal Nehru moved the Resolution on **13 December 1946**. The Assembly adopted it
on **22 January 1947**.

Its principal commitments included:

1. India would become an **independent sovereign republic**.
2. British India, Indian states and willing territories would form a Union.
3. Constituent units would possess autonomy, subject to powers assigned to or inherent
   in the Union, **together with residuary powers**.
4. All power and authority would be derived from the people.
5. Justice—social, economic and political—would be secured.
6. Equality of status, opportunity and before the law would be secured.
7. Freedoms of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association and
   action would be protected subject to law and public morality.
8. Safeguards would protect minorities, backward and tribal areas and disadvantaged
   classes.
9. India's territorial integrity and sovereign rights would be maintained.
10. India would contribute to world peace and human welfare.

✅ **Fact and constitutional contrast:** Clause 3 of the Resolution contemplated
autonomous units retaining residuary powers except those assigned to or inherent in
the Union. The enacted Constitution took a different route: **Article 248**, read with
**Entry 97 of the Union List**, places residuary legislative power with Parliament.

⚠️ **Analysis:** This shift shows that the Resolution fixed broad values without
freezing every federal design choice. Partition, integration and the perceived need for
a strong Union shaped the final allocation.

### 3. The Resolution's constitutional mechanism

⚠️ **Analysis:** The Resolution operated at three levels:

| Level | Function |
|---|---|
| Source of authority | Power derives from the people |
| Character of the polity | Sovereign republic and territorial Union |
| Ends of government | Justice, equality, freedom, safeguards and peace |

This structure later reappears in the Preamble:

```text
Source       → We, the People
Nature       → Sovereign democratic republic
Objectives   → Justice, liberty, equality
Social bond  → Fraternity, dignity, unity
```

The Preamble is the Resolution in **modified constitutional form**, not a word-for-word
copy.

### 4. Why “modified form” matters

The Resolution was a political declaration adopted before the detailed Constitution
was settled. The Preamble was enacted after the constitutional provisions had been
agreed, so its language had to fit the completed document.

Therefore:

| Incorrect inference | Accurate position |
|---|---|
| The Resolution itself became an operative article | It guided drafting; its modified form shaped the Preamble |
| Every phrase in the Preamble appeared identically in 1946 | The value lineage continued through modified language |
| Nehru personally drafted the whole Constitution | He moved the Resolution and chaired important committees; drafting remained collective |

### 5. The Congress Experts Committee as a preparatory bridge

On **8 July 1946**, while Assembly elections were proceeding, the Congress appointed
an Experts Committee to prepare material for constitution-making.

| Member | Role in the committee |
|---|---|
| Jawaharlal Nehru | Chairman |
| M. Asaf Ali | Member |
| K.M. Munshi | Member |
| N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar | Member |
| K.T. Shah | Member |
| D.R. Gadgil | Member |
| Humayun Kabir | Member |
| K. Santhanam | Member |
| Krishna Kripalani | Later co-opted member and convener |

The committee considered Assembly procedure, the appointment of committees and a draft
resolution on constitutional objectives. Granville Austin regarded it as an important
preparatory step towards the eventual Constitution.

✅ **Fact:** It held two sittings: **20–22 July 1946 in New Delhi** and
**15–17 August 1946 in Bombay**.

**Qualification:** This was a Congress committee, not the Constituent Assembly itself.
Its preparatory influence did not substitute for adoption by the Assembly.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The Resolution was aspirational rhetoric. It did not itself create
enforceable rights or institutions.

**Reply:** That is true but incomplete. A foundational resolution performs a different
function from an enforceable article. It supplies criteria against which institutional
choices can be justified and later interpreted.

**Residual limitation:** Values can guide design without dictating one inevitable
institutional arrangement. Justice or equality may support competing policy choices,
so later constitutional text remains controlling.

### Example and its limit

Before architects draw rooms and staircases, a public hospital project must decide its
purpose: universal access, safety, emergency care and dignity. Those values do not draw
the blueprint, but they determine whether the blueprint is acceptable.

The analogy shows the Resolution's guiding role. Its limit is that constitutional
values are politically contested and legally interpreted, not merely technical design
specifications.

- It was moved on 13 December 1946 and adopted on 22 January 1947.
- The Preamble is its modified form.
- “Power derived from the people” coexisted with an indirectly elected Assembly,
  creating a legitimacy problem addressed through later constitutional design.

**Mains answer line**

> The Objectives Resolution was the Constitution's normative compass: it did not write
> every clause, but it fixed the source, character and ends of the future polity.

### Revision notes

   the “how.”
2. Nehru moved it on 13 December 1946.
3. The Assembly adopted it on 22 January 1947.
4. It declared India an independent sovereign republic.
5. It located authority in the people.
6. It joined political freedom with social, economic and political justice.
7. It included equality, freedom and safeguards for vulnerable groups.
8. Clause 3 initially contemplated autonomous units retaining residuary powers; the
   enacted Constitution vested residuary legislative power in Parliament.
9. It protected territorial integrity and affirmed world peace.
10. Its modified form became the Preamble.
11. Modified lineage is not verbatim identity.
12. The Congress Experts Committee supplied preparatory ideas but could not bind the
    Assembly.
13. Aspirational status does not make the Resolution constitutionally unimportant.
14. The Experts Committee sat in New Delhi on 20–22 July and Bombay on
    15–17 August 1946.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the statement “the Objectives Resolution became the Preamble”
correct only when qualified?

**Model answer:** The Resolution supplied the Preamble's normative lineage—popular
authority, sovereignty, justice, equality and freedom—but it did not become the
Preamble verbatim or operate as a constitutional article. The Preamble expressed those
commitments in modified language fitted to the Constitution as finally enacted.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse continuity of values with identity of legal
text.

tribal safeguards, territorial integrity and world peace.

Its importance was structural rather than directly enforceable. Committees could test
institutional proposals against its declared purposes, while its language connected

Preamble, preserving the Resolution's source–nature–objectives framework.

Its initial federal language left residuary powers with units, unlike the enacted
Constitution.

However, it did not itself settle every constitutional mechanism or operate as an
independent legal provision. The completed constitutional text controlled. The
Resolution is therefore best understood as the Constitution's normative compass:
indispensable for direction, but implemented through later clauses and institutions.

*The Assembly did not ask one committee to invent everything. Different bodies settled
different constitutional problems before the full Assembly decided.*

### 1. Why committees were necessary

A modern constitution must address federal power, provincial government, rights,
minorities, tribal areas, procedure, relations with princely states and legal drafting.
If all 299 members had attempted to design every provision from the beginning in
plenary debate, discussion would have been repetitive and technically unmanageable.

⚠️ **Analysis:** The committee method created a sequence:

```text
Specialised investigation
        ↓
Committee recommendation
        ↓
Legal integration into a draft
        ↓
Clause-by-clause Assembly decision
```

Committee recommendations shaped the agenda but did not replace final Assembly
authority.

### 5. Decision, advice, drafting and adoption must be separated

| Function | Main institutional location |
|---|---|
| Political and constitutional choice | Thematic committees and full Assembly |
| Comparative/legal advice | Constitutional Adviser and experts |
| Conversion into integrated draft language | Drafting Committee and Chief Draftsman |
| Final authority | Full Constituent Assembly |

This table prevents four recurring errors:

1. committees did not finally enact the Constitution;
2. the Drafting Committee did not independently decide every policy;
3. the Constitutional Adviser did not chair the Drafting Committee;
4. the full Assembly was not a ceremonial rubber stamp.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Real decisions were made by a small leadership group before plenary
debate, making the Assembly's openness superficial.

**Reply:** Leadership and pre-negotiation were undeniably important, but committee
reports remained subject to amendment and debate. Thousands of proposed amendments and
prolonged clause-by-clause consideration show that plenary review was substantive.

**Residual concern:** Formal opportunity to debate does not prove equal influence among
all members. Leadership concentration should be acknowledged without reducing the
entire process to elite dictation.

### Example and its limit

A complex court judgment may involve separate research on facts, precedent, procedure
and remedy before one bench issues the final decision. Division of labour improves
quality without transferring final judicial authority to each researcher.

The analogy explains functional specialisation. Its limit is that constitution-making
involves political bargaining and popular legitimacy, not only technical expertise.

fundamental rights, minorities and tribal areas. Rajendra Prasad's Rules and Steering
Committees organised proceedings.

supplied comparative and legal advice. The Drafting Committee under B.R. Ambedkar
integrated committee decisions into a coherent draft, assisted by Chief Draftsman S.N.

This division improved specialisation and made a vast task manageable. It also explains
why crediting Ambedkar's exceptional leadership need not imply that he singly authored
the Constitution.

agenda power. Yet committee concentration coexisted with real plenary revision. The
Constitution is therefore best understood as collectively authorised, expertly advised
and institutionally drafted.

## Lesson 6 — B.N. Rau, Ambedkar and the Drafting Process

Progress: 6 / 9 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: From committee decisions to draft language

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

CA search: "Not required for this historical drafting mechanism"
CA found: No separate current-affairs linkage used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: four different forms of constitutional authorship

```text
COMMITTEES
settle policy choices and safeguards
        ↓
B.N. RAU — CONSTITUTIONAL ADVISER
comparative research + adviser draft + legal advice
        ↓
DRAFTING COMMITTEE — B.R. AMBEDKAR, CHAIR
reconcile decisions + structure provisions + revise text
        ↓
S.N. MUKERJEE — CHIEF DRAFTSMAN
precise legislative formulation
        ↓
FULL ASSEMBLY
debate + amend + adopt
```

*Each stage performed indispensable work; none alone exhausted constitutional
authorship.*

### 1. The Constitutional Adviser

Sir B.N. Rau served as Constitutional Adviser to the Assembly. His work included:

- comparative study of constitutional systems;
- organisation of committee recommendations into an initial adviser draft;
- legal and structural advice;
- identification of possible institutional formulations.

Rau was neither Chairman nor member of the seven-member Drafting Committee. His role
preceded and supported its work.

Two other officers must be distinguished:

| Officer | Function |
|---|---|
| H.V.R. Iyengar | Secretary to the Constituent Assembly |
| S.N. Mukerjee | Chief Draftsman, responsible for exact legal formulation |

Administrative coordination, constitutional advice and legislative drafting were
separate professional tasks.

### 2. Appointment and membership of the Drafting Committee

✅ **Fact:** The Assembly appointed the Drafting Committee on **29 August 1947**.

Its original seven members were:

1. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar — Chairman;
2. N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar;
3. Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar;
4. K.M. Munshi;
5. Syed Mohammad Saadullah;
6. B.L. Mitter;
7. D.P. Khaitan.

Changes in membership:

- N. Madhava Rau replaced B.L. Mitter after Mitter resigned because of ill health.
- T.T. Krishnamachari replaced D.P. Khaitan after Khaitan died in 1948.

The distinction between original members and later replacements is a standard
close-option trap.

### 3. What the Drafting Committee actually did

⚠️ **Analysis:** The committee did not begin with a blank page. It considered:

- reports and proposals of Assembly committees;
- the Constitutional Adviser's work;
- decisions already taken by the Assembly;
- requirements of internal coherence;
- legal language capable of operating across a large federation.

Its task was both technical and constitutional:

```text
Many policy decisions
        ↓
remove contradiction and duplication
        ↓
translate choices into articles and schedules
        ↓
create one internally connected draft
```

### 4. Drafts and public consultation

The Drafting Committee produced a draft published in **February 1948**. The public was
given about eight months to discuss it and submit comments, criticisms and suggestions.

After considering responses, a second draft was published in **October 1948**.

The committee sat for **141 days**. This figure refers to the committee's sittings, not
the total time spent by the Assembly on constitution-making.

Public consultation did not amount to a referendum, but it prevented drafting from
being entirely closed:

```text
First published draft
        ↓
public comments and criticism
        ↓
committee reconsideration
        ↓
revised draft
        ↓
Assembly readings
```

### 5. Ambedkar's distinctive role

Ambedkar's contribution was exceptional because he:

- chaired the Drafting Committee;
- reconciled multiple committee decisions and legal formulations;
- piloted and defended the Draft Constitution in the Assembly;
- explained provisions through forceful constitutional argument;
- responded to amendments and criticism.

It is common to call him the “Father” or “chief architect” of the Constitution. In a
careful answer, treat these as recognition of leadership—not as a claim that other
institutions or people were irrelevant.

### 6. Collective authorship does not diminish individual contribution

Two false extremes should be rejected:

| Extreme | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Ambedkar alone wrote the Constitution | Ignores the Assembly, committees, Rau, Mukerjee and public consultation |
| Ambedkar merely copied decisions made by others | Ignores reconciliation, legal design, defence and leadership |

The accurate position is:

> Ambedkar was the central drafting leader within a collective constitution-making
> process.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If most policy choices came from committees and much comparative work
from Rau, Ambedkar's celebrated status is exaggerated.

**Reply:** Constitutional leadership is not measured only by originating every idea.
Integrating divergent decisions into workable text, defending the structure before a
large Assembly and revising it through debate are creative and consequential acts.

**Residual qualification:** Respect for Ambedkar should not become a personality cult
that erases institutional authorship or the contributions of less celebrated members.

### Example and its limit

An engineer may not invent every material or decide the public purpose of a bridge, yet
the engineer's integration of requirements into a safe structure remains creative and
indispensable.

The analogy clarifies integration. Its limit is that a constitution derives authority
from political deliberation, not professional design alone.

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Assess the respective contributions of B.N. Rau and

constitutional alternatives into coherent legal text. He then introduced, explained
and defended the Draft Constitution, responding to amendments during prolonged

place in constitutional history.

and the full Assembly debated and adopted the provisions.

Thus, Rau was an indispensable adviser and Ambedkar the central drafting leader, while
constituent authority remained collective. This formulation recognises individual
achievement without converting constitutional history into biography.

**Prompt:** Distinguish the functions of the Objectives Resolution, thematic committees,

### Visual: the draft passes through four filters


*Adoption, signing and commencement were related but distinct constitutional acts.*

### 1. First reading: debate on the general scheme

✅ **Fact:** Ambedkar introduced the Draft Constitution on **4 November 1948**. The Assembly held
a general discussion until 9 November.

At this stage, the central question was not the wording of every clause. Members
assessed the overall design:

- the balance between Union and states;
- rights and social transformation;
- parliamentary government;
- judicial safeguards;
- the detail and length of the text.

### 2. Second reading: clause-by-clause constitution-making

The second reading ran from **15 November 1948 to 17 October 1949**. Members examined
the draft clause by clause.

| Measure of deliberation | Figure |
|---|---:|
| Amendments proposed | 7,653 |
| Amendments discussed | 2,473 |
| Draft Constitution debated | 114 days |

⚠️ **Analysis:** The difference between proposed and discussed amendments does not show that the rest
were secretly ignored. Proposals may overlap, be withdrawn, ruled out or become
unnecessary after related decisions. The important point is that the plenary process
was capable of altering the Drafting Committee's text.

### 3. Third reading: final constitutional judgment

The third reading began on **14 November 1949**. Here the Assembly judged the
Constitution as a completed whole. Ambedkar moved that the Constitution as settled by
the Assembly be passed.

The motion was carried on **26 November 1949**. This is the date stated in the
Preamble:

> the people of India, in the Constituent Assembly, adopted, enacted and gave the
> Constitution to themselves.

### 4. The Preamble was enacted last

The Constitution as adopted contained:

- a Preamble;
- 395 Articles;
- 8 Schedules.

The Preamble was enacted after the rest of the Constitution. This ensured that its
statement of identity and purposes conformed to the document the Assembly had actually
accepted.

**UPSC trap:** “Based on the earlier Objectives Resolution” does not mean “enacted
before the rest of the Constitution.”

### 5. Adoption and signature: resolving the date conflict

✅ **Fact with attribution:** The Assembly adopted the Constitution on
**26 November 1949**. The official Parliament report records “Signing of the
Constitution” in the final sitting on **24 January 1950**. Standard constitutional
accounts consistently record **284 signatories**.

The correct sequence is:

```text
26 November 1949 → constitutional adoption
24 January 1950  → signatures at final session
26 January 1950  → general commencement
```

Some secondary prose has compressed presence, adoption and signature into the
26 November account. For exam purposes, preserve the three-stage chronology above and
avoid saying that 284 members signed on adoption day.

### 6. Time, sessions and comparative scale

The Assembly held **11 sessions** over **2 years, 11 months and 18 days**. It studied
the constitutions of about **60 countries**. Constitution-making cost approximately
**₹64 lakh**.

The 11 sessions were:

| Session | Period |
|---:|---|
| 1 | 9–23 December 1946 |
| 2 | 20–25 January 1947 |
| 3 | 28 April–2 May 1947 |
| 4 | 14–31 July 1947 |
| 5 | 14–30 August 1947 |
| 6 | 27 January 1948 |
| 7 | 4 November 1948–8 January 1949 |
| 8 | 16 May–16 June 1949 |
| 9 | 30 July–18 September 1949 |
| 10 | 6–17 October 1949 |
| 11 | 14–26 November 1949 |

The Assembly met once more on 24 January 1950 for its final session and signatures.

Comparative duration should be used cautiously:

| Country | Articles in the cited contemporary comparison | Time taken |
|---|---:|---|
| United States | 7 | Less than 4 months |
| Canada | 147 | About 2 years and 6 months |
| Australia | 128 | About 9 years |
| South Africa | 153 | About 1 year |

The table disproves any simple rule that fewer months always indicate better
constitution-making. Countries differed in document length, political conditions and
the tasks assigned to their conventions.

### 7. Other constitutional and national functions

Beyond drafting and ordinary legislation, the Assembly:

- adopted the National Flag on 22 July 1947;
- ratified India's continued Commonwealth membership in May 1949;
- adopted the National Anthem and National Song on 24 January 1950;
- elected Dr. Rajendra Prasad as the first President of India on 24 January 1950.

These acts show the Assembly managing a wider transition from colonial rule to
republican statehood.

### 8. The physical Constitution and Assembly identity

The Assembly adopted the **elephant** as its seal or symbol. The original English
Constitution was handwritten in a flowing italic style by **Prem Behari Narain
Raizada**. Artists from Shantiniketan, including **Nandalal Bose** and **Beohar
Rammanohar Sinha**, beautified the document; Sinha illuminated the Preamble.

The Hindi calligraphy was prepared by **Vasant Krishan Vaidya**, with artistic
decoration associated with Nandalal Bose.

These roles must not be confused:

| Contribution | Person/institution |
|---|---|
| Constitutional advice | B.N. Rau |
| Legal drafting | S.N. Mukerjee and the drafting machinery |
| English calligraphy | Prem Behari Narain Raizada |
| Artistic illumination | Shantiniketan artists |

Calligraphy and art made the original document a national artefact; they did not confer
legal authority, which came from adoption by the Assembly.

### 9. Strongest objection and reply: was the Assembly too slow?

**Objection:** Nearly three years was excessive. The United States completed its
Constitution in less than four months, and Naziruddin Ahmed derided the Drafting
Committee as a “Drifting Committee.”

**Reply:** Duration must be judged against complexity. India was constitution-making
during Partition, integration of princely states and institutional transfer. The
Assembly studied about 60 constitutions, processed specialised reports and considered
thousands of amendments.

**Residual limitation:** Thoroughness does not mean every delay was necessary or every
provision ideal. The defensible claim is not that the process was perfect, but that
elapsed time alone cannot prove drift.

### Example and its limit

A short contract can be drafted quickly; a national framework distributing power,
protecting rights and administering diversity requires more reconciliation.

The comparison explains why page count and time matter. Its limit is that longer
drafting does not automatically guarantee a better Constitution.

### Visual: the legal switchboard at the end of the Constitution


*Part XXII closes the Constitution by naming it, controlling commencement and removing
the principal colonial constitutional statutes. Article 394A was a later insertion, not
an original branch of Article 393 or a subordinate part of Article 394.*

### 1. Five dates that perform different work

| Date | Constitutional event |
|---|---|
| 13 December 1946 | Objectives Resolution moved |
| 22 January 1947 | Objectives Resolution adopted |
| 26 November 1949 | Constitution adopted; specified provisions commence |
| 24 January 1950 | Final session and signatures |
| 26 January 1950 | Remaining Constitution commences; India becomes a republic |

Dates are not decorative facts here. Each marks a different legal or political act.

### 2. Article 393 — short title

✅ **Fact:** Article 393 states that the document may be called the
**Constitution of India**.

This directly defeats the claim that no article gives the Constitution its official
short title.

### 3. Article 394 — commencement in two stages

✅ **Fact:** The whole Constitution did not begin operating on one day.

The following provisions came into force on **26 November 1949**:

- Articles 5–9;
- Article 60;
- Article 324;
- Articles 366–367;
- Articles 379–380;
- Article 388;
- Articles 391–393.

They substantially concerned citizenship, elections, transitional institutions,
definitions and short title.

The remaining provisions commenced on **26 January 1950**, constitutionally called the
date of commencement.

```text
26 Nov 1949
Adoption + selected transition provisions
            ↓
institutional preparation
            ↓
26 Jan 1950
general constitutional commencement
```

### 4. Why 26 January?

The date honoured the **Purna Swaraj Day of 1930**, following the Congress's Lahore
Session resolution of December 1929.

The commencement date therefore joined:

- anti-colonial memory;
- legal commencement;
- republican statehood.

On 26 January 1950, India was a **sovereign democratic republic**. “Socialist” and
“secular” were added to the Preamble only by the 42nd Amendment in 1976.

India's republican status meant an elected constitutional head rather than continued
Dominion status under the Crown. Continued Commonwealth membership, ratified in May
1949, did not negate republican status.

### 5. Article 395 — constitutional repeal

✅ **Fact:** With commencement, Article 395 repealed:

- the Indian Independence Act 1947;
- the Government of India Act 1935, including amending or supplementary enactments.

The **Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act 1949** continued. Do not include it
among the repealed Acts.

The logic was replacement:

```text
Colonial constitutional authority
1935 Act + 1947 Act
            ↓ repealed by Article 395
Constitution-derived authority
```

### 6. Article 394A — authoritative Hindi text

The original Constitution did not create an authoritative Hindi text. The **58th
Constitutional Amendment Act, 1987** inserted Article 394A.

It provides for:

- an authoritative Hindi translation of the Constitution under presidential
  authority;
- incorporation of prior amendments;
- authoritative Hindi translations of later amendments;
- revision where necessary to ensure equivalent meaning.

**Trap:** Article 394A was not part of the original three-article Part XXII. Originally,
Part XXII contained Articles 393, 394 and 395.

### 7. Constitution Day: the single contemporary linkage

✅ **Fact:** The Government of India designated **26 November as Constitution Day
(Samvidhan Divas) in 2015** to promote constitutional values and recall the
Constitution's adoption in 1949. This is an **executive/governmental designation**, not
a constitutional provision.

✅ **Fact:** A year-long commemoration of the **75th anniversary of adoption** began on
**26 November 2024**. The date must remain attached to that anniversary programme; it
should not be presented as a permanently continuing undated campaign.

✅ **Single contemporary linkage:** An official national observance was held in the
Central Hall of **Samvidhan Sadan on 26 November 2025**.

Its exam use is narrow and precise:

```text
Contemporary observance
        ↓
recalls adoption on 26 Nov 1949
        ≠
general commencement on 26 Jan 1950
```

Do not use the observance to blur the two dates or treat Constitution Day as a
constitutional provision. It is a governmental observance; the adoption date comes
from the constitutional history itself.

adoption day. Therefore, saying “the Constitution came into force only on 26 January”
is also overbroad.

democratic republic. The date was chosen to honour Purna Swaraj Day of 1930. Article
395 simultaneously displaced the principal colonial constitutional framework by
repealing the Government of India Act 1935 and Indian Independence Act 1947.

The dates are therefore connected but not interchangeable. The first marks adoption
and limited immediate operation; the second marks general commencement and republican
statehood. Yet it is also imprecise to say that nothing commenced before 26 January,
because Article 394 deliberately created a staged transition.

### Plural voices and expertise before evaluation

✅ **Fact:** The Assembly's diversity was not merely an anonymous claim. Representative
examples include:

| Member | Verified field of intervention | What the example establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Hansa Mehta | Equality and women's status | Women advanced substantive citizenship claims |
| Dakshayani Velayudhan | Untouchability and social transformation | A Dalit woman member challenged social hierarchy |
| Begum Aizaz Rasul | Opposition to separate electorates | Minority citizenship was debated beyond communal separation |
| Durgabai Deshmukh | Social reform, language and public institutions | Women participated across institutional questions |

✅ **Fact:** Congress held overwhelming numbers, but the Assembly also drew on expertise
outside Congress organisational membership, notably B.R. Ambedkar, and on independent
legal expertise including Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar.

⚠️ **Limit:** Named participation proves plural contribution, not proportional equality
or the absence of Congress agenda power.

### 1. The six standard criticisms

#### A. Not representative

Members were not directly elected on universal adult franchise.

#### B. Not sovereign

Critics argued that the Assembly was created through British proposals and initially
held its sessions with British permission rather than by an independently asserted
popular constituent mandate.

#### C. Time-consuming

Critics compared its nearly three-year duration with the shorter American convention.
Naziruddin Ahmed called the Drafting Committee a “Drifting Committee.”

#### D. Congress-dominated

Congress won 208 of 296 elected seats. Granville Austin described the Assembly as a
one-party body in a substantially one-party country and connected the Assembly closely
with the Congress.

#### E. Lawyer-politician dominated

Critics argued that lawyers and politicians exercised disproportionate influence while
other sections were insufficiently represented. They linked this professional
concentration to the Constitution's bulk and complicated legal language.

#### F. Hindu-dominated

Viscount Simon called it “a body of Hindus,” while Winston Churchill alleged that it
represented “only one major community in India.” League boycott and Partition
unquestionably reduced Muslim participation.

### 2. Replies must concede the kernel before rebutting

| Criticism | What must be conceded | Strongest reply | Residual concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Not representative | No universal direct election | Broad social presence, committees and future adult franchise | Inclusion was unequal |
| Not sovereign | British-created at origin | Independence Act made it fully sovereign | Later sovereignty does not cure original mandate |
| Too slow | Process lasted nearly three years | Scale, Partition, 60 constitutions and amendment scrutiny | Not every delay was necessarily useful |
| Congress-dominated | Congress had overwhelming numbers | Congress contained ideological and provincial diversity; non-Congress expertise mattered | Agenda power remained concentrated |
| Lawyer-politician dominated | Drafting core was elite and legalistic | Legal expertise produced precision and enforceability | Complexity reduced accessibility |
| Hindu-dominated | Muslim participation fell sharply | Minority and tribal channels remained; communal breadth was not zero | League absence remained a major deficit |

⚠️ **Analysis:** A reply that denies every criticism is weaker than one that identifies
the valid kernel and then measures its actual consequence.

### 3. Borrowed Constitution or adapted synthesis?

The framers examined about 60 constitutions. Important institutional sources included:

- the Government of India Act 1935 for much administrative and federal structure;
- British parliamentary government;
- American rights and judicial ideas;
- Irish Directive Principles;
- Canadian, Australian, South African, French, Japanese and other constitutional
  devices.

The criticism is that India assembled foreign provisions rather than creating an
original Constitution.

The reply depends on the difference between **borrowing** and **transplantation**:

| Borrowing with adaptation | Mechanical transplantation |
|---|---|
| Selects a device for an Indian problem | Copies without regard to context |
| Alters relations and safeguards | Preserves the source design unchanged |
| Combines devices from several systems | Imports one complete foreign model |
| Subjects choices to Assembly debate | Treats foreign authority as decisive |

India combined parliamentary government with a written supreme Constitution, judicial
review, federal structures, emergency powers, social-revolution commitments and
universal adult franchise. The combination and purpose mattered as much as the source.

> **Answer line:** The Constitution borrowed institutional vocabulary but constructed
> an Indian constitutional grammar.

### 4. Constituent Assembly Debates as interpretive aids

The Debates can help answer:

- what problem a provision addressed;
- why one phrase was preferred to another;
- what alternatives were considered;
- how a constitutional value was understood at enactment.

Their place is subordinate:

```text
ENACTED CONSTITUTIONAL TEXT
            ↓ controls
structure, precedent and doctrine
            ↓ may be illuminated by
Constituent Assembly Debates
```

The Debates are not themselves constitutional law. A member's speech cannot add words
to, remove words from or override the enacted provision.

### 5. Original intent and the living Constitution

Two examples show why the Debates cannot mechanically freeze meaning.

#### Article 21 and due process

The framing history records the choice of “procedure established by law” rather than
the American phrase “due process of law.” Yet in *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India*
(1978), the Supreme Court required the procedure under Article 21 to be fair, just and
reasonable.

This does not mean the Court erased history. It means constitutional meaning developed
through text, structure, rights and precedent beyond the narrowest historical
expectation.

#### Basic structure

In *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973), the Court derived implied limits on
the amending power even though Article 368 does not enumerate a list called “basic
structure.”

Supporters see protection of constitutional identity; critics see judicial creation.
Either way, the example proves that Indian constitutional interpretation is not strict
historical originalism.

### 6. Ambedkar's constitutional-working insight

Ambedkar repeatedly connected constitutional success with the conduct of political
actors. The safe analytical use is to paraphrase:

> A workable constitutional text can be made ineffective by those who operate it, while
> responsible constitutional conduct can sustain institutions.

Do not place an elegant sentence in quotation marks unless its exact wording and source
are verified.

This insight links making with working:

```text
Good text
   + constitutional morality
   + institutions acting within limits
   + democratic habits
   = viable constitutional government
```

### 7. A graded verdict on legitimacy

The Assembly fails a strict present-day test of direct universal election. It also
suffered from Congress predominance, reduced Muslim participation and elite leadership.

Yet it:

- became legally sovereign;
- contained substantial social and ideological diversity;
- used specialised committees and outside expertise;
- published drafts and received public comments;
- debated amendments extensively;
- wrote universal adult franchise into the new order;
- produced a Constitution that gained broad and durable acceptance.

Its legitimacy is therefore **deliberative, constitutional and consequential**, not
identical to the electoral mandate of a modern constituent convention.

### Example and its limit

A court judgment may be legitimate despite an unelected judiciary because authority
comes from jurisdiction, reasons, procedure and review—not electoral representation.

The analogy clarifies plural sources of legitimacy. Its limit is crucial: a constituent
assembly founds the legal order and therefore faces a stronger democratic-authorisation
problem than an ordinary court.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the Constituent Assembly Debates be constitutionally valuable
without being legally binding?

**Model answer:** The Debates can illuminate a provision's historical problem, purpose,
alternatives and original understanding, especially where text is ambiguous. But
constituent authority was exercised through the Constitution finally enacted, not
through every individual speech. The text, structure and binding judicial doctrine
therefore control.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the choice as “ignore history” versus “obey

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Critically assess the legitimacy of India's
Constituent Assembly. In your answer, explain whether extensive borrowing and reliance
on Constituent Assembly Debates weaken the Constitution's authority.

**Complete model response:**

The Constituent Assembly's legitimacy was substantial but imperfect. It was indirectly
elected through legislatures based on restricted franchise, partly nominated by
princes and dominated numerically by Congress. League boycott and Partition further
reduced Muslim participation. It therefore fails the strict test of a directly elected
modern constituent convention.

Legitimacy, however, also arose from process and consequence. The Indian Independence
Act made the Assembly sovereign. Its socially varied membership, specialised
committees, expert advice, public comments and clause-by-clause consideration of
thousands of amendments created deliberative authority. Most importantly, it
constitutionalised universal adult franchise and produced a durable framework broadly
accepted beyond its original membership.

Hansa Mehta, Dakshayani Velayudhan and Begum Aizaz Rasul illustrate substantive gender,
Dalit and minority interventions, while Ambedkar supplied major non-Congress expertise.

Borrowing does not negate authorship. The framers adapted British parliamentary
government, American rights, Irish Directive Principles and the 1935 Act within a
distinctive synthesis of constitutional supremacy, federalism and social
transformation.

Similarly, Constituent Assembly Debates illuminate purpose but do not bind courts or
override enacted text. *Maneka Gandhi* and *Kesavananda Bharati* show that constitutional
meaning can develop beyond narrow historical expectation.

Thus, the Assembly lacked a complete founding mandate but earned constitutional
legitimacy through sovereign, reasoned and transformative constitution-making.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — demand versus mechanism

**Question:** Arrange the following by function rather than merely by date: M.N. Roy's
proposal, Nehru's adult-franchise formulation, August Offer and Cabinet Mission.

**Model answer:** Roy initiated the idea; the Congress and Nehru converted it into a
democratic nationalist claim; the August Offer accepted the claim in principle; the
Cabinet Mission supplied the operative but indirectly elected institutional scheme.

**Misconception to avoid:** The first British acceptance did not itself constitute the
Assembly.

## Check 2 — three tests of representation

**Question:** Can an Assembly be socially broad and deliberatively active while still
being electorally deficient?

**Model answer:** Yes. The Assembly contained many communities, ideological positions
and specialised channels for rights and safeguards, but its members were not directly
elected through universal adult franchise. Social presence and deliberation mitigate;
they do not erase the electoral deficit.

**Misconception to avoid:** Representation is neither exhausted by demographic presence
nor reducible to one unqualified label.

## Check 3 — institutional authorship

**Question:** Complete the functional chain: thematic committees → B.N. Rau → Drafting
Committee → S.N. Mukerjee → full Assembly.

**Model answer:** Committees settled political and constitutional choices; Rau supplied
comparative and legal advice; the Drafting Committee integrated decisions; Mukerjee
gave precise legislative form; the full Assembly debated, amended and adopted.

**Misconception to avoid:** A sequence of contributions does not mean later stages
merely copied earlier ones.

## Check 4 — the constitutional date ladder

**Question:** State one unique legal function of each date: 26 November 1949,
24 January 1950 and 26 January 1950.

**Model answer:** On 26 November the Constitution was adopted and specified provisions
commenced; on 24 January 284 members signed at the final Assembly session; on 26 January
the remaining provisions commenced and India became a republic.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assign adoption, signature and general commencement
to one date.

## Check 5 — historical purpose versus binding law

**Question:** If a framer's speech and the enacted constitutional text point in
different directions, which controls and why?

**Model answer:** The enacted text controls because it is the form through which the
Assembly collectively exercised constituent authority. A speech may illuminate
purpose or alternatives, but it cannot amend the provision that the Assembly adopted.

**Misconception to avoid:** Historical relevance does not create legal supremacy.

## Check 6 — legitimacy verdict

**Question:** Give a one-sentence verdict that neither romanticises nor dismisses the
Assembly.

**Model answer:** The Assembly lacked a direct universal electoral mandate but earned
substantial legitimacy through post-Independence sovereignty, social and ideological
breadth, committee deliberation, public consultation, extensive debate and the creation
of universal adult franchise.

**Misconception to avoid:** A balanced verdict is not indecision; it identifies the
precise test on which the Assembly fails and those on which it succeeds.

## 10 marks — Ambedkar and collective authorship

**Question:** “Describing B.R. Ambedkar as the chief architect of the Constitution is
compatible with recognising its collective authorship.” Explain in 150 words.

**Directive decoding:** “Explain” requires the apparent tension to be resolved through
a clear causal account, not a list of personalities.

**Model answer:**

Ambedkar's description as the Constitution's chief architect recognises his exceptional
leadership without implying solitary authorship. As Chairman of the seven-member
Drafting Committee, he led the integration of committee reports and constitutional
alternatives into coherent legal text. He introduced, defended and clarified the Draft
through extensive Assembly debate.

Yet the Constitution emerged from a wider institutional chain. The Objectives
Resolution supplied its value framework; Nehru- and Patel-led committees settled Union,
provincial, rights and safeguards questions; Constitutional Adviser B.N. Rau provided
comparative and legal advice; Chief Draftsman S.N. Mukerjee refined legislative
language; public comments informed revision; and the full Assembly amended and adopted
the text.

Thus, Ambedkar was the central drafting leader within, not outside, collective
constitution-making. Denying the collective process exaggerates biography; denying
his integrative and persuasive role understates constitutional leadership.

**Why this earns marks:** It resolves the tension, names evidence from each institutional
stage and ends with a qualified thesis.

## 15 marks — Objectives, committees and deliberation

**Question:** Analyse how the Objectives Resolution and the committee system together
converted political independence into a workable constitutional design. Answer in
250 words.

**Directive decoding:** “Analyse” requires the relationship between value and
institutional mechanism to be demonstrated.

**Model answer:**

Political independence answered who should govern India; constitution-making also had
to answer for what purposes and through which institutions. The Objectives Resolution,
moved by Nehru on 13 December 1946 and adopted on 22 January 1947, supplied the first
answer. It located authority in the people and committed the future republic to
sovereignty, justice, equality, freedom, safeguards and world peace. Its modified form
became the Preamble.

The committee system converted this normative charter into specialised constitutional
choices. Nehru-led committees addressed Union powers and institutions. Patel's
Provincial Constitution Committee and Advisory Committee examined provincial design,
rights, minorities and tribal areas. Rajendra Prasad's Rules and Steering Committees
organised deliberation. B.N. Rau then supplied comparative legal advice, while the
Drafting Committee under Ambedkar integrated decisions into a coherent draft.

The relationship was therefore sequential: values guided design; committees translated
values into choices; drafting converted choices into enforceable text; and the full
Assembly amended and authorised it.

The Resolution's unit-level residuary-power proposal and the enacted Union allocation
also show that values guided, but did not freeze, institutional design.

Neither element was sufficient alone. A resolution without institutions would remain
aspirational, while technical institutions without a value framework could lack
democratic direction. Their interaction converted anti-colonial sovereignty into a
workable constitutional state.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains mechanism across stages and avoids treating either
the Resolution or committees as self-sufficient.

## 20 marks — democratic deficit and constitutional legitimacy

**Question:** “The Constituent Assembly's authority rested less on the perfection of
its mandate than on the quality and consequences of its constitution-making.”
Critically examine in 250 words.

**Directive decoding:** “Critically examine” requires the claim's strengths and limits,
followed by a graded verdict.

**Model answer:**

The Assembly's founding mandate was imperfect. Provincial representatives were
indirectly elected through legislatures based on restricted franchise, princely-state
representatives were nominated, and Congress won 208 of the 296 elected seats. League
boycott and Partition further weakened Muslim participation. By a strict universal
electoral test, the Assembly was deficient.

Its authority nevertheless developed through process. The Indian Independence Act
made it sovereign. Socially varied membership and specialised subcommittees created
channels for rights, minorities and tribal concerns. The Assembly drew on B.N. Rau's
comparative advice, published drafts for public comment and debated the text for 114
days. Of 7,653 proposed amendments, 2,473 were discussed, demonstrating plenary
revision rather than automatic acceptance of the Drafting Committee.

Hansa Mehta, Dakshayani Velayudhan and Begum Aizaz Rasul provide named evidence of
women's, Dalit and minority interventions, though presence did not ensure proportional
power.

Consequences also mattered. The Assembly wrote universal adult franchise into the
constitutional order, created enforceable rights and established institutions capable
of democratic transfer. The Constitution's continued acceptance expanded authority
beyond the Assembly's original electorate.

Yet process cannot retrospectively produce a direct 1946 mandate, and elite or Congress
influence should not be concealed. The Assembly was therefore neither perfectly
representative nor illegitimate. Its authority was substantially earned through
sovereign deliberation and transformative consequences, while its founding democratic
deficit remains part of an honest constitutional history.

**Why this earns marks:** It concedes the strongest criticism, uses precise evidence,
tests both process and consequence, and gives a graded verdict.

# REMEDIATION

## Error family 1 — collapsing all authority into one person

**Faulty idea:** Ambedkar wrote the Constitution; everyone else merely approved it.

**Repair chain:**

```text
Objectives → policy committees → Rau's advice → Drafting Committee
→ Mukerjee's formulation → Assembly amendment → collective adoption
```

**Retrieval cue:** Ask, “Who decided, who advised, who drafted and who authorised?”

## Error family 2 — treating indirect election as nomination

**Faulty idea:** Because the Assembly was not directly elected, all its members were
nominated.

**Repair:** Provincial representatives were elected indirectly through provincial
legislatures using PR–STV; princely-state representatives were nominated by rulers.

**Retrieval cue:** Election chain versus nomination route.

## Error family 3 — using one membership figure everywhere

| Figure | Correct context |
|---:|---|
| 389 | Planned strength under Cabinet Mission |
| 296 | British Indian allocation / elected-seat result |
| 211 | Attendance at first meeting |
| 299 | Post-Partition Assembly strength |
| 284 | Members signing on 24 January 1950 |

**Retrieval cue:** Plan → election → first sitting → Partition → signature.

## Error family 4 — confusing constitutional dates

**Faulty chain:** Drafting Committee appointed on 26 November; Constitution signed and
fully commenced the same day.

**Repair chain:**

```text
29 Aug 1947  Drafting Committee
4 Nov 1948   Draft introduced
26 Nov 1949  Adopted; selected provisions commence
24 Jan 1950  Signed
26 Jan 1950  General commencement
```

## Error family 5 — overclaiming representativeness

**Faulty idea:** Presence of many communities proves the Assembly was fully democratic.

**Repair:** Social breadth is evidence of inclusion; it cannot replace direct universal
election. Use a two-column criticism-and-reply structure.

## Error family 6 — denying sovereignty throughout

**Faulty idea:** British origin means the Assembly always lacked sovereign power.

**Repair:** Concede its origin under the Cabinet Mission, then explain the legal
transformation under the Indian Independence Act 1947.

## Error family 7 — treating borrowing as copying

**Faulty idea:** A provision with a foreign source is unoriginal.

**Repair:** Ask how the device was altered, combined and directed towards Indian
problems. Originality may lie in synthesis and purpose.

## Error family 8 — binding original intent

**Faulty idea:** A framer's speech fixes constitutional meaning permanently.

**Repair hierarchy:**

```text
Enacted text → constitutional structure → binding precedent
                         ↑
             illuminated, not displaced, by Debates
```

## Error family 9 — overstating the adult-franchise constraint

**Faulty idea:** A direct election in 1946 was literally impossible.

**Repair:** State that restricted colonial rolls, Partition uncertainty, princely-state
relations and communal conflict made it administratively and politically difficult.
Do not turn difficulty into a proven impossibility.

## Error family 10 — mishandling key status

**Faulty idea:** A defensible substantive answer may be presented as an official key.

**Repair:** Separate three claims:

1. official paper wording verified;
2. constitutional analysis supports a conclusion;
3. official, unavailable or provisional key status.

Never merge them.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## The claim to constituent power

- M.N. Roy first proposed a Constituent Assembly in 1934.
- The Congress officially demanded it in 1935.
- Nehru's 1938 formulation required adult franchise and freedom from outside
  interference.
- August Offer 1940 accepted the demand in principle.
- Cripps proposals 1942 promised post-war constitution-making.
- The League wanted two Constituent Assemblies.
- Cabinet Mission arrived on 24 March 1946; its 16 May Plan rejected that demand and
  supplied one operative scheme.

> **Core distinction:** The nationalist demand created the claim; the Cabinet Mission
> created the initial mechanism.

## Composition and the representative problem

```text
389 = 296 British India + 93 princely states
296 = 292 Governors' Provinces + 4 Chief Commissioners' Provinces
```

- Roughly one seat per million population.
- British Indian seats divided among Muslim, Sikh and General categories.
- Community members in provincial legislatures elected representatives by PR–STV.
- Provincial legislatures rested on restricted colonial franchise.
- Princely-state rulers nominated their representatives.
- Congress 208; Muslim League 73; others 15.
- The Assembly was indirectly elected, partly elected and partly nominated.
- Gandhi was not a member.
- Distinguish electoral, social and deliberative representation.

## Opening and changing membership

- First meeting: 9 December 1946.
- League boycott; 211 attended.
- Temporary President: Sachchidananda Sinha.
- President: Rajendra Prasad.
- Vice-Presidents: H.C. Mookherjee and V.T. Krishnamachari.
- Princely states joined gradually.
- Partition reduced strength from 389 to 299:
  - provinces: 296 to 229;
  - princely states: 93 to 70.

## Named plural voices

- Hansa Mehta: equality and women's status.
- Dakshayani Velayudhan: untouchability and social transformation.
- Begum Aizaz Rasul: minority citizenship and opposition to separate electorates.
- Durgabai Deshmukh: social reform, language and public institutions.
- Rajkumari Amrit Kaur: rights, welfare and social reform.
- Ambedkar and Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar illustrate important expertise beyond a simple
  Congress-caucus account.
- These examples prove substantive contribution, not proportional representation.

## Objectives Resolution

- Moved by Nehru: 13 December 1946.
- Adopted: 22 January 1947.
- Core ideas:
  - independent sovereign republic;
  - Union and autonomous units;
  - autonomous units retaining residuary powers under clause 3;
  - power from the people;
  - justice, equality and freedom;
  - minority, backward-area and tribal safeguards;
  - territorial integrity;
  - world peace.
- Modified form became the Preamble.
- Federal-design contrast: Article 248 and Union List Entry 97 later vested residuary
  legislative power in Parliament.
- It guided drafting but was not an independent operative article.
- Preamble enacted last so that it conformed to the completed Constitution.
- Congress Experts Committee sittings: 20–22 July 1946 in New Delhi and
  15–17 August 1946 in Bombay.

## Sovereignty and dual function

- Indian Independence Act 1947 made the Assembly fully sovereign.
- It could alter or repeal British law applicable to India.
- It also functioned as Dominion Legislature on separate days.
- Constituent sittings: Rajendra Prasad.
- Legislative sittings: G.V. Mavlankar.
- First Dominion Legislature sitting: 17 November 1947.
- From 26 January 1950, it continued as Provisional Parliament.
- Provisional Parliament ended 17 April 1952; first elected Parliament began in May.

### Drafting Committee

- Appointed 29 August 1947.
- Original members:
  - Ambedkar;
  - N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar;
  - Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar;
  - K.M. Munshi;
  - Syed Mohammad Saadullah;

  - D.P. Khaitan.

- T.T. Krishnamachari replaced Khaitan.
- First published draft: February 1948.
- Public comments invited for about eight months.
- Revised draft: October 1948.
- Drafting Committee sat for 141 days.

> authorised constitutional process.

## Readings and enactment

- First reading: 4–9 November 1948.
- Second reading: 15 November 1948–17 October 1949.
- Amendments proposed: 7,653.
- Amendments discussed: 2,473.
- Third reading began: 14 November 1949.
- Adopted: 26 November 1949.
- Original form: Preamble + 395 Articles + 8 Schedules.
- 284 members signed on 24 January 1950.
- 11 sessions; 2 years, 11 months, 18 days.
- Draft debated for 114 days.
- About 60 foreign constitutions studied.
- Approximate expenditure: ₹64 lakh.

- Commonwealth membership ratified: May 1949.
- National Anthem and National Song adopted: 24 January 1950.
- Rajendra Prasad elected first President: 24 January 1950.

## Borrowing and interpretation

- Borrowing becomes constitutionally creative through selection, adaptation,
  combination and Indian purpose.
- Government of India Act 1935 was a major structural source.
- British, American, Irish and other constitutional devices were recombined.
- Constituent Assembly Debates are persuasive external aids.
- They may illuminate ambiguity, purpose and rejected alternatives.
- They cannot override enacted text.
- *Maneka Gandhi* shows doctrinal growth beyond narrow historical expectation.
- *Kesavananda Bharati* shows implied protection of constitutional identity.
