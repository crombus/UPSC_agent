# Administrative Tribunals — Live Session Edition

A central government clerk is denied a promotion. A commercial firm has a dispute under a supply contract with an arbitration clause. A motor-accident victim wants quick compensation without years of litigation. All three people are told, in different tones, "you don't need the High Court for this." Are they being fobbed off, or correctly routed? This session gives you the exact constitutional and statutory map to tell the difference — why specialised forums exist at all, how far they can go before judicial review has to step back in, why their independence keeps ending up in the Supreme Court, and how they differ from the other non-court forums (Lok Adalats, arbitration) that are often lumped in with them by mistake.

### The learner's problem

If a central government employee is denied a promotion, should the first stop be the High Court, or somewhere else? And why does the Constitution carry two separate Articles about tribunals — 323A and 323B — instead of one general "tribunal Article"? You cannot answer either question without first separating two different things: a constitutional *permission* to legislate, and the *actual tribunal* that only comes into existence once Parliament (or a State Legislature) uses that permission.

### Visual: two permission slips, not two tribunals

```text

recruitment and   industrial/labour disputes, land reforms, ceiling

                  listed matters

(even to cover    whichever has legislative competence

   |                 |

tribunal field       fields, potentially with a hierarchy
```

*Caption: both Articles are enabling provisions inserted together; neither Article, by itself, creates any tribunal — that is a separate statutory step, taught in Lesson 2.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of ordinary courts as a general hospital that must be ready to treat every kind of case. Some disputes are so technical and repetitive — service-law disputes, tax disputes, land-reform disputes — that a specialist clinic staffed with domain experts can diagnose and resolve them faster than sending every such case through the general hospital's queue. Articles 323A and 323B are the constitutional permission slips authorising Parliament, or where appropriate a State Legislature, to build such specialist clinics. A permission slip is not the clinic itself — nobody was treated at a clinic that exists only on paper.

### Technical doctrine

**Article 323A** confines itself to one field — recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, of any State, or of any local or other authority. Only **Parliament** may legislate under it, even where the tribunal being enabled will handle *State* employees' disputes; a State does not itself legislate a service tribunal into existence under 323A.

**Article 323B** is broader in subject but narrower in reach per subject: it lists specific fields (taxation, foreign exchange and import/export, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, ceiling on urban property, elections to Parliament and State legislatures, rent and tenancy rights, and other matters specified in the text) and allows **Parliament or the appropriate State Legislature**, whichever actually has legislative competence over that subject, to create a tribunal for it.

> Answer-grabbing line: Article 323A confines service tribunals to parliamentary creation even for State employees, while Article 323B lets Parliament or a competent State Legislature create tribunals across a list of specified subject fields.

### Step-by-step argument

1. **Premise:** ordinary courts were already burdened, and specific subject areas (service law, tax, land reforms) generate repetitive, technical disputes well suited to specialist adjudication.
2. **Premise:** Parliament wanted a constitutional mechanism for such adjudication, without hard-wiring one fixed tribunal design into the constitutional text itself.
3. **Mechanism:** insert enabling Articles rather than a ready-made tribunal, leaving the actual institutional design (benches, members, procedure) to ordinary legislation — flexible, amendable by statute rather than constitutional amendment.
4. **Mechanism:** because 323B's listed subjects cut across Union and State legislative competence, the "appropriate Legislature" test lets whichever government actually has competence over that subject act; 323A's single field (public-service recruitment/conditions) is treated as a Parliament-only domain even when State employees are covered, because the mechanism for State tribunals runs through Parliament's own law (at a State's request), not through the State's independent legislative power.
5. **Conclusion:** the two Articles therefore differ in both *subject scope* (one field versus many) and *law-making competence* (Parliament-only versus Parliament-or-State), even though both were inserted by the same amendment for the same general purpose.
6. **Qualification:** the original constitutional text also contemplated excluding ordinary-court review of tribunal decisions, preserving only a Supreme Court appeal under Article 136 — a design choice tested directly in Lesson 3.

### Example and its limit

A central government clerk's service dispute is the standard 323A example — Parliament's own Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 (Lesson 2) is the tribunal actually built under this permission. For 323B, the Article itself only names subject *categories* — tax, labour, land reform, elections and the rest — without constitutionally naming any specific tribunal; whichever competent legislature later builds a tribunal for one of these subjects does so by ordinary statute, not by the Article directly. The limit: do not assume every tribunal you encounter elsewhere in the syllabus was created "under Article 323B" merely because its subject appears on that list — some such bodies predate the 42nd Amendment entirely and rest on their own independent statutory basis.

### Comparison: Article 323A and Article 323B

| Axis | Article 323A | Article 323B |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Recruitment and service conditions of public servants | Specified fields: taxation, foreign exchange, labour disputes, land reforms, urban-property ceiling, elections, rent/tenancy and others listed |
| Law-maker | Parliament only (even for State employees, via its own law) | Parliament or the State Legislature, whichever has competence |
| Tribunal pattern | One administrative-tribunal field | Potentially several subject-specific tribunals, possibly hierarchical |
| Original court-exclusion text | Contemplated exclusion except Article 136 appeal | Similar exclusion possibility |
| Current judicial-review position | Survives under *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) | Same basic-structure floor |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Since Article 323A says 'Parliament only,' States must have no power at all over administrative tribunals touching their own employees."

**Reply:** "Parliament only" governs *who may legislate the enabling framework*, not *whose employees the framework can cover*. Parliament's own law under 323A can itself provide for a State Administrative Tribunal at a State's request — so State employees can be brought within a tribunal system without the State Legislature itself ever exercising 323A's law-making power.

**Qualified residual:** keep "who legislates" and "who is covered" analytically separate; collapsing them is a routine source of Prelims and Mains confusion between the two Articles.

### UPSC application

Prelims questions in this zone test the subject/law-maker distinction between the two Articles and the enabling-versus-creating distinction directly. Mains questions typically ask you to distinguish or compare the two Articles, or to use them as the opening constitutional anchor before a wider discussion of tribunal validity (Lesson 3) and competency (Lesson 4).

**Trap:** do not write that Article 323A or 323B itself "creates" CAT or any other named tribunal; both are enabling provisions, and the actual tribunal exists only once the competent legislature has used that power by ordinary statute.

### Revision notes

1. The Forty-second Amendment Act, 1976 inserted Part XIV-A, containing Articles 323A and 323B.
2. Article 323A covers one field only — recruitment and service conditions of public servants (Union, State, local or other authority).
3. Article 323A's law-maker is Parliament only, even when the tribunal being enabled will cover State employees.
4. Article 323B covers several listed fields — taxation, foreign exchange, labour disputes, land reforms, urban-property ceiling, elections, rent/tenancy and other specified matters.
5. Article 323B's law-maker is Parliament or the State Legislature, whichever has legislative competence over that particular subject.
6. Both Articles are enabling provisions; neither creates a tribunal by itself.
7. CAT itself was created only by a later, separate statute — the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 (Lesson 2).
8. The original constitutional text allowed excluding ordinary-court review of tribunal orders, preserving only an Article 136 Supreme Court appeal.
9. 323B anticipates a possible hierarchy of subject-specific tribunals; 323A anticipates a single administrative-tribunal field.
10. Exam-safe distinction: an enabling power, the statute that actually uses it, and the operating tribunal that results are three different things.
11. Do not confuse "who may legislate" (law-maker test) with "whose disputes are covered" (subject-coverage test) under Article 323A.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State government wants a tribunal to adjudicate land-reform compensation disputes. Which Article enables this, and who may legislate it?

**Model answer:** Article 323B enables it, because land reforms is one of its listed subject fields; either Parliament or the State Legislature may legislate, whichever actually holds legislative competence over land reforms for that State.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming any dispute "involving government" automatically falls under Article 323A; 323A is confined strictly to public-service recruitment and service-condition disputes, not every dispute touching government.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why Articles 323A and 323B, though inserted by the same constitutional amendment, assign different law-making competence.

**Model solution:** Articles 323A and 323B, both inserted by the Forty-second Amendment, 1976, enable specialised tribunals but differ in subject and competence. Article 323A is confined to one field — recruitment and service conditions of public servants — and reserves law-making to Parliament alone, even where the tribunal will cover State employees, because the mechanism for State coverage operates through Parliament's own law at a State's request rather than through independent State legislative action. Article 323B instead lists several subjects cutting across Union and State jurisdiction — taxation, labour disputes, land reforms, elections and others — so it sensibly allows "the appropriate Legislature," Parliament or the State Legislature, whichever actually holds competence over that subject, to legislate. The difference therefore tracks each Article's subject design: a single Union-anchored field under 323A, and a multi-subject, competence-sensitive field under 323B.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Correct statement of both Articles' subject scope — 3; correct law-maker distinction with reasoning — 4; link to Union/State competence logic — 2; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

Article 323A gave Parliament permission to legislate a service tribunal — but what did Parliament actually build when it exercised that permission? The next lesson opens the statute itself.

### The learner's problem

If you are a central government employee denied a promotion, where exactly do you file your case, what can that forum actually do, and is its order "as good as" a court's order? Article 323A only told you Parliament *could* legislate a service tribunal; this lesson opens the statute Parliament actually passed.

### Visual: the Original Application journey

*Caption: CAT's process looks and behaves like a court's process at every stage shown here; what happens after the order is the question the next lesson answers.*

### Plain-language intuition

CAT is best understood as a specialist adjudicatory body built by ordinary statute, not as a department inside the government it judges. It hears one narrow category of dispute — public-service recruitment and service-condition grievances for covered employees — through a procedure that looks like a simplified court hearing: notice to both sides, an opportunity to be heard, and a reasoned order at the end. It is not an internal departmental complaints desk, and it is not a constitutional court either; it sits in a specific statutory middle zone.

### Technical doctrine

The **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985**, enacted under Article 323A, establishes the **Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)** — a Principal Bench plus notified Benches, with Judicial and Administrative Members — and separately enables **State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)** and **Joint Administrative Tribunals** for two or more States. CAT's jurisdiction under Section 14 principally covers recruitment and service matters of persons appointed to covered Union civil services, posts and notified bodies. The Act's Section 2(a)-(c) exclusion structure keeps outside it: members of the naval, military, air and other armed forces of the Union; officers or servants of the Supreme Court, High Courts and courts subordinate to them; and the secretarial staff of Parliament, State Legislatures and Union-Territory Legislatures.

### Step-by-step argument

1. **Premise:** Article 323A permitted Parliament to legislate; the 1985 Act is that legislation.
2. **Mechanism:** the Act creates CAT with a Principal Bench and notified Benches, staffed by a mix of judicial and administrative expertise, so the forum combines legal rigour with domain familiarity with service rules.
3. **Mechanism:** procedure is flexible and not rigidly bound by the Code of Civil Procedure, but it still requires notice, hearing, evidence handling and a reasoned order — flexibility is procedural economy, not a lower standard of fairness.
4. **Mechanism:** the Tribunal holds civil-court-type statutory powers — summoning witnesses, requiring documents, accepting affidavits, issuing commissions — plus a contempt power, so its orders carry real adjudicatory weight rather than being merely advisory.
5. **Conclusion:** these features together give CAT a genuine judicial character in function — the exact proposition the 2019 PYQ asks you to explain.
6. **Qualification:** this jurisdiction is purely statutory and bounded by Section 14's coverage and Section 2(a)-(c); CAT does not try criminal offences, hear every dispute merely because government is a party, or decide ordinary civil disputes unrelated to recruitment or service conditions.

### Example and its limit

A central government clerk challenging a disciplinary penalty is a textbook Original Application before CAT. By contrast, that same clerk's spouse suing a private landlord over a tenancy dispute does not go anywhere near CAT, even though one party happens to be a central government employee — jurisdiction is tested by the *subject matter* of the dispute (recruitment/service conditions), never by the mere *identity* of one party as a government employee.

### Comparative treatment

| Dimension | Ordinary civil court | CAT |
|---|---|---|
| Primary role | General civil adjudication across subject matters | Specialist adjudication confined to covered recruitment/service matters |
| Members | Judges | Judicial Members and Administrative Members |
| Procedure | Code of Civil Procedure | Natural justice, not rigidly bound by the CPC |
| Typical advantage claimed | General jurisdiction, settled procedure | Subject expertise, simpler procedure, potentially faster disposal |

| Dimension | CAT | A Commission (e.g., an advisory or investigative body) |
|---|---|---|
| Core function | Binding adjudication of a covered dispute | Investigation, advice or recommendation, unless a specific statute grants adjudicatory power |
| Output | Reasoned, enforceable order | Report or recommendation, ordinarily not self-executing |

Why they differ: CAT exists specifically to adjudicate a bounded category of disputes with binding force, whereas a general-purpose commission exists to inform or advise government action — collapsing the two into one "government body" category is a common institutional-taxonomy error, picked up fully in Lesson 7.

### Criticism and reply

**Objection:** "CAT's members include 'Administrative Members' rather than only judges, so CAT cannot truly adjudicate independently against the very government that appoints its members."

**Reply:** mixed judicial-technical composition is a deliberate design choice meant to combine legal rigour with domain expertise in service-law matters, and is not unique to CAT among specialist adjudicatory bodies. The independence concern is genuine, but it is a question about *how* members are selected, how long they serve and how administratively autonomous the Tribunal is — exactly the ground covered by the independence case law in Lesson 4 — not a flaw inherent in having technical members at all.

**Qualified residual:** composition alone neither proves nor disproves independence; the appointment process, tenure security and administrative autonomy are the real tests, and those tests are litigated repeatedly in the case law that follows.

### UPSC application

The 2019 GS-II PYQ ("The Central Administrative Tribunal... is exercising its powers as an independent judicial authority") is anchored directly in this lesson's judicial-character markers, together with Lesson 3's review-chain qualification. Prelims traps in this zone: CAT is not a constitutional court; CAT does not try criminal offences; "flexible procedure" does not mean "arbitrary procedure" — natural justice, evidence and reasoned orders remain mandatory.

### Revision notes

1. The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 is the statute Parliament enacted under Article 323A.
2. It creates CAT (Principal Bench plus notified Benches) and enables State and Joint Administrative Tribunals.
3. CAT's members include both Judicial Members and Administrative Members.
4. Jurisdiction (Section 14) principally covers recruitment and service matters of covered Union public servants and notified bodies.
5. Section 2(a)-(c) excludes Union armed-forces members; officers/servants of the Supreme Court, High Courts and subordinate courts; and legislative-secretariat staff of Parliament, States and Union Territories.
6. Procedure is flexible and not rigidly bound by the Code of Civil Procedure, but natural justice still applies.
7. CAT holds civil-court-type powers: summoning, document production, affidavits, commissions and contempt.
8. CAT's orders are reasoned and binding, not merely advisory.
9. Jurisdiction is tested by subject matter (recruitment/service conditions), not by whether a party happens to be a government employee.
10. CAT does not try criminal offences and does not absorb every public-employment or civil dispute.
11. Mixed judicial-technical composition is a design choice for expertise, not by itself a measure of independence.
12. Tenure, age, vacancy and appointment-process details remain amendment- and judgment-sensitive; treat exact current figures as dated and re-verify them.

### Concept check

**Question:** A central government employee alleges that a disciplinary order against them was passed without giving them an opportunity to respond. Can CAT examine this complaint, and on what basis?

**Model answer:** Yes. The complaint concerns the service conditions of a covered Union public servant, which is squarely within CAT's statutory jurisdiction, and CAT's procedure requires natural justice even though it is not rigidly bound by the Code of Civil Procedure; a failure to give an opportunity to be heard is exactly the kind of defect CAT can examine and correct through a reasoned order.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming "flexible procedure" means CAT can dispense with natural justice altogether; flexibility concerns formality and pace, not fairness or evidentiary rigour.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain CAT's jurisdiction, its principal statutory exclusions, and the procedural features that give its adjudication a judicial character.

**Model solution:** The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, enacted under Article 323A, creates the Central Administrative Tribunal with a Principal Bench and notified Benches staffed by Judicial and Administrative Members. Its Section 14 jurisdiction principally covers recruitment and service-condition disputes of persons appointed to covered Union public services, posts and notified bodies, subject to express exclusions such as armed-forces personnel and staff of constitutional courts and legislative secretariats. Procedurally, CAT follows natural justice — notice, hearing, evidence and a reasoned order — while remaining free of the Code of Civil Procedure's rigid formality, and it holds civil-court-type powers of summons, document production, affidavits, commissions and contempt. Together, bounded statutory jurisdiction and judicial-style procedure give CAT genuine adjudicatory, judicial-in-function character, though its independence depends on separate appointment and tenure safeguards.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Statutory source and institutional structure — 2; jurisdiction and exclusions — 3; procedural/judicial-character markers — 3; independence qualification — 1; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

CAT looks and behaves like a court in its daily work — but can Parliament simply declare its decisions final, with no ordinary court allowed to review them at all? That exact question reached the Supreme Court twice.

### The learner's problem

If a tribunal is meant to replace the High Court for first-instance service disputes, can the law also remove the High Court's power to review the tribunal's decisions altogether — making the tribunal truly final, subject only to a discretionary Supreme Court appeal? This is not a hypothetical: the original constitutional design tried exactly that, and the Supreme Court had to decide whether it could stand.

### Visual: the review-chain timeline

```text
1976  42nd Amendment: Articles 323A(2)(d) / 323B(3)(d) textually permit excluding High
      Court/Supreme Court judicial review, leaving only a discretionary Article 136 appeal
1985  Administrative Tribunals Act: CAT created as a substitute first-instance forum
1986  (decided 9 Dec; reported (1987) 1 SCC 124) S.P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India:
      upholds the 1985 Act, BUT ONLY IF the tribunal is an "effective alternative
      institutional mechanism" with efficacy equal to a High Court
1997  L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India (seven-judge Bench): judicial review under
      Articles 226/227 (High Court) and Article 32 (Supreme Court) is PART OF THE BASIC
      STRUCTURE; the exclusion clauses in 323A(2)(d)/323B(3)(d) are unconstitutional to
      that extent; tribunal orders now lie to a High Court Division Bench
```

*Caption: review was restored in 1997, but at the cost of adding a tier between the tribunal and the Supreme Court — the efficiency question this creates is picked up directly in Lesson 5.*

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine a referee is appointed for a specialised sport, and the organisers say, "the referee's decision is completely final — no appeal to anyone, except in the rarest case the league commissioner agrees to look at it." The Supreme Court's job here was to ask: is a referee whose rulings can never ordinarily be checked still operating inside the same constitutional order as everyone else, or has the organiser effectively carved out a zone the Constitution does not reach? The Court's answer evolved from "acceptable, if the referee is good enough" (1987) to "no — a basic supervisory check can never be switched off entirely" (1997).

### Technical doctrine

**Basic structure** is the judicially developed doctrine that certain constitutional features are so fundamental that not even a constitutional amendment can abolish them. In *L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India* (1997), a seven-judge Bench held that **judicial review of legislation and administrative action under Articles 226/227 (High Courts) and Article 32 (Supreme Court) is part of the basic structure**, and that the exclusion-of-review clauses inserted into Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d) by the 42nd Amendment are **unconstitutional to that extent**.

### Step-by-step argument

1. **Premise:** the original design wanted tribunals to be genuinely final, both for speed and to reduce the ordinary courts' caseload — hence the exclusion clauses.
2. **Mechanism (decision dated 9 December 1986; reported as (1987) 1 SCC 124):** in *S.P. Sampath Kumar*, the Supreme Court initially tolerated substitution of the High Court by a tribunal, but only on condition that the tribunal was an "effective alternative institutional mechanism" with efficacy equal to the High Court it replaced — an efficacy test, not an unconditional green light.
3. **Mechanism (1997):** *L. Chandra Kumar*, a larger seven-judge Bench, revisited the question and held that Articles 226/227 and 32 judicial review is basic structure, so it cannot be excluded by an ordinary constitutional amendment's effect through 323A/323B — the 1987 efficacy-test compromise was superseded by an outright restoration of review.
4. **Mechanism:** the ordinary chain is CAT/SAT order -> Division Bench of the territorial High Court under Articles 226/227 -> possible special leave to appeal to the Supreme Court under Article 136 from the High Court's decision.
5. **Conclusion:** tribunals became **supplemental** to constitutional courts — first-instance specialist forums — rather than **substitutes** for them.
6. **Qualification:** this restores supervision but lengthens the chain by one tier; a tribunal may decide constitutional questions arising in its own proceedings at first instance, but it cannot test the vires of the very parent statute that creates it.

### Example and its limit

A CAT or SAT order rejecting a service claim can be challenged before the territorial High Court's Division Bench under Articles 226/227 — this is the *ordinary* route after *L. Chandra Kumar*. A further approach to the Supreme Court is ordinarily by seeking special leave under Article 136 from the High Court's decision; Article 136 is discretionary and does not bypass the required High Court review chain.

### Comparative treatment

| Dimension | *S.P. Sampath Kumar* (1987) | *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) |
|---|---|---|
| Bench size | Smaller Bench | Seven-judge Bench |
| Core holding | Substitution of courts by tribunals upheld, conditional on efficacy | Articles 226/227/32 review is basic structure; conditional efficacy test superseded |
| Status of exclusion clauses | Tolerated subject to the efficacy condition | Held unconstitutional to the extent they exclude High Court/Supreme Court review |
| Resulting review route | Primarily an Article 136 Supreme Court appeal | CAT/SAT order -> High Court Division Bench -> possible Article 136 special leave from the High Court decision |

Why they differ: the 1987 Bench tried to preserve finality by conditioning it on institutional quality; the larger 1997 Bench concluded that no institutional-quality safeguard can substitute for the structural guarantee of ordinary constitutional-court review itself.

### Criticism and reply

**Objection:** "If judicial review can never be excluded, tribunalisation has failed at its central goal of reducing litigation to one final forum."

**Reply:** the tribunal still fully absorbs the first-instance adjudicatory burden — every covered dispute still begins and is substantively decided at the specialist forum, which is itself the main efficiency gain. What was preserved is only the *supervisory* layer, not a right to relitigate the whole dispute afresh at the High Court; "no permanent finality" is not the same as "no efficiency gain."

**Qualified residual:** genuine efficiency after 1997 depends on first-instance tribunal quality — adequate benches, timely appointments, domain expertise — rather than on stripping away review; this is the precise thread Lesson 5 develops through the rationalisation story.

### UPSC application

The 2018 GS-II PYQ ("How far do you agree... tribunals curtail the jurisdiction of ordinary courts? ... discuss the constitutional validity and competency of the tribunals") is centred on this lesson, drawing on Lesson 1's constitutional source and Lesson 4's competency/independence case law as supporting material. Trap: writing that tribunal decisions are "immune" from High Court review, or that Article 136 is the ordinary review route — both are false after *L. Chandra Kumar*.

### Revision notes

1. Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d), as originally enacted, permitted excluding High Court and Supreme Court judicial review of tribunal decisions.
2. *S.P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India* was decided on 9 December 1986 and reported as (1987) 1 SCC 124; it upheld tribunal substitution conditional on an "effective alternative institutional mechanism."
3. *L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India* (1997), a seven-judge Bench, held that judicial review under Articles 226/227 and 32 is part of the basic structure.
4. The exclusion clauses in 323A(2)(d)/323B(3)(d) were held unconstitutional to the extent they exclude such review.
5. CAT/SAT orders now lie to a Division Bench of the territorial High Court; a further Supreme Court route is discretionary special leave under Article 136 from the High Court decision.
6. Tribunals are supplemental first-instance forums, not substitutes for constitutional courts, after 1997.
7. A tribunal may decide constitutional questions arising at first instance but cannot test the vires of its own parent statute.
8. Restoring review added a review tier; it did not remove the tribunal's first-instance adjudicatory role.
9. Article 136 remains a discretionary, exceptional Supreme Court route, not the ordinary post-tribunal remedy.
10. The real efficiency question after 1997 shifts to first-instance tribunal quality (Lesson 5), not to the possibility of stripping review.
11. Do not describe the 1987 and 1997 holdings as saying the same thing — 1987 conditioned substitution; 1997 restored review as a structural guarantee.

### Concept check

**Question:** A litigant argues that because CAT's decisions can be appealed to the Supreme Court under Article 136, there is no need to also allow High Court review under Articles 226/227. Evaluate this argument using *L. Chandra Kumar*.

**Model answer:** The argument is incorrect. *L. Chandra Kumar* held that Articles 226/227 and 32 judicial review is part of the basic structure and cannot be excluded merely because a discretionary Article 136 appeal exists; Article 136 is an exceptional, discretionary remedy, not a structural substitute for ordinary High Court supervisory review, so tribunal orders remain reviewable by a High Court Division Bench regardless of the Article 136 option.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Article 136's availability as satisfying the basic-structure requirement of judicial review; the two are legally and functionally distinct remedies.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Trace how the judicial-review position on tribunal decisions changed between 1987 and 1997, and explain the constitutional principle that produced the change.

**Model solution:** The 42nd Amendment's Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d) originally allowed excluding High Court and Supreme Court judicial review of tribunal orders, leaving only a discretionary Article 136 appeal. In *S.P. Sampath Kumar v Union of India* — decided on 9 December 1986 and reported as (1987) 1 SCC 124 — the Supreme Court upheld this substitution model, but conditioned it on an "effective alternative institutional mechanism" with efficacy equal to the court replaced. In *L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India* (1997), a seven-judge Bench held Articles 226/227 and 32 review to be basic structure and invalidated the exclusion clauses to that extent. The shift was from institutional efficacy as a substitute for review to constitutional supervision as non-negotiable. Today's ordinary chain is CAT/SAT -> territorial High Court Division Bench -> possible Article 136 special leave from the High Court decision.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Correct statement of original exclusion design — 3; *Sampath Kumar* holding and efficacy test — 4; *L. Chandra Kumar* holding and basic-structure reasoning — 5; the principle explaining the shift — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

Judicial review survived intact — but review of a decision does not, by itself, guarantee that the people making that decision are genuinely independent of the government whose actions they judge. That became the next battleground.

### The learner's problem

Government is usually the largest single litigant appearing before service and regulatory tribunals. If government also controls who gets appointed to those tribunals, how long they serve, and how well the tribunal is funded, can the tribunal realistically judge the government fairly? This is not an abstract worry — it produced a specific, repeatedly litigated constitutional doctrine.

### Visual: the independence doctrine ladder

```text
Union of India v R. Gandhi (2010)
  NCLT/NCLAT tribunalisation of company-law jurisdiction upheld ONLY IF qualifications,
  selection, tenure and administration preserve independence equivalent to the judicial
  work transferred -- sets the "functional equivalence" test
        |
        v
Madras Bar Association v Union of India (2014) -- the NTT case
  National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005 STRUCK DOWN: vesting the High Court's power over
  substantial questions of law in an executive-dominated tribunal violates separation
  of powers/basic structure -- an outer limit, not just a safeguards checklist
        |
        v
Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank Ltd (2019)
  2017 Tribunal Rules invalidated as compromising independence; the Money-Bill route by
  which the governing Finance Act, 2017 was passed is referred to a larger Bench -- still
  unsettled, mark as CURRENT
```

*Caption: each decision addresses a different failure mode — composition safeguards (2010), an outright design limit (2014), and the rules actually implementing those safeguards (2019) — together they form one continuous independence doctrine, not three unrelated cases.*

### Plain-language intuition

Picture a referee whose contract, salary and reappointment all depend entirely on one of the two teams playing the match. Even an honest referee working under such a contract faces a structural conflict of interest the rules should remove before the match starts, not after a result is disputed. The courts here are essentially insisting that whenever a tribunal takes over work a court used to do, the *referee's contract* — qualifications, selection, tenure, removal, administrative control — must look as secure as a judge's, not as dependent as an employee's.

### Technical doctrine

The **functional equivalence principle**, set out in *Union of India v R. Gandhi* (2010), holds that when judicial work is transferred from a court to a tribunal, the tribunal's members' **qualifications, selection, tenure, removal and administrative conditions must preserve independence and separation of powers equivalent to the judicial function being transferred**. The judgment also recognised that Parliament may create tribunals through its ordinary legislative competence under Article 246 read with the Seventh Schedule; Articles 323A and 323B are enabling, not exhaustive. *Madras Bar Association v Union of India* (2014) went further for the National Tax Tribunal: some design choices (vesting a High Court's core power over "substantial questions of law" in an executive-dominated body) fail this principle so completely that the tribunal itself, not merely one defective rule, was struck down.

### Step-by-step argument

1. **Premise:** company-law adjudication (then before the High Court) is a core judicial function with real consequences for parties, including the government as a regulator or litigant.
2. **Mechanism (2010):** *R. Gandhi* accepts tribunalising this function (creating the NCLT/NCLAT) but conditions acceptance on qualifications, selection, tenure and administration matching the independence the High Court itself would have provided.
3. **Mechanism (2014):** *Madras Bar Association*'s NTT case shows the limit of conditional acceptance — some transfers (a High Court's power over substantial questions of law, handed to an executive-dominated body) are struck down outright, because no safeguard package can cure that particular design choice.
4. **Mechanism:** Parliament's Finance Act, 2017 (passed as a Money Bill, itself a contested legislative route) rationalised several tribunals and centralised rule-making power over appointments, tenure and service conditions.
5. **Mechanism (2019):** *Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank Ltd* invalidates the 2017 Tribunal Rules framed under that Act as compromising independence, and separately refers the underlying Money-Bill characterisation question to a larger Bench — a reference that remained unresolved, so it must be marked current and re-checked rather than treated as settled.
6. **Conclusion:** a recurring pattern emerges — Parliament or the Executive enacts a tribunal-design framework, the Supreme Court finds specific defects and strikes them down, and a fresh framework is attempted — a cycle Lesson 5 traces through to its most recent stage.
7. **Qualification:** functional equivalence is a flexible, whole-design standard (who selects, how long members serve, who can remove them, who funds and administers the tribunal) rather than a fixed headcount or a single rigid rule.

### Example and its limit

The NCLT/NCLAT is the standard example of tribunalisation accepted subject to safeguards; the National Tax Tribunal is the standard example of tribunalisation rejected outright. The limit: the objection in the NTT case was to the *design* — an executive-dominated body exercising a High Court's core power over substantial questions of law — not to specialist adjudication of tax law as such; do not generalise this into "tribunals can never handle tax disputes."

### Comparative treatment

| Case | What was being transferred | Outcome | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *R. Gandhi* (2010) | Company-law jurisdiction to NCLT/NCLAT | Upheld, conditionally | Safeguards (qualifications, selection, tenure, administration) must match the judicial work transferred |
| *Madras Bar Association*/NTT (2014) | High Court's power over substantial questions of law, to an executive-dominated tribunal | Struck down outright | Some transfers to an executive-dominated body are incompatible with separation of powers regardless of safeguards |
| *Rojer Mathew* (2019) | Appointment/tenure rules framed under the Finance Act, 2017 | Rules invalidated; Money-Bill question referred | Executive-framed rules compromised independence; the legislative route itself remained unsettled |

### Criticism and reply

**Objection:** "Since almost every major reform attempt — the 2017 rules, and later the 2021 Act (Lesson 5) — has been partly struck down, judicial review has made Parliament's tribunal-design power practically meaningless."

**Reply:** each decision struck down specific defective features (executive-dominated composition, inadequate tenure, a flawed rule-making process), not Parliament's power to design tribunals at all; Parliament retains full power to legislate tribunal structure, provided the design satisfies the functional-equivalence floor. The repeated strike-downs show the safeguard is being actively enforced, not that the underlying legislative power is illusory.

**Qualified residual:** persistent case-by-case correction is costly — in litigation time, uncertainty for sitting members, and repeated redesign — which is precisely why a durable, independent, prospective institutional solution was eventually pressed by the courts instead of further one-off corrections (Lesson 5).

### UPSC application

This case law supplies the independence doctrine behind the "competency" limb of the 2018 GS-II PYQ and the reform-assessment limb of the 2025 GS-II PYQ. Trap: treating every adverse ruling as proof that tribunalisation itself is unconstitutional, rather than that a specific design feature was defective.

### Revision notes

1. *Union of India v R. Gandhi* (2010) sets the functional-equivalence principle for transferring judicial work to a tribunal.
2. Functional equivalence requires qualifications, selection, tenure, removal and administration equivalent to the judicial function transferred.
3. *Madras Bar Association v Union of India* (2014), the NTT case, struck down the National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005 outright.
4. The NTT objection was to an executive-dominated body holding a High Court's power over substantial questions of law, not to tax-tribunal adjudication as a category.
5. The Finance Act, 2017 (a Money Bill) rationalised tribunals and centralised rule-making power over appointments and service conditions.
6. *Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank Ltd* (2019) invalidated the 2017 Tribunal Rules for compromising independence.
7. *Rojer Mathew* also referred the Money-Bill characterisation of the Finance Act, 2017 to a larger Bench — treat this as unresolved and re-verify its current status.
8. A recurring pattern links these decisions: enact a framework, courts find defects, framework is redesigned.
9. Functional equivalence is a whole-design test, not a single fixed numerical or compositional rule.
10. Do not read any single adverse ruling as invalidating tribunalisation as a category; each struck down a specific design defect.
11. The independence doctrine here directly supports, but is distinct from, the rationalisation and reform story in Lesson 5.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did the Supreme Court strike down the National Tax Tribunal outright in 2014, while in 2010 it had accepted the NCLT/NCLAT subject to conditions?

**Model answer:** The NCLT/NCLAT transfer in *R. Gandhi* (2010) could be rendered constitutionally acceptable by imposing qualification, selection, tenure and administrative safeguards equivalent to the judicial function transferred. The National Tax Tribunal, by contrast, vested a High Court's own power over substantial questions of law in a body dominated by executive appointees — a design the Court held incompatible with separation of powers regardless of any safeguard package, because the core judicial function itself, not merely its staffing, had been misallocated.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming both cases apply the identical "add more safeguards" remedy; the 2014 case identifies an outer limit where no safeguard can cure the transfer.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine how the functional-equivalence principle evolved from *R. Gandhi* (2010) through *Madras Bar Association*/NTT (2014) to *Rojer Mathew* (2019).

**Model solution:** The functional-equivalence principle, established in *Union of India v R. Gandhi* (2010), holds that when judicial work is transferred to a tribunal, the tribunal's qualifications, selection, tenure, removal and administration must preserve independence equivalent to the judicial function transferred — the Court there accepted the NCLT/NCLAT model on this conditional basis. *Madras Bar Association v Union of India* (2014), concerning the National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005, extended the doctrine by identifying an outer limit: vesting a High Court's own power over substantial questions of law in an executive-dominated tribunal cannot be cured by any safeguard package and must be struck down outright, because the transfer itself, not merely its staffing details, violates separation of powers. *Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank Ltd* (2019) then tested the implementing rules framed under the Finance Act, 2017, and found the 2017 Tribunal Rules compromised independence in practice, while separately referring the Act's Money-Bill passage route to a larger Bench as an unresolved constitutional question. Read together, the three decisions show the doctrine deepening from a conditional safeguards test (2010), to an absolute design limit (2014), to scrutiny of the actual implementing rules and the legislative route used to frame them (2019) — a progressively stricter and more procedurally searching independence standard.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** *R. Gandhi* principle correctly stated — 4; *Madras Bar Association*/NTT outer-limit reasoning — 4; *Rojer Mathew* rules-and-route holding — 4; explicit account of the doctrine's progressive evolution — 2; coherent, qualified 250-word structure — 1.

The independence battleground did not stay inside the courtroom — Parliament kept attempting a durable statutory framework, right up to the most recent reform. That full rationalisation story, including what is actually operative today, is next.

### The learner's problem

A friend tells you, "Take it to a Lok Adalat; it is basically informal arbitration. If settlement fails, it can become a Permanent Lok Adalat." Both claims are wrong. Ordinary Lok Adalat, Permanent Lok Adalat and arbitration are three distinct legal routes, with different entry points, powers and review controls.

### Visual: three routes, not one ladder

```text
ORDINARY LOK ADALAT (Chapter VI)
Pending case / eligible pre-litigation matter -> compromise effort
       -> settlement: award/deemed decree
       -> no settlement: pending case returns; parties use ordinary remedy

PERMANENT LOK ADALAT (Chapter VI-A, inserted in 2002)
Separate pre-litigation application for public-utility dispute
       -> statutory conciliation
       -> failure: PLA may decide eligible dispute on merits

ARBITRATION
Arbitration agreement -> private adjudication -> award
       -> limited statutory challenge under section 34
```

*Caption: failed settlement before an ordinary Lok Adalat never converts that proceeding into a PLA case; PLA must be separately invoked before litigation within its own public-utility jurisdiction.*

### Plain-language intuition

An ordinary Lok Adalat is a settlement table: it can help parties agree, but cannot impose a winner. A PLA is a separately established public-utility forum: it first tries settlement and may then decide an eligible dispute. Arbitration is a privately chosen judge: parties consent in advance to adjudication through an arbitration agreement. Their purposes also differ: Lok Adalats answer an access deficit, especially for citizens and poorer litigants, while arbitration answers a commercial-autonomy deficit by letting parties choose private adjudication.

### Part A — Ordinary Lok Adalat

Under the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**, anchored in Article 39A, an ordinary Lok Adalat may take a pending case or an eligible pre-litigation matter, excluding non-compoundable offences. Its legal power depends on genuine compromise. *State of Punjab v Jalour Singh* (2008) confirms that it has no adjudicatory role: if settlement fails, a pending case returns to the referring court and a pre-litigation party remains free to pursue the ordinary remedy.

An agreed award is deemed a civil-court decree and Section 21 provides no statutory appeal. That finality is not absolute constitutional immunity: writ review survives on narrow grounds such as fraud, want of genuine consent, jurisdictional error or constitutional illegality.

### Part B — Permanent Lok Adalat

**Chapter VI-A**, inserted in **2002**, creates PLAs for one or more **public utility services**. The route is separately invoked at the pre-litigation stage. Illustrations include transport, postal or telephone service, supply of power/light/water, public conservancy or sanitation, hospital/dispensary service and insurance, together with other notified services.

```text
Eligible public-utility dispute, not yet before a court
        -> application to PLA
        -> while PLA proceedings continue, parties cannot invoke another court
        -> conciliation first
        -> settlement OR, if conciliation fails, merits decision within jurisdiction
```

The merits power is bounded by statutory exclusions and the **currently notified pecuniary limit**; do not memorise a stale rupee ceiling. The PLA is guided by natural justice, objectivity, fair play, equity and other principles of justice, and is not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure or the Indian Evidence Act. *InterGlobe Aviation Ltd v N. Satchidanand* (2011) reinforces that ordinary Lok Adalat and PLA are legally distinct; the latter's limited merits power cannot be casually transferred to the former.

### Part C — Arbitration

Arbitration is private adjudication under the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**, based on party consent expressed through an arbitration agreement. The arbitrator decides an arbitrable civil/commercial dispute; the award is enforceable as a decree and may be challenged only on limited Section 34 grounds. Criminal liability is not arbitrable.

### Comparative treatment

| Axis | Ordinary Lok Adalat | Permanent Lok Adalat | Arbitration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal source | LSA Act, Ch. VI | LSA Act, Ch. VI-A (2002) | A&C Act, 1996 |
| Entry stage | Pending or eligible pre-litigation matter | Separately invoked pre-litigation public-utility dispute | Arbitration agreement |
| Core method | Compromise only | Conciliation first; limited merits adjudication after failure | Private adjudication |
| If settlement fails | Matter returns to ordinary route | PLA may decide eligible merits | Arbitrator proceeds to award |
| Institutional purpose | Affordable access and consensual closure, especially for citizens and poorer litigants | Accessible pre-litigation resolution of public-utility disputes | Commercial autonomy over forum, procedure and adjudicator |
| Cost/fee | Free; court fee is refunded when a referred pending case settles | Free statutory legal-services route | Parties bear arbitrator and institutional fees |
| Procedure | Justice, equity, fair play and legal principles | Natural justice/equity; no binding CPC/Evidence Act | Governing Act and party-agreed procedure |
| Outcome/control | Deemed decree; no statutory appeal; narrow writ review survives | Deemed decree/statutory finality; constitutional review survives | Enforceable award; limited Section 34 challenge |
| Criminal boundary | Compoundable offences may settle; non-compoundable excluded | Non-compoundable offences excluded | Criminal liability not arbitrable |

Why they differ: ordinary Lok Adalat finality rests on consent; PLA's authority rests on a special pre-litigation public-utility statute; arbitration rests on contractual consent to adjudication. They are complementary responses to different justice-system deficits, not rivals or substitutes.

### Step-by-step argument

1. Article 39A and the 1987 Act address the access deficit, especially for citizens and poorer litigants, through free legal-services institutions and court-fee refund on settled referred cases.
2. Ordinary Lok Adalat can facilitate compromise but cannot adjudicate contested merits.
3. Failure before ordinary Lok Adalat sends the matter back to the ordinary route; it does not produce a PLA conversion.
4. Chapter VI-A separately creates PLA for pre-litigation public-utility disputes and bars another court route while those proceedings continue.
5. PLA conciliates first, then may adjudicate within the notified pecuniary limit and statutory exclusions.
6. Arbitration answers a different need: commercial autonomy through consensual private adjudication, with arbitrator and institutional fees borne by the parties.
7. Each forum's finality remains bounded by its own statutory or constitutional control.

### Example and its limit

A motor-accident claim settled by agreement at an ordinary Lok Adalat ends in a compromise award. If that settlement effort fails, the claim returns to the ordinary legal route; it does not become a PLA matter. By contrast, an electricity-billing dispute may be brought separately to a PLA before court proceedings because electricity supply is a public utility. The limit: even a PLA cannot ignore its notified pecuniary ceiling, sectoral jurisdiction or criminal exclusion.

### Criticism and reply

**Objection:** "Statutory finality means a Lok Adalat or PLA award can never be questioned."

**Reply:** Section 21 removes a statutory appeal from an ordinary Lok Adalat award, and Chapter VI-A gives PLA awards strong finality. Neither provision removes the High Court's constitutional writ power where fraud, want of consent, jurisdictional error, denial of natural justice or constitutional illegality is shown.

**Qualified residual:** review is exceptional rather than a merits appeal; courts protect legality without turning every settlement award into another appellate round.

**Purpose qualification:** Lok Adalats and arbitration are complementary, not interchangeable: the former widen affordable access, while the latter protect commercial choice over private adjudication.

### UPSC application

The 2024 GS-II PYQ requires three separate limbs: explain both forums, distinguish their source/process/finality, and answer the civil-criminal boundary. Add the PLA qualification only after clearly stating that it is a separate Chapter VI-A institution, not an ordinary Lok Adalat transformed after failure.

### Revision notes

1. Article 39A is the constitutional anchor; the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 supplies the statute.
2. Ordinary Lok Adalat handles pending or eligible pre-litigation matters but cannot decide contested merits.
3. *Jalour Singh* (2008): an ordinary award must rest on compromise; failed settlement returns to the ordinary route.
4. Section 21 gives no statutory appeal, but narrow writ review survives for fraud, want of consent, jurisdictional error or constitutional illegality.
5. PLA is a separate Chapter VI-A institution inserted in 2002.
6. PLA is pre-litigation and limited to public-utility services and the notified pecuniary framework.
7. Public-utility illustrations include transport, communications, power/water, sanitation, hospitals and insurance.
8. While PLA proceedings continue, the parties cannot invoke another court for the same dispute.
9. PLA conciliates first and may decide eligible merits only after conciliation fails.

### Concept check

**Question:** A commercial dispute under a contract containing an arbitration clause also involves an allegation of forgery, a non-compoundable offence. Can the arbitral tribunal resolve the entire matter?

**Model answer:** No. The arbitral tribunal can resolve the arbitrable civil/commercial dispute arising from the contract, but criminal liability — including a non-compoundable offence such as forgery — is not arbitrable and must proceed separately through the criminal justice system.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming an arbitration clause transfers every civil and criminal consequence of the transaction to the arbitrator.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish ordinary Lok Adalats, Permanent Lok Adalats and arbitration by legal source, consent, merits power and finality.

**Model solution:** Ordinary Lok Adalats operate under Chapter VI of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 and secure compromise in pending or eligible pre-litigation matters. They cannot adjudicate contested merits; failed settlement returns the matter to the ordinary route. A Section 21 award is a deemed decree with no statutory appeal, though narrow writ review survives for fraud, want of consent, jurisdictional error or constitutional illegality. Permanent Lok Adalats are separate Chapter VI-A bodies, inserted in 2002, for pre-litigation public-utility disputes. They conciliate first and may decide eligible merits after failure, within statutory exclusions and the notified pecuniary limit. Arbitration rests on an agreement under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996; a private tribunal adjudicates civil/commercial disputes and its award faces limited Section 34 challenge. Thus, compromise, statutory hybrid adjudication and contractual adjudication must not be conflated.

### The learner's problem

Under exam-time pressure, a question could ask about any combination of the institutions you have met — tribunals, High Courts, commissions, Lok Adalats, arbitration, departmental appeals. How do you instantly classify what is actually being asked, instead of mixing up a CAT order with a commission's recommendation, or a Lok Adalat award with an arbitral award?

### Visual: the complete institutional taxonomy

```text
CAT (and other Article 323A/323B tribunals)
   statutory service/subject adjudication -> binding reasoned order -> High Court
   Division Bench review (Lesson 3) -> possible Article 136 special leave
HIGH COURT
   constitutional court -> writ/supervisory jurisdiction -> broad public-law control
COMMISSION (e.g., an advisory or investigative body)
   investigation/advice/report, UNLESS a specific statute grants adjudicatory power
LOK ADALAT
   ordinary: compromise award; no statutory appeal, narrow writ review survives
   PLA: separate pre-litigation public-utility route; limited merits power
ARBITRAL TRIBUNAL
   consensual private adjudication under an arbitration agreement -> enforceable
   award -> limited Section 34 challenge only
DEPARTMENTAL APPEAL
   administrative reconsideration within the executive -> NOT independent judicial
   adjudication
```

*Caption: every dispute-resolution body in this topic belongs to exactly one of these six boxes; the box tells you its source of authority, how it decides, and how (or whether) its decision can be reviewed.*

### Plain-language intuition

Treat this as a sorting exercise you can run in under ten seconds on any exam question: ask "is this body deciding by statute-given adjudicatory power, by party consent, by investigation/advice only, or by internal administrative reconsideration?" The answer sorts the body into exactly one of the six boxes above, and the box then tells you everything else you need — whether its decision is binding, and who can review it.

### Technical doctrine

The complete taxonomy rests on one organising question: **what kind of authority is the body exercising, and over what source?** A tribunal's authority is statutory and adjudicatory, flowing from Article 323A/323B legislation; a commission's is ordinarily advisory or investigative unless its own statute grants it adjudicatory power; a Lok Adalat's is conciliatory, flowing from the Legal Services Authorities Act; an arbitral tribunal's is consensual, flowing from the parties' own agreement; a departmental appellate authority's is purely internal-administrative, with no independent judicial character at all.

### Step-by-step argument

1. **Premise:** six distinct bodies have appeared across this topic, each solving a different structural problem (Lessons 1-6).
2. **Mechanism:** classify each by its *source of authority* — constitutional-tribunal statute, general constitutional jurisdiction, advisory/investigative statute, conciliation statute, party agreement, or internal administrative hierarchy.
3. **Mechanism:** once classified, each body's review pathway follows — CAT/SAT to High Court Division Bench and then possible Article 136 special leave; a commission's report is not self-executing unless its statute says otherwise; an ordinary Lok Adalat award has no statutory appeal but remains open to narrow writ review; an arbitral award faces a limited Section 34 challenge; a departmental appeal remains inside the executive.
4. **Conclusion:** the whole topic is therefore not six unrelated institutions but one coherent map of how the Indian legal system distributes dispute-resolution work between constitutional courts, statutory tribunals, consensual private forums, and purely administrative mechanisms.
5. **Qualification:** "specialised" does not automatically mean "tribunal," and "binding" does not automatically mean "court" — always check the actual source of authority before assuming the category.

### Example and its limit

A specific institution can look similar across two boxes on the surface while sitting in different boxes by source of authority: CAT is a statutory service tribunal subject to the *L. Chandra Kumar* chain; PLA is a separate Chapter VI-A pre-litigation public-utility forum with limited post-conciliation merits power and constitutional review. The limit of any single taxonomy table is variation: Article 323B State tribunals and SATs differ in workload, staffing and practical independence, so verify the governing statute and current institutional setting before making a uniform claim.

### Concept check

**Question:** A newly created body is empowered by its own statute to investigate complaints and also to pass a binding order imposing a penalty. Which taxonomy box does it belong to, and why?

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cross-lesson check 1 (Lessons 1, 2)

**Question:** A State Legislature passes a law purporting to create its own Administrative Tribunal for its employees' service disputes, without any Parliamentary law or Parliamentary request mechanism involved. Using Lessons 1 and 2, identify the defect.

**Model answer:** This is constitutionally defective. Article 323A reserves law-making for service tribunals to Parliament alone, even where the tribunal will cover State employees; a State's own tribunal coverage under this framework runs through Parliament's law (via the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 mechanism enabling State Administrative Tribunals at a State's request), not through independent State legislation. A State Legislature attempting to create such a tribunal on its own, outside that mechanism, misapplies Article 323A's law-maker rule.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that because Article 323B lets State Legislatures legislate within their competence, the same freedom extends to Article 323A's service-tribunal field; the two Articles assign law-making competence differently.

### Cross-lesson check 2 (Lessons 3, 4)

**Question:** After *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) restored judicial review, did the independence litigation in Lesson 4 (*R. Gandhi*, *Madras Bar Association*/NTT, *Rojer Mathew*) become unnecessary?

**Model answer:** No. *L. Chandra Kumar* addressed a different problem — whether a tribunal's *decisions* could be excluded from ordinary-court review — and restored that review chain. The independence case law addresses a separate problem: whether the tribunal's *members themselves* are selected, tenured and administered in a way that preserves independence from the executive at the point of first-instance adjudication, before any review even begins. Restoring review supervises outcomes after the fact; independence doctrine protects the fairness of the process that produces those outcomes in the first place. Both safeguards operate together, neither substituting for the other.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating judicial review (Lesson 3) and member independence (Lesson 4) as the same safeguard addressed twice; they protect against different failure points in the tribunal's lifecycle.

### Cross-lesson check 3 (Lessons 4, 5)

**Question:** Explain, in one connected account, why the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026's design features (Chief Justice of India consultation, fixed tenure, defined removal grounds) are not accidental choices.

**Model answer:** Each 2026 design feature answers a specific defect the Lesson 4 case law identified: *R. Gandhi*'s functional-equivalence principle required selection and tenure safeguards equivalent to the judicial work transferred, which the 2026 Act answers through CJI consultation for the Chairperson and Judicial Members and a fixed five-year/age-based tenure; the recurring strike-downs of executive-dominated rules (2017 Rules in *Rojer Mathew*; the 2021 Act's re-enacted provisions in 2025 INSC 1330) are answered by removing bare executive discretion over appointment and by requiring a judicially headed inquiry for sensitive removal grounds. The 2026 Act's text therefore reads as a direct, feature-by-feature response to a documented litigation history, not an unrelated policy preference.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the 2026 Act's safeguards as generic "good governance" language; each feature maps to a specific, previously litigated defect.

### Cross-lesson check 4 (Lessons 6, 7)

**Question:** A public-utility electricity-billing dispute fails to settle before an ordinary Lok Adalat. Does that proceeding convert into a Permanent Lok Adalat case? Identify the legally correct next-route possibilities.

**Model answer:** No. An ordinary Lok Adalat and a Permanent Lok Adalat are separate statutory institutions. Failed ordinary-Lok-Adalat settlement sends a pending case back to the referring court, while a pre-litigation party remains free to pursue the ordinary remedy. An eligible public-utility dispute may instead be brought through the separate Chapter VI-A PLA route only before litigation, within its notified pecuniary and subject-matter limits. If properly invoked, the PLA conciliates first and may decide eligible merits after conciliation fails. The taxonomy therefore turns on the source and entry route, not merely on the words "Lok Adalat."

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating PLA as an appellate or conversion stage after ordinary Lok Adalat failure.

### Cross-lesson check 5 (Lessons 1, 7)

**Question:** State, in one sentence each, the opening-lesson distinction between Articles 323A and 323B and the closing-lesson institutional-taxonomy test, and show how the second generalises the first.

**Model answer:** Lesson 1: Article 323A assigns one field (service tribunals) to Parliament alone, while Article 323B assigns several listed fields to whichever legislature actually holds competence over each subject. Lesson 7: classify any dispute-resolution body by its actual source of authority — statutory-adjudicatory, constitutional, advisory, conciliatory, consensual or internal-administrative — rather than by its name or apparent similarity to another body. The second generalises the first because both rules reject surface-level or labelled categorisation in favour of checking the precise legal source of authority — first for two specific constitutional Articles, and finally across the entire institutional landscape this topic covers.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Lesson 1's Article-specific distinction and Lesson 7's general taxonomy test as unrelated rules taught at different points, rather than as the same "check the actual source of authority" method applied first narrowly and then comprehensively.

**Question:** Explain why both the original exclusion-of-review design (Lesson 3) and the original 2021 tenure/age/panel design (Lesson 5) were eventually held unconstitutional, using one common constitutional principle.

**Model solution:** Both designs failed the same underlying test: each attempted to secure efficiency or administrative convenience by weakening a safeguard the Constitution treats as non-negotiable. The original exclusion-of-review clauses in Articles 323A(2)(d)/323B(3)(d) tried to remove ordinary-court supervision entirely, which *L. Chandra Kumar* held violates the basic-structure guarantee of judicial review. The 2021 Act's re-enacted age, panel-composition and tenure provisions tried to retain executive-favourable appointment control, which 2025 INSC 1330 held incompatible with the functional-equivalence independence standard traced from *R. Gandhi*. In both instances, a design choice that traded a constitutional safeguard for administrative convenience was struck down — the common principle being that efficiency gains cannot be purchased by weakening judicial review or institutional independence.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Correct identification of both struck-down designs — 4; the common underlying principle — 4; coherent 150-word structure — 2.

### Synthesis Mains — 15 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "Every attempt to make tribunals more efficient in India has eventually run into the same constitutional ceiling." Discuss with reference to this topic's judicial-review and independence case law.

**Model solution:** The statement is substantially accurate, and this topic's case law shows precisely which ceiling recurs. The original 42nd Amendment design sought efficiency through finality — excluding ordinary-court review of tribunal decisions — but *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) held that Articles 226/227 and 32 judicial review is part of the basic structure, placing a permanent ceiling on any attempt to make tribunals fully final. Later efficiency efforts shifted from excluding review to streamlining appointments and tenure: the Finance Act, 2017's centralised rule-making was invalidated in *Rojer Mathew* (2019) for compromising independence, and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021's four-year-tenure, minimum-age and panel framework was again struck down in 2025 INSC 1330 for repeating earlier defects. Each attempt therefore hit the same ceiling in a different guise — basic-structure judicial review in 1997, and the functional-equivalence independence standard from *R. Gandhi* in 2019 and 2025 — both ultimately protecting the same constitutional value: that specialised, efficient adjudication must never be purchased at the price of independent, reviewable justice. The qualification is that the ceiling has not prevented reform outright; it has instead repeatedly redirected reform toward designs — most recently the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026's National Tribunals Commission — that pursue efficiency within, rather than by weakening, that ceiling.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Accurate statement of the 1997 review ceiling — 4; accurate statement of the 2019/2025 independence ceiling — 4; explicit identification of the shared underlying value — 4; qualification that reform continues within the ceiling — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 20 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "India's tribunal system illustrates that institutional reform is a continuing negotiation between efficiency and independence, never a one-time settlement." Critically evaluate with reference to this topic's complete timeline.

**Model solution:** This topic's timeline supports the statement closely. Articles 323A/323B (1976) first negotiated efficiency against access through specialist forums and court-exclusion clauses; *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) restored High Court review as a basic-structure floor. The negotiation then moved to institutional design: *R. Gandhi* (2010) required functional equivalence, *Madras Bar Association*/NTT (2014) identified an outer limit, and *Rojer Mathew* (2019) invalidated executive-tilted rules. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 repeated defects and was partly struck down in 2025 INSC 1330. The 2026 Act is the latest response: Act 17 of 2026 was assented and gazetted on 13 August, fully commenced on 25 August by S.O. 4713(E), and followed by service-condition rules at end-September. Its Commission, selection, waiting-list, appointment-timeline, Data Grid, Secretariat and judge-led removal inquiry seek efficiency with independence. Yet publicly confirmed NTC appointment names remain uncertain, and legal design cannot itself cure vacancies, capacity gaps or executive influence. Reform is therefore a continuing independence-versus-capacity negotiation.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** Accurate 1976-1997 account — 4; accurate 2010-2019 independence account — 4; accurate 2021-2025 account — 4; accurate enacted/commenced/rules/appointments status and features of the 2026 Act — 5; explicit independence-versus-capacity verdict — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### 1. Why tribunals at all — Articles 323A and 323B (Lesson 1)

- 42nd Amendment, 1976 inserted Part XIV-A: Articles 323A (service tribunals) and 323B (listed subjects).
- Article 323A: one field (public-service recruitment/service conditions); Parliament-only law-maker, even for State employees.
- Article 323B: several listed fields (taxation, foreign exchange, labour disputes, land reforms, urban-property ceiling, elections, rent/tenancy, others); Parliament or the competent State Legislature. State-created Article 323B tribunals and SATs may vary in workload and practical independence.
- Both Articles are enabling, not self-executing; no tribunal exists until a competent legislature actually legislates.
- *R. Gandhi* (2010) also confirms that Parliament may create tribunals under Article 246 read with the Seventh Schedule; Articles 323A/323B are enabling, not exhaustive.

### 2. CAT's institutional architecture (Lesson 2)

- Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, enacted under Article 323A, creates CAT (Principal Bench plus notified Benches) and enables State/Joint Administrative Tribunals.
- CAT members include Judicial Members and Administrative Members.
- Section 14 jurisdiction covers recruitment/service matters of covered Union public servants and notified bodies. Section 2(a)-(c) excludes armed-forces personnel, Supreme Court/High Court staff, and the secretarial staff of Parliament and State/Union-territory legislatures.
- Procedure follows natural justice, is not rigidly CPC-bound, and carries civil-court-type powers plus contempt power.
- Jurisdiction is tested by subject matter, not by a party's identity as a government employee.

### 3. The judicial-review settlement (Lesson 3)

- *S.P. Sampath Kumar*, decided 9 December 1986 and reported at (1987) 1 SCC 124, upheld substitution conditionally on an effective alternative institutional mechanism.
- *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997), a seven-judge Bench, held Articles 226/227 and 32 judicial review to be basic structure; exclusion clauses in 323A(2)(d)/323B(3)(d) unconstitutional to that extent.
- CAT/SAT orders lie to a High Court Division Bench; any further Supreme Court route is possible special leave under Article 136 from the High Court decision, not an automatic appeal.
- Tribunals became supplemental first-instance forums, not substitutes for constitutional courts.

### 4. Independence on trial (Lesson 4)

- *Union of India v R. Gandhi* (2010): Parliament may legislate tribunals through Article 246/Seventh Schedule beyond Articles 323A/323B, but qualifications, selection, tenure, removal and administration must match the judicial work transferred.
- *Madras Bar Association v Union of India* (2014), the NTT case: struck down a High Court's power over substantial questions of law being vested in an executive-dominated tribunal — an outer limit no safeguard can cure.
- Finance Act, 2017 (a Money Bill) centralised rule-making power; *Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank Ltd* (2019) invalidated the 2017 Rules and referred the Money-Bill question to a larger Bench (unresolved).
- A recurring pattern: enact a framework, courts find defects, framework is redesigned.

### 5. Rationalisation and the current legal status (Lesson 5)

- Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021: abolished specified appellate tribunals; prescribed four-year tenure, minimum age 50, search-cum-selection committee.
- 2025 INSC 1330 (19 November 2025): struck down the 2021 Act's re-enacted age/panel/tenure/allowance provisions; made *MBA (IV)/(V)* controlling; directed a National Tribunals Commission within four months.
- 9 March 2026 order: temporary continuity for specified incumbents until 8 September 2026 or maximum age — not a reversal of the November 2025 judgment.
- Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 (Act 17 of 2026): assented/gazetted 13 August; all provisions commenced 25 August by S.O. 4713(E); service-condition rules notified end-September.
- Its First Schedule covers 16 tribunals, including CAT at item 3 and SATs at item 4. Section 14 routes qualifications, selection, appointment and service conditions through the 2026 Act and rules notwithstanding parent enactments; it neither repeals the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 nor alters CAT/SAT jurisdiction.
- CAT/SAT selection follows the NTC route; Section 13 substitutes the concerned State Chief Secretary for the Union Secretary for a SAT. The panel recommends one name plus one waiting-list name, and appointment is to occur within three months.
- The NTC includes a Secretariat headed by a Central Government Secretary and a National Tribunals Data Grid; removal inquiry is by a sitting Supreme Court judge nominated by the CJI.
- Section 5 gives the NTC Chairperson and every NTC Member five years or age 70. Section 17 separately gives a covered-tribunal Chairperson five years/70 and a covered-tribunal Member five years/67. Any summary merging these offices is unreliable.
- [CURRENT] The Act, commencement and rules are settled; only publicly confirmed NTC appointment names remain uncertain.
- Abolishing a tribunal reduces pendency only if judges, staff, records and infrastructure move with the transferred workload.

### 6. Lok Adalats versus arbitration (Lesson 6)

- Ordinary Lok Adalat: Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (Article 39A); compromise only in pending or eligible pre-litigation matters. Failed settlement returns to the ordinary route.
- A Section 21 award is a deemed decree with no statutory appeal, but writ review survives for fraud, want of consent, jurisdictional error or constitutional illegality.
- PLA: separate Chapter VI-A mechanism inserted in 2002, invoked before litigation for notified public utilities such as transport, postal/telegraph/telephone, power/water, sanitation, hospitals and insurance, within the currently notified pecuniary limit.
- During PLA proceedings the same dispute cannot be invoked before another forum. After failed conciliation, PLA may decide eligible merits using natural justice, objectivity, fair play and equity, without being bound by the CPC or Evidence Act.
- *Jalour Singh* (2008) governs ordinary Lok Adalat's non-adjudicatory character; *InterGlobe Aviation* (2011) reinforces the ordinary-LA/PLA distinction.
- Arbitration: Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (UNCITRAL Model Law basis); consensual, contractual, private adjudication; award enforceable as a decree, limited Section 34 challenge.
- Lok Adalat is free, with court fee refunded when a referred pending case settles; arbitration parties bear arbitrator and institutional fees.
- Purpose differs: Lok Adalats address citizens' access deficit, especially for poorer litigants; arbitration addresses commercial autonomy. They are complementary, not rivals or substitutes.
- Civil disputes and compoundable criminal matters may reach a Lok Adalat; non-compoundable offences cannot go to either forum; criminal liability is never arbitrable.

### 7. Synthesis — taxonomy, criticism and the complete answer architecture (Lesson 7)

- Six institutional categories: statutory tribunals, constitutional courts, commissions, Lok Adalats, arbitral tribunals, departmental appellate authorities.
- Classify by source of authority first: statutory-adjudicatory, constitutional, advisory/investigative, conciliatory, consensual, or internal-administrative.
- The demand-directive map links a question's phrasing to its correct answer spine and supporting lessons.
- The whole-project criticism of tribunalisation is answered by identifying which specific condition (review, independence, transition design) was failing at each historical stage.
- Final Mains spine: rationale -> constitutional/statutory source -> structure/jurisdiction -> procedure/powers -> review chain -> independence case law -> rationalisation/current status -> qualified verdict.
