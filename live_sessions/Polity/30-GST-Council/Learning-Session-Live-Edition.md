# GST Council — Live Session Edition

Open the Constitution at Article 246 and you will find two neat, separate lists: Parliament's exclusive taxing powers and the States' exclusive taxing powers, kept apart since 1950 precisely so that neither government could tax the same transaction as the other. Then look at Article 246A, inserted in 2016, and the neat separation is gone: Parliament and every State legislature now hold the *same* power, over the *same* subject, at the *same* time. That is not a drafting accident. It is the constitutional core of the Goods and Services Tax, and almost everything else in this topic — the voting arithmetic, the compensation bargain, *Mohit Minerals*, the recurring "is it binding?" argument — exists to manage the consequences of that one, deliberate act of pooling.

Most wrong answers on this topic come from collapsing distinctions the Constitution keeps carefully apart: the GST Council (Article 279A) is not the Finance Commission (Article 280); a Council *recommendation* is not a *rate*; a *rate notification* is not a *constitutional amendment*; the Centre's one-third vote *weight* is not the *quorum*; and "not binding on legislatures" does not mean "irrelevant to the executive." This session is built around getting those distinctions exactly right, in the order a learner actually needs them: first the problem GST solves, then the three constitutional articles that solve it, then the institution's exact anatomy, then the decision rule that makes it work, then the tax mechanics and boundaries it polices, then the money bargain that made States agree to it, then the Supreme Court's reading of the whole design, then the federalism verdict, and finally the answer-writing method that converts all of this into marks.

### 1. Start with the question a learner actually asks

Why did removing "many indirect taxes" require amending the Constitution at all? Parliament and State legislatures already had the power to change their own tax rates by ordinary law. Could they not simply have agreed, by treaty-like consensus, to harmonise rates without touching Articles 246, 268 or the Seventh Schedule?

The answer is that pre-GST India did not have one divided tax; it had several constitutionally *separate* taxes, each anchored in its own Entry of the Union or State List, each with its own taxable event. Central excise attached to **manufacture**. Service tax attached to **rendering a taxable service**. State VAT and the Central Sales Tax attached to **sale**. Entry tax and octroi attached to **entry into a local area**. No ordinary law could merge these into one supply-based tax, because no single legislature possessed the full bundle of constitutional power needed to tax manufacture, sale and service together — Parliament could not touch "sale" (a State subject) and no State could touch "manufacture" or "services" (Union subjects) at the scale GST needed. Only a constitutional amendment could create one shared field broad enough to hold a single, supply-based levy.

> The pre-GST problem was not merely "too many rates." It was **divided constitutional competence plus broken credit chains** — and only an amendment, not an ordinary statute, could fix the first half of that problem.

### 2. Cascading: the mechanism, made concrete

"Cascading" sounds abstract until you see the arithmetic. A **value-added** tax is supposed to tax only the value a business adds at its own stage, by letting it credit the tax it already paid on its inputs against the tax it owes on its output. Pre-GST India approximated this *within* each separate levy but broke down badly *between* levies — central excise paid on a manufactured good could not easily be credited against State VAT charged later on its sale, and vice versa, because they were legally and administratively different taxes collected by different governments.

| Stage | Without full cross-credit | With GST-style credit |
|---|---|---|
| input purchase | tax paid becomes a sunk cost if the credit chain breaks | eligible tax enters an electronic credit ledger |
| production | tax may be charged on a price that already includes an earlier, uncredited tax | output liability is reduced by eligible input credit |
| distribution | the embedded, uncredited tax repeats at each stage | tax follows value added, not gross turnover |
| final consumer | pays an opaque, cumulative burden that is larger than the sum of the nominal rates | pays an invoice-visible, destination-based levy, subject to compliance |

Work a small example. Suppose a manufacturer pays ₹10 of excise on inputs that cannot be credited against the State VAT a wholesaler later charges on the sale price (which already includes that ₹10). The wholesaler's VAT is now calculated on a price inflated by an uncreditable tax — a tax charged *on* a tax. Repeat this at every stage with entry tax, octroi and service tax added in, and the final embedded burden on the consumer can comfortably exceed the sum of the nominal rates anyone intended to charge. This is cascading: not "double taxation" in the lay sense of two governments taxing the identical event, but tax compounding on tax because credit chains between different constitutional levies did not reliably connect.

### 3. Origin versus destination: the second half of the design problem

A second, independent problem sat alongside cascading. Most pre-GST indirect taxes were **origin-linked**: a manufacturing State taxed what it produced, regardless of where the good was finally consumed. A good made in State A and consumed in State B generated tax revenue for State A, even though State B's residents bore the final economic burden and used State B's public services. This made manufacturing States net beneficiaries of production taxes and raised a predictable objection from States that were large consumers but smaller producers.

```text
PRODUCTION STATE A ---- goods/services ----> CONSUMPTION STATE B
      old concern: tax revenue followed origin, not destination

GST DESIGN:
tax travels with the invoice and settlement architecture
                         |
                         v
revenue significance moves toward the State of final consumption
```

Article 269A, studied fully in Lesson 4, is the constitutional answer to this second problem: it places inter-State levy and collection with the Union and requires Parliament to apportion the proceeds toward the destination State, on the Council's recommendation. Keep cascading and the origin-destination problem as two *separate* design puzzles — a strong answer never treats "GST removed cascading" and "GST shifted revenue toward destination" as the same claim, because they are solved by different mechanisms (input-tax credit for the first, Article 269A for the second).

### 4. What pre-GST levies actually became

| Pre-GST levy family | Broad post-GST treatment | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| central excise on most goods | subsumed into GST | retained constitutional field for specified petroleum and tobacco goods |
| service tax | subsumed | services are now taxed within the same supply-based GST, not a separate Union levy |
| State VAT/sales tax on most goods | subsumed | retained for specified petroleum products and alcoholic liquor for human consumption |
| Central Sales Tax | replaced operationally by IGST settlement | inter-State supply now uses the Article 269A architecture, not CST |
| entry tax/octroi | largely subsumed | local-government own-revenue consequences remain a live policy concern |
| luxury/entertainment taxes | largely subsumed | local-body entertainment/amusement taxation survives within a narrowed State entry |
| basic customs duty | **not** GST | IGST may additionally apply on imports, alongside customs |
| stamp duty and electricity duty | **not** subsumed as GST levies | remain under their own constitutional/statutory fields |

Do not claim "GST is one tax that replaced all indirect taxes." It is a dual, supply-based levy that subsumed *most* Central and State indirect taxes while deliberately leaving several — customs, stamp duty, electricity duty, and (for now) specified petroleum and alcohol — outside it. "One Nation, One Tax" is a political slogan a strong Mains answer should use carefully, qualified rather than repeated uncritically.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If the real problem was simply "too many uncoordinated taxes," why could the Union and States not have achieved the same result through a cooperative tax-harmonisation treaty or a model-law convention — the way countries sometimes coordinate indirect taxes without touching their constitutions?

**Reply.** A voluntary harmonisation agreement cannot *create* a legislative field that does not exist. Before 2016, no legislature — Union or State — possessed the constitutional power to impose a single, supply-based tax reaching both goods and services across both manufacture-stage and sale-stage transactions; each level's power stopped at its own Entry in the Seventh Schedule. A treaty-style agreement to "harmonise rates" would still have left India with legally separate excise, service tax, VAT and CST statutes, each amendable unilaterally by its own legislature and each vulnerable to the next government reversing course. Only a constitutional amendment could lock in a *shared field* that neither level could unilaterally exit, which is precisely why Article 246A (Lesson 3) is drafted as a freestanding, "notwithstanding" power rather than an ordinary addition to the Concurrent List.

**Residual.** The honest qualification is that constitutionalising the field solved the competence problem but did not, by itself, guarantee that cascading would vanish or that destination States would be fully satisfied — both depend on how the resulting institution (the Council) and statutes (CGST, SGST, IGST) are actually designed and operated, which is the subject of the rest of this topic.

### Concept check

**Question:** A classmate says, "GST's main achievement was replacing dozens of taxes with one tax, which automatically ended cascading." Identify the two separate errors in this claim.

**Model answer:** First, GST did not replace "dozens of taxes with one tax" — it subsumed *most* Central and State indirect taxes into a dual CGST/SGST/IGST structure, while deliberately leaving petroleum, alcohol for human consumption, electricity duty, stamp duty and basic customs duty outside it; several separate taxes continue to exist alongside GST. Second, ending cascading was not an automatic consequence of having fewer named taxes — it required a specific mechanism, input-tax credit, that lets a business offset eligible input tax against output liability; merely consolidating tax *names* without a working, reliably matched credit chain would not by itself stop tax from compounding on tax.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "fewer taxes" and "no cascading" as the same achievement. Reducing the number of levies is a competence-and-administration change; removing cascading is a credit-mechanism change, and GST needed both, achieved through different constitutional and statutory tools.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** "The case for GST was constitutional, not merely fiscal." Explain, with reference to the pre-GST division of taxing powers. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model solution (150 words):**
Before GST, indirect taxation in India was divided among constitutionally separate fields: central excise on manufacture, service tax on services, State VAT and Central Sales Tax on sale, and entry tax/octroi on entry into a local area. Each field belonged exclusively to one legislature under a specific Seventh Schedule entry, so no Union law could reach "sale" and no State law could reach "manufacture" or "services" at the scale a single, supply-based tax required. This was a constitutional ceiling, not a policy choice any government could simply reverse by ordinary statute. Reform therefore needed a constitutional amendment creating a genuinely shared taxing field — which is exactly what the 101st Amendment's Article 246A did, giving Parliament and State legislatures simultaneous GST competence. The fiscal benefits (reduced cascading, a common market) followed only after this prior constitutional reallocation of power, not before it.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require explicitly naming at least three separate pre-GST taxable events (manufacture, sale, service) tied to their constitutional ownership, plus the explicit inference that only an amendment — not ordinary legislation — could merge them; an answer that lists pre-GST taxes without this constitutional-ceiling reasoning caps at six of ten marks.

### Visual: before-and-after constitutional map

```text
BEFORE 2016
exclusive Union/State tax entries + a separate service-tax Article 268A

101st AMENDMENT ACT, 2016
  |-- inserts Article 246A: shared GST competence
```

Caption: the Amendment is often summarised as "it brought in GST." A precise answer instead names which article does which specific job — competence, inter-State flow, institution, definition, and transition.

### 1. The Amendment's name signals its scope

Its full name is the **Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016**. "Constitution amendment" is the operative phrase: this instrument alters the fundamental distribution-of-power text, not merely a tax schedule. It received presidential assent and came into force through notified provisions in September 2016, and the Council itself was constituted by presidential order on **15 September 2016** — before GST law itself commenced on 1 July 2017. Hold that sequence: the *institution* (Council) had to exist and start negotiating *before* the *statutes* (CGST, SGST, IGST Acts) could be drafted on the basis of its recommendations.

Keep one further numbering distinction precise, because it is a frequent close-option Prelims trap: the enabling **Bill** that Parliament debated and passed was numbered the **122nd Constitution Amendment Bill**; once ratified by the States and assented to, it became the **101st Amendment Act**. The Bill number and the Act number are different things, counted on different registers (Bills introduced versus Amendments actually enacted) — never present "122nd" as the Act's own number.

### 2. Four insertions, one omission, several reworkings

| Amendment move | Significance | Exam caution |
|---|---|---|
| inserted Article 246A | created a special, shared legislative field for GST | not an ordinary Concurrent List entry — Lesson 3 explains why this distinction matters |
| inserted Article 269A | constitutionalised inter-State GST (IGST) levy, collection and apportionment | Parliament formulates place-of-supply principles; this is not the same as Article 246A's competence question |
| inserted Article 279A | created the GST Council as a recommendatory body | the Council recommends; it does not replace Parliament or State legislatures |
| inserted Article 366(12A) | defined "goods and services tax" for the first time | the definition **excludes** alcoholic liquor for human consumption; petroleum is inside the definition but its commencement is deferred |
| omitted Article 268A | removed the separate constitutional basis for service tax | do not quote Article 268A as current law — it no longer exists |
| amended Article 368's proviso | brought changes to Article 279A (among other federal provisions) within the "special majority plus half-State-ratification" category of amendment | shows that the Council's design is treated as federally significant, not a routine administrative detail |
| amended Entries 54 and 84, Seventh Schedule | preserved specified petroleum/tobacco and alcohol fields for continuing Union/State taxation | not every pre-GST indirect tax vanished from the Lists |
| Section 18 of the Amendment Act | directed Parliament to enact a compensation law for States, by law, for five years, on Council recommendation | this is the constitutional root of the Compensation Act studied in Lesson 11 |

The Article 368 point deserves a moment, because it is an easy fact to lose. Most ordinary constitutional amendments need only a special majority in each House of Parliament. A smaller federal category — covering, among other things, the election of the President, the executive power of the Union and the States, the Supreme Court and High Courts, representation of States in Parliament, and Article 368 itself — additionally needs ratification by at least one-half of the State legislatures. Provisions relating to Article 279A were brought within this ratification requirement, which is a strong textual signal that the framers of the 101st Amendment treated the GST Council's federal design as constitutionally weighty, not as an ordinary administrative body that Parliament alone could reshape at will.

### 3. Worked example: why "the Amendment created GST" is imprecise

Imagine answering a Prelims-style statement: *"The 101st Amendment Act, 2016 introduced the Goods and Services Tax in India."* This sounds obviously true, and a loosely worded version of it often is treated as correct for Prelims purposes. But a Mains-grade answer should go one level deeper: the Amendment did not *itself* impose any tax, fix any rate, or exempt any supply. It built the *constitutional machinery* — shared competence (246A), an inter-State settlement bridge (269A), a negotiating institution (279A) and a definition (366(12A)) — through which Parliament and the States could *then* enact the CGST, SGST and IGST Acts that actually impose GST. The tax itself commenced on 1 July 2017, nearly a year after the Amendment and the Council's constitution. Confusing "the Amendment" with "the tax" costs precision in any answer that asks you to "explain the significance" of the 101st Amendment specifically, because the significance lies exactly in this separation of constitutional design from statutory implementation.

### 4. Comparative treatment: constitutional design versus statutory implementation

| Layer | Example | What it does | What it cannot do alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitution | Articles 246A, 269A, 279A, 366(12A) | allocates competence, settles inter-State flow, creates the negotiating forum, defines the tax | cannot itself fix a rate, exempt a supply, or specify compliance procedure |
| primary legislation | CGST Act, SGST Acts, IGST Act, Compensation to States Act | creates the charge, liability, input-tax credit, procedure and remedies | cannot override the constitutional allocation of competence it operates within |
| delegated law | rate and exemption notifications, rules | supplies the operational rate, classification, condition and effective date | cannot be issued without the statutory authority — and, where the statute says so, without a Council recommendation |

This three-layer ladder recurs across the whole topic — Lesson 7 builds it out fully for the Council's own recommendation-to-law chain, and Lesson 12 shows the Supreme Court relying on exactly this layering in *Mohit Minerals*.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If the Amendment inserted so much new machinery — three articles, a new definition, a repealed article, reworked Schedule entries — is calling it "one amendment" misleading? Should it not be treated as several independent reforms bundled together for convenience?

**Reply.** The bundling is deliberate and internally coherent, not an accident of drafting convenience. Every inserted or altered provision serves one single constitutional objective: converting mutually exclusive Union and State taxing fields into a shared, negotiated GST field, while preserving a route for inter-State settlement and institutional coordination. Article 246A without Article 279A would create shared competence with no mechanism for the Union and States to agree on how to exercise it jointly; Article 279A without Article 269A would leave inter-State supply with no settlement bridge. The insertions are functionally interdependent, which is exactly why they were enacted as one Amendment rather than as separate, sequential reforms.

**Residual.** The honest qualification is that "one Amendment, one coherent design" does not mean "one simple design." Students frequently under-describe the Amendment by naming only Article 279A (the Council) and forgetting 246A, 269A and 366(12A) — each of which does separate, non-substitutable constitutional work, developed fully in the next three lessons.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student writes, "The 101st Amendment Act fixed GST at a uniform rate across India." Is this accurate?

**Model answer:** No. The 101st Amendment built the *constitutional machinery* for GST — shared legislative competence under Article 246A, the inter-State settlement bridge under Article 269A, the negotiating Council under Article 279A, and the tax's definition under Article 366(12A) — but it did not itself prescribe any rate. Rates are fixed through delegated notifications issued under the CGST and corresponding State/Union Territory statutes, on the GST Council's recommendation, as Lessons 6, 7 and 9 explain. The Amendment is the constitutional foundation; the rate is a much later, statute-and-notification-based operational outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "the Amendment introduced GST" as equivalent to "the Amendment fixed GST's rates." The Amendment is exclusively about constitutional competence, structure and definition; rate-setting belongs entirely to the statutory and delegated layers built on top of it.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish the specific constitutional change each of Articles 246A, 269A and 279A introduced through the 101st Amendment. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model solution (150 words):**
The 101st Amendment solved three distinct constitutional problems through three separate insertions. Article 246A created legislative *competence*: it gives Parliament and every State legislature simultaneous power to make GST laws, overriding the earlier rule that the Union and States could never share the same taxing field, while reserving inter-State supply exclusively to Parliament. Article 269A solves a different problem, *inter-State revenue flow*: it has the Union levy and collect IGST on inter-State supplies and requires Parliament to apportion the proceeds toward the States, on Council recommendation, so destination States are not left without revenue. Article 279A solves a third, institutional problem, *coordination*: it creates a standing Union-State forum to negotiate rates, exemptions and model laws rather than leaving each legislature to design GST unilaterally. Competence, revenue flow and coordination are three different constitutional jobs, and no single article performs more than one of them.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require each article tied to its own distinct function (competence / inter-State flow / coordination) using the correct verb for each; an answer that describes all three articles as "the Council's powers" collapses the distinction and caps at five of ten marks.

## Lesson 3: Article 246A — A Special Shared Taxing Power

Progress: 3 / 15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Why GST competence is not an ordinary Concurrent List entry

```text
━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Basic/Advanced GST Council owners and the Complete Topic Package's Article 246A analysis
CA search: not required — Article 246A's text has not changed since 2016; this is a stable doctrinal subtopic
CA found: none relevant for this static constitutional subtopic
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

### Visual: Article 246A's competence grid

```text
                    GST ON A SUPPLY
                          |
          +---------------+----------------+
          |                                |
   INTRA-STATE SUPPLY              INTER-STATE SUPPLY
          |                                |
  Parliament AND the State        Parliament ALONE
  legislature BOTH may            (Article 246A(2))
  legislate (246A(1))
```

Caption: Article 246A(1) pools competence for intra-State supply; Article 246A(2) immediately takes inter-State supply back out of that pool and gives it to Parliament alone. Both clauses matter — most learners remember only the first.

### 1. Read the clause close to its own words

Article 246A(1) says that, **notwithstanding anything contained in Articles 246 and 254**, Parliament and, subject to clause (2), the legislature of every State have power to make laws with respect to goods and services tax imposed by the Union or by that State. Article 246A(2) then carves out an exception: Parliament has **exclusive** power to make laws with respect to GST where the supply of goods or services takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.

Two words carry most of the exam weight here: **"notwithstanding"** and **"exclusive."** "Notwithstanding Articles 246 and 254" signals that Article 246A is not an ordinary addition to the Union, State or Concurrent Lists governed by the normal competence-and-repugnancy rules of Article 246 and the normal Union-law-prevails rule of Article 254. It is a free-standing, special field that simply overrides the ordinary Seventh Schedule architecture for GST. "Exclusive," in clause (2), tells you that the pooling in clause (1) is not total — Parliament keeps sole control of the inter-State slice.

### 2. Why "notwithstanding Articles 246 and 254" matters

Think about what would happen *without* that notwithstanding clause. If GST had simply been added as a new Concurrent List entry, Article 254's normal rule would apply: where a State GST law conflicts with a Union GST law on the same matter, the Union law would prevail and the State law would be void to the extent of the repugnancy (subject to Article 254(2) presidential assent for State laws that received it). That is the ordinary logic of Concurrent List federalism — final, residual authority tilts toward the Union. Article 246A deliberately steps outside that logic. It is drafted as a free-standing, simultaneous competence rather than a Concurrent List entry precisely so that Parliament's GST law does not automatically override a State's GST law by the ordinary repugnancy template. The pooling is meant to be genuinely *joint*, not Union-trumps-State dressed up as sharing.

> Article 246A is not "GST was added to the Concurrent List." It is a stand-alone competence that *excludes* the ordinary Article 246/254 machinery for this one field.

### 3. Worked example: who may tax what, exactly

| Supply field | Parliament | State legislature |
|---|---:|---:|
| intra-State GST (e.g., a sale wholly within Karnataka) | yes | yes, for State GST on that supply |
| inter-State GST (e.g., a sale from Karnataka to Tamil Nadu) | exclusive | no independent inter-State GST law |
| specified petroleum before a recommended commencement date | existing non-GST Union/State fields continue (Lesson 10) | existing non-GST Union/State fields continue |
| alcoholic liquor for human consumption | outside the Article 366(12A) GST definition entirely | State excise/sales-tax fields remain, untouched by 246A |

A State cannot, on its own authority, legislate a GST-style levy on a good leaving its borders for another State — that field belongs to Parliament alone under clause (2). Equally, Parliament cannot claim that its intra-State GST law automatically displaces a State's intra-State GST law merely because both exist on the same supply — clause (1)'s simultaneous, non-hierarchical competence does not import Article 254's repugnancy rule by default.

### 4. Comparative treatment: Article 246A against an ordinary Concurrent List subject

| Feature | Ordinary Concurrent List subject | Article 246A GST field |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Seventh Schedule, List III | stand-alone article, outside the three Lists |
| Governing competence rule | Article 246 | Article 246A, "notwithstanding" Article 246 |
| Conflict rule | Article 254 — Union law prevails over a repugnant State law (subject to 254(2) assent) | not imported by default — 246A is drafted as simultaneous, pooled power, not hierarchical concurrency |
| Inter-jurisdictional transactions | handled as any other Concurrent List matter | expressly reserved to Parliament *alone* under clause (2) |
| Practical effect | Union policy preference usually prevails on conflict | design forces negotiation through the Council (Article 279A) rather than relying on a default override |

This comparison explains why Article 279A had to exist at all. If 246A had been an ordinary Concurrent List entry, Union preference could simply be imposed through Article 254 whenever States disagreed, and a negotiating forum would be a convenience rather than a necessity. Because 246A is deliberately *not* hierarchical, the Constitution needed a separate institution — the Council — to produce the common design that Article 254 would otherwise have imposed by default. Article 246A explains *why* a GST Council had to be built; Article 279A (Lesson 5) explains *what* that institution actually looks like.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If Article 246A gives Parliament and every State legislature the *same* power over the *same* field at the *same* time, and there is no Article 254-style override, what stops the Union and a State from enacting genuinely contradictory GST rules on an identical intra-State supply, with no constitutional tie-breaker at all?

**Reply.** The Constitution does not leave this entirely to chance. In practice, both the Union and every State enact *their own* GST statute (CGST and the relevant SGST Act) on terms negotiated and recommended through the Article 279A Council beforehand, so the content of the two statutes is designed to be substantially harmonised model legislation rather than independently invented rules that might collide. The Council's recommendatory role (Lessons 6-8) is therefore not a decorative add-on; it is the practical mechanism that prevents the "simultaneous, non-hierarchical" structure of Article 246A from producing chaos. Where a genuine dispute nonetheless arises about statutory interpretation or validity, ordinary judicial review remains available, as *Mohit Minerals* (Lesson 12) itself demonstrates.

**Residual.** The honest qualification, developed fully in Lesson 12, is that *Mohit Minerals* confirms the Council's recommendations do not bind either legislature as a matter of law — so the harmonisation Article 246A relies on is a product of political and institutional practice working *through* the Council, not a separate constitutional guarantee that harmonisation must occur.

### UPSC application

**Syllabus location.** GS-II: significant provisions of the Constitution; Centre-State relations; the 101st Amendment and its structural consequences.

**Where this lesson is tested.** Directly feeds the verified **2023 GS-II Q15** demand (Lesson 15) and is routinely the first substantive paragraph of any strong answer that names the 101st Amendment's constitutional mechanism.

**Probable framings.** "Article 246A departs from the ordinary logic of Concurrent List federalism." Examine. Or a Prelims statement testing whether Parliament's GST power over inter-State supply is exclusive or shared.

**Traps.**
- Describing Article 246A as "an addition to the Concurrent List" — it explicitly operates "notwithstanding" Articles 246 and 254, outside the ordinary List structure.
- Forgetting clause (2)'s carve-out and claiming States share power over inter-State GST too.
- Assuming Article 246A alone guarantees harmonised Union-State GST laws — harmonisation is achieved through the Council's recommendatory practice, not a constitutional guarantee.

**Answer use.** When a question asks about GST's constitutional basis, state Article 246A's two clauses in one tight paragraph — the notwithstanding pooling, then the inter-State exclusivity carve-out — before moving to Article 269A or 279A.

### Revision notes

1. Article 246A(1): Parliament and every State legislature have simultaneous power to make GST laws, "notwithstanding anything in Articles 246 and 254."
2. Article 246A(2): Parliament has *exclusive* power over GST on inter-State supply.
3. "Notwithstanding Articles 246 and 254" means Article 246A is a free-standing special field, not an ordinary Concurrent List entry.
4. Article 254's usual Union-law-prevails repugnancy rule is not imported into Article 246A by default.
5. This is why Article 246A is described as a **pooling of tax sovereignty** rather than a transfer of it — both levels gained a shared field and neither can simply override the other by default.
6. Because there is no automatic tie-breaker, the Council's recommendatory, harmonising role (Articles 279A) becomes practically indispensable, not merely convenient.
7. Alcoholic liquor for human consumption sits outside the Article 366(12A) GST definition entirely — Article 246A does not touch it.
8. Petroleum is within the GST definition but its commencement is deferred under Article 279A(5), studied in Lesson 6.
9. *Mohit Minerals* (Lesson 12) treats Article 246A's simultaneous competence as the reason Council recommendations cannot bind either legislature.
10. The "notwithstanding" wording is a frequent close-option Prelims trap — do not describe Article 246A as merely an ordinary List entry worded differently.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State government claims that because Article 246A gives it GST legislative power, it may independently fix a GST rate on a supply moving from its territory to another State. Is this claim correct?

**Model answer:** No. Article 246A(2) reserves GST on inter-State supply exclusively to Parliament. A State's simultaneous legislative power under Article 246A(1) extends only to intra-State supply; it has no independent competence to legislate GST on a supply that crosses into inter-State trade or commerce. That inter-State field, including its rate and settlement architecture, is governed by Parliament's law under Articles 246A(2) and 269A.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading Article 246A(1)'s "simultaneous power" as unconditional and extending it to every GST transaction. The simultaneity is expressly limited by clause (2)'s inter-State carve-out, which the student must check before answering any competence question.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** "Article 246A is better described as a pooling of tax sovereignty than as a transfer of it." Explain this statement with reference to its exact wording. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model solution (150 words):**
Article 246A(1) gives Parliament and every State legislature *simultaneous* power to make GST laws, expressly "notwithstanding anything contained in Articles 246 and 254" — provisions that would otherwise assign GST to one exclusive list and let a Union law override a conflicting State law. By switching off that ordinary hierarchy, Article 246A does not transfer States' taxing power to the Union, nor the Union's power to the States; both levels retain law-making power over the same intra-State GST field at the same time, subject only to Parliament's separate exclusive competence over inter-State supply under clause (2). This is "pooling," not "transfer," because neither level loses its seat at the table — each surrendered only its earlier *exclusive* fields (excise, VAT and the rest) for a *shared* one, with harmonisation achieved through Council negotiation rather than one level's constitutional override of the other.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require the exact "notwithstanding Articles 246 and 254" textual anchor plus an explicit pooling-versus-transfer distinction; an answer asserting "pooling" without identifying which ordinary rule Article 246A switches off caps at six of ten marks.

### Why the next lesson follows

Article 246A tells us *who may legislate*. It does not tell us *how* GST actually reaches the State where a good or service is finally consumed when a supply crosses State lines — that mechanical and revenue-sharing problem belongs to Article 269A, studied next.


### Visual: simplified IGST settlement flow

```text
SUPPLIER IN STATE A
      |
charges IGST on an inter-State supply
      |
Government of India levies and collects (Article 269A(1))
      |
place-of-supply principles (Parliament's law, Article 269A(5))
      |
apportionment between Union and States, as Parliament provides by law
on the Council's recommendation
      |
DESTINATION STATE B receives its apportioned revenue significance
```

Caption: IGST is best understood as a clearing bridge, not a third, separate final tax burden sitting alongside CGST and SGST.

### 1. Read the clause close to its own words

Article 269A(1) provides that GST on supplies in the course of inter-State trade or commerce is **levied and collected by the Government of India**, and such tax is apportioned between the Union and the States in the manner Parliament may by law provide, **on the recommendation of the GST Council**. Article 269A(5) lets Parliament formulate the principles for determining the **place of supply**, and for when a supply of goods or services takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce. A further provision treats imports into India as inter-State supplies for this purpose, bringing cross-border inflows inside the same settlement architecture as domestic inter-State movement.

Notice what Article 269A does *not* do: it does not give a State the power to collect IGST on a supply leaving its territory, and it does not let the Union keep the entire IGST amount as its own revenue. It creates a Union *collection* mechanism paired with a mandatory *apportionment* step toward the States — mirroring, in institutional form, the problem Lesson 1 identified: pre-GST origin States captured revenue that destination States' residents economically bore.

### 2. Why collection and final revenue destination are different questions

A common error treats "the Union collects IGST" as meaning "the Union keeps IGST." Article 269A deliberately separates these. The Government of India is the *collecting* authority — a practical, single-window arrangement, since an inter-State transaction would otherwise require coordinating two States' tax administrations for every shipment. But the *economic destination* of that revenue is governed by the apportionment Parliament must legislate, on the Council's recommendation, so that the State where the good or service is actually consumed receives the apportioned share its residents' consumption generated.

```text
WHO COLLECTS?                         WHO ULTIMATELY BENEFITS?
Government of India                   Union AND the destination State,
(single-window collection)            through statutory apportionment
                 \                           /
                  ----  TWO DIFFERENT QUESTIONS  ----
         collection authority  ≠  final revenue destination
```

### 3. Worked example: place of supply decides the destination

Suppose a company registered in Maharashtra sells goods to a buyer registered in Karnataka. Because the supply is inter-State, IGST applies rather than CGST-plus-SGST. The place-of-supply rules Parliament enacts under Article 269A(5) determine that Karnataka, as the State of consumption, is the relevant destination for apportionment purposes — not Maharashtra, where the supplier is located. The settlement system then credits the apportioned revenue significance toward Karnataka through the statutory and system-based mechanics of the IGST Act, rather than Maharashtra retaining it simply because the invoice originated there. This is the formal, constitutional expression of the destination principle introduced conceptually in Lesson 1.

### 4. Comparative treatment: three legal layers behind one rate

| Layer | Example | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Constitution | Article 269A | fixes the collecting authority (the Union) and requires apportionment by parliamentary law on Council recommendation |
| primary legislation | the IGST Act | defines the charge, place-of-supply rules in detail, and the credit/settlement mechanism |
| delegated law | notifications and rules under the IGST Act | operational classification, documentation and effective dates |

Do not attempt to describe the exact arithmetic of IGST settlement between governments as if it were constitutional text — the precise calculation is a statutory and systems matter. A constitutional-level answer should state the *allocation principle* (Union collects; apportionment follows parliamentary law on Council recommendation, guided by place-of-supply rules) without inventing a settlement formula the Constitution itself does not specify.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If the Government of India both collects IGST and sits at the table (through the Union Finance Minister and Union Minister of State) when the Council recommends apportionment principles, does the Union not enjoy an institutional advantage in deciding how much of "its own" collection it must actually share?

**Reply.** The objection correctly identifies a real asymmetry in administrative convenience, but it understates the constraints on how that advantage can be used. Article 269A requires apportionment to be fixed by *parliamentary law*, not executive discretion, and that law must follow a Council recommendation in which the States collectively hold two-thirds of the weighted vote (Lesson 8) — so the Union cannot unilaterally decide the apportionment formula even though it administers collection. The design keeps collection centralised for *administrative* efficiency while keeping the *distributive* decision subject to the same weighted, consensus-seeking process that governs every other Council recommendation.

**Residual.** The qualification worth keeping is that administrative control over collection timing, systems and enforcement still gives the Union practical influence over how smoothly and promptly apportioned revenue actually reaches destination States — a point connected to the compensation and cess-servicing disputes studied in Lesson 11, where delays in fund flow, not merely formula design, have driven federal friction.

**Syllabus location.** GS-II/GS-III: Centre-State financial relations; the 101st Amendment's structural provisions.

**Where this lesson is tested.** Supports the verified **2019 GS-III Q1** demand's revenue-implications limb (Lesson 1) and the constitutional-architecture limb of **2023 GS-II Q15** (Lesson 15), alongside Article 246A and 279A.

**Probable framings.** "Explain how Article 269A operationalises the destination principle for inter-State GST." Or a Prelims statement testing who levies and collects IGST.

**Traps.**
- Saying a State "levies IGST" on an inter-State supply — only the Government of India levies and collects it.
- Confusing "who collects" with "who ultimately benefits" — apportionment toward destination States is a separate, mandatory statutory step.
- Inventing a specific settlement percentage or formula and presenting it as constitutional text — the Constitution fixes the principle, not the arithmetic.

**Answer use.** When a question requires explaining IGST's constitutional basis, state the levy/collection authority, the place-of-supply link, and the apportionment requirement in three short, separate sentences rather than one blended description.

### Revision notes

1. Article 269A(1): GST on inter-State supply is levied and collected by the **Government of India**.
2. The proceeds are apportioned between Union and States **as Parliament provides by law**, on the **GST Council's recommendation**.
3. Article 269A(5) lets Parliament formulate **place-of-supply** principles and determine when a supply is inter-State.
4. Imports are treated as inter-State supplies for this architecture.
5. "Who collects" and "who ultimately benefits" are different questions — collection is centralised for administrative convenience; benefit follows statutory apportionment toward the destination.
6. IGST is a clearing bridge, not a third, separate final-incidence tax layered on top of CGST and SGST.
7. The exact settlement arithmetic is statutory/systems-based, not written into the constitutional text — do not invent a formula.
8. Apportionment requires parliamentary law, constraining unilateral Union discretion even though the Union administers collection.
9. Administrative control over collection timing still gives the Union practical influence over fund-flow promptness — a thread connected to Lesson 11's compensation and cess disputes.
10. Article 269A is the constitutional expression of the origin-to-destination shift introduced conceptually in Lesson 1.

### Concept check

**Question:** A trader says, "Since my State government collects my State's share of GST on my inter-State sale, my State legislated that collection under Article 246A." What is wrong with this statement?

**Model answer:** The statement is incorrect on the collecting authority. Under Article 269A(1), GST on an inter-State supply is levied and collected by the **Government of India**, not by the trader's own State — Article 246A(2) in fact reserves exclusive legislative power over inter-State GST to Parliament, so no State legislates or collects it directly. What the trader's State eventually receives is its apportioned share of that Union-collected IGST, distributed under parliamentary law following the place-of-supply rules and the Council's recommendation — a statutory entitlement to a share, not a State-exercised collection power.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that because a State's treasury eventually benefits from IGST apportionment, the State must have collected or legislated that tax itself. Collection and legislative competence over inter-State GST belong to the Union alone; the State's role is limited to receiving its statutorily apportioned share.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Explain how Article 269A operationalises the destination principle for inter-State supplies, and identify what it deliberately leaves to Parliament rather than fixing itself. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution (250 words):**
Article 269A answers a problem Article 246A does not: once goods or services cross State lines, which government should collect the tax, and which State should ultimately benefit from it? Its solution has three linked elements. First, levy and collection are centralised with the Government of India, avoiding the administrative complexity of coordinating two States' tax machinery for every inter-State transaction. Second, the proceeds must be apportioned between the Union and the States **as Parliament provides by law**, so the ultimate distribution is a statutory, not executive, decision. Third, that apportionment is explicitly tied to a **GST Council recommendation**, bringing the same weighted, negotiated Union-State process (Lesson 8) that governs rates and exemptions into the inter-State settlement question as well.

Article 269A(5) adds a further, deliberately open element: Parliament, not the Constitution itself, formulates the **place-of-supply** principles that determine where a supply is treated as having occurred, and when a supply counts as inter-State at all. This is a conscious design choice — place-of-supply rules require detailed, frequently refined commercial and technical judgment (digital services, composite supplies, import treatment) that a rigid constitutional text cannot anticipate, so the Constitution fixes only the destination-oriented *principle* (apportionment on Council recommendation) and leaves the *operational detail* to ordinary, more easily amendable legislation.

The result operationalises the destination principle without freezing its mechanics: the State of actual consumption is meant to receive the apportioned revenue significance, while Parliament retains the flexibility to refine exactly how "place of supply" is determined as commercial practice evolves.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require naming all three elements (centralised collection, statutory apportionment, Council-recommendation link) plus the explicit place-of-supply delegation and its stated rationale; an answer naming only "Union collects, States get a share" without the place-of-supply delegation caps at ten of fifteen marks.

Caption: the constitutional roll and the administrative support staff are easy to blur. Only the top block votes; the bottom block assists.

### 1. Read the clause close to its own words

Article 279A(2) fixes three categories of constitutional membership: the **Union Finance Minister** as Chairperson; the **Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance** as a member; and a **Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation, or any other Minister nominated by each State Government**, as members. Article 279A(3) then lets the **State members choose one among themselves** to be Vice-Chairperson, for whatever period they decide. Nothing in the constitutional text makes the Revenue Secretary, the CBIC Chairperson, or any Council Secretariat official a voting member — those arrangements exist only because the Union Cabinet separately approved a Secretariat, with the Revenue Secretary as its ex-officio Secretary and the CBIC Chairperson as a permanent, non-voting invitee.

### 2. Why the distinction between constitutional members and administrative support matters

A learner might reasonably ask: since the Secretariat, the ex-officio Secretary and the CBIC invitee are present at every meeting and visibly shape the agenda, why does it matter whether they are "constitutional" members or merely "administrative" support?

It matters because only constitutional members carry a **vote** under Article 279A(9)'s weighted-voting formula (Lesson 8). If the Revenue Secretary or CBIC Chairperson could vote, the Union's effective voting weight would silently expand beyond the one-third the Constitution assigns it, and the careful Centre-States balance built into the formula would be distorted without any textual amendment. Keeping the administrative layer strictly non-voting preserves the exact arithmetic the framers wrote into the Constitution — which is precisely why this distinction is one of the most frequently tested close-option facts in the entire topic.

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL VOTING MEMBERS              ADMINISTRATIVE, NON-VOTING SUPPORT
(fixed by Article 279A(2)-(3))             (Cabinet-approved arrangement)
      |                                              |
 carry weighted votes under                   no vote under Article 279A(9);
 Article 279A(9)                              attend, advise, administer
```

### 3. Worked example: the Vice-Chairperson's unusual flexibility

Unlike the Chairperson, whose identity is constitutionally fixed to whoever holds the office of Union Finance Minister, the Vice-Chairperson is chosen by the State members **from among themselves**, and the Constitution leaves even the *period* of that office entirely to the State members' own decision — it is not fixed at, say, a uniform two-year term by the constitutional text itself. This is a deliberate design choice: it gives States collective control over an internal leadership question without requiring a constitutional amendment every time practice around that office evolves.

The operational detail that fills this constitutional gap sits one level down, in the Council's own procedural rules rather than in Article 279A itself: the official Conduct of Business Rules currently give the Vice-Chairperson a two-year term, or until the person ceases to be a member or resigns, whichever is earlier, and the same rules set ordinary meeting notice at seven days, circulation of agenda notes at least three days before a meeting, and a shorter two-day route for an emergency meeting with the Chairperson's approval. Keep this distinction precise: Article 279A fixes *that* a Vice-Chairperson exists and *who* chooses one; the Conduct of Business Rules fix *how long* that person serves and *how much notice* a meeting requires.

### 4. Comparative treatment: constitution versus administrative support, side by side

| Feature | Source | Voting status |
|---|---|---|
| Union Finance Minister | Article 279A(2)(a) | carries the Union's vote |
| Union Minister of State, Revenue/Finance | Article 279A(2)(b) | part of the Central representation |
| State minister/nominated minister | Article 279A(2)(c) | carries that State's vote |
| Vice-Chairperson | Article 279A(3), chosen by State members | remains a State member — no additional vote beyond the member's own |
| Revenue Secretary as ex-officio Secretary | Union Cabinet administrative decision | no vote |
| CBIC Chairperson as permanent invitee | Union Cabinet administrative decision | explicitly non-voting |
| Council Secretariat | Union Cabinet administrative decision | institutional support, not a constitutional fourth tier of membership |

Notice that the Vice-Chairperson row does not create an extra vote — that person is counted as the State member they already were; the office is a leadership designation inside the existing State bloc, not an additional seat.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If the Revenue Secretary and CBIC Chairperson have no vote, why does their constant administrative presence matter at all? Is calling the Council a "Union-State" body not somewhat misleading if a non-elected, Union-appointed official effectively drafts the agenda and technical analysis the voting members then approve?

**Reply.** The objection correctly identifies a genuine source of informal Union influence — agenda-setting and technical framing are real levers of power that a bare voting count does not capture. But this influence operates through *administrative support*, not through an extra constitutional vote: the Revenue Secretary cannot cast a ballot that changes whether a proposal reaches three-fourths of the weighted vote (Lesson 8), and the formal decision remains with the Chairperson, the Union Minister of State, and the State ministers alone. The informal influence is real and worth naming in an evaluative answer; it is not, however, evidence that the constitutional voting arithmetic itself has been altered.

**Residual.** The honest qualification is that "non-voting" is not the same as "non-influential." A complete answer on Council composition should state the constitutional voting roll precisely, and then separately name administrative agenda-setting as a *different*, non-constitutional channel of influence — never blend the two into a single claim about "who votes."

### UPSC application

**Syllabus location.** GS-II: composition, powers and functions of constitutional bodies; Centre-State relations.

8. The Vice-Chairperson does not add a seat; the office sits within the existing State bloc.

### Concept check

**Question:** A news report states, "The CBIC Chairperson's vote helped the Council reach the required three-fourths majority at its latest meeting." Is this statement constitutionally accurate?

Only the Union Finance Minister, the Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance, and each State's nominated minister carry a vote under Article 279A(9). A report describing the CBIC Chairperson's "vote" as contributing to a three-fourths majority misdescribes a non-voting administrative role as if it carried constitutional voting weight.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that officials who are visibly present and influential at Council meetings — the Revenue Secretary, the CBIC Chairperson, Secretariat staff — therefore carry a constitutional vote. Presence and influence are not the same as a vote; only the three categories in Article 279A(2) vote.

**Model solution (150 words):**

### Visual: eight recommendation buckets

| Bucket | Exact constitutional subject |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Union, State and local-body taxes, cesses and surcharges that may be subsumed into GST |
| 2 | goods and services that may be subjected to, or exempted from, GST |
| 3 | model GST laws, principles of levy, Article 269A apportionment, and place-of-supply principles |
| 4 | the threshold turnover below which goods and services may be exempted from GST |
| 5 | rates, including floor rates with bands |

| 8 | any other matter relating to GST that the Council decides |

Caption: Article 279A(4) is not a general "the Council may discuss anything tax-related" clause — it is a specific, enumerated list, with "any other GST-related matter" as its only open-ended item.

### 1. The Council does not have a free-floating power to legislate

A learner might assume that because the Council negotiates so much of GST's practical content, it must possess something like a general legislative power over the subject. It does not. Article 279A(4) lists eight specific recommendation fields; the Council's authority to "decide" anything is explicitly bounded to **GST-related matters**, and even its broadest bucket (item 8) is not a licence to recommend on unrelated fiscal or administrative subjects. This matters directly for institutional-boundary questions: the Council cannot, for instance, recommend how the divisible pool under Article 270 should be devolved among States — that is the Finance Commission's constitutionally separate job (Lesson 14) — however closely the two institutions' work may appear related in practice.

### 2. The separate petroleum-date duty

Article 279A(5) is worded as a distinct, mandatory instruction rather than merely one more discretionary bucket: the Council **shall recommend the date** on which GST is levied on **petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, petrol, natural gas and aviation turbine fuel**. These five products are already within the Article 366(12A) definition of goods and services tax; what is deferred is only the **commencement** of that levy on them, pending a date the Council itself must recommend.

```text
FIVE SPECIFIED PETROLEUM PRODUCTS
      |
within the Article 366(12A) GST definition already
      |
Article 279A(5): the Council SHALL recommend a commencement date
      |
CGST Act section 9(2): Government notifies that date, once recommended
      |
GST then applies to that product from the legally effective date
```

Treat "petroleum is permanently outside GST" and "petroleum is already fully inside GST" as equally wrong. The accurate formulation is: constitutionally capable of GST inclusion, but its actual commencement depends on a Council recommendation the Constitution has not yet required to produce a specific date.

### 3. Worked example: threshold exemption versus a rate recommendation

A Council recommendation that "businesses with turnover below a specified amount are exempt from registration" uses bucket 4 (threshold turnover); a recommendation that "a particular good attracts an 18 per cent rate" uses bucket 5 (rates, including floor rates with bands). These sound similar — both reduce or shape a taxpayer's liability — but they operate through different statutory hooks (registration/exemption thresholds versus rate schedules) and a precise answer should not blur them into one undifferentiated "GST relief" category.

### 4. Comparative treatment: recommendation buckets against what they are *not*

| What the Council may recommend | What it may *not* do by itself |
|---|---|
| which taxes/cesses are subsumed into GST | abolish a tax outside the GST field entirely (for example, stamp duty) |
| taxable or exempt goods/services, thresholds, rates | enact the statute that gives those recommendations legal effect |
| model GST laws and place-of-supply principles | bind Parliament or a State legislature to adopt that model law unchanged |
| the petroleum commencement date | unilaterally bring petroleum under GST without a subsequent notification under the relevant statute |
| special disaster-period rates | authorise expenditure or borrowing — that is a separate fiscal-constitutional domain |

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If "any other GST-related matter" (bucket 8) is open-ended, does Article 279A(4) not effectively give the Council an unlimited mandate dressed up as an enumerated list — since almost any economic question can be framed as "GST-related" if a government wants it discussed there?

**Reply.** The open-endedness is real but is still bounded by the field the other seven buckets define: subsumption, taxable/exempt classification, model law and place-of-supply, thresholds, rates, disaster rates, and State-specific provisions. "GST-related" must be read in that enumerated context, not as a free-standing grant covering any fiscal subject the Union or a State wishes to raise. A proposal to recommend, say, income-tax policy or a State's general borrowing limit would fall well outside even the broadest reading of bucket 8, because it bears no genuine relationship to the GST design the other seven buckets already define.

**Residual.** The honest qualification is that the *practical* boundary of "GST-related" is tested case by case in Council practice rather than by a bright-line constitutional list — which is exactly why institutional-comparison questions (Lesson 14) matter: they let you show precisely where the Council's remit ends and a neighbouring body's remit (Finance Commission, Inter-State Council) begins.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "currently taxed outside GST" as equivalent to "constitutionally excluded from GST." Alcoholic liquor for human consumption is genuinely excluded by the Article 366(12A) definition (Lesson 10); petroleum's position is different and must not be described using the same "excluded" language.

### Visual: the recommendation workflow

```text
ISSUE IDENTIFIED
      |
officer-level/committee analysis + State and Union inputs

proposal placed on the Council's agenda

discussion, modification, consensus or formal vote

COUNCIL RECOMMENDATION
      |
law / rule / notification / portal implementation
      |

```

Caption: the Council's output is a negotiated recommendation. Whether that recommendation becomes an enforceable legal obligation depends on a separate, traceable legal-effect step.

### 1. The question a taxpayer actually needs answered

A business owner reading a news headline — "GST Council recommends new rate for item X" — needs to know something the headline does not tell them: **is this already the law?** The honest answer is: not yet, and not automatically. A Council recommendation is the Council's own collective, negotiated position. It becomes a binding legal rate only when the competent government issues the matching notification (or, for a more fundamental change, when a legislature enacts or amends a statute) under the specific statutory authority that permits it.

### 2. Who does what in the chain

| Actor | Constitutional/statutory role | Cannot be confused with |
|---|---|---|
| GST Council | recommends common design (rates, exemptions, model laws) | Parliament or a State legislature |
| Parliament | enacts the CGST and IGST Acts and their amendments | the Council Secretariat |
| State legislature | enacts the corresponding SGST Act | the Finance Commission |
| Union/State government | issues delegated notifications where the statute authorises it | the primary legislature itself |
| CBIC/State tax administration | administers and enforces the law | the Council's weighted vote |
| courts/tribunals | interpret the law and review the legality of executive action | a rate-setting political forum |

### 3. Worked example: two different statutory hooks for two different jobs

CGST Act section 9(1) authorises the Government to notify rates **on the Council's recommendation**, within the statutory ceiling Parliament has fixed. Section 11 separately authorises the Government to grant public-interest exemptions by notification, again **on the Council's recommendation**. These are two different statutory levers doing two related but distinct jobs — rate-fixing and exemption-granting — and a precise answer should name the relevant section rather than blending "the Council changed the rate" and "the Council granted an exemption" into one undifferentiated sentence.

```text
COUNCIL RECOMMENDS a rate/category/date
                 |
statutory authority checked (which section permits this?)
                 |
Gazette notification issued
                 |
classification + conditions + effective date now legally apply
```

### 4. Comparative treatment: exempt, nil-rated and zero-rated are not synonyms

| Category | Output tax | Input-credit consequence, broadly | Example logic |
|---|---:|---|---|
| exempt supply | no output tax | credit may require reversal or restriction | an exemption notification under section 11 |
| nil-rated supply | the rate schedule itself states nil | generally treated within the exempt-supply framework for credit purposes | a zero entry in the rate tariff |
| zero-rated supply | a special IGST-law category | credit/refund pathway may remain available | exports and qualifying SEZ supplies |
| non-GST/outside-levy supply | GST does not presently apply at all | the GST credit chain can break entirely at this point | alcohol; petroleum before its commencement date |

A Council "recommendation" announcing a broad rate reform, such as the 56th meeting's recommended 5 per cent, 18 per cent and special 40 per cent de-merit categories, is evidence of a negotiated policy direction — not, by itself, proof of any single item's legally applicable rate on a given date. The safest practice, and the one the official 56th-meeting FAQ itself recommended, is to identify the applicable notification, its classification, its conditions and its effective date before stating a current rate.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If every Council recommendation must still pass through a separate legal-effect step, is the Council's "recommendatory" status not simply a polite way of saying it has no real power at all — with the actual decisions made later, by executives and legislatures who could, in principle, ignore the recommendation entirely?

**Reply.** In practice, this near-total ignoring scenario is rare, because the statutory hooks themselves (CGST Act sections 9 and 11, for example) are frequently drafted to require executive action *on the Council's recommendation* — meaning the executive cannot lawfully notify a rate or exemption at all without that prior recommendation, even though the recommendation itself does not bind a legislature's primary law-making power. *Mohit Minerals* (Lesson 12) draws exactly this distinction: recommendations are persuasive rather than binding *on legislatures*, but where a statute conditions delegated executive action on a Council recommendation, the executive remains bound by that statutory condition. The Council's power is therefore real at the executive-notification stage even though it is not binding at the primary-legislative stage.

**Residual.** The honest qualification is that this two-track reality — persuasive for legislatures, conditioning for notifications — is exactly the kind of nuance a loosely worded "the Council's recommendations aren't binding" sentence erases. A precise answer always specifies *which* stage of the chain it is describing.

The safe practice is to treat the headline as evidence of the Council's recommendation and to verify the applicable notification before treating any rate as settled.

This layered system preserves legislative authority while enabling coordinated national design; its main practical risk is communication, since recommendation, enactment, notification and implementation can occur at noticeably different times.

### Visual: the four-number memory board

| Number | Meaning |
|---:|---|
| 1/2 | quorum — one-half of the total Council membership must be present |
| 1/3 | the Union's weight, out of votes cast |
| 2/3 | all States' combined weight, out of votes cast |
| 3/4 | the weighted-vote threshold a proposal must reach to pass |

Caption: four different fractions, four different jobs. Confusing any two of them is the single most common error on this topic.

### 1. Start with the puzzle the formula solves

If the Union and the States each have one vote per member, and the Union side is permanently smaller in headcount (one Finance Minister plus one Minister of State, against roughly thirty State ministers), a simple one-member-one-vote system would let the States simply outvote the Union on every contested proposal. Conversely, if votes were weighted by population or by revenue contribution, larger or wealthier States could dominate smaller ones, and the Union's distinct, national perspective could be marginalised. Article 279A(9) solves both problems at once with a single formula: it does not weight by headcount or by State size — it fixes the **Union's bloc** at exactly one-third of the votes cast, and **all States collectively** at exactly two-thirds, regardless of how many individual ministers happen to attend, and then requires **three-fourths** of that weighted total to pass anything.

### 2. Read the clause close to its own words, then do the arithmetic

Article 279A(7) fixes quorum at **one-half of the total number of members**. Article 279A(9) then provides that every decision of the Council must be taken by a majority of **not less than three-fourths of the weighted votes of the members present and voting**, with the Central Government's vote having a weight of **one-third** of the total votes cast, and the votes of all State Governments together having a weight of **two-thirds** of the total votes cast, "in that meeting."

```text
PASS if:

Union support weight + State support weight >= 3/4

Union support weight  = 1/3, if the Union votes yes; 0, if it votes no
State support weight  = (States voting yes ÷ States present and voting) × 2/3
```

Now work the derivation that follows directly from this formula. If the Union supports a proposal, it contributes 1/3 = 4/12 toward the 3/4 = 9/12 threshold. The remaining 5/12 must come from the States' 2/3 bloc. The fraction of *State* votes (not of the whole Council) required is therefore:

```text
required State support  = (5/12) ÷ (2/3)  =  5/8  =  62.5%
```

So: **if the Union votes in favour, at least five-eighths (62.5 per cent) of the State votes present and voting must also support the proposal** for it to pass, subject to rounding to a whole number of States. If the Union votes against, the maximum any coalition of States alone can reach is 2/3 ≈ 66.7 per cent, which is below the 3/4 = 75 per cent threshold — so **the States can never pass a proposal the Union opposes**, no matter how unanimous they are. This is the precise, derivable basis for the much-quoted claim that "the Union can block, but cannot pass alone, while States can block the Union's unilateral action, but cannot pass over its objection."

### 3. Worked example: coalition arithmetic with twenty States present and voting

| Union | States in favour | Weighted total | Result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| yes | 13 of 20 | 1/3 + (13/20 × 2/3) ≈ 0.767 | passes |
| yes | 12 of 20 | 1/3 + (12/20 × 2/3) ≈ 0.733 | fails — just short of 3/4 |
| no | all 20 | 2/3 ≈ 0.667 | fails, however unanimous the States are |
| yes | 0 | 1/3 | fails |

The second row is worth sitting with: twelve of twenty States (60 per cent of those voting) supporting a Union-backed proposal is still *not enough* — the formula genuinely requires the specific 62.5 per cent derived above, not a simple majority of States. This is exactly the kind of numerical trap a close-option Prelims question is built to test.

### 4. Comparative treatment: consensus practice versus the formal constitutional rule

| Dimension | Consensus practice | Formal constitutional vote |
|---|---|---|
| objective | reduce visible federal division | resolve a proposal when a formal vote actually occurs |
| legal threshold | political agreement among those present | at least three-fourths of the weighted vote |
| Union's position | negotiates toward agreement | holds a fixed one-third weight |
| States' position | negotiate a collective voice | together hold a fixed two-thirds weight |
| exam line | "the Council usually decides by consensus" | "Article 279A(9) fixes the binding legal threshold at three-fourths" |

The Council's own institutional practice emphasises consensus-based decision-making far more often than formal recorded votes. This is a genuine operating fact, worth stating in an evaluative answer — but it does not repeal Article 279A(9). Consensus reduces the *political* cost of disagreement; the weighted-vote rule remains the *legal* fallback whenever consensus genuinely fails, and its mere existence shapes every negotiation that happens under its shadow, whether or not a formal vote is ever actually called.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If the Union can always block a proposal it opposes, while needing the support of only 62.5 per cent of voting States to pass one it favours, is this not simply a disguised Union veto dressed up as a "weighted," ostensibly balanced formula?

**Reply.** The objection correctly identifies a genuine asymmetry — the Union's blocking position is unconditional, while no equivalent, unconditional blocking position exists for the States as a bloc. But the formula is not a disguised unilateral Union power either: the Union *alone* holds only one-third, which is nowhere near enough to *pass* anything without substantial State support, and 62.5 per cent of voting States is a demanding threshold that cannot be met by courting only a handful of aligned States — it typically requires persuading a genuinely broad cross-section of the thirty-odd States and Union Territories with representation. The asymmetry is real (a blocking position for one side, a high coalition bar for the other), but "asymmetric balance" is a more accurate description than "disguised veto," because the Union cannot simply impose outcomes; it can only prevent them.

**Residual.** The honest qualification, which any strong evaluative answer should state explicitly, is that this is **accommodative but not equal** federalism: both sides are constrained, but not identically. That precise, two-part formulation — accommodative, not equal — is the single most useful phrase for a 15- or 20-mark answer on this topic, and it is the thread Lesson 15 ties back to the direct 2023 PYQ.

### UPSC application

**Syllabus location.** GS-II: federal structures and devolution of powers; the GST Council's functioning and accommodative federalism.

**Where this lesson is tested.** This is the single most Prelims-tested mechanical fact in the entire topic, and it is a central evidentiary pillar of the verified **2023 GS-II Q15** Mains demand, synthesised fully in Lesson 15.

**Probable framings.** A Prelims question asking for the exact quorum, Union weight, State weight or passing threshold, sometimes with distractor fractions. Or a Mains demand asking you to "explain the voting design and its federal significance."

**Traps.**
- Confusing quorum (one-half of total membership) with the Union's vote weight (one-third) — these are different fractions answering different questions.
- Assuming a simple majority (more than half) passes a Council decision — the actual threshold is three-fourths of the *weighted* vote.
- Believing the States can pass any proposal if they are unanimous — their maximum combined weight (two-thirds) is still below the three-fourths threshold.
- Stating that the Union needs only a bare majority of States' support — the derived figure is five-eighths (62.5 per cent) of State votes present and voting.

**Answer use.** For any question on the Council's decision-making, state the four numbers (1/2, 1/3, 2/3, 3/4) first, then derive the blocking/coalition consequence in one or two sentences, rather than describing the formula only in prose without the underlying fractions.

### Revision notes

1. Quorum: **one-half** of the total number of Council members (Article 279A(7)).
2. Decision threshold: **at least three-fourths** of the weighted votes of members present and voting (Article 279A(9)).
3. The Union's vote weight: **one-third** of votes cast.
4. All States' combined vote weight: **two-thirds** of votes cast.
5. If the Union supports a proposal, at least **five-eighths (62.5 per cent)** of State votes present and voting must also support it.
6. If the Union opposes a proposal, it automatically fails — the States' maximum possible weight (two-thirds) is below the three-fourths threshold.
7. The Union can therefore **block** any proposal alone but can never **pass** one without substantial State support.
8. Abstentions change the denominator, because the rule refers to members "present and voting," not every member on the constitutional roll.
9. Consensus is the Council's dominant *practice*, but it operates alongside, not instead of, the binding Article 279A(9) formula.
10. The precise, examinable verdict is "accommodative but not equal" federalism — both sides are constrained, but not identically.

### Concept check

**Question:** At a Council meeting, 18 of 25 States present and voting support a proposal, and the Union also votes in favour. Does the proposal pass?

**Model answer:** Yes. With the Union voting in favour, it contributes 1/3 toward the 3/4 threshold. The States' contribution is (18/25) × 2/3 ≈ 0.48. Adding the Union's 1/3 ≈ 0.333 gives a total weighted vote of approximately 0.813, which exceeds the required 0.75. Since 18 of 25 is 72 per cent, comfortably above the derived minimum requirement of 62.5 per cent of State votes present and voting (given Union support), the proposal passes.

**Misconception to avoid:** Checking only whether a simple majority of States (more than half) supports a proposal. The relevant threshold, once the Union's support is factored in, is the derived 62.5 per cent of State votes present and voting — a materially higher bar than a bare majority.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Explain the GST Council's voting arithmetic and evaluate its federal significance. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution (250 words):**
Article 279A fixes four numbers defining the Council's decision rule. Quorum requires one-half of total membership present. A decision needs at least three-fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting. The Union holds one-third of votes cast; all States together hold two-thirds.

The arithmetic prevents unilateral passage by either side. The Union alone holds only one-third — far short of three-fourths. States acting alone reach at most two-thirds, still below the threshold, however unanimous. If the Union supports a proposal, the remaining five-twelfths must come from the States; dividing this by their two-thirds weight shows at least five-eighths, or 62.5 per cent, of State votes present and voting must then support it.

The federal significance follows from this asymmetry. The Union holds an unconditional blocking position: it alone can prevent any proposal from passing. No equivalent blocking position exists for the States as a bloc, since their maximum weight never reaches the threshold without Union support. Yet the Union cannot pass anything unilaterally either — it needs a broad State coalition, not a handful of aligned States. Consensus practice lowers the everyday political cost of this division, but operates alongside, not instead of, the binding weighted-vote rule.

The voting design is therefore best described as accommodative but not equal federalism: both the Union and the States are genuinely constrained by the formula, but the nature of that constraint — a guaranteed block for one side, a demanding coalition requirement for the other — is not identical.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require the complete four-number formula, the derived 62.5 per cent figure (or equivalent correct derivation), and the explicit "accommodative, not equal" verdict; an answer stating the four numbers without the derivation or the verdict caps at ten of fifteen marks.

### Visual: dual levy map

|---|---|---|
| intra-State supply | CGST + SGST/UTGST | a shared tax base through parallel Union and State/UT laws |

Caption: an ordinary intra-State sale is never taxed by only one government under GST — CGST and SGST/UTGST apply together, as parallel Union and State levies on the same transaction.

### 1. The practical problem input-tax credit solves

A tax that simply applies to a business's full turnover at every stage, with no credit for tax already paid on inputs, taxes the *same* value repeatedly as a good or service moves through a supply chain — exactly the cascading problem Lesson 1 introduced. CGST Act section 16 creates the entitlement framework for eligible input tax charged on inward supplies, subject to statutory conditions and restrictions, and section 49 governs how a taxpayer actually uses that credit through the electronic credit ledger. Together, they convert GST from a tax on gross turnover into something closer to a tax on value added at each stage.

### 2. Worked numerical example

```text
INPUT PURCHASE: tax paid on inputs      = 18
        |
OUTPUT SALE: tax liability on output    = 30
        |
eligible input-tax credit used          = 18
        |
NET CASH TAX ACTUALLY PAID              = 12
```

This illustration is deliberately simple. Real eligibility depends on statutory conditions, blocked-credit rules, correct documentation, and whether the specific supply qualifies for credit at all — exempt supplies, certain blocked categories, and invoice mismatches can each interrupt this ideal chain. A Mains answer should present the mechanism and its *limits* together, not the clean arithmetic alone.

### 3. Rates, exemptions and the notification problem, revisited with numbers

CGST Act section 9(1) authorises notified rates on Council recommendation within the statutory ceiling; section 11 authorises public-interest exemptions by notification, also on Council recommendation. The 56th Council meeting recommended a broad structure of roughly 5 per cent and 18 per cent rate bands, with a special 40 per cent de-merit rate for specified goods, with most changes intended from 22 September 2025. This lesson deliberately does **not** freeze a commodity-by-commodity current rate list: as Lesson 7 established, the applicable legal rate for any specific supply must be read from the statute and the relevant Gazette notification, classification and effective date — not from a remembered meeting headline, however recent.

### 4. Comparative treatment: the inverted-duty problem

```text
INPUT TAX RATE HIGH
       |
credit accumulates faster than it is used
       |
OUTPUT TAX RATE LOWER
       |
cash-flow pressure, refund claims, or classification disputes follow
       |
Council may recommend a rate correction
       |
a notification gives that correction legal effect
```

An **inverted duty structure** arises where the tax rate on a business's inputs exceeds the rate on its output, so eligible credit accumulates faster than the business can use it against output liability. This creates working-capital pressure and refund-processing demand, and rate rationalisation exercises (including the broader 56th-meeting reform) are partly aimed at reducing the number of sectors affected by this mismatch. Rate correction must still balance fewer inversions against revenue neutrality, consumer price effects, and the inevitable sectoral lobbying any rate change provokes — there is rarely a correction that satisfies every affected industry simultaneously.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If input-tax credit is conditional on documentation, matching and statutory eligibility, is the "value-added, non-cascading" character of GST not somewhat oversold — in practice, do blocked credits, mismatches and exemptions not reintroduce a meaningful amount of embedded, uncredited tax anyway?

**Reply.** The objection is factually grounded: ITC is a conditional entitlement, not an automatic, frictionless pass-through, and genuine breaks in the credit chain do occur wherever a supply is exempt, a credit category is statutorily blocked, or documentation fails to match between supplier and recipient. The honest response is not to deny this but to be precise about scope: these breaks are *exceptions within a largely functioning value-added design*, not evidence that the underlying cascading problem from the pre-GST era has simply returned in full. The pre-GST problem was *structural* — entirely separate levies under different legislatures with no designed credit bridge at all; the present problem is *administrative* — a single, designed credit mechanism that still requires compliance discipline to work smoothly.

**Residual.** A complete answer should therefore state the ITC mechanism, its numerical logic, and then name at least one genuine limitation (exemption-driven credit reversal, blocked credits, or inversion-driven accumulation) rather than presenting GST's value-added design as a frictionless, fully solved problem.

### Revision notes

1. An ordinary intra-State supply attracts **CGST plus SGST/UTGST** together, as parallel Union and State/UT levies.
2. An inter-State supply attracts **IGST** (Lesson 4).
3. Exports and qualifying SEZ supplies are **zero-rated** under IGST law, preserving a credit/refund route despite relief from output tax.

6. CGST Act section 9(1) authorises rate notifications on Council recommendation; section 11 authorises exemption notifications on Council recommendation.
7. The 56th Council meeting (3 September 2025) recommended a broad 5 per cent/18 per cent structure plus a special 40 per cent de-merit rate — a dated reform anchor, not a frozen commodity-wise rate list.
8. An inverted duty structure arises when the input tax rate exceeds the output tax rate, accumulating unused credit and creating cash-flow pressure.

### Concept check

**Question:** A firm's inputs are taxed at 18 per cent and its output at 5 per cent, and it reports accumulating, unused input-tax credit every month. Name the phenomenon and its likely consequence.

**Model answer:** This is an **inverted duty structure** — the input tax rate exceeds the output tax rate, so eligible credit accumulates faster than the firm can use it against its (lower) output liability. The likely consequence is cash-flow pressure on the firm and, depending on the statutory refund provisions available, a claim for refund of the accumulated, unutilised credit; persistent sectoral inversion is also one of the recurring reasons the Council periodically recommends rate rationalisation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Describing this situation as "double taxation" of the same value. It is a rate-structure mismatch between input and output stages within a single, designed credit mechanism, not two separate, uncoordinated taxes compounding on the same transaction.

**Model solution (150 words):**
Input-tax credit converts GST from a tax on gross turnover into a tax closer to value added at each stage. CGST Act section 16 creates the entitlement to credit eligible input tax, and section 49 governs its use through the electronic credit ledger: a business nets its output liability against tax already paid on inputs, paying cash only on the difference. This directly addresses the cascading problem of pre-GST indirect taxation, where credit chains between separately constituted levies did not reliably connect. The mechanism's genuine limitation is that it is conditional, not automatic: exempt supplies, blocked credit categories, and invoice mismatches between supplier and recipient can each interrupt the chain, and an inverted duty structure — where input rates exceed output rates — can cause credit to accumulate faster than it is used, creating cash-flow pressure the Council periodically tries to correct through rate rationalisation.

### Visual: three different exclusion logics

| Item | Legal position | Correct wording |
|---|---|---|
| alcoholic liquor for human consumption | excluded from the **definition** of GST itself, by Article 366(12A) | constitutionally outside the GST definition |
| petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, petrol, natural gas, aviation turbine fuel | within the Article 366(12A) definition; commencement is **deferred** under Article 279A(5) | capable of future GST inclusion from a Council-recommended, notified date |

Caption: a learner who memorises only "these three are outside GST" will fail any question that asks *why*, because the three operate through entirely different legal mechanisms.

### 1. Why three items, three different mechanisms

Treating alcohol, petroleum and electricity as one undifferentiated "outside GST" category is the single most common boundary error in this topic, because it erases a genuinely important distinction: a **definitional exclusion** cannot be reversed by a Council recommendation and a notification — it would require amending Article 366(12A) itself. A **deferred-commencement** item, by contrast, is already inside the definition and needs only a recommended date and a notification to become fully taxable under GST. And a **retained-entry** item like electricity sits under its own, separately worded constitutional entry, whose relationship to GST depends on how the GST statutes themselves choose to treat it, rather than on Article 366(12A) at all.

### 2. Worked example: what each mechanism would require to change

| Item | What would be required to bring it fully, uniformly under GST |
|---|---|
| alcoholic liquor for human consumption | a further constitutional amendment to Article 366(12A)'s definition itself |
| specified petroleum products | a Council recommendation of a commencement date, followed by the corresponding notification under the relevant GST statute |
| electricity | a change in how GST law treats electricity's supply, operating alongside (not replacing) the State's retained Entry 53 taxing field |

This table is a useful diagnostic for any question that asks "how could X be brought under GST?" — the honest, differentiated answer depends entirely on which of the three mechanisms applies to the specific item named.

### 3. The retained Seventh Schedule entries behind the boundary

State List Entry 54 retains a State power to tax the sale of specified petroleum products and of alcoholic liquor for human consumption, subject to that entry's own limits; State List Entry 53 separately retains a State power to tax the consumption or sale of electricity. These retained entries are the reason pre-GST-style State taxation of these specific items continues to operate *alongside* GST rather than having been entirely subsumed by it — a partial, not total, survival of the pre-2016 Seventh Schedule architecture for these particular goods.

```text
MOST GOODS AND SERVICES
         |
   subsumed into GST via Articles 246A/269A/279A
         |
BUT: alcohol (definitional exclusion), petroleum (deferred), electricity (retained entry)
         |
   continue, wholly or partly, under their own separate constitutional/statutory fields
```

### 4. Comparative treatment: why this boundary resists a single memorised rule

| Confusion | Why it is wrong |
|---|---|
| "Everything outside today's rate schedule is constitutionally excluded" | false — petroleum is inside the definition, merely deferred; only alcohol is a true definitional exclusion |
| "Alcohol will eventually be brought under GST once the Council recommends a date" | false — alcohol's exclusion is written into the Article 366(12A) definition itself, not deferred by Article 279A(5); only a further constitutional amendment could change this |
| "Electricity is excluded exactly like alcohol" | false — electricity's position rests on a separately worded retained entry (Entry 53), not the Article 366(12A) exclusion clause that covers alcohol |

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If petroleum inclusion requires only a Council recommendation and a notification — no constitutional amendment at all — why has it remained excluded in practice for so long, despite recurring reform proposals? Does this not suggest the "merely deferred" framing understates how entrenched the exclusion really is?

**Reply.** The objection correctly identifies that the legal mechanism being simple does not make the political and fiscal decision easy. State VAT on fuel is a substantial, flexible own-revenue source that several States are reluctant to surrender for a uniform, Council-negotiated GST rate; bringing petroleum under GST would also affect prices, inflation expectations and the input-tax credit chains of transport, aviation and power-linked sectors in ways that require careful, product-specific modelling rather than a single uniform decision. The *legal* bar is low (a recommendation plus a notification); the *political and fiscal* bar is high, because the decision redistributes real revenue and policy flexibility between the Union and States.

**Residual.** The honest qualification, useful for any "should petroleum be brought under GST" evaluative question, is that the constitutional design deliberately keeps this a **negotiated, phased decision** rather than an automatic one — the low legal bar exists precisely so that inclusion can happen gradually, product by product, once the Council judges the fiscal and political trade-offs manageable, rather than being forced all at once by constitutional compulsion.

Caption: the five-year entitlement and the post-2022 cess are two different things. Conflating them is the single most common error in this lesson.

### 1. Why States needed a guarantee, not just a shared table

Lesson 3 showed that Article 246A pools taxing sovereignty rather than transferring it — but pooling still meant States gave up *independent* VAT, entry tax and other levies in exchange for a *shared*, negotiated GST field. A rational State government considering this exchange would reasonably ask: what if our actual GST revenue, under the new shared system, turns out lower than what our old, independent taxes would have raised? Section 18 of the 101st Amendment Act answers this directly: it required Parliament to provide, by law, for compensation to States for revenue loss arising from GST's introduction, for a period of five years, on the Council's recommendation. This is the compensation bargain — the political and fiscal price that made the pooling in Article 246A acceptable to States in the first place.

### 2. The calculation logic, worked through

The GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017 gave this constitutional direction concrete statutory form. It fixed the financial year ending 31 March 2016 as the **base year**, projected a **14 per cent** annual nominal growth rate in protected State revenue for the transition period, and defined compensation as the statutory gap between that **projected protected revenue** and a State's **actual revenue** as the Act defines it.

```text
BASE-YEAR REVENUE: FY 2015-16
          |
apply 14% projected nominal annual growth, compounding each year
          |
PROJECTED PROTECTED REVENUE for that year
          |
minus ACTUAL REVENUE as defined by the Act
          |
COMPENSATION PAYABLE, financed through a compensation cess and fund
```

Hold the structure of this formula precisely: it protects a State's *revenue trajectory* against a *fixed assumed growth rate*, not against any and every revenue shortfall a State might separately experience for unrelated reasons. A **compensation cess**, levied on demerit and luxury goods, financed a non-lapsable fund that reimbursed States whenever their actual revenue fell short of the formula's protected figure.

### 3. The COVID shock: why the bargain was tested exactly as designed, then overwhelmed

The formula was built to absorb ordinary revenue shortfalls. COVID-19 produced an extraordinary, simultaneous shock on both sides of it: GST and cess collections fell sharply as economic activity contracted, while the protected-revenue figure (still growing at the fixed 14 per cent assumption) and States' own pandemic-era health and welfare expenditure both rose. The compensation requirement under the Act's own formula came to exceed what the cess fund could actually finance from current collections, producing a genuine disagreement over whether and how the Union should borrow to bridge the gap. Back-to-back loans were eventually arranged, and cess collection continued beyond the original transition period specifically to service that debt and its interest.

### 4. Comparative treatment: four concepts that are easy to run together

| Concept | What it is | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| the constitutional direction | Section 18 of the 101st Amendment Act | a perpetual, open-ended payment guarantee |
| the Compensation Act's formula | 14 per cent projected growth over an FY 2015-16 base, for the transition period | ordinary Finance Commission devolution (a different institution, a different formula) |
| the compensation cess | an earmarked levy and fund mechanism financing the formula's payouts | a State's general, unconditional GST revenue share |
| the pandemic-era back-to-back loans | emergency financing for the COVID-era shortfall | an extension of the five-year compensation entitlement itself |

This table matters because the most common error on this topic is treating continued cess collection *after* June 2022 as proof that the "compensation guarantee" is still legally running. It is not: the **statutory five-year entitlement ended in June 2022**. What continues afterward, per the official 56th-meeting FAQ, is cess collection on *specified tobacco-related* goods, earmarked specifically to discharge **loan and interest liabilities** from the pandemic-era borrowing — a debt-servicing exercise, not a renewed or extended entitlement under the original 14 per cent formula.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If the compensation formula assumed a fixed 14 per cent annual growth rate regardless of actual economic conditions, was it not simply an overly generous promise that was always going to collapse under any sufficiently large shock — making the "credibility device" description in this lesson's framing somewhat too charitable toward its original design?

**Reply.** The 14 per cent figure was not meant to track macroeconomic conditions precisely; it was meant to give States a *predictable, formula-based floor* they could plan multi-year spending against, replacing the uncertainty of a system with no transition guarantee at all. For four of its five years, before the pandemic, the formula operated broadly as designed, without requiring emergency borrowing. The genuine design limitation the pandemic exposed was different: the formula had no **built-in shock-sharing rule** for an economy-wide disruption of COVID's scale, so when both sides of the equation (collections down, protected-revenue requirement up) moved against the fund simultaneously, the existing statutory and cess-fund architecture had no pre-agreed answer for who should bear the resulting financing gap, and an ad hoc political negotiation over borrowing had to fill that void.

**Residual.** The honest qualification, useful for any reform-oriented answer, is that the compensation bargain's core five-year design worked largely as intended; its visible failure was the *absence of a pre-agreed, rules-based mechanism for a systemic shock*, which is precisely the gap Lesson 14's reform agenda proposes to close with a negotiated shock-sharing framework.

### Visual: case pathway

```text

  |
IGST already charged on import value, which includes freight
  |
a SEPARATE ocean-freight reverse-charge notification is additionally issued
  |
CHALLENGE: competence + who actually bears the charge + composite-supply conflict

  |-- Council recommendations are persuasive for legislatures, not binding
  |-- the executive remains bound by a statute's own conditions on notification
  +-- the separate ocean-freight levy is invalid within the existing composite-supply scheme
```

Caption: the case is often remembered only for "recommendations are not binding." That single line is correct but incomplete — the judgment also constrains the *executive*, and it struck down one specific levy for a specific statutory reason.

### 1. The dispute, in plain terms

An importer bringing goods into India on a cost-insurance-freight (CIF) basis already pays IGST on the import's value, which itself includes the freight component paid to the foreign shipping line. A separate notification nonetheless required the Indian importer to additionally pay IGST on that same ocean-freight component, under a reverse-charge mechanism, treating the freight as a distinct taxable service received by the importer. Mohit Minerals and other importers challenged this as, in effect, taxing the same freight value twice within one composite import transaction, and also questioned whether the executive had the legal competence to impose this additional liability through delegated notification at all.

### 2. The Court's central constitutional holding

The Supreme Court held that the GST Council's recommendations are **not binding** on the Union and State legislatures; they have only **persuasive value** within what the Court described as a framework of **collaborative dialogue**. The Court tied this conclusion directly to Article 246A: because Parliament and every State legislature hold *simultaneous* legislative power over GST, and Article 246A contains no language subordinating that power to Article 279A, a legislature cannot be constitutionally compelled to adopt a Council recommendation merely because the Council made it. Treating Council recommendations as binding edicts would, in the Court's reasoning, improperly subordinate legislatures to an intergovernmental recommendatory body.

```text
Article 246A: Parliament AND State legislatures hold SIMULTANEOUS power
                           |
       nothing in the text subordinates that power to Article 279A
                           |
   Council recommendations therefore bind NEITHER legislature as a matter of law
                           |
   their force is PERSUASIVE, within collaborative Union-State dialogue
```

### 3. The second, frequently missed holding: statutes still bind the executive

The judgment did not stop at legislatures. It separately held that where a GST statute makes delegated rule-making or notification power *conditional* on a Council recommendation — as several CGST and IGST provisions do (Lesson 7) — the **executive remains bound by that statutory condition**. "Not binding on legislatures" is therefore not the same proposition as "the executive may notify whatever it wishes regardless of the Council." The two holdings work together: legislative *autonomy* is protected from Council compulsion, while executive *action* remains constrained by whatever conditions the enacted statute itself attaches to a Council recommendation.

### 4. Why the ocean-freight levy itself failed

The specific ocean-freight notification was ultimately held invalid not because the Council's involvement was improper, but because the separate freight levy conflicted with the **statutory composite-supply scheme** already in place: IGST on the import had already captured the freight component within the import's assessed value, so taxing the identical freight component again, separately, under a reverse-charge notification duplicated a charge the existing statutory design had already accounted for. This is a narrower, statute-specific finding — it does not mean every Council-linked notification is invalid, only that this particular one conflicted with how the CGST/IGST composite-supply framework already treated the transaction.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If Council recommendations do not bind legislatures, and the Court itself struck down one Council-linked notification, has the judgment not significantly weakened the Council's practical authority — reducing "cooperative federalism through a negotiating forum" to a body whose output can be set aside by either a legislature's inaction or a court's statutory reading?

**Reply.** The objection overstates the weakening. The Court explicitly did **not** hold that the Council had become irrelevant, nor did it invalidate the Council as an institution or suggest that its recommendations lack practical weight — "persuasive value within collaborative dialogue" is a real, operative status, not a dismissal. What the Court protected was specifically **legislative autonomy** (a legislature cannot be legally compelled by a recommendation) and **statutory fidelity** (the executive cannot ignore a statute's own condition requiring a Council recommendation, nor can a notification override the composite-supply scheme Parliament itself enacted). Both holdings strengthen, rather than diminish, the rule of law surrounding the Council's work — they simply refuse to let the Council function as a legislature or override Parliament's own statutory design.

**Residual.** The honest qualification is that this ruling shifts the Council's authority from **hierarchical command** to **negotiated harmonisation backed by statutory conditions** — a real, if less absolute, form of authority. Harmonisation now depends on political interdependence, shared credit chains and common-market incentives working *through* the Council's persuasive recommendations, not on any constitutional power to compel compliance.

Caption: GST is best described as neither full centralisation nor full State autonomy, but **pooled sovereignty** — both levels surrendered something, and neither can act entirely alone afterward.

### 1. The evaluative question this lesson answers

Having studied the architecture (Lessons 2-7), the voting mechanism (Lesson 8), the boundaries (Lesson 10), the compensation bargain (Lesson 11) and the Supreme Court's reading (Lesson 12), the natural question is: taken together, what *kind* of federalism does this add up to? Indian federalism discourse typically distinguishes three operating modes, and GST displays elements of all three simultaneously — a strong answer must show *which* evidence supports *which* mode, rather than declaring GST simply "cooperative" or simply "coercive."

### 2. Three operating modes, with GST's own evidence for each

| Mode | GST evidence | Value | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| cooperative | consensus-based Council practice (Lesson 8); common rules and model laws; IGST settlement (Lesson 4) | genuine harmonisation across 28+ jurisdictions | unequal bargaining power may be obscured behind the language of "consensus" |
| competitive | States compete on administrative efficiency, compliance facilitation and ease of doing business within the shared GST framework | administrative innovation | a race that could, in principle, pressure enforcement standards or burden taxpayers unevenly |
| confrontational | the compensation dispute (Lesson 11); recurring rate-rationalisation friction; the long-pending petroleum-inclusion debate (Lesson 10) | forces real grievances into the open, rather than suppressing them | risk of fragmentation and an accumulating trust deficit if grievances are not resolved |

### 3. Gains and autonomy costs, matched honestly against each other

| Claimed gain | Named mechanism | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| reduced cascading | input-tax credit (Lesson 9) | blocked or mismatched credit can still interrupt the chain |
| a common market | a harmonised base plus IGST settlement | exclusions (Lesson 10) and compliance variation across sectors remain |
| destination-based revenue | Article 269A (Lesson 4) | producing States faced genuine transition concerns, addressed only partly by compensation |
| a joint institutional voice | Article 279A membership and vote (Lessons 5, 8) | the Union retains a unilateral blocking position within that same vote |
| procedural predictability | the Council's recommendation process (Lesson 7) and *Mohit Minerals*' statutory discipline (Lesson 12) | frequent rate and policy change can itself create compliance cost |

State autonomy did not vanish under GST — it shifted from **unilateral rate-setting** toward **negotiated rule-making plus a retained legislative competence that *Mohit Minerals* explicitly protects**. That shift is real, and it is not costless, but it is also not a simple story of States losing power to the Union; it is an exchange of one kind of power (independent, unilateral taxation) for another (a guaranteed, weighted seat in a standing negotiating forum, backed by retained legislative competence a court has confirmed).

### 4. Comparative treatment: how the three federalism lenses interact over time

```text
2017: pooling begins — cooperative design, consensus-heavy early practice
          |
rate-rationalisation friction accumulates — competitive and confrontational elements emerge
          |
2020-2022: COVID shock tests the compensation bargain — confrontational federalism visible
          |
2022: Mohit Minerals reaffirms legislative autonomy — a cooperative-law corrective
          |
ongoing: petroleum inclusion, rate 2.0, cess-servicing debates — all three modes continue to coexist
```

A strong evaluative answer treats these three modes not as a sequence that resolves into one final state, but as **persistent, simultaneously present features** of a single institutional design — the Council is cooperative in form, occasionally competitive in administrative practice, and periodically confrontational whenever a specific bargain (compensation, rate rationalisation, petroleum inclusion) comes under genuine fiscal pressure.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If consensus can "conceal unequal bargaining power," as this lesson itself concedes, is calling GST's design "accommodative federalism" not simply a flattering label for an arrangement where the Union still holds the only unconditional blocking position in the entire structure?

**Reply.** The objection correctly identifies a genuine asymmetry, already established precisely in Lesson 8: the Union's blocking position is real and unconditional, while no equivalent blocking position exists for the States as a bloc. But "accommodative" does not require *perfect* symmetry to be an accurate description — it requires that *both* sides be genuinely constrained compared to the pre-GST alternative, where each level could act entirely unilaterally within its own exclusive field. Before 2016, the Union needed no State's agreement whatsoever to set its own excise or service-tax policy, and States needed no Union agreement to set VAT; after 2016, *neither* side can unilaterally redesign the shared GST field without engaging the other through the Council, and *Mohit Minerals* additionally confirms that neither side can be legally compelled to adopt the other's preferred outcome. That is a genuine increase in mutual accommodation, even though the increase is not evenly distributed.

**Residual.** The precise, examinable formulation — already introduced in Lesson 8 and worth repeating here as the lesson's own verdict — is **accommodative but not equal federalism**: real, mutual constraint compared to the pre-GST baseline, but an asymmetric constraint that favours the Union's blocking position over any equivalent State-side veto.

GST's design is genuinely more cooperative than the pre-GST baseline, where each level acted entirely unilaterally within its own exclusive taxing field — the Council forces continuous negotiation, and *Mohit Minerals* protects both levels' retained legislative competence. But it is not "equal": the Union alone holds an unconditional blocking position under Article 279A(9)'s weighted-vote formula, while no equivalent blocking position exists for the States as a bloc. A more accurate conclusion is that GST represents accommodative, but not equal, federalism — genuine mutual constraint, asymmetrically distributed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "more cooperative than before" as equivalent to "fully equal now." Degree of cooperation and degree of equality are separate claims, and GST's own voting arithmetic (Lesson 8) shows the second claim is false even where the first is substantially true.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Assess whether GST has strengthened cooperative federalism while weakening State fiscal autonomy. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution (250 words):**
GST simultaneously strengthens intergovernmental cooperation and narrows unilateral State tax choice, and a sound answer must hold both halves together.

**Cooperation strengthened.** Article 279A places every State at a ministerial table alongside the Union. The one-third Union, two-thirds States and three-fourths threshold prevents unilateral passage by either side. Article 269A requires common inter-State settlement rather than unilateral origin-based taxation, and the five-year Compensation Act enabled States to accept a destination-based system with a transition guarantee. Shared rules reduce both cascading and internal tax barriers between States.

**Autonomy constrained.** States surrendered VAT, entry tax and other independently set levies and cannot unilaterally redesign the common base without credit-chain consequences. The Union's one-third weight gives it an unconditional blocking position no State coalition can match. Compliance systems, recurring rate-rationalisation, and the June 2022 end of the compensation entitlement can each deepen revenue dependence, particularly for States with narrower own-tax bases.

**Constitutional counterweight.** *Mohit Minerals* treats Council recommendations as persuasive rather than binding, preserving States' Article 246A power even where they depart from a recommendation. States also retain their own taxing fields over alcohol, electricity and (until notified) specified petroleum. GST therefore collectivises autonomy rather than extinguishing it.

The correct test is not whether States remain fully sovereign over rates — they plainly do not — but whether negotiated decisions rest on transparent evidence, credible transitions and fair dispute resolution. GST is cooperative in design and centralising in some fiscal effects; its legitimacy depends on making pooled sovereignty genuinely reciprocal.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require both halves (cooperation strengthened; autonomy constrained) with named evidence for each, plus the *Mohit Minerals* counterweight and an explicit reciprocity-based test rather than a one-sided verdict; an answer favouring only one half caps at nine of fifteen marks.

### Visual: what Article 279A(11) covers, and what it does not

Caption: Article 279A(11) is an *intergovernmental* dispute clause. It is easy, but wrong, to assume any GST-related tribunal automatically satisfies it.

### 1. The constitutional duty, and the gap beneath it

Article 279A(11) requires the Council to **establish a mechanism to adjudicate disputes** arising from a Council recommendation or its implementation, between the Government of India and one or more States, between the Government of India and States on one side and one or more other States on the other, or between two or more States. This is a clear, mandatory constitutional instruction. What has not been verified is a complete, separately published official mechanism that specifically performs this *intergovernmental* adjudicatory function — existing Council committees, ordinary political negotiation at Council meetings, the taxpayer appeal hierarchy, and the GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT) are all genuine institutions, but none of them is the same thing as the Article 279A(11) mechanism, which is specifically designed for Union-State and State-State disputes, not taxpayer-versus-administration disputes.

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL DUTY: establish an intergovernmental adjudicatory mechanism
                              |
   officially visible Council committees, negotiation, recorded minutes
                              |
   taxpayer appeals and GSTAT handle a DIFFERENT kind of dispute entirely
                              |
   no complete, separately verified Article 279A(11) mechanism located
                              |
   reform need: a neutral panel, defined jurisdiction, reasoned decisions, a clear
   relationship to Article 131 and judicial review
```

### 2. Why GSTAT is not a substitute, even though it is easy to assume otherwise

GSTAT exists to hear statutory appeals from taxpayers and tax administrations under the GST laws — it resolves disputes about whether a particular assessment, classification or demand against a *specific taxpayer* is correct in law. Article 279A(11) is aimed at an entirely different category of dispute: disagreement *between governments themselves* about a Council recommendation or its implementation — for instance, a disagreement between two States over how a particular apportionment principle should be applied, or between the Union and a State over whether an agreed recommendation has actually been implemented as promised. Conflating the two because both involve "GST disputes" erases the intergovernmental-versus-taxpayer distinction that Article 279A(11)'s own wording is built around.

### Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection.** If a constitutionally mandatory mechanism under Article 279A(11) has not been verifiably established nearly a decade after the Council itself was constituted, does this not suggest the Council is willing to treat inconvenient constitutional duties as optional whenever political negotiation happens to work well enough without them?

**Reply.** The objection correctly identifies a genuine, unresolved institutional gap, and this lesson does not minimise it. But the gap is better explained by the Council's heavy reliance on **consensus practice** (Lesson 8) than by deliberate avoidance of a legal duty: where disputes have so far been managed through negotiation at Council meetings rather than through a formal adjudicatory body, the practical *need* to activate a separate mechanism has been correspondingly lower — not because the duty has been forgotten, but because the underlying disputes have, so far, mostly been resolved (or at least managed) through the ordinary weighted-vote and consensus process Lesson 8 describes. This is a genuine institutional weakness precisely *because* it depends on continued political goodwill rather than a pre-agreed, rules-based procedure — exactly the vulnerability a sufficiently serious future dispute could expose.

**Residual.** The honest qualification, and the correct reform-oriented conclusion, is that an unoperationalised Article 279A(11) is a **standing institutional risk**, not a crisis that has already materialised — it remains untested precisely because consensus has mostly held, which is not the same as proof that the mechanism is unnecessary.

**Mains question:** Suggest an accountability and reform framework that can make the GST Council a more durable institution of fiscal federalism. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Model solution (250 words):**
The Council's durability depends on whether States and taxpayers can trace a recommendation from evidence to legal effect to fiscal outcome, and on whether genuine disputes have a predictable resolution route.

**First, improve deliberative transparency.** Publish fuller minutes, dissent and the revenue assumptions behind a recommendation, while protecting confidential taxpayer data. A notification tracker should separately mark the Council's recommendation, the statutory authority relied upon, the Gazette instrument, and its effective date — addressing the recommendation-versus-legal-effect confusion Lesson 7 identifies.

**Second, strengthen the evidence base.** An independent technical unit could publish distributional, State-revenue, inflation and compliance modelling with assumptions and error bands disclosed, advising rather than acquiring rate-setting power.

**Third, operationalise Article 279A(11).** A neutral intergovernmental panel needs a defined jurisdiction, clear timelines, reasoned decisions and an explicit relationship to judicial review — kept clearly separate from the ordinary taxpayer appeal route and GSTAT.

**Fourth, rebuild post-compensation trust.** Publish compensation-cess and loan-servicing accounts, avoid implying a permanent revenue guarantee, and design a rules-based shock-sharing facility for a future disruption, learning from the COVID-era gap in Lesson 11.

**Fifth, simplify without sacrificing revenue discipline.** Use a stable, predictable rate-review calendar, target inverted-duty sectors directly, and support small-business compliance without weakening anti-evasion enforcement.

Articles 246A, 269A and 279A already supply competence, settlement and a negotiating forum; *Mohit Minerals* protects legislative autonomy within that forum. Reform should therefore improve legitimacy, evidence and dispute resolution — not replace the Council's negotiated design with either unilateral Union command or unchecked technocracy.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks cut across lessons. Each is designed around a distinction the file has built across more than one lesson, which no single lesson's concept check could test alone.

## Cumulative check 1 — four fractions, one formula, under pressure

**Question:** A commentator writes: "The GST Council needs a simple majority of its members to pass a proposal, and the Centre alone holds half the vote." Identify every distinct error in this single sentence.

**Model answer:** The sentence contains at least two separate errors, each traceable to Lesson 8. First, the passing threshold is not a simple majority — Article 279A(9) requires **at least three-fourths** of the weighted votes of members present and voting, a materially higher bar. Second, the Centre does not hold half the vote; it holds **one-third**, with all States together holding the remaining **two-thirds**. The sentence also implicitly confuses quorum (one-half of total membership, a separate fraction governing whether a meeting can validly proceed at all) with the passing threshold (three-fourths of the weighted vote) — the two numbers answer completely different questions and must never be conflated.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating any two correctly remembered GST Council fractions as interchangeable or substitutable. Quorum, the Union's weight, the States' weight and the passing threshold are four distinct numbers, each governing a different stage of the decision process.

## Cumulative check 2 — three boundaries, three mechanisms, held apart

**Question:** A student writes, "Alcohol, petroleum and electricity are all treated identically under GST law — none of them is currently taxed under the regular CGST/SGST structure." A classmate says this is a fair summary because the practical outcome (not currently GST-taxed) is the same for all three. Evaluate the classmate's defence.

**Model answer:** The classmate's defence is unsound, because this topic's distinctions (Lesson 10) are built precisely to show that an *identical practical outcome* can rest on *entirely different legal mechanisms*, and the mechanism — not merely the outcome — is what close-option questions test. Alcoholic liquor for human consumption is excluded from the Article 366(12A) **definition** of GST itself, a mechanism only a further constitutional amendment could change. Petroleum's five specified products are **within** that same definition, with only their **commencement** deferred under Article 279A(5), reversible by a Council recommendation and a notification alone. Electricity is addressed by neither mechanism — its position rests on the State's **retained Entry 53** taxing power, operating alongside whatever treatment GST law separately gives it. A question asking "how could each item be brought fully under GST?" would require three different answers, which proves the classmate's "practically identical" defence collapses exactly where it is tested.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a shared practical outcome (not currently taxed under the standard CGST/SGST structure) as proof of a shared legal mechanism. The mechanism behind an outcome, not merely the outcome itself, is what distinguishes correct and incorrect answers on this topic.

## Cumulative check 3 — reading "persuasive" correctly across the whole file

**Question:** Using the precise vocabulary this file has built across Lessons 7 and 12, classify each of the following as (a) legally binding on a legislature, (b) legally binding on the executive issuing a notification, or (c) neither: (i) a Council recommendation that a State is free to disregard when enacting its own SGST amendment; (ii) a Council recommendation that a statute makes a condition precedent for a valid rate notification; (iii) a Council press release describing a recommended rate change, before any notification is issued.

**Model answer:** (i) is **neither (a) nor (b) in the sense of compulsion** — more precisely, it illustrates that a Council recommendation is not binding on a legislature at all; the State retains its Article 246A competence and may enact differently, as *Mohit Minerals* confirms. (ii) is **binding on the executive** — where a statute conditions a notification on a Council recommendation, *Mohit Minerals* holds the executive must satisfy that statutory condition before validly notifying. (iii) is **neither binding nor itself a legally effective change** — it is evidence of the Council's position, but Lesson 7's recommendation-to-law chain shows that legal liability requires the matching notification, not the press release describing it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Using "not binding" as a single, undifferentiated label for every stage of the Council's recommendation process. The file's own evidence requires separating legislative non-bindingness, executive statutory conditioning, and the simple absence of legal effect in a press release — three different propositions, not one.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These three questions are distinct from every lesson-local question in the file and from each other, and are intended to be attempted only after the whole topic is complete.

## Final 10-mark question — 150 words

**Question:** "The GST Council's authority rests on structured interdependence, not legislative supremacy." Examine this statement with reference to the Council's voting design. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer (150 words):**
The statement is accurate. Article 279A(9) fixes the Union's weight at one-third and all States' combined weight at two-thirds of the votes cast, with a three-fourths threshold required to pass any proposal. Neither side can act unilaterally: the Union alone cannot reach three-fourths, and States acting alone max out at two-thirds, below the threshold. This is not legislative supremacy for either level — it is structured interdependence, where a proposal can only pass through a genuine coalition spanning both levels, with the Union needing roughly five-eighths of voting States' support once it has committed its own one-third. The asymmetry is real: the Union alone can block, while no equivalent State-only blocking position exists. But blocking power is not the same as the power to pass outcomes unilaterally, which is precisely why "structured interdependence" is the more accurate description than "legislative supremacy" for either side.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require the complete four-number formula and an explicit statement distinguishing blocking power from passing power; an answer stating only "neither side can pass alone" without the blocking-power asymmetry caps at six of ten marks.

## Final 15-mark question — 250 words

**Question:** "Every one of the GST Council's major controversies is, at bottom, a disagreement about who absorbs the cost of a shared tax base." Discuss with reference to at least three distinct controversies covered in this topic. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer (250 words):**
The claim holds across the topic's recurring disputes, each of which redistributes a cost between the Union and the States, or between States.

**Compensation.** The five-year statutory guarantee let States accept destination-based taxation and the loss of independent rate-setting; its June 2022 lapse shifted the cost of any revenue shortfall from a shared, formula-protected fund back toward each State's own fiscal position, while continued cess collection shifted pandemic-era borrowing costs toward specified-goods consumers rather than general budgets.

**Petroleum inclusion.** Bringing specified petroleum products under GST would shift revenue-flexibility costs from States (which retain independent VAT-style control) toward a negotiated, Council-set national rate, while easing input-tax-credit costs currently borne by transport, aviation and power-linked sectors.

**Rate rationalisation and inverted duty.** Correcting an inverted duty structure shifts cash-flow costs away from affected businesses, but any revenue shortfall created by a lower output rate must be absorbed somewhere in the shared base — a Union-State negotiation about whose revenue adjusts.

**Weighted voting itself.** The Union's one-third blocking weight against the States' two-thirds allocates *who bears the cost of disagreement* — the Union prevents change at no cost to itself, while States bear the cost of needing a demanding five-eighths coalition to prompt change the Union does not already favour.

**Verdict.** In each case, the real argument is not whether a given rupee should exist, but which government should carry the cost of the other's benefit — which is why no voting formula, however carefully balanced, can satisfy every stakeholder equally.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require at least three distinct, correctly explained controversies plus the unifying cost-allocation verdict stated explicitly; an answer describing controversies without the unifying verdict caps at nine of fifteen marks.

## Final 20-mark question — 250 words

**Question:** "India's GST design has become more rule-based without becoming fully depoliticised." Critically evaluate this statement with reference to the Council's architecture, decision rule and current limitations. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer (250 words):**
The statement is accurate, and the evidence supports both halves equally.

**The rule-based half.** Article 246A replaced unilateral, exclusive Union and State taxing fields with a shared, textually fixed competence; Article 269A constitutionalises inter-State settlement rather than leaving it to ad hoc arrangement; Article 279A's weighted-vote formula (one-half quorum, one-third Union, two-thirds States, three-fourths threshold) is a precise, derivable rule, not a vague consultative norm; and CGST Act sections 9 and 11 condition rate and exemption notifications on a Council recommendation, a discipline *Mohit Minerals* confirms binds the executive.

**The still-political half.** Consensus, not formal voting, dominates Council practice, and can obscure unequal bargaining power even while operating alongside the binding formula. The Union retains an unconditional blocking position no State coalition can override. The compensation bargain's June 2022 lapse exposed genuine federal trust tension rather than a closed transition. Petroleum inclusion remains politically contested despite its low legal bar. And Article 279A(11)'s dispute mechanism remains, as far as verified, unoperationalised — a duty resting on political goodwill rather than a tested procedure.

**Verdict.** The architecture has reduced one kind of politics — unilateral, uncoordinated tax design by either level acting alone — while leaving another fully intact: the Union's blocking advantage, the unresolved dispute-mechanism gap, and recurring fights over compensation and petroleum. "More rule-based" and "not fully depoliticised" are both true at once, and a complete answer must hold both rather than resolving the tension either way.

**Scoring note (unique to this question):** Full marks require at least three named pieces of evidence for each half of the statement, plus an explicit final sentence holding both halves together; an answer favouring only one half caps at twelve of twenty marks.

# REMEDIATION

Each entry below is an error this topic actually produces, why it happens, the correction, and the lesson to reread.

## Error family 1: the ordinary-Concurrent-List error

**The error.** Describing Article 246A as an ordinary addition to the Concurrent List, or assuming Article 254's usual Union-law-prevails rule applies to GST by default.

**Why it happens.** Learners generalise from the familiar Concurrent List pattern without noticing Article 246A's "notwithstanding Articles 246 and 254" wording.

**The correction.** Article 246A is a free-standing, special competence that expressly operates outside the ordinary Article 246/254 machinery. **Reread Lesson 3.**

## Error family 2: the collection-equals-benefit error

**The error.** Assuming that because the Government of India levies and collects IGST, the Union also keeps the entire proceeds as its own revenue.

**The correction.** Article 269A requires statutory apportionment toward destination States, on the Council's recommendation; collection and ultimate revenue benefit are separate questions. **Reread Lesson 4.**

## Error family 3: the voting-member error

**The error.** Treating the Revenue Secretary or the CBIC Chairperson as constitutional, voting members of the Council.

**The correction.** Article 279A(2) fixes only three constitutional categories — the Union Finance Minister, the Union Minister of State for Revenue/Finance, and each State's nominated minister. The Revenue Secretary and CBIC Chairperson are Cabinet-approved administrative arrangements, explicitly non-voting. **Reread Lesson 5.**

## Error family 4: the unlimited-mandate error

**The error.** Reading "any other GST-related matter" (Article 279A(4)'s eighth bucket) as an unbounded general power, or assuming the Council can recommend on devolution, borrowing or unrelated fiscal subjects.

**The correction.** The residual bucket is bounded by the enumerated context of the other seven buckets; it is not a licence to recommend on any fiscal topic merely because it touches money. **Reread Lesson 6.**

## Error family 5: the self-executing-recommendation error

**The error.** Treating a Council press release or meeting outcome as automatically, immediately binding law the moment it is reported.

**The correction.** Legal effect requires either legislative enactment/amendment or a delegated notification issued under specific statutory authority, with its own classification, conditions and effective date. **Reread Lesson 7.**

## Error family 6: the quorum-versus-weight error

**The error.** Confusing the one-half quorum requirement with the Union's one-third vote weight, or assuming a simple majority passes a Council decision.

**The correction.** Four distinct fractions govern different stages: one-half (quorum), one-third (Union weight), two-thirds (States' combined weight), three-fourths (the passing threshold). **Reread Lesson 8.**

## Error family 7: the credit-is-automatic error

**The error.** Describing input-tax credit as an unconditional, automatic entitlement that eliminates cascading entirely, with no remaining friction.

**The correction.** ITC is statutorily conditioned, subject to blocked-credit rules, documentation and matching; exemptions and inverted duty structures can each interrupt the chain. **Reread Lesson 9.**

## Error family 8: the single-exclusion-logic error

**The error.** Describing alcohol, petroleum and electricity as excluded from GST "for the same reason" or under the same constitutional provision.

**The correction.** Three different mechanisms apply: alcohol is a definitional exclusion (Article 366(12A)); petroleum's commencement is deferred (Article 279A(5)); electricity rests on a retained Seventh Schedule entry (Entry 53). **Reread Lesson 10.**

## Error family 9: the compensation-still-running error

**The error.** Treating continued compensation-cess collection after June 2022 as proof that the original five-year compensation entitlement is still legally in force.

**The correction.** The statutory five-year entitlement ended in June 2022; continued cess collection on specified goods, per official material, services pandemic-era loan and interest liabilities — a distinct, debt-servicing purpose. **Reread Lesson 11.**

## Error family 10: the Council-is-irrelevant error

**The error.** Over-reading *Mohit Minerals* to claim the Council became powerless, irrelevant, or that every Council-linked notification is now invalid.

**The correction.** The judgment held recommendations are persuasive for legislatures, not that the Council is irrelevant; it separately held the executive remains bound by a statute's own condition requiring a Council recommendation; and the specific ocean-freight outcome turned on a composite-supply conflict, not a blanket rule against Council-linked notifications. **Reread Lesson 12.**

## Error family 11: the one-sided-verdict error

**The error.** Concluding a federalism-evaluation answer with either uncritical praise ("GST proves Indian federalism is now fully cooperative") or pure cynicism ("the Council is merely a Union rubber stamp"), without evidence for the competing mode.

**The correction.** GST displays cooperative, competitive and confrontational elements simultaneously; the precise, examinable verdict is "accommodative, but not equal" federalism. **Reread Lesson 13.**

## Error family 12: the institutional-confusion error

**The error.** Describing the Finance Commission as devolving "GST revenue" in a way that implies the Council sets devolution shares, or treating GSTAT as the Article 279A(11) intergovernmental mechanism.

**The correction.** The GST Council (Article 279A) coordinates tax design; the Finance Commission (Article 280) redistributes the already-collected divisible pool — distinct constitutional jobs. GSTAT hears taxpayer appeals; Article 279A(11) concerns government-to-government disputes, for which no complete official mechanism was verified at this file's control date. **Reread Lesson 14.**

## Error family 13: the breadth-without-selection error

**The error.** Attempting to include the complete architecture, full voting derivation, entire compensation timeline and complete case-law holding matrix in a single word-limited Mains answer, running out of words before reaching a conclusion.

**The correction.** Match the question's framing to the thesis-selector table, select only the dimensions the specific question demands, and preserve space for an explicit, qualified verdict. **Reread Lesson 15.**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Why a GST compact was needed

- Pre-GST India had constitutionally separate taxes: excise (manufacture), service tax (services), VAT/CST (sale), entry tax/octroi (entry into a local area).
- No ordinary statute could merge these; only a constitutional amendment could create a shared taxing field.
- Cascading means tax compounding on tax from broken cross-credit between separately constituted levies.
- A second, independent problem was origin-linked revenue capture by production States rather than consumption States.
- GST subsumed *most*, not all, pre-GST indirect taxes — petroleum, alcohol, electricity duty, stamp duty and customs remain outside its current core.

## 2. The 101st Amendment — constitutional surgery

- Full name: the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016; the enabling Bill was the **122nd Amendment Bill** — never present 122 as the Act's own number.
- Inserted Articles 246A, 269A, 279A and 366(12A); omitted Article 268A; amended Seventh Schedule Entries 54 and 84 and Article 368's proviso.
- Section 18 directed a five-year compensation law for States.
- The Council was constituted by presidential order on 15 September 2016, before GST law commenced on 1 July 2017.
- Changes to Article 279A require special majority plus ratification by at least half the State legislatures.

## 3. Article 246A — pooled, not transferred, sovereignty

- Clause (1): Parliament and every State legislature hold simultaneous GST competence, "notwithstanding" Articles 246 and 254.
- Clause (2): Parliament alone holds exclusive power over inter-State GST.
- Article 254's usual repugnancy rule is not imported by default — this is a free-standing field, not an ordinary Concurrent List entry.
- This is why the Council (Article 279A) is practically indispensable, not merely convenient.

## 4. Article 269A — the inter-State bridge

- The Government of India levies and collects IGST on inter-State supplies.
- Proceeds are apportioned between Union and States by parliamentary law, on Council recommendation.
- Parliament formulates place-of-supply principles (clause (5)); imports are treated as inter-State supplies.
- Collection authority and ultimate revenue benefit are separate questions — never conflate them.

## 5. Article 279A — exact composition

- Chair: Union Finance Minister. Member: Union Minister of State, Revenue/Finance. Members: one nominated minister per State.
- Vice-Chairperson: chosen by State members from among themselves, for a period they decide.
- The Conduct of Business Rules currently give the Vice-Chairperson a two-year term (or until ceasing to be a member/resigning, whichever is earlier), with seven days' ordinary meeting notice, agenda circulation at least three days ahead, and a two-day emergency-meeting route with Chair approval.
- Revenue Secretary (ex-officio Secretary) and CBIC Chairperson (permanent, non-voting invitee) are administrative arrangements, not constitutional members.
- Only constitutional members carry a vote under Article 279A(9).

## 6. What the Council may recommend

- Eight buckets under Article 279A(4): subsumed taxes/cesses; taxable/exempt goods and services; model laws/place of supply; thresholds; rates including floor bands; disaster-period rates; special State provisions; any other GST-related matter.
- Article 279A(5) separately requires a recommended commencement date for five specified petroleum products.
- The residual bucket is bounded by the other seven, not an unlimited mandate.

## 7. Recommendation-to-law chain

- A recommendation is a negotiated position, not self-executing law.
- CGST Act section 9 (rates) and section 11 (exemptions) authorise notification on Council recommendation — two distinct statutory hooks.
- Exempt, nil-rated and zero-rated supplies carry different input-credit consequences.
- Safest current-affairs practice: name the meeting and date, say "recommended," identify the notification, state the effective date.

## 8. Voting mathematics — the signature mechanism

- Quorum: 1/2 of total membership. Union weight: 1/3. States' combined weight: 2/3. Passing threshold: 3/4.
- If the Union supports a proposal, at least 5/8 (62.5%) of State votes present and voting must also support it.
- If the Union opposes, States' maximum weight (2/3) remains below the 3/4 threshold — they can never pass over Union objection.
- Verdict: accommodative, but not equal, federalism.

## 9. GST mechanics the Council shapes

- Intra-State: CGST + SGST/UTGST. Inter-State: IGST. Exports/qualifying SEZ supplies: zero-rated.
- Input-tax credit (sections 16, 49) reduces cascading but is statutorily conditioned, not automatic.
- Inverted duty structure: input rate exceeds output rate, accumulating unused credit.
- The 56th Council meeting (3 September 2025) recommended a broad 5%/18%/40% structure — a dated anchor, not a frozen commodity list.

## 10. Constitutional and statutory boundaries

- Alcohol: definitional exclusion (Article 366(12A)) — requires a further amendment to change.
- Petroleum (five products): within the definition, commencement deferred (Article 279A(5)) — reversible by recommendation plus notification.
- Electricity: retained State Entry 53 — a different mechanism from both alcohol and petroleum.

## 11. GST compensation — the transition bargain

- Section 18 of the Amendment directed a five-year compensation law; the 2017 Act used FY 2015-16 as base year, 14% projected growth.
- Compensation = statutory gap between projected protected revenue and actual revenue, financed by a compensation cess/fund.
- The five-year entitlement **ended in June 2022**.
- COVID-19 caused collections to fall while the protected-revenue requirement rose; back-to-back loans bridged the gap; cess collection continues for debt servicing — not an extension of the entitlement.
- This file's single verified current-affairs anchor (checked 2 October 2026): the 56th meeting remains the latest official outcome; a 57th meeting was reported scheduled for 7 October 2026, with no official result yet.

## 12. *Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022)

- Recommendations are persuasive, not binding, on legislatures — tied to Article 246A's simultaneous competence.
- Where a statute conditions notification on a recommendation, the executive remains bound by that condition.
- The ocean-freight levy failed because it duplicated value already captured within the composite-supply scheme — a narrow, statute-specific outcome, not a blanket rule.
- The Council was not rendered irrelevant.

## 13. Cooperative, competitive and confrontational federalism

- GST is pooled sovereignty — neither full centralisation nor full State autonomy.
- Three modes coexist: cooperative (consensus, common rules), competitive (administrative performance), confrontational (compensation, rate, petroleum disputes).
- State autonomy shifted from unilateral rate-setting toward negotiated rule-making plus retained legislative competence.
- The precise verdict: accommodative, but not equal, federalism.

## 14. Disputes, institutional comparison and reform

- Article 279A(11) requires an intergovernmental adjudicatory mechanism — distinct from taxpayer appeals/GSTAT; none was verified as complete at this file's control date.
- GST Council (Article 279A, GST design) vs Finance Commission (Article 280, devolution) vs Inter-State Council (Article 263, broad coordination) vs Zonal Councils (statutory, regional).
- Reform agenda: transparency, independent modelling, operational Article 279A(11), a stable rate calendar, post-compensation trust-building, small-business support, local-government impact review, a notification tracker.

## Final 14 rapid-recall facts

1. 101st Amendment, 2016; GST launch from 1 July 2017; Council constituted 15 September 2016.
2. Article 246A is special, shared competence — "notwithstanding" Articles 246 and 254, not an ordinary Concurrent List entry.
3. Parliament alone holds exclusive power over inter-State GST (Article 246A(2)).
4. Article 269A: Union levies/collects IGST; apportionment by parliamentary law on Council recommendation.
5. Article 279A: Chair = Union Finance Minister; Vice-Chair chosen by State members.
6. Revenue Secretary and CBIC Chairperson are non-voting administrative arrangements.
7. Quorum = 1/2; Union weight = 1/3; States' weight = 2/3; passing threshold = 3/4.
8. With Union support, 5/8 of State votes present and voting are needed.
9. Alcohol: definitional exclusion. Petroleum: deferred commencement. Electricity: retained State entry.
10. Compensation's five-year entitlement ended June 2022; post-2022 cess services pandemic-era loans.
11. *Mohit Minerals* (2022): recommendations persuasive for legislatures; statutes still bind the executive.
12. GST Council ≠ Finance Commission ≠ Inter-State Council ≠ Zonal Councils.
13. Article 279A(11)'s intergovernmental dispute mechanism remains, as far as verified, unoperationalised.
14. The examinable verdict for GST's federalism: accommodative, but not equal.

## Final verdict bank

- **Institutional:** The GST Council is a constitutional bargaining forum whose authority rests on structured interdependence, not legislative supremacy.
- **Federal:** GST pooled, rather than transferred or preserved, tax sovereignty; its legitimacy depends on reciprocal accommodation and credible transition commitments.
- **Judicial:** *Mohit Minerals* preserves harmonisation through persuasion for legislatures while keeping delegated executive action within statutory bounds.
- **Reform:** transparent evidence, an operational Article 279A(11) mechanism, and clear recommendation-to-notification tracking are the next stage of cooperative fiscal federalism.
