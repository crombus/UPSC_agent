# Federal System — Live Session Edition

India’s federal design answers a difficult constitutional question: **how can a vast and diverse country preserve meaningful regional self-government without weakening national unity?** The Constitution answers through divided authority, a strong Union, judicially protected State spheres, differentiated arrangements for particular regions, and institutions that convert disagreement into negotiation.

### 1. Begin with a simple problem

Every large country must decide which matters should be handled nationally and which should be handled regionally. Defence, currency and foreign affairs normally require national coordination. Policing, agriculture, health and local administration often require sensitivity to regional conditions.

A **unitary system** gives the national government ultimate legal authority. Regional institutions may be powerful and democratically elected, but their competence is derived from national law. A **federal system** divides authority through the Constitution itself. The national and regional governments are not merely superior and subordinate offices; each derives its competence from the higher constitutional settlement.

Britain, France, Japan and China are standard unitary illustrations in the textbook comparison, while the United States, Switzerland, Australia, Canada and Russia illustrate federal forms. The examples do not imply identical internal designs; they show that both categories contain substantial variation.

> **Answer line:** Federalism is a constitutional distribution of governing authority between two levels, each operating directly upon citizens within an assigned sphere.

### 2. Federalism is not the same as decentralisation

| Question | Administrative decentralisation | Constitutional federalism |
|---|---|---|
| Source of regional power | National or State legislation | Constitution |
| Can the higher government withdraw it unilaterally? | Ordinarily yes | Not within the protected sphere |
| Is an independent constitutional umpire necessary? | Not always | Normally yes |
| Typical purpose | Better administration | Self-government plus shared government |

A unitary State can be highly decentralised. A federation can, in practice, become heavily centralised. Therefore, the amount of day-to-day local freedom does not alone answer the constitutional classification.

### 3. The complete eight-axis comparison

| Axis | Federal pattern | Unitary pattern | India-specific qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Levels/governments | National and regional governments are constitutionally coordinate within assigned spheres | National government is legally supreme; regional governments remain subordinate in source | Union and States both derive authority from the Constitution, but the Union is structurally stronger |
| Source/distribution of powers | Constitution itself divides competence | National authority grants or reallocates regional competence | Seventh Schedule divides fields; Parliament holds residue and specified State-field entry routes |
| Written Constitution | The territorial bargain is ordinarily recorded in an authoritative constitutional text | A unitary constitution may be written or unwritten because regional authority need not be entrenched | India’s detailed written Constitution defines Union and State institutions, powers and limits |
| Constitutional supremacy | Higher law ordinarily binds both levels and invalidates excess of competence | A constitution may be supreme, but no protected territorial division is necessary | The written supreme Constitution binds Union and States |
| Amendment | Federal bargain ordinarily requires participation of both levels or special rigidity | National institutions ordinarily control constitutional change | Specified federal provisions require Parliament plus ratification by at least half the States; States cannot initiate amendments |
| Judiciary | Independent umpire enforces competence boundaries | Territorial disputes need not be decided against a protected constitutional division | Integrated judiciary, but the Supreme Court performs federal review and Article 131 adjudication |
| Representation/bicameralism | Territorial units receive a national second-chamber voice, often equally | Second chamber may be absent or need not represent regions constitutionally | Rajya Sabha represents States unequally and possesses special powers under Articles 249 and 312 |
| Territorial security | Constituent units ordinarily enjoy strong protection against unilateral alteration | National authority may create or reshape regions | Parliament may alter State area, boundary or name after consultation without State consent |

The table prevents a shallow conclusion. India passes the source, supremacy and adjudication tests, but departs from the classical model through Union residue, unequal State representation and weak territorial security.

### 4. Two routes to federation

The standard distinction is between **coming-together** and **holding-together** federations.

```text
COMING TOGETHER
Previously independent units
        ↓ agreement
Create a common federation
        ↓
Units usually retain strong protection
Example: United States

HOLDING TOGETHER
One large political unit
        ↓ constitutional distribution
Grants territorial autonomy
        ↓
Union commonly remains stronger
Examples: Canada and India
```

The older textbook terms are **integration** and **disintegration**. “Disintegration” here does not mean national collapse. It means that a previously more centralised political space is constitutionally divided into national and regional spheres.

The United States, formed under its federal Constitution in 1787, is the standard oldest modern federation. Canada’s 1867 arrangement exemplifies an older strong-centre federation. Constituent units carry different names across systems—States in the United States, cantons in Switzerland, provinces in Canada and republics within the Russian federation—without the name alone determining their powers.

### 5. Why countries choose federalism

Federalism performs two functions simultaneously:

1. **shared rule** for common national purposes; and
2. **self-rule** for territorially concentrated identities and local needs.

India’s large geographical scale and socio-cultural diversity made this combination attractive. A purely centralised administration risked insensitivity and overload. A loose confederation risked instability and secession after Partition. Federalism offered regional autonomy without treating the Union as a revocable alliance.

### 6. Comparison as an argument

The United States is a classic coming-together federation. Its States pre-dated the federal Constitution, retain the ordinary residue, receive equal representation in the Senate and enjoy strong territorial protection.

Canada and India are strong-centre, holding-together federations. Their constitutions use national power to preserve unity while recognising provincial or State spheres. India goes further through single citizenship, an integrated judiciary and Parliament’s power to alter State boundaries.

India resembles the Canadian tendency in three connected respects: holding-together formation, preference for the term “Union,” and a deliberately strong national government.

The comparison matters because it explains why importing an American checklist mechanically can misclassify India.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the Union is much stronger, India should simply be called unitary.

**Reply:** Unequal power does not erase constitutional division. The relevant question is whether States possess authority directly from the Constitution in fields the Union cannot ordinarily absorb at will. India satisfies that test through the Seventh Schedule, State executives and legislatures, constitutional amendment requirements, judicial review and a State-representing second chamber.

**Qualified residual:** India is not a classical federation of sovereign pre-existing units. It is a federation created to hold together a diverse Union, so central strength is part of its federal design rather than proof that no federation exists.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** distinguish constitutional division from mere administrative delegation.
- **Mains:** begin classification answers with the source-of-power test, not with a scholar’s label.
- **Trap:** “Coming together” and “holding together” describe formation tendencies; they do not by themselves determine how centralised a federation later becomes.
- **Answer line:** *India adopted federalism not to unite sovereign States but to reconcile national integrity with regional autonomy inside one indestructible Union.*

### Revision notes

1. Unitary systems vest ultimate legal authority in the national government.
2. Federal systems divide authority through the Constitution itself.
3. Regional governments can exist in both unitary and federal systems.
4. Decentralisation is delegation; federalism is constitutionally protected distribution.
5. Coming-together federations arise from previously separate units.
6. Holding-together federations distribute power within a larger political unit.
7. The United States is the standard coming-together example.
8. Canada and India illustrate strong-centre, holding-together designs.
9. Federalism combines shared rule with territorial self-rule.
10. India’s size and diversity supplied the practical case for federalism.
11. A strong Union does not automatically destroy federal character.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the existence of elected regional governments not by itself prove that a country is federal?

**Model answer:** Elected regional governments may exist because a national legislature has delegated authority to them. Federalism requires more: their competence must arise from the Constitution and be protected against ordinary unilateral withdrawal by the national government. The source and security of power, not the mere existence of regional elections, supplies the constitutional test.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate political decentralisation with federalism; a unitary State may decentralise extensively without constitutionally dividing sovereignty.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Distinguish constitutional federalism from administrative decentralisation. Why did India require the former?

**Model answer — 141 words:**
Administrative decentralisation transfers functions from a higher government to subordinate territorial bodies; the delegating authority ordinarily retains legal power to redesign or withdraw that transfer. Constitutional federalism is different: the Constitution itself allocates competence between national and regional governments, each acting directly within its sphere.

India required this stronger arrangement because its scale and diversity demanded both shared rule and regional self-government. National subjects such as defence, currency and foreign affairs needed unity, while public order, agriculture, health and local conditions required territorially responsive government. Mere delegation would have left State authority dependent entirely on Union preference.

Yet India did not adopt a compact among sovereign States. Partition, integration and development needs encouraged a holding-together federation with a strong Union. Thus Indian federalism is neither loose confederation nor administrative convenience: it is constitutionally protected regional autonomy within an indestructible national framework.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** source-of-power distinction 3; reversibility/security contrast 2; Indian diversity and scale 2; strong-Union qualification 2; direct conclusion 1.

### 1. Why a feature-list needs a theory

The familiar seven features are not seven unrelated facts. They solve three recurring federal problems:

| Federal problem | Institutional answer |
|---|---|
| Who governs? | Dual polity |
| Who may legislate on what? | Constitutional division of powers |
| What law is highest? | Written and supreme Constitution |
| Can one level rewrite the bargain? | Rigidity for federal provisions |
| Who resolves disputes? | Independent judiciary |
| How do territorial units enter national lawmaking? | Bicameralism |

The federal test therefore asks whether regional autonomy is **created, protected, adjudicated and represented**.

### 2. The seven essential features

1. **Dual polity:** national and regional governments possess constitutionally assigned institutions and responsibilities.
2. **Written Constitution:** the bargain must be identifiable rather than dependent only on changing political convention.
3. **Division of powers:** legislative subjects are allocated between levels.
4. **Supremacy of the Constitution:** neither level may lawfully exceed the constitutional grant.
5. **Rigidity:** core federal provisions cannot be rewritten by one level acting through an ordinary majority.
6. **Independent judiciary:** disputes require an umpire institutionally separate from both contestants.
7. **Bicameralism:** a second chamber gives territorial units a route into national legislation.

### 3. Necessary, supportive and variable features

Not every federation expresses each feature identically. Upper Houses may represent units equally, by population or through State governments. Judicial structures may be dual or integrated. The residue may rest with the Union or the units.

That variation suggests a disciplined hierarchy:

```text
CORE
constitutional division + supremacy + adjudication

PROTECTIVE
rigidity + territorial representation

VARIABLE
equal or unequal seats, location of residue,
integrated or dual courts
```

An unequal Rajya Sabha weakens one classical safeguard, but it does not nullify the rest of India’s constitutional distribution.

### 4. Independent judiciary as a federal safeguard

An independent judiciary performs two connected tasks:

- it invalidates laws that exceed legislative competence; and
- under Article 131, the Supreme Court hears specified legal-right disputes between the Union and States or among States.

Article 262 creates a special water-dispute exception where Parliament may provide adjudication and exclude ordinary court jurisdiction over the defined dispute. The exception proves that federal adjudication is designed article by article, not through one unlimited jurisdiction.

### 5. Bicameralism: representation, not necessarily equality

Rajya Sabha represents the States, but the Fourth Schedule gives them unequal seats broadly linked to population. This differs from the United States Senate, where every State receives two seats.

The standard textbook range is one to thirty-one Rajya Sabha seats for a State, reinforcing that India recognises representation without adopting equality of representation.

The correct reasoning is:

```text
Rajya Sabha = federal feature because States receive a second-chamber voice
Rajya Sabha ≠ classical equal-State chamber because representation is unequal
```

Its special powers under Articles 249 and 312 strengthen its federal role: it can authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List matter in the national interest and enable creation of a new All-India Service.

### Verified PYQ linkage

**2021 Prelims GS-I, Question 86**

> Which one of the following in Indian polity is an essential feature that indicates that it is federal in character?
>
> (a) The independence of judiciary is safeguarded.
>
> (b) The Union Legislature has elected representatives from constituent units.
>
> (c) The Union Cabinet can have elected representatives from regional parties.
>
> (d) The Fundamental Rights are enforceable by Courts of Law.

**Demand:** distinguish a feature that bears on the territorial organisation of constitutional power from features that may arise in several forms of democratic government.

**Approach:** classify every option by its constitutional function—territorial organisation, representation, rights protection or ordinary political participation—without assuming that every valuable institution is uniquely federal.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Lists of federal features are formalistic because political parties and fiscal dependence determine real autonomy.

**Reply:** Political and fiscal forces undeniably shape the working balance, but they operate within a constitutional opportunity structure. Party dominance cannot permanently transfer a State List field without constitutional authority; fiscal dependence does not abolish elected State governments; and courts can enforce the distribution.

**Qualified assessment:** Formal institutions are necessary but not sufficient. A complete answer must combine constitutional design with political and financial practice.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** classify institutions by the constitutional function they perform before selecting among close options.
- **Mains:** organise the seven features by function—creation, protection, adjudication and representation.
- **Trap:** neither bicameralism nor judicial review should be treated as conclusive in isolation; examine how each operates inside the complete territorial-power design.
- **Answer line:** *A federation exists not because it has two sets of offices, but because the Constitution protects the competence of each level through supremacy, rigidity and impartial adjudication.*

### Revision notes

1. Dual polity creates two constitutionally recognised levels.
2. A written Constitution makes the distribution ascertainable.
3. Division of powers is the operational core of federalism.
4. Constitutional supremacy binds both Union and States.
5. Rigidity prevents unilateral rewriting of the federal bargain.
6. Judicial independence protects competence boundaries.
7. Bicameralism supplies territorial participation in national lawmaking.
8. Equal upper-house representation is classical but not universally necessary.
9. Rajya Sabha seats are unequal, yet the House performs federal functions.
10. Articles 249 and 312 give Rajya Sabha special federal powers.
11. Formal design must be combined with analysis of political and fiscal practice.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should a federal diagnosis rely on the interaction of several institutions rather than bicameralism alone?

**Model answer:** A second chamber may exist in a unitary system and may represent political interests rather than constitutionally protected territories. Federal character becomes durable only when territorial representation operates with constitutional division, supremacy, rigidity and independent adjudication. The institutions must therefore be read as a connected protection system, not as isolated labels.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer federalism merely from the presence of two legislative chambers; ask whom the second chamber represents and whether regional power is constitutionally protected.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** “No single institutional feature proves federalism.” Explain through the essential features of a federation.

**Model answer — 147 words:**
Federalism is a system of mutually supporting safeguards, not a one-feature classification. Dual governments may exist in a decentralised unitary State; bicameralism may exist without territorial autonomy; and judicial review may protect rights without dividing legislative competence.

A genuine federation therefore combines a written and supreme Constitution with a constitutional distribution of powers. Partial rigidity prevents the national legislature from unilaterally rewriting the bargain. An independent judiciary adjudicates competence disputes, while a second chamber brings territorial units into national lawmaking.

India illustrates why the features must be read together. Rajya Sabha does not give States equal representation, and the Union holds residuary power, yet the Seventh Schedule, State institutions, Article 368 ratification requirements and judicial review create protected State spheres. Political or fiscal centralisation may weaken their operation, but does not erase them.

Thus federal character lies in the combined architecture of creation, protection, adjudication and representation.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** rejection of single-feature test 2; constitutional core 3; protective institutions 2; Indian application 2; synthesis 1.

### 1. Why “Union” was chosen

The Constitution does not describe India as a “Federation of States.” Article 1 uses **“Union of States.”** B.R. Ambedkar explained that the phrase rejects two implications of a classical compact:

1. the Indian federation did not arise from an agreement among sovereign States; and
2. no State has a right to secede.

“Union” therefore communicates **indissolubility**, not the absence of federalism. Union and States still derive their authority from the Constitution rather than States functioning as ordinary departments of the Union.

### 2. Territory of India and Union of India

Article 1 makes the **territory of India** broader than the States alone. It includes:

- territories of the States;
- Union Territories specified in the First Schedule; and
- territories that may be acquired.

This matters because every Indian territory need not have identical federal status. States are constituent units with constitutionally organised governments; Union Territories are administered through a different constitutional design.

### 3. The reorganisation interface

Articles 2–4 illustrate the strong-centre character of India’s federation:

| Provision | Federal-system significance |
|---|---|
| Article 2 | Parliament may admit or establish new States |
| Article 3 | Parliament may form States and alter their area, boundaries or names |
| Article 4 | Such legislation is not treated as an Article 368 constitutional amendment |

An Article 3 Bill requires the President’s recommendation. Where a State is affected, the President refers the proposal to its legislature for views within a specified period. Those views are consultative, not binding.

> **Answer line:** India protects State competence more strongly than State territory: a State governs constitutionally assigned subjects, but it has no constitutional veto over alteration of its own boundaries.

### 4. Why this does not make States municipal bodies

Territorial vulnerability and functional autonomy are different questions.

```text
BOUNDARY SECURITY
India: weak State veto

LEGISLATIVE / EXECUTIVE COMPETENCE
India: constitutionally distributed and judicially protected
```

Parliament can alter a State’s map, but it cannot by ordinary preference abolish the entire Seventh Schedule distribution. This distinction prevents two errors: treating Indian States as sovereign, or reducing them to revocable administrative agencies.

### 5. Reorganisation as accommodation

The central power has sometimes appeared coercive, but territorial reorganisation has also enabled federal accommodation. Linguistic States allowed administration and political participation to align with major language communities. New States have responded to regional aspirations, administrative viability and demands for representation.

**Limit:** Reorganisation is not automatically democratic merely because Parliament has legal power. Poor consultation may damage federal trust. Constitutional authority and cooperative political practice are separate standards.

### 6. Cession is different

Internal reorganisation under Article 3 must not be confused with ceding Indian territory to a foreign country. *In re Berubari Union* (1960) treated cession as requiring constitutional amendment under Article 368. The distinction is between rearranging units inside India and parting with sovereign territory.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If Parliament can alter States without consent, India’s States lack genuine federal status.

**Reply:** Classical federations usually give units stronger territorial protection, so the objection identifies a real centralising feature. But federal status also depends on constitutionally sourced legislative, executive and financial competence. India deliberately combines weaker territorial security with meaningful functional autonomy.

**Qualified assessment:** Article 3 makes India unlike the United States, but it is evidence of a **holding-together, Union-weighted federation**, not proof of a wholly unitary State.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** the Constitution says “Union of States,” not “Federation”; State consent is not required under Article 3.
- **Mains:** use the distinction between territorial vulnerability and functional autonomy.
- **Trap:** “Indestructible Union of destructible States” is explanatory shorthand, not constitutional text.
- **Answer line:** *The Union is permanent, State boundaries are alterable, but State competence remains constitutional rather than delegated.*

### Revision notes

1. Article 1 calls India a Union of States.
2. “Union” rejects a compact-based right to secede.
3. It does not erase the constitutional distribution of powers.
4. Territory of India includes States, Union Territories and acquired territory.
5. Article 2 concerns admission or establishment of new States.
6. Article 3 concerns formation and alteration of existing units.
7. State views under Article 3 are consultative, not binding.
8. Article 4 keeps reorganisation outside Article 368.
9. State territory is less secure than State legislative competence.
10. Linguistic and later reorganisation can accommodate identity.
11. Cession abroad requires a different constitutional route.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can India be an “indestructible Union” while Parliament remains able to alter State boundaries?

**Model answer:** Indestructibility refers to the Union as a whole and the absence of a State right to secede. Alterability concerns the internal map: Articles 2–4 permit Parliament to create or reshape constituent units. The Union therefore remains permanent while its territorial organisation can change. This weakens State territorial protection without converting constitutionally assigned State powers into mere delegation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer that “Union of States” means either a voluntary confederation or a unitary administration with no protected State sphere.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** “The Constitution protects State functions more strongly than State boundaries.” Discuss.

**Model answer — 146 words:**
India is an indestructible Union with alterable constituent units. Under Articles 3 and 4, Parliament may form, rename or alter a State through ordinary legislative procedure after obtaining, but not accepting, the concerned legislature’s views. States therefore lack the territorial veto found in classical federations such as the United States.

Their functional position is stronger. State legislatures and executives derive authority directly from the Constitution; the Seventh Schedule assigns them fields, federal provisions receive Article 368 protection, and courts review legislative competence. Parliament cannot treat these powers as ordinary administrative delegation, although specified constitutional routes permit Union entry into the State field.

This asymmetry reflects India’s holding-together design. Flexible boundaries enabled linguistic and regional accommodation, but unilateral or poorly consulted reorganisation can weaken trust. Hence the Constitution secures the Union and the division of governing authority while leaving the internal map unusually open to parliamentary change.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** Articles 3–4 mechanism 3; functional-autonomy contrast 3; comparative/holding-together explanation 2; consultation critique 1; verdict 1.

That territorial flexibility must now be placed beside the institutions that give States genuine constitutional authority.

### 1. Dual polity without dual sovereignty

India has Union and State legislatures, executives and public administrations. States are constitutionally created governments, not delegations of Parliament. Yet they are not sovereign in the classical international-law sense: they lack a right to secede, independent constitutions and independent foreign relations.

The best formulation is **dual government under one sovereign Constitution**.

### 2. Written and supreme Constitution

The Constitution establishes the institutions, powers and limits of both levels. Union and State laws must conform to it. Neither Parliament nor a State legislature is legally sovereign in the British sense.

Supremacy performs a federal function because disputes are decided against the constitutional allocation, not according to whichever legislature is politically stronger.

### 3. Division of legislative powers

Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule establish three fields:

| List | General orientation | Illustrative fields |
|---|---|---|
| Union List | National coordination | Defence, foreign affairs, currency |
| State List | Regional governance | Public order, police, agriculture, public health, prisons, local government |
| Concurrent List | Shared legislative concern | Criminal law, marriage, education, forests |

A common examination convention records Union 100, State 61 and Concurrent 52 entries, while omitted and inserted entry numbering can produce different literal counts. In examination answers, the constitutional function of the Lists is more important than presenting the numbers as immutable.

Residuary competence belongs to Parliament through Article 248 read with Union List Entry 97. That is a centralising qualification, but it operates inside a constitutionally divided system.

### 4. Partial rigidity

Article 368 uses more than one amendment route. Provisions central to the federal structure—including specified Union–State relations and judicial arrangements—require a parliamentary special majority plus ratification by at least half the State legislatures.

States cannot initiate a constitutional amendment, and not every provision requires ratification. Nevertheless, the ratification requirement prevents Parliament acting alone on the protected federal core.

### 5. Independent judiciary

The Supreme Court protects federalism through:

- judicial review of legislative competence;
- Article 131 original jurisdiction over specified federal legal disputes;
- review of exceptional central action, especially Article 356 after *S.R. Bommai*.

Judicial independence therefore turns the written distribution from political advice into enforceable law.

### 6. Bicameralism and its limitation

Lok Sabha represents the people. Rajya Sabha represents the States, but not equally. Larger States receive more seats. India consequently lacks the equal-State counterweight supplied by the United States and Australian Senates.

Rajya Sabha still matters federally because it is permanent and possesses special powers under Articles 249 and 312. Its party composition, however, may cause members to vote primarily along party lines rather than as instructed State delegations.

### 7. Example and limit

**Example:** **Prisons are State List Entry 4.** State governments administer prisons under their own rules within the legal framework applicable to them.

**What this establishes:** the Constitution assigns significant coercive and welfare responsibilities to States.

**Limit:** Parliament may still influence the field through constitutional powers, national legislation within its competence, model laws, funding and judicially enforceable rights. State-list location is meaningful autonomy, not isolation.

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The seven features present an idealised picture because Union dominance in finance, parties and administration overwhelms them.

**Reply:** Working conditions can make autonomy unequal or constrained, but the federal features remain legally consequential. State laws, governments and political mandates cannot be abolished by executive instruction; central action must find constitutional authority and remains reviewable.

**Qualified assessment:** Indian federalism is constitutionally real but operationally unequal. That is why “Union-weighted federation” is more precise than either “perfect federation” or “unitary State.”

### UPSC application

- **Mains:** attach an Article or institutional example to every claimed feature.
- **Trap:** do not call Rajya Sabha an equal-representation chamber.
- **Answer line:** *India’s federal character rests on constitutionally created State authority; its centralising bias affects the balance within federalism rather than eliminating federalism itself.*

**Model answer — 203 words:**

This structure prevents States from being treated as ordinary administrative agencies. In *S.R. Bommai*, the Supreme Court accordingly recognised federalism as part of the basic structure and affirmed that States are not mere appendages.

Yet India is not a classical coming-together federation. Parliament holds residuary competence under Article 248 and Entry 97, State representation in Rajya Sabha is unequal, States lack separate constitutions and citizenship, the judiciary and audit machinery are integrated, and Parliament may alter State boundaries without their consent. Emergency and State-list legislative routes further strengthen the Union.

These features do not erase constitutional State spheres; they make their protection less symmetrical than in the United States. “Union-weighted federation” therefore captures both propositions: federalism is legally real, but national unity and coordinated governance receive deliberate institutional priority.

The fourteen features are easier to understand when grouped by **what kind of leverage** they give the Union.

### 1. Power-distribution advantages

The Union List contains fields requiring nationwide coordination and political weight. Parliament also prevails in irreconcilable Concurrent-List conflict under Article 254, subject to the limited State-law exception in clause (2). Article 248 and Union List Entry 97 place the residue with Parliament.

These rules mean that unforeseen subjects and many high-capacity functions begin from a Union advantage.

### 2. States are not territorially indestructible

Articles 3–4 allow Parliament to alter State areas, boundaries and names without obtaining the State legislature’s consent. This is one of the clearest departures from classical American federalism.

The qualification remains essential: alterable boundaries do not mean that the Union may ignore the constitutional distribution of State functions.

### 3. One Constitution and a relatively flexible amendment system

States do not frame separate constitutions. Jammu and Kashmir was the historical exception before the 2019 constitutional changes. Further, only a defined set of federal amendments requires State ratification; large portions of the Constitution can be amended by Parliament without it, and States cannot initiate amendments.

### 4. Unequal territorial representation

Rajya Sabha seats vary by population. Smaller States therefore lack the equal-representation shield found in the United States Senate and Australian Senate. Party voting may further reduce the House’s capacity to operate as a chamber of State governments.

**Reply to overstatement:** Rajya Sabha is not federally meaningless. Its elected membership, permanence and Articles 249/312 powers still give States an institutional channel.

### 5. Emergency centralisation

Part XVIII can enlarge Union executive, legislative or fiscal authority without formally amending the federal Constitution. A National Emergency affects the general federal distribution; President’s Rule has sharper State-specific consequences; Financial Emergency authorises fiscal directions.

This is an exceptional design, not India’s ordinary operating rule. Post-44th Amendment safeguards and judicial review qualify the transformation.

### 6. Institutional unity

India has:

- **single citizenship**, not separate Union and State citizenship;
- an **integrated judiciary**, enforcing both Union and State law;
- an **integrated audit system** under the CAG;
- one Election Commission for parliamentary and State-legislature elections.

Institutional unity makes the polity administratively coherent but reduces the organisational separateness associated with dual federalism.

### 7. All-India Services

Article 312 permits All-India Services common to the Union and States. IAS, IPS and the **Indian Forest Service (IFoS)** serve in State cadres but are recruited and structured through national arrangements.

| Federal criticism | Administrative defence |
|---|---|
| Common cadres allow Union influence in State administration | They supply professional continuity, common standards and national coordination |
| Cadre control can constrain elected State governments | Officers also acquire local experience and implement State policy |

The institution is therefore a federal bridge and a centralising lever at the same time.

### 8. Parliament’s access to the State List

Parliament may enter the State field through separate constitutional routes:

- Article 249: Rajya Sabha national-interest resolution;
- Article 250: National Emergency;
- Article 252: request by two or more States;
- Article 253: implementation of international obligations;
- Articles 356–357: President’s Rule arrangement.

These routes must not be collapsed. They have different triggers, duration and consequences.

### 9. Governor and reserved State Bills

The Governor is appointed by the President and holds office during presidential pleasure under Articles 155–156. This creates potential Union influence, but the Governor is constitutionally the head of the State, not simply an employee or agent of the Union.

Under Articles 200–201, a Governor may reserve a State Bill for presidential consideration. The President may assent or withhold assent, and a non-Money Bill may be directed back for reconsideration. It is safer to state the constitutional options precisely than to import the Union-Bill vocabulary of “absolute” and “suspensive” veto mechanically.

The general parliamentary rule remains that the Governor ordinarily acts on the Council of Ministers’ aid and advice. Article 200 creates an exceptional constitutional field: reservation is its clearest discretionary function, and the 2025 Article 143 opinion also recognised a bounded discretion in choosing among the three first-stage options. That discretion must give due regard to ministerial advice, serve constitutional purposes and cannot become a personal or indefinite veto.

### 10. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Taken together, the fourteen features reduce the States to municipalities.

**Reply:** The features establish Union superiority in several dimensions but do not make State authority derivative. State legislatures possess exclusive fields, State governments hold independent democratic mandates, federal amendments require State participation, and courts enforce the distribution.

**Qualified residual:** The criticism becomes strongest when constitutional levers combine with one-party dominance, fiscal dependence and weak consultation. Centralisation is therefore both textual and political, but its intensity varies over time.

- **Trap:** a centrally appointed Governor is not constitutionally described as the Union’s agent.
- **Answer line:** *India’s centralising provisions make the Union predominant, not legally unlimited; their exercise remains bounded by constitutional purpose, State spheres and judicial review.*

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate both to call the Governor merely the Union’s agent and to deny that the office can centralise Indian federalism?

**Model answer:** The Governor is the constitutional head of a State and performs duties under the Constitution, so the office cannot be reduced to Union agency. However, the unitary label goes too far. Union and State governments derive authority from the Constitution; the Seventh Schedule protects significant State fields; selected federal amendments require State ratification; and courts adjudicate competence. In *S.R. Bommai*, the Supreme Court recognised federalism as a basic feature and rejected the view that States are mere appendages.

India is therefore not a unitary State decorated with regional offices. It is a holding-together federation deliberately weighted toward national unity. The better verdict is a centralised or Union-weighted federation whose legitimacy depends on restrained use of its centralising powers.

### 1. Territorial and subject-matter competence

Article 245 addresses the territorial reach of laws. Article 246, read with the Seventh Schedule, distributes subject matter.

Parliament legislates for the whole or any part of India and may enact laws with extra-territorial operation. State legislatures normally legislate for their State, subject to a sufficient territorial connection where a law reaches beyond the boundary.

### 2. Three Lists, different purposes

The Union List holds subjects requiring national scale. The State List protects regional governance. The Concurrent List recognises areas where national consistency and regional legislation may both be legitimate.

The 42nd Amendment moved five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List:

1. education;
2. forests;
3. weights and measures;
4. protection of wild animals and birds; and
5. administration of justice and constitution of subordinate courts, excluding the Supreme Court and High Courts.

This shift illustrates that the federal balance can change through constitutional amendment rather than ordinary Union preference.

### 3. Residuary power

Article 248 and Union List Entry 97 give Parliament exclusive power over matters, including taxes, not enumerated in the State or Concurrent Lists.

The residue is reached only after honest classification. Parliament cannot relabel a matter that substantially belongs to the State List merely by calling it new or unenumerated.

### 4. Five routes into the State field

```text
R — Rajya Sabha national-interest resolution: Article 249
N — National Emergency: Article 250
S — States request Parliament: Article 252
I — International obligation: Article 253
P — President’s Rule: Articles 356–357
```

These routes do not prove that the State List is meaningless. They show that exclusive competence has constitutionally specified exceptions. Article 252 is especially cooperative: States themselves invite parliamentary legislation, and later changes ordinarily require Parliament.

### 5. Repugnancy under Article 254

Repugnancy concerns conflict between Union and State laws in the **Concurrent field**.

- Under Article 254(1), the Union law prevails and the State law is void to the extent of repugnancy.
- Under Article 254(2), a State law reserved for and assented to by the President may prevail in that State.
- Parliament may later override even that State law.

The presidential-assent route is therefore a qualified space for State variation, not permanent State supremacy.

### 6. The competence doctrines

| Doctrine | Question it answers | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Pith and substance | What is the law’s true nature? | Incidental overlap may survive |
| Territorial nexus | Is the State’s connection real and relevant? | Concerns reach, not List classification |
| Colourable legislation | Is power being used indirectly when unavailable directly? | Bad political motive alone is insufficient |
| Harmonious construction | Can apparently overlapping provisions coexist? | Cannot erase an express priority rule |
| Repugnancy | Do Union and State laws actually conflict in the Concurrent field? | Not the default test for every List overlap |

State competence is constitutionally real and ordinarily exclusive, while Union supremacy operates as a conflict rule and an exceptional coordination mechanism. This asymmetry explains why India remains federal in structure but centralised in its safeguards for national action.

### 1. Administrative relations: obligation and coordination

Articles 256–257 require State executive power to ensure compliance with parliamentary law and not impede Union executive power. The Union may issue constitutionally authorised directions.

Directions are not a general power to administer every State subject. Their legitimacy depends on the constitutional purpose and provision invoked.

Articles 258 and 258A permit reciprocal entrustment of functions with consent. The Union may entrust functions to States, and a State may entrust functions to the Union. Administrative performance can therefore be shared without automatically transferring legislative ownership.

### 2. Article 355, Article 365 and coercion

Article 355 imposes a Union duty to protect States against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure constitutional government. Article 365 permits non-compliance with valid Union directions to contribute to a presidential conclusion that State government cannot be carried on according to the Constitution.

Neither provision automatically produces President’s Rule. Article 356 requires its own constitutional satisfaction and remains judicially reviewable.

### 3. All-India Services as an administrative bridge

IAS, IPS and IFoS link national recruitment and standards with State-level administration. They provide continuity and coordination, but cadre control and deputation disputes may create friction.

Their dual character is the point: common services can strengthen both national capacity and State implementation while also increasing Union leverage.

### 4. CBI consent and the federal police field

Police and public order fall in State List Entries 1–2. The CBI has no dedicated CBI statute; it exercises police powers through the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. Sections 5 and 6 make ordinary extension of those powers into a State dependent on State consent.

Consent may be general or case-specific. Withdrawal of general consent limits the executive route. It does not remove the power of the Supreme Court or a High Court to order a CBI investigation under Articles 32 or 226. *State of West Bengal v. Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights* (2010) preserves that judicial route.

### 5. Financial relations: why dependence persists

The Constitution distributes taxing and spending powers through Articles 268–281 and borrowing powers through Articles 292–293.

| Mechanism | Federal function |
|---|---|
| Assigned and shared taxes | Connect collection with distribution |
| Article 270 divisible pool | Enables vertical and horizontal tax devolution |
| Article 275 grants | Constitutionally structured grants-in-aid |
| Article 282 grants | Public-purpose spending with wider discretion |
| Article 280 Finance Commission | Periodic recommendation on devolution and grants |
| Article 293 State borrowing | Allows borrowing within India, with Union consent in the specified debt condition |

Vertical imbalance arises because the Union controls several buoyant taxes while States carry large expenditure responsibilities. Horizontal imbalance arises because States differ in population, income, geography and fiscal capacity.

### 6. GST as shared taxing space

Article 246A creates simultaneous Union and State power over GST, with Parliament’s special position over inter-State supply. Articles 269A and 279A structure apportionment and the GST Council.

This is not a new Concurrent-List entry. It is a special constitutional arrangement that pools previously separate tax fields.

### 7. Local government: the third-tier interface

The 73rd and 74th Amendments constitutionalised Panchayats and Municipalities. Articles 243G and 243W enable State legislatures to devolve functions; Articles 243H and 243X address finances; State Finance Commissions review local fiscal arrangements.

The key federal insight is that devolution “up to local levels” depends heavily on States. Constitutional recognition does not automatically transfer every Eleventh- or Twelfth-Schedule subject or the necessary funds and staff.

It is not absolute because constitutional courts may order a CBI investigation to enforce legal and fundamental rights. Consent limits the executive route, while judicial power preserves constitutional remedies.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say either that the CBI has automatic nationwide police jurisdiction or that State withdrawal of consent binds the Supreme Court and High Courts.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** “Legislative autonomy without administrative and financial capacity is incomplete federalism.” Analyse in the Indian context.

**Model answer — 209 words:**
The Seventh Schedule may authorise a State to legislate, but policy becomes real only through personnel, institutions and revenue. Administrative relations therefore accompany legislative distribution. Articles 256–257 permit bounded Union directions; Articles 258–258A enable consensual entrustment; and All-India Services supply common capacity while also creating Union influence. CBI consent illustrates the balance: policing is a State field, executive investigation ordinarily needs consent, but constitutional courts preserve a rights-based override.

Financially, States undertake major welfare and development expenditure while depending on shared Union taxes and grants. Article 280 Finance Commissions, Articles 270 and 275 transfers, GST arrangements and borrowing under Article 293 structure this interdependence. Predictable devolution can support autonomy; discretionary grants, cesses outside the divisible pool, borrowing conditions and rigid scheme design can narrow it.

The same problem extends downward. Parts IX and IXA constitutionalise local government, but States determine much functional, fiscal and personnel devolution. Local bodies may consequently possess electoral legitimacy without sufficient “three Fs.”

The answer is not administrative separation. Common services, transfers and joint institutions are necessary in an integrated economy. The federal test is whether coordination remains rule-bound, consultative and sufficiently predictable to preserve meaningful State choice. Indian federalism is therefore a distribution not only of laws, but of implementation capacity and fiscal room.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** central thesis 2; administrative mechanisms 4; financial mechanisms 4; CBI/local examples 2; coordination counterpoint 1; qualified conclusion 2.

Once capacity is added to competence, the next question is whether every constituent unit must possess exactly the same constitutional arrangement.

### 1. What asymmetry means

**Symmetrical federalism** gives constituent units broadly identical constitutional powers. **Asymmetric federalism** gives particular units different powers, safeguards or institutional forms within the same Union.

India uses asymmetry to accommodate:

- distinctive customary practices;
- tribal self-governance;
- accession and integration histories;
- regional-development imbalance;
- capital-city and Union-Territory requirements.

> **Answer line:** Asymmetric federalism substitutes constitutionally bounded difference for the false choice between forced uniformity and territorial separation.

### 2. State-specific provisions

Articles 371–371J create different mechanisms rather than one generic “special status.”

| Purpose | Illustrative provision |
|---|---|
| Regional-development boards and opportunity | Article 371; Article 371J |
| Customary law, social practices and land safeguards | Articles 371A and 371G |
| Legislative committees for specified areas | Articles 371B and 371C |
| Public-employment and educational opportunity | Article 371D |
| Integration settlement | Article 371F |
| Bounded gubernatorial law-and-order responsibility | Article 371H |

The distinctions matter. A consent shield over specified parliamentary laws is different from a development-board obligation or a minimum Assembly size.

### 3. Fifth and Sixth Schedule interface

The Fifth Schedule concerns Scheduled Areas through arrangements involving the Governor, Tribal Advisory Councils and presidential powers. The Sixth Schedule creates autonomous district and regional councils in specified north-eastern tribal areas.

These are not interchangeable with Article 371 provisions. They arise from different constitutional locations and institutional mechanisms.

### 4. Union Territories and Delhi

Union Territories are not simply small States. Their constitutional administration falls under Articles 239–241, with varied arrangements. Article 239AA gives Delhi a legislature and Council of Ministers while retaining distinctive Union powers.

The broader federal lesson is that territorial democracy can exist in more than one constitutional form, but the allocation of authority must be read from the particular provision.

### 5. Article 370 status discipline

Article 370 remains printed in Part XXI but was rendered inoperative through the 2019 constitutional orders and declaration. Article 35A ceased to operate with the supersession of the 1954 Order. In *In re Article 370* (2023), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional result and rejected a claim of separate internal sovereignty.

Jammu and Kashmir’s Union-Territory framework must therefore not be described as the former special-status State arrangement.

### 6. Why asymmetry can strengthen unity

Uniformity may appear equal but can impose unequal burdens on territorially concentrated communities. Tailored safeguards can reduce the cost of belonging to a larger Union by protecting local institutions and resources.

**Example:** Articles 371A and 371G protect specified customary-law and land matters unless the relevant State Assembly resolves otherwise. The safeguard creates a consent mechanism inside the Union rather than sovereignty outside it.

**Limit:** Asymmetry can generate demands for equivalent treatment elsewhere, create complexity and provoke claims of unequal citizenship. It must remain tied to a defensible purpose and clear constitutional text.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Different constitutional treatment violates equality among States and fragments national law.

**Reply:** Federal equality need not mean identical institutions. The Constitution may respond differently to materially different historical, cultural and geographic conditions. Citizens remain part of one constitutional order, and the safeguards are legally bounded.

**Qualified residual:** Asymmetry is stabilising when it protects a clearly identified interest and operates transparently. It becomes corrosive when used as indefinite political privilege without accountable institutions or periodic evaluation.

### Concept check

Articles 371 and 371J support regional-development and opportunity arrangements; Article 371F embodies Sikkim’s integration settlement. It is therefore best understood as bounded accommodation. When linked to objective historical or social conditions and accompanied by accountable institutions, constitutional difference makes a diverse Union more durable rather than less equal.

The modes do not form a historical sequence. They can operate at the same time across different issues.

### 1. Cooperative federalism

Cooperative federalism means Union and States coordinate because neither can solve the problem effectively alone.

Examples include:

- GST Council deliberation under Article 279A;
- Finance Commission transfers;
- Inter-State Council consultation under Article 263;
- Zonal Council coordination;
- disaster and public-health coordination;
- implementation of centrally supported programmes through State machinery.

Cooperation is not obedience. It requires reciprocal voice, information sharing and a reasonable opportunity to shape policy.

### 2. Competitive federalism

Competitive federalism describes States competing for investment, jobs, infrastructure, administrative performance and policy reputation.

Two named Indian instances are the **NITI Aayog SDG India Index** and the **Aspirational Districts Programme**. The Index compares State and Union-Territory performance across Sustainable Development Goal indicators, making relative progress visible. The Aspirational Districts Programme uses indicator tracking, convergence and comparative performance to encourage district administrations and their State governments to improve outcomes.

These mechanisms are **performance benchmarking**, not constitutional coercion. They do not transfer a State subject to the Union or legally compel a State to adopt one policy. Their influence comes from reputation, comparison, information and incentives.

Competition can:

- encourage experimentation;
- make performance visible;
- allow successful State policies to diffuse;
- give citizens comparative information.

It can also produce a race to the bottom through excessive concessions, weak labour or environmental enforcement, and neglect of poorer regions with lower initial capacity.

Ranking evidence must be handled cautiously. Indicator choice, data quality, starting conditions and differences in administrative capacity affect results. Rankings can focus attention and support policy learning, but they may encourage gaming or treat unequal States as directly comparable. A sound federal use pairs comparison with contextual interpretation and capacity support.

### 3. Confrontational and coercive federalism

Confrontation arises where levels disagree over competence, institutions or resources. It is not automatically unconstitutional. Litigation, political protest and refusal to consent can be legitimate forms of federal bargaining.

Coercive federalism is a stronger analytical label for situations where the Union uses superior fiscal, administrative or political leverage to narrow State choice. The label is evaluative, not constitutional text.

Typical fields include:

- Governor–ministry conflict;
- State withdrawal of general CBI consent;
- disputes over cesses, grants or borrowing conditions;
- the **Cauvery inter-State river-water dispute** and difficulties of timely adjudication and implementation;
- conditional programme design;
- disagreement over legislation in overlapping fields.

### 4. GST as accommodative federalism

The 101st Amendment created a shared tax field and a joint recommendatory institution. States surrendered several independent indirect-tax powers, while the Union accepted a decision structure in which it cannot pass a proposal alone.

In *Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022), the Supreme Court held that GST Council recommendations are persuasive rather than binding and emphasised collaborative dialogue. The case preserves simultaneous legislative power while recognising the Council’s coordinating importance.

Conflict reveals where constitutional boundaries and resource arrangements are contested. Judicial decisions, commission reports and inter-governmental bargains may then clarify the rules.

**Limit:** confrontation becomes destructive where institutions are bypassed, consultation is symbolic, fiscal pressure substitutes for constitutional persuasion, or every disagreement becomes partisan obstruction.

The GST Council gives formal State participation; Finance Commission transfers have constitutional grounding; Article 263 enables consultation; judicial review remains available. The answer is to strengthen voice and transparency, not to deny interdependence.

Political alignment also changes behaviour: a party may favour central power at the Union and autonomy when governing a State.

The two labels are therefore not opposites. Cooperation describes an important mode; bargaining describes the process through which cooperation, competition and conflict are negotiated. *S.R. Bommai* supplies a constitutional floor, while institutions such as the GST Council and courts provide bargaining forums.

India is best called bargaining federalism with cooperative institutions. Its success depends on transparent consultation and on preventing unequal fiscal or administrative power from converting negotiation into command.

```

No single institution is the general manager of Indian federalism. Each has a distinct legal source and function.

### 1. Article 131 and the Supreme Court

Article 131 gives the Supreme Court exclusive original jurisdiction over specified disputes:

- Union versus one or more States;
- Union and one or more States versus other States;
- State versus State.

The dispute must involve a question of fact or law on which a legal right depends. Purely political disagreement is insufficient.

Article 131 should not be confused with Article 32 rights jurisdiction, appellate jurisdiction or Article 143 advisory jurisdiction.

### 2. River-water disputes

Article 262 authorises Parliament to provide adjudication of inter-State river-water disputes and to exclude court jurisdiction over the defined dispute. The Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 supplies the tribunal mechanism.

The **Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal**, constituted in **1990** under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, is the canonical illustration. Its long adjudicatory history shows the delay problem; the **2018 statutory scheme and management institutions** show that an award or judgment still requires an implementation architecture. A tribunal can determine allocations, but it cannot by itself create real-time data trust, reservoir coordination or political compliance among Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Puducherry.

Water disputes combine:

- upstream and downstream interests;
- rainfall and hydrological uncertainty;
- State electoral politics;
- tribunal delay;
- implementation difficulties.

Adjudication can allocate rights, but durable management also needs data sharing, basin institutions and compliance.

### 3. Inter-State Council

Article 263 permits the President to establish a council to:

- inquire into and advise upon disputes;
- discuss subjects of common interest;
- recommend better coordination.

The Inter-State Council was established in 1990 following the Sarkaria Commission’s recommendation. It is advisory, not a court and not a binding federal cabinet.

Its weakness is not constitutional insignificance but irregular use. Consultation cannot build trust if the forum rarely meets or receives issues only after positions harden.

### 4. Zonal Councils and the North-Eastern Council

Five Zonal Councils are statutory bodies under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 and are chaired by the Union Home Minister. They promote regional coordination on matters such as borders, transport, security and development.

The North-Eastern Council arises under a separate 1971 statute. It is not one of the five Zonal Councils.

### 5. Finance Commission, GST Council and NITI Aayog

| Institution | Legal source | Central federal function | Binding? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finance Commission | Article 280 | Tax devolution and grants recommendations | Advisory recommendations |
| GST Council | Article 279A | Shared GST design recommendations | Persuasive, not binding |
| NITI Aayog | Executive resolution | Policy coordination, knowledge and competition | Advisory |

The classification prevents common Prelims errors. A body may perform a major federal role without itself being constitutional or statutory.

### 6. Full faith and credit

Article 261 requires recognition of public acts, records and judicial proceedings throughout India and makes final civil judgments executable across the country according to law.

This quiet provision supports an integrated legal space. Federalism does not mean that each State is a separate legal universe.

### 7. Reform commissions

The Rajamannar Committee pressed strongly for State autonomy. The Sarkaria Commission defended a strong Union but recommended consultation, neutral Governors, sparing Article 356 use and an Inter-State Council. The Punchhi Commission revisited Governors, emergency design and inter-governmental consultation.

These recommendations are persuasive reform evidence, not self-executing constitutional law.

### 8. 2026 demand: making dispute resolution effective

The 2026 GS-II federalism question links diversity accommodation with institutional effectiveness. Lesson 8 supplies the asymmetric-federalism and regional-diversity half; this lesson supplies the dispute-resolution half.

An effective architecture requires:

- clear jurisdiction between Article 131 adjudication and Article 262 water-dispute mechanisms;
- regular Inter-State Council consultation before disputes harden;
- stronger Zonal Council follow-up for regional coordination;
- common and transparent data, especially for river and fiscal disputes;
- time-efficient tribunal processes and credible implementation;
- reasoned Union and State decisions that preserve judicial review without converting every political disagreement into litigation.

### 9. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** India has many federal forums but little binding State power within them.

**Reply:** Binding adjudication and consultation perform different tasks. Courts and tribunals decide legal disputes; councils enable compromise before or beyond litigation. Making every recommendation binding could itself weaken legislative accountability and flexible bargaining.

**Qualified residual:** Advisory design works only when meetings are regular, agendas are shared early, data are credible and governments explain departures from consensus.

- **Answer line:** *Indian federalism disperses dispute management across courts, tribunals, councils and commissions because adjudication can settle rights, while consultation must sustain relationships.*

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can an advisory institution such as the Inter-State Council remain important when the Supreme Court can decide federal disputes?

**Model answer:** Courts decide legal rights after a dispute has become justiciable. Many federal disagreements concern policy design, administrative coordination or political trust and cannot be solved adequately through winner–loser adjudication. An advisory council permits early consultation, information sharing and negotiated adjustment. Its value depends on regular, serious use rather than formal binding power alone.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Evaluate whether India’s inter-governmental institutions are adequate to manage federal conflict.

Article 262 enables specialised river-water adjudication. Article 263 supports the advisory Inter-State Council, while statutory Zonal Councils address regional coordination. The weakness lies in use and design. Tribunals may face delay and implementation problems. The Inter-State Council’s advisory nature is compounded when it meets irregularly. Zonal forums cannot resolve every high-political dispute, and fiscal institutions operate against unequal bargaining power. NITI Aayog lacks the constitutional independence of the Finance Commission.

Reform should therefore improve regularity, early agenda sharing, common data, time-bound follow-up and reasoned disclosure where consensus is rejected. Courts should remain available for legal rights, but political institutions must act before every conflict becomes litigation.

The architecture is broadly adequate in variety but uneven in operation. India needs stronger institutional habits rather than one super-body for all federal questions.

### 1. The constitutional logic

Emergency provisions allow the Union to respond to extraordinary threats without replacing the Constitution itself. They temporarily alter the working balance.

The three emergencies are distinct:

| Article 356 | Union institutions assume specified functions where State constitutional machinery has failed |
| Article 360 | Union may issue extensive financial directions |

Detailed triggers, approvals, duration and rights effects belong to emergency doctrine; the federal-system question is **what happens to divided government**.

### 2. National Emergency

During a National Emergency, Union executive power and Parliament’s legislative reach expand. State governments and legislatures do not automatically disappear. The federation moves toward unitary working, but through a temporary constitutional mechanism.

The 44th Amendment tightened safeguards after the 1975 experience. This illustrates a central principle: emergency capacity and protection against abuse must be designed together.

### 3. President’s Rule

Article 356 addresses failure of constitutional machinery in a State. The Union may assume State executive functions and Parliament may exercise State legislative power, while High Court powers remain protected.

Article 355, a Governor’s report or Article 365 non-compliance may supply relevant material, but none creates automatic constitutional breakdown.

### 4. The *Kesavananda Bharati* limitation method

*Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) established the basic-structure method: Parliament possesses a wide power to amend the Constitution under Article 368, but that power cannot be used to destroy its basic identity. The doctrine does not freeze every constitutional provision. It distinguishes permissible change from structural destruction.

*S.R. Bommai* later applied that limitation method to the federal principle by expressly treating federalism as part of the basic structure. The connection matters: *Kesavananda Bharati* supplies the constitutional test, while *Bommai* identifies federalism as one of the protected structural commitments.

### 5. From *State of Rajasthan* to *S.R. Bommai*

In *State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (1977), the Court adopted a broad and largely deferential approach to presidential satisfaction. This reflected the earlier high-water mark of central power.

*S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994) transformed the position:

- Article 356 proclamations are judicially reviewable;
- disputed legislative majority should ordinarily be tested on the floor;
- relevant material and constitutional purpose matter;
- invalid action may attract restoration;
- federalism is a basic feature;
- States are not mere appendages of the Union.

The case does not abolish Article 356. It converts emergency power from near-political discretion into constitutionally reviewable power.

### 6. Federalism as a basic-structure limit

The basic-structure doctrine does not freeze every existing detail of Union–State relations. It prevents constitutional change or State action from destroying the federal principle itself.

The difficult question is degree. A strong Union, emergency power and temporary State intervention are compatible with federalism. Unlimited destruction of State constitutional existence or complete removal of distributed authority would not be.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A federation that can become unitary during emergency is only federal at the Union’s convenience.

**Reply:** The emergency route is written into the Constitution, subject to triggers, procedure, parliamentary control, temporariness and review. Exceptions do not define the ordinary rule. *Bommai* further prevents Article 356 from becoming an unrestricted partisan weapon.

**Qualified residual:** Formal safeguards cannot remove political abuse entirely. Governors, legislative timing and post-proclamation action remain potential pressure points, so institutional restraint is as important as textual control.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** keep Articles 352, 356 and 360 separate.
- **Mains:** explain emergency centralisation as temporary constitutional transformation, not abolition of States.
- **Trap:** Article 355 or 365 does not automatically establish Article 356 failure.
- **Answer line:** *Emergency provisions make India capable of unitary action, while* Bommai *ensures that exceptional Union power remains answerable to the federal Constitution.*

### Concept check

**Question:** How can emergency centralisation remain compatible with federalism as a basic feature?

**Model answer:** Federalism governs the normal constitutional distribution, while emergency provisions create defined and temporary exceptions for extraordinary conditions. Compatibility depends on lawful triggers, procedure, parliamentary control, review and restoration of ordinary State government. *Bommai* reinforces this by making Article 356 reviewable and preserving a judicially enforceable floor of State autonomy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not argue that any temporary expansion of Union power destroys federalism, or that emergency text makes Union discretion unlimited.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Assess the transformation of Article 356 from a centralising political weapon to a reviewable constitutional remedy.

In earlier practice, broad reliance on gubernatorial reports and political assessments allowed dismissal of State governments, making the provision a major symbol of centralisation. *State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* reflected substantial judicial deference to presidential satisfaction.

The position changed decisively with *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*. The Supreme Court held proclamations judicially reviewable, required relevant material, preferred floor tests for disputed majority and recognised the possibility of restoring an unlawfully dismissed government. It also affirmed federalism as a basic feature and rejected the view that States are Union appendages.

Political changes reinforced legal control. Coalition governments and stronger regional parties increased the cost of partisan dismissal, while judicial scrutiny made misuse riskier. Sarkaria and Punchhi recommendations strengthened the last-resort norm.

Article 356 nevertheless remains a remedy, not a dead provision. Genuine constitutional breakdown may require intervention, and courts do not replace the initial constitutional assessment with routine merits review.

The transformation is therefore incomplete but substantial: the text remains centralising, while jurisprudence and political pluralism have converted it from near-discretionary power into an exceptional, reasoned and reviewable mechanism.

### 1. *State of West Bengal v. Union of India* (1963)

The case concerned Parliament’s competence to acquire property vested in a State under the Coal Bearing Areas legislation. The Court rejected the claim that Indian States possessed classical sovereign immunity against such Union law.

Its federal significance is carefully limited:

- India is not a traditional federation formed by sovereign States;
- States do not possess classical sovereignty;
- Parliament may exercise constitutionally granted authority against State interests.

Justice Subba Rao dissented in favour of a stronger account of State autonomy. The case supports the strong-Centre thesis but does not hold that States are mere administrative units.

### 2. *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994)

The nine-judge decision supplies the modern constitutional floor:

- federalism is a basic feature;
- States have an independent constitutional existence;
- within allotted spheres, States are supreme;
- Article 356 is reviewable;
- exceptional Union override does not convert the exception into the rule.

The two cases can be reconciled:

BOMMAI

          =
NO SECESSION OR SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY,
BUT A JUDICIALLY PROTECTED STATE SPHERE
```

Ambedkar’s position is more constitutional than classificatory: India establishes a dual polity but can operate with federal or unitary emphasis according to circumstances.

### 4. Quasi-federal and holding-together are not synonyms

**Holding-together** describes formation and design purpose. **Quasi-federal** is an evaluative claim that federal features are subordinated to unitary ones.

India can be holding-together without accepting Wheare’s full judgement. Likewise, “sui generis” should not substitute for evidence; it is a conclusion after comparing concrete features.

### 5. Party politics and the moving balance

National parties often favour centralisation when controlling the Union because uniform programmes and Union institutions increase their governing capacity. Regional parties commonly demand autonomy because their electoral base is territorially concentrated.

But position matters more than permanent ideology:

- national parties demand autonomy when in State opposition;
- regional parties may centralise power within their own States;
- coalition periods give regional actors bargaining leverage at the Union;
- single-party parliamentary majorities can increase central coordination.

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Is “quasi-federal” an adequate description of the Indian Constitution? Critically evaluate.

**Model answer — 186 words:**
K.C. Wheare’s “quasi-federal” label captures important departures from classical federalism. Parliament holds residuary power, may alter State boundaries, enjoys several routes into the State field and operates through integrated judicial, audit and electoral institutions. Rajya Sabha does not provide equal State representation, while Governors and emergency provisions create further Union leverage.

The label is nevertheless incomplete. Union and States both derive authority from the Constitution. The Seventh Schedule protects significant State fields; selected amendments require State ratification; State governments carry independent democratic mandates; and courts review competence. In *S.R. Bommai*, the Supreme Court recognised federalism as a basic feature and rejected the description of States as appendages.

Other labels illuminate dimensions Wheare misses. Granville Austin’s cooperative federalism explains shared institutions, Morris-Jones’s bargaining federalism captures political negotiation, and Alexandrowicz’s *sui generis* warning discourages mechanical American comparison. *State of West Bengal* still reminds us that States are not classically sovereign.

“Quasi-federal” is therefore useful as a centralisation thesis, not as a final constitutional verdict. India is more accurately a holding-together, Union-weighted federation: centralised in several textual choices, but federal in the source and judicial protection of State authority.

### 1. Article 200 after the 2025 Presidential Reference

The relevant recent constitutional development is the Supreme Court’s advisory opinion in *In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India*, **2025 INSC 1333**, delivered on **20 November 2025**. No superseding official decision was located through 30 September 2026.

The starting rule remains parliamentary government: under Article 163 and *Shamsher Singh*, the Governor ordinarily acts on the Council of Ministers’ aid and advice. Discretion is exceptional, constitutional and not absolute. Within Article 200, reservation of a Bill is the clearest discretionary function; the 2025 opinion also recognised discretion in choosing among the three first-stage options, exercised after giving due regard to ministerial advice and to the Governor’s duty to preserve the Constitution.

The Court summarised the Article 200 position as follows:

- the Governor has three constitutional options: assent; reserve the Bill for presidential consideration; or, for a non-Money Bill, withhold assent and return it with comments;
- the Governor has no power to withhold a Bill *simpliciter* and keep it in permanent limbo;
- discretion principally protects the reservation/return dialogue and must remain tied to constitutional purpose rather than personal preference;
- assent-stage choices are ordinarily not open to merits review before the Bill becomes law;
- courts should not invent fixed timelines where the Constitution specifies none;
- Article 142 cannot create “deemed assent”;
- in glaring, prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction, a court may issue limited mandamus requiring the Governor to discharge the constitutional function within a reasonable period without dictating the merits.

This later Article 143 opinion displaced the controlling parts of *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 April 2025) that had prescribed timelines and used deemed assent. It also rejected that judgment’s narrower account of Article 200 discretion. What survives is not an unlimited Raj Bhavan veto: ordinary ministerial responsibility remains the baseline, reservation-centred discretion remains exceptional, and indefinite inaction remains reviewable.

### 2. Administrative and investigative friction

State withdrawal of general CBI consent expresses autonomy over policing, while constitutional-court power prevents consent from becoming an absolute barrier to justice. Similar tension appears wherever national agencies operate in State-governed fields.

The durable reform is rule-bound coordination: clear statutory authority, transparent reasons, judicial remedies and safeguards against partisan deployment.

### 3. Fiscal stress

Federal conflict arises from:

- the vertical mismatch between Union revenue capacity and State expenditure duties;
- horizontal differences among States;
- grants and schemes that may narrow State discretion;
- cesses and surcharges outside the divisible pool;
- borrowing conditions;
- the need to finance Panchayats and Municipalities.

The conflict is not simply “Centre versus States.” Richer and poorer States may disagree over equalisation, population, performance and contribution. Fiscal federalism must balance national solidarity, State autonomy and incentives.

The Sixteenth Finance Commission submitted its report on **17 November 2025** for the **2026–31** award period and retained **41 per cent** vertical devolution. It demonstrates continuity in rules-based sharing while leaving disputes over the divisible pool, horizontal criteria, grants and borrowing unresolved.

### 4. Representational federalism

Lok Sabha representation broadly follows population, while Rajya Sabha does not equalise State representation. The long-standing inter-State seat-allocation freeze reflects a bargain: demographic performance should not immediately reduce political weight.

Article 81 seeks a broadly uniform seat–population ratio across States, while Article 80 and the Fourth Schedule allocate Rajya Sabha seats unequally. **Article 82** supplies the post-census readjustment and delimitation gateway. The 42nd Amendment froze inter-State seat allocation on the 1971 census basis. The 84th Amendment extended the freeze until readjustment after the first census taken after 2026 while permitting constituency readjustment without changing inter-State seat totals; the 87th Amendment shifted that readjustment basis to the 2001 census. The freeze on allocation among States must therefore be distinguished from redrawing constituencies within a State.

Parliament tested the bargain in April 2026. The **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026**, the **Delimitation Bill, 2026** and the **Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026** were introduced on **16 April 2026**. The constitutional-amendment Bill was negatived on **17 April 2026** by **298 votes to 230 among 528 members present and voting**: it cleared the Article 368(2) total-membership limb but did not secure not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. The linked Bills did not pass.

The proposed change from the existing Article 81 ceiling of **550 to 850** belongs only to the text of the defeated 131st Amendment Bill; it never became law. Article 329(a) protects delimitation and seat-allotment laws from ordinary judicial challenge, so the lasting settlement must be politically negotiated within the amendment framework. No State-wise projection, projected women’s-seat count, implementation year, publication date or delimitation-date forecast is constitutionally safe to infer from the defeated package.

The structural conflict is between:

- **democratic equality:** citizens should not carry permanently unequal vote weight; and
- **federal equity:** States should not be politically penalised for successful population stabilisation.

> **Representational-federalism verdict:** The population rule cannot remain suspended indefinitely, but reapportionment without a compensating federal safeguard would impose the cost of demographic success on particular States. Because India lacks an equal-representation upper chamber and Article 329(a) narrows the later judicial route, a durable answer must be a broad constitutional bargain rather than a numerical fait accompli.

### 5. River, language and territorial disputes

Water disputes reveal that constitutional allocation cannot substitute for ecological interdependence. Language and reorganisation demands show that identities remain politically territorial. Federal institutions must permit negotiation without converting every regional demand into either secession or central suppression.

### 6. Local devolution

The syllabus extends federal concern “up to local levels.” Panchayats and Municipalities are constitutionally recognised, but States retain decisive roles in assigning functions, revenue and personnel.

This creates a federal paradox: States demand autonomy from the Union while sometimes withholding autonomy from local bodies. A principled federalism must apply subsidiarity downward as well as autonomy upward.

### 8. Final integrated assessment

India is:

- **federal in constitutional source**, because State authority is not delegated;
- **centralised in design**, because the Union possesses stronger lists, residue and exceptional powers;
- **holding together in formation**, because the federation did not arise from a compact of sovereign States;
- **asymmetric in territorial accommodation**, because some units receive tailored arrangements;
- **cooperative and competitive in governance**;
- **confrontational and bargaining in political practice**;
- **limited by the basic structure and judicial review**.

### 9. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Persistent disputes over Governors, finance and agencies show that cooperative federalism is merely rhetorical.

**Reply:** The disputes show unequal power, but they also show that States possess institutions, mandates and legal claims capable of resistance. Courts, elections, coalition politics and inter-governmental forums have repeatedly altered Union behaviour.

**Qualified verdict:** Indian federalism is resilient but not self-executing. It survives because political conflict is usually channelled through constitutional institutions; it improves only when those institutions are used regularly, transparently and with reciprocal restraint.

- **Answer line:** *The future of Indian federalism depends not on weakening the Union or insulating the States, but on making interdependence rule-bound, consultative and constitutionally trustworthy.*

### Concept check

**Question:** What is the constitutional balance struck by the 2025 Article 143 opinion on gubernatorial assent?

**Model answer:** The Governor ordinarily acts on ministerial aid and advice, but Article 200 contains exceptional constitutional discretion, principally concerning reservation and the choice among three first-stage options after due regard to that advice. The opinion bars merits review of assent-stage choices, fixed judicial timelines and Article 142 deemed assent, while allowing limited mandamus against prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction. It therefore displaces the contrary timeline/deemed-assent reasoning in the April 2025 Tamil Nadu Governor judgment without creating an unlimited gubernatorial veto.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say either that the Governor is independent of ministerial government in all functions or that Article 200 discretion permits permanent withholding without return, reservation or assent.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Indian federalism is constitutionally resilient but institutionally trust-deficient. Critically examine and suggest a reform architecture.

**Model answer — 227 words:**
Indian federalism is resilient because State authority derives from the Constitution. The Seventh Schedule, elected State governments, State participation in specified amendments, judicial review and *S.R. Bommai*’s basic-structure holding preserve an autonomy floor. Asymmetric provisions also accommodate regional and cultural diversity.

Trust nevertheless remains uneven. Governor–ministry conflict delays legislation; central agencies generate consent disputes; conditional finance and borrowing controls constrain choice; river adjudication is slow; and inter-governmental councils are used irregularly. States may also withhold functions, funds and functionaries from local bodies. Representation adds conflict between citizen equality and protection of States that achieved demographic transition.

Recent evidence shows different constitutional bargains. The Sixteenth Finance Commission retained 41 per cent vertical devolution for 2026–31. The April 2026 proposal to raise the Article 81 ceiling from 550 to 850 failed the Article 368 voting threshold and never became law.

The 2025 Article 143 opinion preserves another balance: aid and advice is the ordinary rule; Article 200 discretion is bounded, especially around reservation; merits review, fixed timelines and deemed assent are unavailable; prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction permits limited mandamus. It displaces the contrary Tamil Nadu timeline/deemed-assent approach.

Reform requires regular Inter-State Council meetings, early State consultation, predictable transfers, reasoned gubernatorial action, neutral appointments, common dispute data, efficient tribunals and genuine local devolution. India needs a strong Union exercised through consultation, reasons and review, alongside strong and responsible States.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Classification

**Question:** A country has elected provinces and extensive delegated powers, but its national parliament can abolish them by ordinary law. Is it federal?

**Model answer:** Not constitutionally. It may be deeply decentralised, but the provinces’ authority remains legally derivative and withdrawable. Federalism requires constitutionally sourced and protected regional competence.

## Check 2 — Union of States

**Question:** Does the absence of the word “federation” in Article 1 settle India’s constitutional classification?

**Model answer:** No. “Union of States” denies a compact-based secession right and reflects holding-together formation. Classification depends on the complete design: divided competence, constitutional supremacy, rigidity, judicial review and State representation.

## Check 3 — Centralisation

**Question:** Which is more analytically precise: “India is unitary during normal times” or “India has constitutionally specified centralising devices”?

**Model answer:** The second. Normal government remains constitutionally divided. The Union possesses stronger lists, residue, institutional levers and exceptional State-field routes, but these operate within a federal Constitution.

## Check 4 — Asymmetry

**Question:** Why is Article 371A not equivalent to the Sixth Schedule?

**Model answer:** Article 371A is a State-specific Part XXI consent shield over specified laws concerning Naga practices, customary justice and land/resources. The Sixth Schedule creates autonomous district and regional councils under Article 244(2) in specified tribal areas. Their source, institution and mechanism differ.

## Check 5 — Institutions

**Question:** Match the problem to the mechanism: legal-right dispute, river-water dispute, fiscal devolution and policy consultation.

**Model answer:** Article 131 Supreme Court jurisdiction addresses specified federal legal-right disputes; Article 262 and tribunal legislation address inter-State river-water disputes; the Finance Commission recommends devolution and grants; the Inter-State Council supplies advisory consultation.

## Check 6 — Working modes

**Question:** Can the same GST system be described as cooperative and confrontational?

**Model answer:** Yes. Its shared constitutional field and Council embody cooperation, while disagreements over compensation, rates, legal effect or fiscal autonomy produce confrontation. Modes describe different aspects of the same arrangement.

## Check 7 — Emergency

**Question:** What prevents Article 356 from making State autonomy dependent entirely on Union satisfaction?

**Model answer:** Constitutional triggers, parliamentary approval, temporariness and especially *S.R. Bommai* judicial review. Courts may test relevant material, prefer a floor test and remedy unconstitutional use.

## Check 8 — Final synthesis

**Question:** Supply one sentence that captures Indian federalism without using an unqualified label.

**Model answer:** India is a holding-together, Union-weighted and asymmetric federation in which constitutionally protected State authority operates through continuing cooperation, competition and bargaining under a judicially enforceable federal floor.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final Practice 1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why is the phrase “centralised federation” more defensible than either “unitary State” or “classical federation” for India?

**Model answer — 136 words:**
India is not unitary because Union and State governments derive authority from the Constitution. The Seventh Schedule assigns State fields, selected federal amendments require State ratification, courts review competence and Rajya Sabha provides territorial representation. *S.R. Bommai* accordingly treats federalism as a basic feature and States as more than Union agencies.

India is not a classical federation either. It was not formed by sovereign States; Parliament holds residuary power, may alter State boundaries without consent and can enter the State field through several constitutional routes. Citizenship, courts, audit and election machinery are integrated, while emergency provisions and appointed Governors increase Union leverage.

“Centralised federation” preserves both truths. Federalism describes the constitutional source and protection of State authority; centralisation describes the unequal distribution of powers and institutions within that framework. The label is therefore analytical, not contradictory.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** federal evidence 3; centralising evidence 3; *Bommai* or Article support 1; rejection of extreme labels 1; precise conclusion 2.

## Final Practice 2 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Examine whether asymmetric and cooperative federalism are complementary strategies for governing Indian diversity.

**Model answer — 189 words:**
Asymmetric federalism accommodates diversity by giving particular territories differentiated safeguards. Articles 371–371J address customary law, land, regional development, integration and opportunity; the Fifth and Sixth Schedules use distinct institutions for tribal areas; Union Territories and Article 239AA demonstrate graded territorial government. These devices reduce the cost of belonging to one Union by protecting interests that uniform rules may overlook.

Cooperative federalism addresses a different need: interdependence. Shared taxation, fiscal transfers, river management, disaster response and national programmes require Union and States to coordinate. The GST Council, Finance Commission, Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils provide varied forums.

The two strategies are complementary. Asymmetry recognises legitimate difference; cooperation enables differently situated units to act together. Without asymmetry, cooperation may become coerced uniformity. Without cooperation, differentiated autonomy may produce fragmentation or policy paralysis.

Both also carry risks. Special arrangements can appear privileged or become institutionally opaque. Cooperative forums can reflect unequal fiscal and agenda-setting power. Safeguards therefore require clear purpose, representative participation, reasoned decisions and judicially enforceable limits.

India’s diversity cannot be governed by sameness or separation alone. Bounded constitutional difference combined with regular inter-governmental cooperation offers the more durable federal strategy.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** define both concepts 3; asymmetry evidence 3; cooperation evidence 3; complementarity mechanism 3; risks and safeguards 2; verdict 1.

## Final Practice 3 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** “Indian federalism is a continuing constitutional negotiation between autonomy, national capacity and democratic equality.” Critically analyse.

**Model answer — 219 words:**
State autonomy is constitutionally real. Union and State institutions derive authority from the Constitution; the Seventh Schedule distributes competence; selected amendments require State ratification; and *S.R. Bommai* protects federalism as a basic feature. Asymmetric provisions further recognise that meaningful autonomy may require different safeguards for different territories.

National capacity, however, receives deliberate priority. Parliament controls the residue, possesses several State-field entry routes and can alter State boundaries without consent. Single citizenship, integrated courts, audit, elections, All-India Services and emergency powers promote unity and coordinated action. These choices respond to India’s scale, Partition experience and developmental needs, but can enable over-centralisation.

Democratic equality creates a third claim. Population-based representation supports equal vote weight, while federal equity resists penalising States for demographic performance. Fiscal equalisation similarly balances contribution, need and comparable public services. Local devolution asks whether States that seek autonomy from the Union will extend it downward.

The negotiation occurs through courts, elections, Finance Commissions, the GST Council, inter-governmental bodies and political bargaining. Conflict over Governors, agencies, finance and rivers is therefore not abnormal, though unequal leverage can make bargaining coercive.

Reform must institutionalise consultation, reasoned constitutional action, predictable transfers, neutral offices, efficient adjudication and genuine local devolution. India’s federal success lies not in permanently choosing one value, but in preventing autonomy, capacity or equality from destroying the other two.

**Rubric — 20 marks:** autonomy dimension 4; national-capacity dimension 4; equality/representation/fiscal dimension 4; institutions and conflict 3; critical limitations 2; reform and synthesis 3.

# REMEDIATION

## Error 1 — “The Constitution calls India a federation”

**Why it fails:** Article 1 says “Union of States.” Federal character must be proved through the architecture.

**Repair:** Write: “Although the Constitution does not use the word federation, it creates a constitutionally divided polity.”

## Error 2 — “Strong Centre means unitary State”

**Why it fails:** Strength and constitutional source are different questions.

**Repair:** Identify the State’s protected fields and then qualify them through Union-weighted devices.

## Error 3 — “Rajya Sabha gives equal representation to States”

**Why it fails:** Seats vary broadly with population.

**Repair:** Call it a State-representing but unequally composed chamber; add Articles 249 and 312.

## Error 4 — “Every Union–State conflict is repugnancy”

**Why it fails:** Article 254 applies to actual conflict in the Concurrent field.

**Repair:** Classify the law through pith and substance before testing repugnancy.

## Error 5 — “Article 365 automatically causes President’s Rule”

**Why it fails:** Non-compliance may contribute to a conclusion; Article 356 retains its own test and review.

**Repair:** Use “may support,” never “automatically triggers.”

## Error 6 — “CBI consent is either absolute or meaningless”

**Why it fails:** Consent constrains Union executive investigation; constitutional courts retain power.

**Repair:** Separate executive and judicial routes.

## Error 7 — “Article 371, Sixth Schedule and special category status are the same”

**Why it fails:** They differ in constitutional source, institution and effect; special category status is not a constitutional category.

**Repair:** Name the exact provision and mechanism.

## Error 8 — “Cooperation means absence of conflict”

**Why it fails:** Federations bargain continuously.

**Repair:** Show how cooperation, competition and confrontation coexist across issues.

## Error 9 — “Emergency exceptions define the ordinary system”

**Why it fails:** Temporary exceptions do not erase normal constitutional division.

**Repair:** Explain trigger, safeguard, review and restoration.

## Error 10 — “A scholar’s label is the conclusion”

**Why it fails:** Labels without Articles, cases and institutions are assertion.

**Repair:** Treat the label as a thesis to be proved and qualified.

## Error 11 — “Governor assent now has a court-fixed deadline”

**Why it fails:** The 20 November 2025 advisory opinion rejects judicially prescribed fixed timelines and Article 142 deemed assent, displacing those controlling parts of the April 2025 Tamil Nadu Governor approach.

**Repair:** Begin with the Governor’s ordinary duty to act on the Council of Ministers’ aid and advice; identify bounded Article 200 discretion, principally around reservation; then state non-justiciability on merits and the limited mandamus rule for prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction.

## Error 12 — “Local government is independent of the State”

**Why it fails:** Parts IX and IXA constitutionalise local bodies, but State law remains central to functions, finance and personnel.

**Repair:** Use the three-F framework and explain the State-local relationship.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Core definition

- Federalism constitutionally distributes authority between national and regional governments.
- Both levels derive power from the Constitution and act directly upon citizens.
- Administrative decentralisation alone is not federalism because delegated power remains withdrawable.
- India is a holding-together federation designed to reconcile national unity with regional autonomy.

## Formation and terminology

- Coming together: previously separate units create a federation; United States is the standard example.
- Holding together: a larger political unit constitutionally distributes territorial power; India and Canada illustrate strong-centre versions.
- *Foedus* means treaty or agreement, but India did not arise from a compact of sovereign States.
- Article 1: “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.”
- “Union” rejects a State right to secede; it does not erase federal distribution.

## Essential federal features

1. Dual polity.
2. Written Constitution.
3. Constitutional division of powers.
4. Supremacy of the Constitution.
5. Partial rigidity for the federal bargain.
6. Independent judiciary.
7. Bicameralism.

> **Rapid line:** creation + protection + adjudication + representation = federal character.

## Indian constitutional expression

- Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule divide legislative fields.
- Study convention: Union 100, State 61, Concurrent 52; numbering complications require caution.
- Article 248 and Union List Entry 97 place the genuine residue with Parliament.
- Article 368 requires half-State ratification for specified federal provisions.
- Article 131 gives the Supreme Court exclusive original jurisdiction over specified federal legal-right disputes.
- Rajya Sabha represents States unequally and has special powers under Articles 249 and 312.

## Union of States and territory

- Territory of India includes States, Union Territories and acquired territory.
- Articles 2–4 permit admission, formation and alteration of units.
- State legislative views under Article 3 are not binding.
- Article 4 keeps such reorganisation outside Article 368.
- India therefore protects State functions more strongly than State boundaries.
- *Berubari* distinguishes internal reorganisation from cession of territory abroad.

## Fourteen centralising features

1. Strong Centre: larger and weightier Union field, Concurrent priority and Union residue.
2. Alterable State boundaries.
3. Single Constitution.
4. Relatively flexible amendment for many provisions.
5. Unequal Rajya Sabha representation.
6. Emergency centralisation.
7. Single citizenship.
8. Integrated judiciary.
9. All-India Services.
10. Integrated CAG audit.
11. Parliament’s State-list entry routes.
12. President-appointed Governor.
13. Integrated Election Commission.
14. Presidential role in reserved State Bills.

## Legislative architecture

- Article 245: territorial reach.
- Article 246: List distribution.
- Article 246A: special simultaneous GST competence; not an ordinary Concurrent entry.
- Five Union State-list routes: Articles 249, 250, 252, 253 and 356.
- Article 254: Concurrent-field repugnancy.
- Article 254(2): President-assented State variation, subject to later parliamentary override.
- Pith and substance: true nature of law.
- Territorial nexus: real connection for State extra-territorial reach.
- Colourable legislation: indirect evasion of competence limits.
- Harmonious construction: reconcile before applying supremacy.
- Prisons are placed in **State List Entry 4** and are administered by State Governments.

## Administrative architecture

- Articles 256–257: compliance and bounded directions.
- Articles 258–258A: consensual reciprocal entrustment.
- Article 355: Union protection and constitutional-government duty.
- Article 365: non-compliance may contribute to, but does not automatically establish, Article 356 failure.
- Article 312: All-India Services; IAS, IPS, IFoS.
- CBI: DSPE Act basis; ordinary State operation requires consent under Section 6.
- General and case-specific consent are distinct.
- Constitutional courts may order a CBI probe without State consent.

## Financial architecture

- Articles 268–281 structure taxes, distribution, grants and Finance Commission recommendations.
- Article 270 divisible pool supports tax devolution.
- Article 275 grants are distinct from Article 282 public-purpose grants.
- Article 280 Finance Commission addresses vertical and horizontal distribution and grants.
- Articles 292–293 govern Union and State borrowing.
- Fiscal federalism must balance autonomy, equalisation and national solidarity.
- GST pools taxing authority through Articles 246A, 269A and 279A.
- Sixteenth Finance Commission report submitted 17 November 2025 for 2026–31 retained 41 per cent vertical devolution.

## Representational federalism

- Article 81 links Lok Sabha allocation to population; Rajya Sabha does not provide equal State representation.
- Article 82 supplies the constitutional readjustment/delimitation gateway after the relevant census figures are published.
- 42nd Amendment froze inter-State allocation on the 1971 census basis.
- 84th Amendment extended the freeze until readjustment after the first census taken after 2026.
- 87th Amendment shifted constituency-readjustment basis to the 2001 census.
- Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, Delimitation Bill and Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill were introduced 16 April 2026.
- The constitutional-amendment Bill was negatived 17 April 2026 by 298:230 of 528 present and voting, clearing the total-membership limb but failing the Article 368(2) two-thirds present-and-voting limb.
- Proposed 550→850 ceiling was defeated Bill text only and never became law.
- Article 329(a) narrows ordinary judicial challenge to delimitation and seat-allotment law.
- No State projection, women’s-seat count or implementation/publication/delimitation-date forecast is safe.
- **Verdict:** reapportionment must be paired with negotiated federal safeguards because India lacks an equal-State upper chamber.

## Local-government interface

- 73rd Amendment: Part IX, Panchayats, Eleventh Schedule.
- 74th Amendment: Part IXA, Municipalities, Twelfth Schedule.
- Articles 243G/243W enable State-law devolution; they do not automatically transfer all listed subjects.
- State Finance Commissions address State-local fiscal relations.
- Federalism must work downward through functions, funds and functionaries.

## Asymmetric federalism

- Different units may receive different safeguards within one Union.
- Articles 371–371J cover distinct purposes; never treat them as one generic status.
- Articles 371A and 371G contain specified Assembly-consent shields.
- Fifth Schedule and Sixth Schedule machinery are distinct from Part XXI provisions.
- Union Territories and Article 239AA show graded territorial government.
- Article 370 is presently inoperative; J&K’s former State arrangement must not be described as continuing.
- Special category status is not a constitutional category.

## Working modes

- Cooperative: shared institutions and joint implementation.
- Competitive: investment, performance and policy experimentation; named instances include NITI Aayog’s **SDG India Index** and **Aspirational Districts Programme**.
- Performance rankings benchmark and incentivise; they do not transfer constitutional competence or themselves coerce States.
- Ranking limits include indicator choice, data quality, unequal starting capacity and possible gaming.
- Confrontational: disputes over power, consent and resources; **Cauvery** is the named river-water example.
- Coercive: analytical label for bargaining dominated by superior leverage.
- The modes coexist.
- Morris-Jones’s bargaining federalism captures their interaction.

## Institutions

| Institution | Nature | Core use |
|---|---|---|
| Supreme Court under Article 131 | Constitutional jurisdiction | Federal legal-right disputes |
| Water-dispute tribunals | Statutory under Article 262 framework | Inter-State river-water adjudication; Cauvery Tribunal constituted in 1990 |
| Inter-State Council | Article 263-authorised advisory body | Consultation and coordination |
| Zonal Councils | Statutory | Regional coordination |
| Finance Commission | Constitutional | Devolution and grants recommendations |
| GST Council | Constitutional | Shared GST recommendations |
| NITI Aayog | Executive | Policy coordination and competitive federalism |

## Emergencies and the autonomy floor

- Article 352 alters the general federal working.
- Article 356 addresses State constitutional breakdown.
- Article 360 permits financial directions.
- State governments continue under Article 352; Article 356 has sharper State-specific effects.
- *State of Rajasthan* reflected earlier deference.
- *S.R. Bommai* makes Article 356 reviewable and prefers floor tests.
- Federalism is part of the basic structure.

## Cases

- *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973): Article 368 amendment power is wide but cannot destroy the Constitution’s basic structure; this supplies the limitation method later applied to federalism.
- *State of West Bengal v. Union of India* (1963): States lack classical sovereign immunity; India is not traditionally federal in the compact sense.
- *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994): federalism is a basic feature; States are not appendages; Article 356 is reviewable.
- *State of West Bengal v. CPDR* (2010): constitutional courts may order CBI investigation without State consent.
- *Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022): GST Council recommendations are persuasive, not binding.
- *In re Article 370* (2023): upheld the present inoperative result and rejected separate internal sovereignty.
- *In re Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills* (2025): aid and advice is the ordinary rule; Article 200 contains bounded discretion, especially reservation; assent-stage merits review, fixed timelines and Article 142 deemed assent are unavailable; prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction permits limited mandamus; contrary Tamil Nadu 2025 timeline/deemed-assent reasoning is displaced.

## Scholar labels

- Wheare: quasi-federal.
- Santhanam: financial and planning-based unitary bias.
- Appleby: extremely federal.
- Morris-Jones: bargaining federalism.
- Jennings: federation with strong centralising tendency.
- Alexandrowicz: *sui generis*.
- Granville Austin: cooperative federalism.
- Ambedkar: dual polity capable of federal or unitary emphasis according to circumstances.

## High-yield traps

- Constitution does not use “Federation” for India.
- “Union of States” does not mean unitary government.
- Rajya Sabha does not give equal State seats.
- Residue belongs to Parliament.
- IFoS means Indian Forest Service in the All-India Services context.
- Article 254 is not the test for every overlap.
- Article 365 does not automatically trigger Article 356.
- GST is governed by Article 246A, not an ordinary Concurrent entry.
- CBI consent is real but does not bind constitutional courts.
- Article 371, Fifth/Sixth Schedules and special category status differ.
- Emergency exception is not the ordinary rule.
- Scholar labels are analytical characterisations, not constitutional holdings.
- Defeated 2026 delimitation proposals are not law or precedent.
- Governor discretion under Article 200 is constitutional but not absolute or a permanent veto.

## Ready-made answer verdicts

- **Nature:** India is a constitutionally federal, Union-weighted system.
- **Formation:** It is a holding-together federation, not a compact of sovereign States.
- **Working:** It is bargaining federalism operating through cooperation, competition and confrontation.
- **Diversity:** It uses asymmetric federalism to integrate differentiated regions.
- **Legal floor:** *Bommai* prevents central strength from becoming unlimited destruction of State autonomy.
- **Reform:** Strong national capacity must operate through consultation, reasoned decisions, predictable finance and judicially reviewable limits.
