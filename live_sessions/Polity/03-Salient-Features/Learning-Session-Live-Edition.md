# Salient Features of the Indian Constitution — Complete Live-Session Edition

The Constitution is not a bag containing seventeen unrelated facts. It is a designed
system. It creates power, divides it, makes it accountable, protects liberty, directs
social change and preserves enough flexibility to survive new conditions.

*The same Constitution that gives government power also tells government where that

### 1. Begin with an ordinary problem

Imagine a large housing society. It needs a committee to collect money, maintain common
areas and resolve disputes. If the committee has no authority, nothing gets done. If it
has unlimited authority, it may collect arbitrary charges or punish critics.

A political community faces the same double problem on a much larger scale:

1. public power must be created so collective decisions can be made; and
2. public power must be limited so rulers do not become arbitrary.

The analogy establishes the need for authority and restraint. Its limit is equally
important: a State is not a voluntary housing society. It possesses coercive legal power,
governs a territory and claims authority over all persons within its jurisdiction.

### 2. Constitution and constitutionalism

A **constitution** is the fundamental framework that:

- identifies the political community;
- creates the legislature, executive and judiciary;
- allocates powers among them;
- distributes authority between Union and States;
- protects rights;
- declares public goals; and
- provides rules for peaceful change.

**Constitutionalism** is the further principle that government must actually remain
limited, accountable and governed by law.

| Constitution | Constitutionalism |
|---|---|
| Creates and organises public power | Prevents that power from becoming arbitrary |
| May exist as a formal document | Requires effective limits and institutions |
| Answers “who may do what?” | Asks “is power lawfully and accountably exercised?” |
| Can exist even in an authoritarian State | Cannot coexist with effectively unlimited rule |

> **Decisive distinction:** a country may possess a constitution without practising
> constitutionalism.

### 3. What does “State” mean here?

In political theory, a **State** is not merely a government department or a particular
elected ministry. It is the organised political community combining:

```text
Population + definite territory + government + sovereignty
```

The government changes after elections; the State continues. The Constitution structures
the State and regulates governments acting in its name.

This distinction matters because UPSC often uses the word “State” in three different
senses:

| Use | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Political theory | The complete organised political community |
| Indian federalism | A constituent State such as Kerala or Rajasthan |
| Fundamental Rights, Article 12 | Constitutionally defined public authorities against which many rights operate |

Treating these three meanings as interchangeable causes avoidable errors.

### 4. The chief purpose: constitute and limit power

A Constitution does more than create offices. An ordinary statute can create an agency.
The chief constitutional task is deeper:

```text
Authority is created
        ↓
Its field and procedure are defined
        ↓
Competing organs are connected and checked
        ↓
Rights place some interests beyond ordinary political convenience
        ↓
Courts and accountability institutions enforce limits
```

Therefore, the best short formulation is:

> A Constitution constitutes public power and simultaneously limits its exercise.

It also expresses values and social goals, but these functions operate through the prior
architecture of authority and restraint.

### 5. Rule of law

**Rule of law** means that public power is exercised through law rather than personal
will. Its central elements include:

1. limitation of governmental power;
2. equality before law;
3. protection of liberty and civil rights;
4. known procedures and reasons;
5. independent adjudication; and
6. remedies against unlawful action.

Rule by law is not automatically rule of law. A ruler may use formally enacted laws as
tools of domination. Rule of law additionally asks whether power is limited, general,
reviewable and compatible with constitutional rights.

### 6. Law and liberty are not simple enemies

It is tempting to say, “More law means less liberty.” That is incomplete.

```text
No law or enforcement
        ↓
The strong may dominate the weak
        ↓
Formal freedom becomes insecurity

General, rights-respecting law
        ↓
Predictability + protection from violence and arbitrariness
        ↓
Ordered liberty becomes possible
```

Traffic rules restrict how one drives, but they make safe movement possible. The example
shows how law may secure liberty. Its limit is that every law does not enlarge freedom:
vague, discriminatory or disproportionate laws can destroy liberty. The constitutional
question is therefore not “law or liberty?” but “what kind of law, made and reviewed
under what limits?”

### 7. Indian constitutionalism in one map

| Constitutional need | Indian mechanism |
|---|---|
| Popular authority | Elections and representative institutions |
| Limited government | Written and supreme Constitution |
| Rights | Part III and constitutional remedies |
| Accountable executive | Parliamentary responsibility |
| Territorial restraint | Federal distribution of competence |
| Legal control | Judicial review |
| Ethical direction | Preamble, DPSPs and Fundamental Duties |
| Controlled change | Differentiated amendment procedures and basic structure |

No single item proves constitutionalism. Elections without rights may produce
majoritarian domination; courts without institutional restraint may overreach; rights
without remedies may remain promises. The design works through interaction.

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A long constitutional document cannot prevent political actors from
misusing power. Text is powerless without constitutional culture.

**Reply:** The objection correctly identifies the implementation gap, but it does not
make constitutional design irrelevant. Text creates enforceable competences, rights,
procedures and remedies around which institutions and citizens can organise resistance.
Constitutional culture needs a legal structure; legal structure needs constitutional
culture.

**Balanced assessment:** constitutionalism is neither text alone nor goodwill alone. It
is law made effective through institutions, conventions, public reason and civic
vigilance.

### 10. Revision notes

1. A Constitution both creates and limits public power.
2. Constitutionalism means effective limited government.
3. A constitutional document does not by itself guarantee constitutionalism.
4. The political State combines population, territory, government and sovereignty.
5. Government changes; the State continues.
6. Rule of law is broader than mere rule through enacted law.
7. Rule of law includes limited power, equality, liberty and remedies.
8. General and rights-respecting law can make liberty possible.
9. Arbitrary law can destroy liberty; “more law” is not the correct test.
10. India combines electoral, parliamentary, federal, rights-based and judicial controls.
11. Constitutional design and constitutional culture depend on each other.
12. The safest thesis is “constitute power, distribute power, limit power.”

### Concept check

**Question:** A country has a detailed written constitution and regular elections, but the ruling majority can detain critics without review and ignore court orders. Does it possess constitutionalism?

**Model answer:** It possesses a constitutional document and electoral institutions, but not effective constitutionalism. Constitutionalism requires limited and reviewable power, protection of liberty, institutional accountability and compliance with lawful remedies. Elections alone cannot convert arbitrary authority into limited government.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate the existence of a written constitution or elections with constitutionalism; the decisive test is whether power is effectively limited and accountable.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original 10-mark prompt — 150 words:** “A Constitution creates the government, while
constitutionalism prevents the government it creates from becoming arbitrary.” Explain
with reference to India.

**Model (within 150 words):**

A Constitution performs a double function: it constitutes public authority and limits
its exercise. In India, Parts V and VI create Union and State institutions, while the
Seventh Schedule distributes territorial competence. Constitutionalism supplies the
restraining side of this arrangement.

Fundamental Rights limit legislative and executive power; Articles 32 and 226 provide
remedies; parliamentary responsibility subjects the executive to the Lok Sabha; and
judicial review tests ordinary law against the supreme Constitution. Federalism,
independent constitutional bodies and the basic-structure doctrine add further checks.

Yet formal text is insufficient. Emergency experience, misuse of institutions or
non-compliance with judgments can produce constitutionally authorised offices but
unconstitutional practice. Conversely, political conventions cannot reliably restrain
power without legal foundations.

Thus, the Constitution supplies authority and enforceable limits, while constitutional
morality, institutional self-restraint and civic vigilance convert those limits into
constitutionalism.

**Scoring guidance — 10 marks:** definition and thesis 2; Indian mechanisms 4; text–practice
qualification 2; integrated conclusion 2.

The next lesson asks why this framework became unusually detailed and drew from so many
constitutional traditions.

### 1. What does “lengthiest written Constitution” mean?

The Constitution adopted in 1949 contained a Preamble, **395 Articles in 22 Parts** and
**8 Schedules**. Later amendments added, omitted and renumbered provisions. Textbooks
commonly describe the contemporary document as containing **about 470 Articles in 25
Parts and 12 Schedules**, but an exact article total depends on how inserted and omitted
provisions are counted. Parts and Schedules are safer fixed reference points.

Four standard causes explain its detail:

| Cause | Why it enlarged the text |
|---|---|
| Geographical and social diversity | Different regions and communities required accommodation |
| Government of India Act, 1935 | A bulky administrative inheritance supplied detailed structures |
| One constitutional framework for Union and States | Many State-level institutions are also constitutionally organised |
| Dominance of legal luminaries in the Constituent Assembly | Legal expertise encouraged a comprehensive and carefully specified document |

The Constitution includes both governing principles and considerable administrative
detail. Matters left elsewhere to ordinary legislation or established political
conventions were often placed in the constitutional text itself. That gives legal
clarity, but also creates complexity.

The local eighth-edition textbook's dated snapshot further explains how the document
grew: by 2019, amendments had deleted **about 20 Articles and Part VII**, while adding
**about 95 Articles**, four Parts—**IV-A, IX-A, IX-B and XIV-A**—and four Schedules,
**the Ninth to the Twelfth**. These are approximate textbook counts because inserted
and omitted Articles may be counted differently.

### 2. Sources: the high-yield map

| Source | Major features associated with it |
|---|---|
| Government of India Act, 1935 | Federal scheme, Governors, judiciary, Public Service Commissions, emergency and administrative provisions |
| British constitutional practice | Parliamentary government, cabinet system, rule of law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, writs, privileges and bicameralism |
| United States | Fundamental Rights, judicial review, judicial independence, presidential impeachment, removal of higher-court judges and Vice-President |
| Ireland | DPSPs, Rajya Sabha nomination and method of Presidential election |
| Canada | Strong-Centre federation, Union residuary power and Centre-appointed Governors |
| Australia | Concurrent List, freedom of trade and joint sitting |
| Weimar Germany | Emergency suspension framework for rights |
| Former USSR | Fundamental Duties and the Preamble's social, economic and political justice ideal |
| France | Republic and liberty–equality–fraternity |
| South Africa | Elements of amendment procedure and Rajya Sabha election |
| Japan | “Procedure established by law” |

This table is important for Prelims, but it is not enough for Mains. A source does not
tell us how the borrowed device behaves after entering a different constitutional
environment.

**Attribution caution:** the permitted materials are not internally uniform on advisory
jurisdiction. The canonical source table associates it with Canada, while the local
eighth-edition book table also places it among institutional inheritances from the
Government of India Act, 1935. Do not use that single item as an unqualified
source-matching claim without checking the controlling edition or question.

### 3. Borrowed, adapted and rejected

| Foreign influence | Indian adaptation | What India did not accept |
|---|---|---|
| British parliamentary government | Written supreme Constitution, elected President, federal distribution and judicial review | Hereditary monarchy and Westminster parliamentary sovereignty |
| US rights and review | Express restrictions, constitutional writs, DPSP interaction and basic structure | Presidential executive, dual citizenship and rigid organ separation |
| Canadian strong-Centre federalism | Constitutionally protected States and differentiated regional arrangements | A purely administrative unitary system |
| Irish DPSPs | A large social-transformation programme fundamental in governance | Judicial enforceability identical to Fundamental Rights |
| South African amendment influence | Three Indian routes plus substantive basic-structure control | A single uniform amendment threshold |
| Japanese phrase in Article 21 | Indian courts later required procedure to be fair, just and reasonable | A permanently narrow, formal reading of procedure |

The Constitution-makers were selecting tools. They were not surrendering judgment.

### 4. Why the 1935 Act mattered so much

The Government of India Act, 1935 was the largest material and structural source. It
supplied familiar administrative machinery at a moment when the new State needed
continuity.

That continuity had two faces:

```text
Administrative continuity
        ├── prevented institutional vacuum
        └── supplied tested structures

Colonial inheritance
        ├── preserved centralising habits
        └── risked treating citizens as administered subjects
```

The correct assessment is neither “the Constitution copied the 1935 Act” nor “the Act
was insignificant.” Colonial machinery was retained, democratised and placed under
popular sovereignty, rights and judicial review, but some centralising and bureaucratic
tendencies survived.

### 5. The six major criticisms and their strongest replies

#### Criticism 1: a borrowed or patchwork Constitution

**Charge:** It contains nothing original and is merely a “borrowed Constitution,” “bag
of borrowings,” “hotch-potch” or “patchwork” assembled from foreign provisions.

**Strongest reply:** Ambedkar, speaking as Chairman of the Drafting Committee in the
Constituent Assembly, defended the comparative method. He described the Constitution as
having been framed after **“ransacking all the known Constitutions of the World.”** His
rebuttal was not that India had avoided borrowing, but that the fundamentals of written
constitutions were already widely known and the real novelty lay in variations made to
remove defects and fit Indian needs. He therefore treated the charge of a blind copy as
the product of inadequate study. Constitutional ideas are not patented: parliamentary
government without parliamentary sovereignty, federalism without a secession right,
and rights alongside DPSPs demonstrate adaptation rather than imitation.

**Residual:** Borrowing can still fail if a device's political preconditions are absent.
Institutional transplants must be judged in practice.

#### Criticism 2: a carbon copy of the Government of India Act, 1935

**Charge:** Much of the language and administrative structure continued colonial rule.

**Strongest reply:** Continuity prevented a governing vacuum. The new framework
transformed the source of authority: Crown rule became popular sovereignty; limited
franchise became universal adult franchise; executive power became constitutionally
accountable; and rights became judicially enforceable.

**Residual:** Administrative centralisation and official discretion remained powerful,
so decolonisation of governance could not be completed by constitutional text alone.

#### Criticism 3: un-Indian or anti-Indian

**Charge:** The document reflected Western legalism rather than Indian political
traditions.

**Strongest reply:** India was not culturally uniform and no single historical tradition
could represent all citizens. The Constitution also embedded Indian necessities:
abolition of untouchability, affirmative safeguards, linguistic accommodation, village
panchayats as a directive and universal citizenship.

**Residual:** The gap between formal legal institutions and local democratic
participation remains a legitimate concern.

#### Criticism 4: un-Gandhian

**Charge:** It built a centralised parliamentary State instead of a bottom-up federation
of self-sufficient villages.

**Strongest reply:** The framers faced Partition, social hierarchy, economic
underdevelopment and the need to protect individual rights against local domination.
A capable Union and enforceable rights were not dispensable.

**Residual:** The later 73rd and 74th Amendments acknowledge that democratic power also
requires meaningful local self-government.

#### Criticism 5: elephantine size

**Charge:** Excessive detail makes the Constitution cumbersome.

**Strongest reply:** Detail accommodates diversity, defines institutions and reduces
dependence on unwritten conventions in a new democracy. Different amendment routes
prevent every detail from becoming equally rigid.

**Residual:** Excessive constitutionalisation may shift ordinary policy disputes into
litigation and make the text difficult for citizens.

#### Criticism 6: a lawyer's paradise

**Charge:** Legal language and numerous justiciable provisions encourage litigation.

**Strongest reply:** Courts and remedies are necessary where public power is limited and
rights are enforceable. Legal precision can protect citizens from discretion.

**Residual:** Access to constitutional justice is unequal, delay is costly and judicial
resolution cannot replace democratic administration.

### 6. The 42nd Amendment and constitutional change

Several amendments reshaped the original design, including the **7th, 42nd, 44th, 73rd,
74th, 97th and 101st Amendments**. The **42nd Amendment Act, 1976** is commonly called the
“Mini-Constitution” because of the breadth of its changes.

That nickname does not imply that Parliament may replace the constitutional order. The
basic-structure doctrine protects the Constitution's identity—a problem taken up in the
next lesson.

2. Contemporary article totals should be described approximately and with a counting caveat.
3. India now has 25 Parts and 12 Schedules.
4. Diversity, colonial inheritance, a common Union–State framework and dominance of legal luminaries explain length.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is identifying the foreign source of a constitutional provision insufficient for explaining the Indian constitutional design?

**Model answer:** A source establishes ancestry, not present operation. Once borrowed, a provision works within India's written supremacy, parliamentary responsibility, federal distribution, rights, DPSPs and judicial review. India also modified or rejected associated foreign features. A complete explanation must therefore show source, adaptation, interaction and limitation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat “borrowed from” as “copied unchanged”; constitutional devices acquire a different meaning within a different institutional system.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original 15-mark prompt — 250 words:** “The charge that the Indian Constitution is a
bag of borrowings mistakes adaptation for imitation.” Critically examine.

**Model (within 250 words):**

The Constitution drew extensively from the Government of India Act, 1935 and from
British, American, Irish, Canadian, Australian and other constitutional experiences.
This breadth invited the charge of a borrowed or patchwork document.

Ambedkar, as Drafting Committee Chairman answering this charge in the Constituent
Assembly, openly acknowledged comparative borrowing—including the search through known
constitutions—but argued that novelty lay in adapting settled constitutional devices to
remove defects and meet Indian needs.

The charge is incomplete because the framers did not transplant entire systems. India
adopted parliamentary government from Britain but rejected hereditary monarchy and
parliamentary sovereignty, placing the cabinet under a written supreme Constitution.
It drew Fundamental Rights and judicial review from the United States but rejected a
presidential executive, rigid separation and dual citizenship. Canadian centralising
federal features were combined with constitutionally protected States; Irish directives
were expanded into a programme of social transformation.

The Government of India Act supplied much administrative machinery. Yet popular
sovereignty, universal franchise, enforceable rights, republican government and
responsible institutions changed the source and purpose of that machinery.

The criticism nevertheless retains value. Colonial centralisation, legal complexity and
institutions transplanted without supportive political conventions can weaken democratic
operation. Borrowing succeeds only when constitutional culture and administration adapt
with the text.

Thus, the Constitution was not original because every component was unprecedented. Its
originality lay in deliberate selection, rejection and synthesis for India's diversity,
democracy and transformative ambitions.

**Scoring guidance — 15 marks:** framing of criticism 2; named source evidence 3;
three developed adaptations 5; residual limitations 3; qualified conclusion 2.

Borrowing produced a detailed design. The next question is how that design can be
changed without allowing a temporary majority to destroy it.

## Lesson 3 — How can the Constitution change without losing identity?

Progress: 3/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Rigidity, flexibility, amendment routes and the basic-structure boundary

### 🖼️ Visual: three gates of constitutional change

```text
PROPOSED CHANGE
      │
      ├── Gate 1: SIMPLE MAJORITY, outside Article 368
      │      examples: State reorganisation, legislative councils
      │
      ├── Gate 2: ARTICLE 368 SPECIAL MAJORITY
      │      majority of total membership
      │      + two-thirds present and voting
      │
      └── Gate 3: SPECIAL MAJORITY + HALF-STATE RATIFICATION
             protects specified federal provisions

ALL ROUTES REMAIN UNDER THE CONSTITUTION
Article 368 amendment ──► cannot damage basic structure
```

*India grades the difficulty of change according to the constitutional importance of
the provision.*

### 1. Why neither complete rigidity nor complete flexibility works

A completely rigid Constitution may become unable to respond to social change. A
completely flexible Constitution may let an ordinary governing majority rewrite the
rules that brought it to power.

India adopts a mixed design:

| Risk | Constitutional response |
|---|---|
| Constitutional paralysis | Some changes use ordinary legislative majorities |
| Casual alteration of important rules | Most changes require a special majority |
| Union alteration of the federal bargain | Specified matters also require State ratification |
| Destruction through a formally valid amendment | Basic-structure review |

The mechanism matches entrenchment to importance. Flexibility is preserved, but not all
rules are equally exposed.

### 2. Gate 1: simple-majority changes outside Article 368

Some Constitution-affecting changes are made like ordinary legislation and are expressly
treated as outside Article 368. Standard examples include:

- admission or establishment of new States and alteration of State areas, boundaries or
  names under Articles 2–4;
- citizenship legislation under Article 11;
- creation or abolition of State Legislative Councils under Article 169; and
- specified changes relating to the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.

Calling these “amendments by simple majority” is convenient shorthand. The precise point
is that they alter constitutional arrangements through constitutionally authorised
ordinary legislation rather than through Article 368.

### 3. Gate 2: Article 368 special majority

For most constitutional amendments, each House must separately pass the Bill by:

```text
majority of the total membership of that House
                         AND
two-thirds of members present and voting
```

These are two simultaneous requirements. A large majority among a thin attendance may
fail the total-membership limb. A bare total-membership majority may fail the two-thirds
present-and-voting limb.

Further controls:

- the Bill may begin in either House;
- it may be introduced by a minister or private member;
- States cannot initiate an Article 368 Bill;
- there is no joint sitting to break a deadlock; and
- after valid passage, the President shall assent.

### 4. Gate 3: State ratification for the federal core

An additional simple-majority ratification by **not less than half of the State
legislatures** is required for specified federal matters, including:

- election of the President;
- extent of Union and State executive power;
- the Supreme Court and High Courts;
- distribution of legislative powers and the Seventh Schedule;
- representation of States in Parliament;
- Article 279A concerning the GST Council; and
- Article 368 itself.

This is not a general State veto over every amendment. Nor do three-fourths of States
have to ratify, as in the United States.

### 5. Why procedure alone is insufficient

Suppose a government secures the necessary numbers and passes an amendment abolishing
free elections or destroying judicial review. Would numerical compliance make the
change constitutional?

The Supreme Court's answer in **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)** was that
Parliament possesses wide amending power, but cannot alter the Constitution's **basic
structure**.

```text
Power to amend
      ≠
Power to destroy constitutional identity
```

The doctrine does not freeze the text. It distinguishes constitutional change from
constitutional replacement through a constituted organ.

### 6. What belongs to the basic structure?

The list is illustrative and case-developed, not a closed article-wise catalogue.
Recognised elements include:

- supremacy of the Constitution;
- republican and democratic government;
- secularism;
- federalism;
- separation of powers;
- judicial review;
- rule of law;
- free and fair elections;
- dignity and liberty;
- harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs; and
- limited amending power.

Detailed case chronology belongs to the amendment topic. Here the necessary integrating
point is that the mixed amendment procedure protects stability procedurally, while basic
structure protects constitutional identity substantively.

### 7. Strongest criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Basic structure gives unelected judges an undefined veto over
constitutional change and is not expressly written in Article 368.

**Reply:** Article 368 gives amendment power to an institution created by the
Constitution; it does not logically authorise that institution to destroy the source of
its power. Judicial review protects the distinction between amendment and replacement.

**Residual:** Because the list is open-ended, courts must identify basic features through
text, structure, precedent and reasoned necessity, not personal preference.

### 8. Parts and amendment traps

- **Part IX-A** concerns Municipalities.

### Concept check

**Question:** Parliament passes an Article 368 amendment by the required special majority abolishing judicial review, but no State ratification is constitutionally required for the provision changed. Is procedural compliance sufficient?

**Model answer:** No. Procedural validity does not exhaust constitutional validity. Judicial review is recognised as part of the basic structure, so even an amendment passed through the correct parliamentary procedure can be invalid if it destroys that structural feature. State ratification and basic-structure review answer different questions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that satisfying the correct majority automatically makes every constitutional amendment valid; procedure and substantive basic-structure limits are separate.

**Model (within 150 words):**

India avoids both constitutional paralysis and ordinary-majority supremacy through
differentiated amendment.

Specified changes, such as State reorganisation, occur through constitutionally
authorised ordinary legislation outside Article 368. Most provisions require each House
to pass an amendment by a majority of its total membership and two-thirds of members
present and voting. Changes affecting the federal compact additionally require
ratification by not less than half the State legislatures. No joint sitting can override
one House, and the President must assent after valid passage.

Procedure, however, is not the final limit. In *Kesavananda Bharati*, the Supreme Court
held that Parliament may amend every part but cannot damage the Constitution's basic
structure. This preserves constitutional supremacy, democracy, federalism and judicial
review against formally valid destruction.

The system is therefore flexible in institutional detail, entrenched where federal
consent is necessary and substantively rigid at the level of constitutional identity.

### 🖼️ Visual: India's federal–unitary dial


*India distributes power federally but equips the Union with unusual coordinating and
crisis capacity.*

### 1. Begin with the unity–diversity problem

India is too large and diverse for every public decision to be made at one national
centre. Yet at independence, Partition, integration of princely States, economic
coordination and security concerns made a weak Union dangerous.

The Constitution therefore refused both extremes:

```text
Pure centralisation                    Loose compact
States become administrators          Union cannot coordinate common action
                  \                    /
                   \                  /
                    ▼                ▼
              FEDERAL DISTRIBUTION
                 WITH A UNION TILT
```

### 2. Federal features

A federal system does not merely decentralise by political choice. It constitutionally
protects more than one level of government.

India contains the standard federal features:

1. **dual polity**—Union and State governments;
2. **written Constitution**;
3. **constitutional division of powers**;
4. **constitutional supremacy**;
5. a degree of **rigidity**, especially for federal provisions;
6. an **independent judiciary** to adjudicate competence disputes; and
7. **bicameralism** at the Union level.

These features matter together. A second level without protected competence would be
administrative decentralisation, not federalism.

### 3. Why Article 1 says “Union of States”

Article 1 describes India as a **“Union of States”**. The Constitution does not use the
word “Federation.”

The standard implications are:

- the Indian Union did not arise from an agreement among sovereign States; and
- no State possesses a constitutional right to secede.

But “Union” does not mean “unitary.” The same Constitution distributes legislative and
executive authority and supplies judicial remedies when boundaries are crossed.

> **Safe conclusion:** Article 1 denies compact-based secession; it does not erase
> federalism.

### 4. The Union tilt

The Union side is strengthened through:

- residuary legislative power in Parliament;
- Parliament's ability, under specified conditions, to legislate on State subjects;
- Union implementation of international obligations;
- Parliament's power to alter State boundaries under Article 3;
- centrally appointed Governors;
- All-India Services;
- integrated judicial, audit and election arrangements;
- unequal State representation in the Rajya Sabha;
- single citizenship; and
- emergency provisions.

These do not operate with equal intensity at all times. Some are permanent coordinating
features; others are exceptional powers triggered by constitutional conditions.

### 5. Scholarly labels are arguments, not constitutional text

| Description | What it emphasises | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| K.C. Wheare: “quasi-federal” | Strong central features | Can understate protected State competence |
| Morris Jones: “bargaining federalism” | Negotiation in practice | Does not itself define legal distribution |
| Granville Austin: “co-operative federalism” | Joint nation-building | Cooperation may coexist with coercion |
| Ivor Jennings: federation with a centralising tendency | Union-weighted design | Centralising tendency is not unlimited supremacy |
| *Sui generis* | Indian distinctiveness | Should not replace precise feature analysis |

The label should follow the evidence. It should not substitute for it.

### 6. Single Constitution and single citizenship

One Constitution governs the Union and the States. Jammu and Kashmir's former
own-Constitution exception ended in 2019 when the Indian Constitution was applied fully.
India also provides one national citizenship rather than separate Union and State
citizenship.

Single citizenship supports:

- common political membership;
- mobility and national integration; and
- equal civil status across States.

It does **not** eliminate every residence or local qualification. Parliament may prescribe
residence requirements for specified public employment under Article 16(3), and special
regional or tribal protections may validly operate. Such arrangements are differentiated
protection, not separate State citizenship.

### 7. Asymmetry is a method of integration

Federal equality need not require identical arrangements everywhere. Fifth and Sixth
Schedule areas, Articles 371–371J and Union Territories show **asymmetric federalism**:
different constitutional arrangements are used to accommodate different historical,
social and security conditions.

The mechanism is:

```text
Different regional condition
        ↓
Differentiated constitutional arrangement
        ↓
Participation within the Union
        ↓
Integration without complete institutional uniformity
```

Its risk is unequal autonomy or excessive central discretion. Its defence is that
uniform rules can themselves become unjust where circumstances are deeply unequal.

### 8. A current constitutional linkage: Jammu and Kashmir

Until 2019, Jammu and Kashmir operated within an asymmetric arrangement associated with
Article 370 and possessed its own Constitution. The 2019 constitutional measures applied
the Indian Constitution fully, and the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019
reorganised the former State.

**Official status checked on 30 September 2026:**

- Section 4 of the current India Code text forms the **Union Territory of Jammu and
  Kashmir with a Legislature**.
- The Supreme Court's December 2023 Article 370 judgment upheld the 2019 constitutional
  measures and recorded the Union's assurance concerning restoration of statehood.
- The Ministry of Home Affairs material and Gazette notifications located in the
  immediate check did not contain a notification restoring statehood. The safe dated

This linkage illustrates three ideas at once: asymmetry can change, Parliament's
territorial power is strong, and legal status must be reported by distinguishing a
political assurance from an enacted or notified change.

### 9. Emergency centralisation: a constitutional mode, not abolition

The Constitution recognises:

- National Emergency under Article 352;
- President's Rule under Article 356, with Article 365 relevant to specified Union
  directions; and
- Financial Emergency under Article 360.

During an emergency, Union authority may expand substantially. Yet this occurs through
constitutional procedures and is subject to time limits, parliamentary control and
judicial review. The federal Constitution is not formally dissolved.

The analogy of a “federal–unitary switch” is useful only to show intensified
centralisation. Its limit is that safeguards, courts, States and constitutional rules do
not simply disappear.

### 10. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The Union tilt, Governors, Article 356 and fiscal dependence make State
autonomy nominal.

**Reply:** States possess constitutionally assigned fields, elected governments and
judicial protection. In *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994), federalism was treated as
a basic feature and President's Rule became reviewable. Union strength therefore exists
inside a constitutional federal floor.

**Residual:** Formal competence does not guarantee practical equality. Fiscal imbalance,
partisan gubernatorial conduct and central legislation can still produce coercive
federalism.

**Model answer:** Because federalism depends on constitutionally distributed authority, not equal strength. **Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer that every strong-centre federation is unitary; the decisive question is whether regional authority is constitutionally protected.

The federation is nevertheless Union-weighted. Parliament holds residuary power, may

## Lesson 5 — Why does India reject strict separation of powers?

Progress: 5/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Parliamentary government, functional differentiation, overlap and checks

### 🖼️ Visual: differentiation without isolation

```text
                        CONSTITUTION
                             │
          ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
          ▼                  ▼                  ▼
      LEGISLATURE        EXECUTIVE          JUDICIARY
       makes law        implements law      interprets law
          │                  │                  │
          ├──── confidence ──┘                  │
          ├──── scrutiny and finance ──────────┤
          ├──── constitutional amendment ──────┤
          └────────── judicial review ◄────────┘

CORE FUNCTIONS DIFFERENTIATED
POWERS DELIBERATELY OVERLAP
EACH ORGAN CHECKS DOMINATION BY THE OTHERS
```

*India separates core functions but does not place the three organs in airtight
compartments.*

### 1. The parliamentary starting point

The parliamentary system is also called responsible, cabinet or Westminster
government. Its core test is simple:

> The real executive is drawn from the legislature and remains in office only while it
> retains the confidence of the directly elected House.

At the Union:

- the President is the constitutional head;
- the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers form the real political executive; and
- the Council is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3).

At the State level, the Governor, Chief Minister and Council of Ministers operate through
the corresponding constitutional structure.

### 2. Main parliamentary features

| Feature | How it operates |
|---|---|
| Nominal and real executives | Formal acts occur in the constitutional head's name; political direction comes from the ministry |
| Majority-party or coalition government | Lok Sabha confidence determines survival |
| Collective responsibility | The ministry stands or falls together |
| Legislative membership | Ministers normally sit and participate in Parliament |
| Prime-ministerial leadership | The PM coordinates cabinet, majority and executive |
| Dissolution | The lower House may be dissolved under constitutional practice |
| Political solidarity and secrecy | Cabinet presents a common public position |

India borrowed the cabinet model from Britain, but did not borrow British parliamentary
sovereignty. The Indian cabinet operates under a written supreme Constitution, federal
division, rights and judicial review.

### 3. Why India chose responsibility over rigid stability

A presidential executive enjoys a fixed tenure and institutional separation from the
legislature. The parliamentary executive trades some stability for daily political
responsibility.

```text
PRESIDENTIAL PRIORITY                 PARLIAMENTARY PRIORITY
fixed tenure                          continuing confidence
separate electoral mandate           legislative accountability
institutional stability              removable political responsibility
```

Ambedkar's constitutional reasoning preferred greater responsibility to greater
stability. In a diverse and newly independent democracy, an executive answerable to the
representative House offered a familiar and politically responsive model.

### 4. Separation of powers: three possible meanings

The phrase can mean:

1. **institutional separation**—different bodies exist;
2. **functional differentiation**—each has a primary role; or
3. **strict isolation**—no overlap in personnel or function.

India accepts the first two but rejects the third.

Constitutional indicators of differentiation include:

- Article 50: separation of judiciary from executive in the public services of the
  State;
- Articles 121 and 211: legislative discussion of judicial conduct is restricted except
  through removal procedure;
- Articles 122 and 212: courts do not inquire into legislative proceedings merely for
  procedural irregularity; and
- Article 361: specified immunity for the President and Governors.

Article 50 is a DPSP, not a directly enforceable individual right.

### 5. Deliberate overlaps

| Overlap | Why it exists | Constitutional risk |
|---|---|---|
| Ministers sit in Parliament | Responsible government | Majority may dominate scrutiny |
| Ordinances under Articles 123/213 | Urgent temporary law-making | Executive may bypass deliberation |
| Delegated legislation | Technical and administrative detail | Excessive delegation |
| Courts frame procedural rules and punish contempt | Protect adjudicatory function | Institutional self-expansion |
| Parliament exercises constituent power | Democratic constitutional change | Majoritarian constitutional capture |

Overlap is not automatically a violation. The question is whether it serves a
constitutional function and remains checked.

### 6. Checks and balances

```text
EXECUTIVE
   │ answers to confidence, questions, committees and finance
   ▼
LEGISLATURE
   │ makes laws and amendments, but within constitutional limits
   ▼
JUDICIARY
   │ reviews legality and constitutionality, but acts within judicial role
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                                                                   ▼
                                                CONSTITUTIONAL BALANCE
```

Examples include:

- collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha;
- legislative control over taxation and expenditure;
- judicial review under Articles 13, 32 and 226;
- impeachment or removal procedures;
- amendment under Article 368 subject to basic structure; and
- judicial review of executive satisfaction where constitutional standards permit.

### 7. *Ram Jawaya* and the Indian position

In *Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955), the Supreme Court explained that the
Indian Constitution does not recognise separation in absolute rigidity. Functions are
sufficiently differentiated, but the executive is constitutionally connected with the
legislature.

Later basic-structure jurisprudence protects separation of powers against the domination
of one organ. In **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**, the Court established
the basic-structure limitation within which constitutional power operates. In **Indira
Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)**, it applied structural control where constituent
power sought to place the Prime Minister's election beyond ordinary constitutional
adjudication. Together, the cases show that overlap cannot become institutional
supremacy. India rejects isolation but not structural limits.

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Legislative–executive fusion, party majorities and the whip convert
responsible government into cabinet or prime-ministerial domination.

**Reply:** Fusion also makes the executive continuously answerable to the House through
confidence, finance, questions, debate and committees. Judicial review and federal
constraints add external checks.

**Residual:** When the governing party controls a disciplined majority, formal
legislative responsibility can become weak. The quality of committees, internal party
democracy, parliamentary time and respect for deliberation therefore matter.

fusion creates continuing political accountability, unlike a separately elected
fixed-tenure presidential executive.

The Constitution nevertheless differentiates core functions. Articles 121 and 211
protect judicial conduct from ordinary legislative debate; Articles 122 and 212 protect
legislative proceedings from review for mere procedural irregularity; Article 50 directs
separation of judiciary and executive; and Article 361 supplies specified executive
immunity.

Overlap is deliberate. The executive promulgates ordinances and makes delegated
legislation; Parliament exercises constituent power; courts frame rules and punish
contempt. Such overlap is legitimate when functionally necessary and constitutionally
bounded.

Checks prevent overlap from becoming supremacy. The Council of Ministers depends on
Lok Sabha confidence and legislative finance. Courts review law and executive action
under Articles 13, 32 and 226. Parliament may amend the Constitution, but not its basic
structure. *Ram Jawaya* accordingly rejected rigid separation. *Kesavananda Bharati*
established the basic-structure limit, and *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* applied
structural control where an amendment sought to insulate a high constitutional office
from ordinary adjudicatory limits.

The principal weakness is majority control: party discipline can reduce legislative
scrutiny. India therefore needs effective committees, deliberation and judicial
self-restraint. Its model is functional differentiation, accountable overlap and mutual
checks—not watertight separation.

## Lesson 6 — Who is supreme: Parliament, courts or the Constitution?

Progress: 6/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Constitutional supremacy, judicial review and an integrated independent judiciary

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Book context: Checked — parliamentary–judicial synthesis and judicial structure

CA search: "Not run: constitutional supremacy and judicial design are static doctrine"

CA found: None used

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Visual: the constitutional ceiling

```text
                    CONSTITUTION
              supreme source and limit
                /                 \
               /                   \
              ▼                     ▼
        PARLIAMENT               JUDICIARY
 makes ordinary law         interprets and reviews
 amends Constitution        protects rights/structure
              \                     /
               \                   /
                └── neither may cross ──┘
                 constitutional competence
```

*Parliament and courts are powerful because the Constitution empowers them; neither is
legally sovereign over the Constitution.*

### 1. Three different models

| Model | Ultimate legal principle |
|---|---|
| Traditional British model | Parliamentary sovereignty |
| United States model | Written constitutional supremacy with strong judicial review |
| Indian model | Written constitutional supremacy, parliamentary government, judicial review and limited constituent power |

Calling India a combination of “parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy” is
misleading. Indian Parliament is not legally sovereign in the Westminster sense, and
Indian courts do not possess an unlimited authority to govern.

### 2. Parliament's authority

Parliament:

- legislates within Union and Concurrent competence;
- controls public finance;
- holds the political executive accountable;
- participates in removal and electoral processes; and
- exercises constituent power under Article 368.

But ordinary law must conform to the Constitution. Federal distribution, Fundamental
Rights and other constitutional limits remain reviewable. Even constituent power is
limited by basic structure.

### 3. Judicial review

Judicial review allows constitutional courts to test whether public action is:

- within legislative or executive competence;
- consistent with Fundamental Rights;
- procedurally lawful; and
- compatible with constitutional structural limits.

Important textual anchors include Articles **13, 32 and 226**. Review preserves the
supremacy of the Constitution; it is not a personal veto exercised by judges.

### 4. “Procedure established by law” after *Maneka Gandhi*

Article 21 uses the phrase **“procedure established by law,”** associated with Japan.
An older comparison treated this as much narrower than American due process.

After *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978), a procedure depriving life or personal
liberty must be fair, just and reasonable rather than arbitrary.

```text
Text: procedure established by law
                  +
Articles 14, 19 and 21 read together
                  ↓
procedure must satisfy fairness and non-arbitrariness
```

India has not silently replaced the constitutional phrase with the US text. Judicial
interpretation has given the Indian guarantee a substantive fairness dimension.

### 5. Integrated judiciary

India has a single judicial hierarchy:

```text
Supreme Court
      ↓
High Courts
      ↓
District and subordinate courts
```

This system administers both Union and State laws. It differs from the classic US dual
court structure in which federal and State judicial systems possess separate
institutional lines.

Integration promotes:

- uniform constitutional interpretation;
- a common appellate hierarchy;
- enforcement of both Union and State law; and
- national rights protection.

Its limit is that administrative integration does not itself guarantee independence,
access or speed.

### 6. Independence safeguards

Judicial independence means freedom to decide according to law, not freedom from every
form of accountability.

Safeguards include:

- constitutional tenure and removal procedures;
- protected service conditions;
- expenditure charged on the relevant Consolidated Fund;
- limits on legislative discussion of judicial conduct;
- contempt power;
- institutional control over adjudication; and
- constitutional separation of judiciary from executive.

These safeguards serve decisional independence. They do not place judges beyond reasoned
criticism, ethics or constitutionally designed accountability.

### 7. Post-retirement practice: state the rule precisely

A blanket statement that every retired higher-court judge is prohibited from all legal
or public work is incorrect.

- Article 124(7) bars a retired Supreme Court judge from pleading or acting before any
  court or authority within India.
- Article 220 permits a retired permanent High Court judge to practise only before the
  Supreme Court and other High Courts, not before the court in which that judge served
  or subordinate courts within the prohibition.

These provisions concern legal practice. They do not amount to a general constitutional
ban on every post-retirement public assignment.

### 8. Strongest objections and replies

#### Objection to parliamentary limitation

**Claim:** Judicial review lets judges frustrate the democratic majority.

**Reply:** Constitutional democracy is not identical to temporary majority rule.
Parliament derives authority from a Constitution that also protects rights, federalism
and review.

#### Objection to judicial power

**Claim:** An expansive judiciary may enter policy fields and become unaccountable.

**Reply:** Courts need sufficient remedial power to make rights and structural limits
effective.

**Residual:** Review must remain grounded in text, structure, precedent, evidence,
institutional competence and proportionate remedy. Judicial supremacy would contradict
the same constitutionalism that review protects.

### 9. UPSC application

**Common traps**

- India does not follow British parliamentary sovereignty.
- Courts are not above the Constitution.
- Article 21 still says “procedure established by law.”
- *Maneka Gandhi* requires fair, just and reasonable procedure.
- Integrated judiciary and independent judiciary are different ideas.
- Post-retirement restrictions differ between Supreme Court and High Court judges.

**Answer use**

```text
Constitutional supremacy
        ↓
Parliamentary democratic authority
        +
Judicial review
        +
Limited amending power
        ↓
Neither parliamentary nor judicial absolutism
```

### 10. Revision notes

1. India's supreme legal authority is the Constitution.
2. Parliament is not sovereign in the British sense.
3. Courts do not possess unlimited judicial supremacy.
4. Judicial review enforces constitutional supremacy.
5. Articles 13, 32 and 226 are major review anchors.
6. Article 368 power is wide but limited by basic structure.
7. Article 21 retains the phrase “procedure established by law.”
8. *Maneka Gandhi* adds fairness, justice and reasonableness.
9. India has an integrated judicial hierarchy.
10. Integration does not automatically prove independence.
11. Independence safeguards protect impartial adjudication.
12. Judicial accountability remains constitutionally legitimate.
13. Post-retirement legal-practice restrictions must be stated separately.

### Concept check

**Question:** If the Supreme Court may invalidate an Act of Parliament, why is the Court not legally sovereign?

**Model answer:** The Court's power is derived from and limited by the Constitution. Judicial review enforces constitutional competence and rights; it does not authorise judges to replace constitutional text or govern merely because they prefer another policy. Parliament and the judiciary are both constituted and limited authorities under constitutional supremacy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not replace the false claim of parliamentary sovereignty with an equally false claim of unlimited judicial supremacy.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original 10-mark prompt — 150 words:** Explain why constitutional supremacy, rather
than parliamentary or judicial supremacy, best describes India.

**Model (within 150 words):**

India's Parliament possesses democratic law-making authority and constituent power, but
it is not sovereign in the Westminster sense. Ordinary legislation must comply with
Fundamental Rights, federal competence and other constitutional provisions. Article 368
amendments are additionally subject to the basic-structure limitation.

The Supreme Court and High Courts may invalidate unconstitutional action through
judicial review, notably under Articles 13, 32 and 226. This does not make courts
sovereign. Their jurisdiction, interpretive authority and remedies arise from the
Constitution and must remain bounded by text, structure, precedent and institutional
role.

The Indian synthesis therefore combines parliamentary government and democratic
legislation with judicially enforceable limits. Parliament cannot convert electoral
majority into unlimited legal power; courts cannot convert review into general policy
supremacy.

Both organs are authoritative within assigned functions, but the Constitution remains
the source, measure and limit of their authority.

**Scoring guidance — 10 marks:** Parliament's authority and limits 3; judicial review
and limits 3; basic-structure/textual anchors 2; precise constitutional-supremacy verdict 2.

Institutional power is now located and limited. The next lesson asks what substantive
kind of democracy this structure is designed to serve.

## Lesson 7 — What kind of democracy does the Constitution seek?

Progress: 7/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Rights, Directive Principles, Duties, secularism, republic and universal franchise

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Book context: Checked — Parts III, IV and IV-A, secular state and adult franchise

CA search: "Not run: Parts III, IV and IV-A are taught from settled constitutional text"

CA found: None used

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Visual: the democratic settlement

```text
                          WE, THE PEOPLE
                                │
                  UNIVERSAL ADULT FRANCHISE
                                │
                                ▼
                    DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC
                                │
          ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
          ▼                     ▼                     ▼
  FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS        DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES   FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES
  political democracy      social/economic goals  civic responsibility
  enforceable limits       welfare transformation non-justiciable norms
          └─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┘
                                ▼
          LIBERTY + EQUALITY + WELFARE + FRATERNITY
```

*The Constitution does not choose between liberty and social transformation; it places
them in a relationship that must be continually balanced.*

### 1. Fundamental Rights: political democracy

Part III contains six groups of Fundamental Rights:

1. equality, Articles 14–18;
2. freedom, Articles 19–22;
3. protection against exploitation, Articles 23–24;
4. freedom of religion, Articles 25–28;
5. cultural and educational rights, Articles 29–30; and
6. constitutional remedies, Article 32.

Rights limit legislative and executive power and protect the conditions of political
democracy. They are justiciable, but not absolute. The Constitution itself permits
defined restrictions, and courts test whether restrictions stay within constitutional
bounds.

The right to property ceased to be a Fundamental Right after the 44th Amendment and is
now a constitutional legal right under Article 300A.

### 2. Directive Principles: social and economic democracy

Part IV, Articles 36–51, directs the State towards a welfare order. The Principles are
non-justiciable, but Article 37 declares them fundamental in governance and makes their
application a duty of the State.

Their function is different from that of rights:

| Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|
| Limit and discipline State action | Direct positive State action |
| Primarily protect political and civil freedom | Pursue social and economic transformation |
| Judicially enforceable | Politically and constitutionally obligatory, but non-justiciable |
| Often framed as individual claims | Often require legislation, finance and administration |

The distinction does not make DPSPs legally worthless. They guide legislation and
interpretation, but cannot by themselves be enforced as if each were a Fundamental
Right.

### 3. Rights and directives: conflict to harmony

Early constitutional disputes often framed Part III and Part IV as competitors. Later
constitutional development emphasised harmony.

In *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980), the balance between Fundamental Rights and
Directive Principles was treated as part of the Constitution's basic structure.

```text
Rights without social capacity
        → formal liberty amid deprivation

Welfare without enforceable rights
        → benevolent claims vulnerable to coercion

Rights + directives
        → liberty-directed social transformation
```

The analogy of two wheels is useful because both are necessary. Its limit is that they
are not identical wheels: rights and directives differ in enforceability, language and
institutional route.

### 4. Fundamental Duties: citizenship is not only a claim

Part IV-A, Article 51A, contains **11 Fundamental Duties**. Ten were inserted by the
42nd Amendment, 1976; the eleventh was added by the 86th Amendment, 2002.

The Duties were inserted on the recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee**.
Article 51A addresses **citizens only**, and the Duties are **non-justiciable**.

They include duties to:

- respect the Constitution, National Flag and National Anthem;
- uphold sovereignty, unity and integrity;
- promote harmony;
- preserve composite culture;
- protect the environment;
- develop scientific temper;
- safeguard public property; and
- provide educational opportunity to children in the specified age group.

Duties are non-justiciable. Legislation may give effect to particular obligations, but
Article 51A is not a free-standing licence to curtail rights.

### 5. The complete 42nd Amendment Preamble trap

The **42nd Amendment Act, 1976** inserted all three expressions—**Socialist, Secular and
Integrity**—into the Preamble. “Secular” was not the only addition, and none of these
three words should be presented as part of the Preamble's original 1950 wording.

### 6. A secular State

India has no official State religion. “Secular” was added to the Preamble by the 42nd
Amendment, but secular commitments were already present in equality and religious
freedom provisions.

Relevant anchors include Articles 14–16, 25–30 and the Preamble.

The textbook contrast is:

| Strict wall model | Indian constitutional approach |
|---|---|
| State and religion institutionally separated as far as possible | State may engage with religion to protect freedom, equality and reform |
| Often described as negative secularism | Often described as equal respect or principled engagement |

The phrase **positive secularism** is useful but incomplete. State engagement may protect
minorities, open institutions to excluded groups or regulate secular activities. It may
also become selective, paternalistic or majoritarian.

> **Qualified formulation:** Indian secularism permits principled engagement with
> religion, but that engagement remains limited by equality, liberty, non-discrimination
> and institutional neutrality.

### 7. Communal representation and remedial representation

The Constitution rejected colonial separate electorates based on religion. This should
not be confused with constitutionally authorised reservations for Scheduled Castes and
Scheduled Tribes.

- separate electorates divide the electorate by communal identity;
- remedial reservation operates within a common electoral citizenship to address
  historical exclusion.

The distinction is purpose and mechanism, not merely the presence of group-sensitive
rules.

### 8. Universal adult franchise

The Constitution adopted universal adult franchise despite poverty, social inequality
and widespread illiteracy. This was not a gradual concession to property-owning groups;
it was a foundational commitment to political equality.

The voting age was reduced from **21 to 18** by the **61st Amendment Act, 1988**, with
the change operating for elections thereafter.

Universal franchise:

- makes democracy broad-based;
- affirms equal political worth;
- allows marginal groups to organise electorally; and
- makes government periodically answerable.

Its limit is that equal votes do not automatically create equal political influence.
Money, information, social hierarchy and access to institutions shape effective
participation.

### 9. Republic and democratic citizenship

A republic has a non-hereditary head of State. India combined a parliamentary executive
with an elected President, unlike Britain's hereditary monarch.

The President is not a separately elected political executive like the US President.
Republic describes the non-hereditary character and source of the office; parliamentary
government describes the executive–legislature relationship.

### 10. National symbols and a routed objective distinction

Respect for the National Flag appears among Fundamental Duties, but technical Flag Code
dimensions are statutory or executive-rule details rather than a “salient feature” of
constitutional structure.

**2023 Prelims Q84 — direct route:** exact official wording verified. It asked about a
claimed standard size of **600 mm × 400 mm** and the prescribed **3:2** ratio under the
Flag Code of India, 2002. The official key is not held locally, so no answer is disclosed
or inferred here. The question should be treated as a narrow factual application, not as
an invitation to expand this topic into a full law of national symbols.

### 11. Strongest objections and replies

**Objection to the rights–welfare settlement:** judicially enforceable civil rights may
privilege individuals with access to courts, while social needs remain non-justiciable.

**Reply:** DPSPs structure legislation, budgets and interpretation, while rights prevent
transformative policy from becoming arbitrary. The solution is effective welfare
governance, not weakening rights.

**Objection to Indian secularism:** State engagement with religion invites inconsistent
intervention and majoritarian capture.

**Reply:** Complete withdrawal may preserve entrenched inequality and leave minorities
unprotected. Engagement is legitimate only when anchored in equality, freedom,
non-discrimination and reasoned neutrality.

**Residual:** constitutional secularism is tested less by slogans than by even-handed
application and protection of individual as well as group rights.

### 12. UPSC application

**Prelims focus**

- six Fundamental Rights, not seven;
- Parts III, IV and IV-A;
- enforceability distinctions;
- 42nd, 44th, 61st and 86th Amendments;
- secularism present in the original scheme even though the Preamble word was added later;
- republic versus parliamentary government.

**Mains frame**

```text
Political democracy: rights + franchise + accountability
Social democracy: equality + dignity + anti-exclusion
Economic democracy: welfare-oriented directives
Civic dimension: duties
Qualified by: enforceability, resources and institutional fairness
```

### 13. Revision notes

1. Part III contains six groups of Fundamental Rights.
2. Rights are justiciable but not absolute.
3. Right to property is under Article 300A, not Part III.
4. Part IV contains non-justiciable DPSPs fundamental in governance.
5. *Minerva Mills* protects rights–directives balance.
6. The Swaran Singh Committee recommended adding Fundamental Duties.
7. Part IV-A, Article 51A contains 11 non-justiciable Duties addressed to citizens only.
8. Duties cannot automatically override rights.
9. The 42nd Amendment inserted Socialist, Secular and Integrity into the Preamble in 1976.
10. India has no official State religion.
11. Positive secularism must be qualified by equality and neutrality.
12. Separate electorates differ from remedial reservations.
13. Universal adult franchise affirmed equal political worth from the beginning.
14. Voting age fell from 21 to 18 through the 61st Amendment.
15. Republic means a non-hereditary head, not a presidential executive.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it incorrect to say that Directive Principles are constitutionally unimportant because courts cannot directly enforce them?

**Model answer:** Non-justiciability distinguishes their enforcement route, not their constitutional importance. Article 37 makes them fundamental in governance; they guide legislation, policy and interpretation and pursue social and economic democracy. They cannot ordinarily be claimed like Fundamental Rights, but the constitutional design seeks harmony between Parts III and IV.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate non-justiciability with optionality or treat DPSPs as judicially enforceable rights; both errors erase their distinct constitutional function.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original 15-mark prompt — 250 words:** The Indian Constitution combines political
liberty, social transformation and civic responsibility rather than choosing one over
the others. Examine.

**Model (within 250 words):**

The Constitution's democratic project is distributed across rights, directives,
franchise and duties.

Fundamental Rights secure political democracy by limiting arbitrary power, protecting
equality and liberty and providing remedies. Universal adult franchise gives each adult
citizen equal formal political worth and makes government electorally accountable.

Directive Principles pursue social and economic democracy. They direct the State towards
welfare, distributive justice and conditions in which formal liberty can become
meaningful. Though non-justiciable, Article 37 makes them fundamental in governance.
*Minerva Mills* accordingly treated harmony between Parts III and IV as structurally
important.

Fundamental Duties add civic responsibility: respect for constitutional institutions,
harmony, environmental protection and scientific temper. They remind citizens that
democracy depends on conduct as well as claims, but cannot become a general pretext to
override rights.

Secularism illustrates the synthesis. India protects conscience and minority
institutions while permitting principled State engagement for equality and reform.
Such engagement must remain even-handed to avoid majoritarian capture.

The design therefore rejects both rights without social capacity and welfare without
liberty. Its success depends on rights-respecting policy, effective administration,
equal political participation and responsible citizenship.

**Scoring guidance — 15 marks:** rights and franchise 3; DPSP mechanism 3; Duties 2;
secularism with limit 3; interaction and case anchor 2; qualified conclusion 2.

and fiscal mediation. The next lesson studies the constitutional bodies guarding those
functions.

## Lesson 8 — How are key democratic functions protected from ordinary politics?

Progress: 8/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Independent constitutional bodies and institution-specific safeguards

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Book context: Checked — Election Commission, CAG, Public Service Commissions and Finance Commission

CA search: "Not run: the bodies are taught from their settled constitutional design"

CA found: None used

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Visual: different functions require different shields

| Constitutional function | Body | Main danger | Relevant safeguard logic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electoral competition | Election Commission of India | Ruling-party manipulation | Constitutional status, tenure/removal protection and independent authority under Article 324 |
| Public audit | Comptroller and Auditor-General | Executive concealment or financial pressure | Tenure/removal protection, charged expenditure and reporting to legislatures |
| Merit recruitment/advice | UPSC and State PSCs | Patronage and arbitrary recruitment | Constitutional status, protected conditions and removal procedure |
| Periodic fiscal distribution | Finance Commission | Ad hoc Union–State bargaining | Constitutional constitution at intervals, defined reference and reasoned recommendations |

*Institutional independence is designed around the specific pressure each body faces.*

### 1. Why ordinary departments are not enough

An ordinary ministry is politically directed by the elected executive. That is normally
democratic. But some functions lose credibility if the incumbent government directly
controls every decision.

Examples:

- the government should not supervise its own election;
- a department should not be the final auditor of its own expenditure;
- political patronage should not determine every civil-service selection; and
- federal tax distribution needs a structured constitutional process.

The Constitution therefore establishes bodies outside the ordinary departmental chain.

### 2. Election Commission

Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of specified elections in
the Election Commission.

Its constitutional purpose is not to remove politics from elections—elections are
political—but to protect the fairness of the rules and administration under which
political competition occurs.

Independence must be assessed through:

- appointment design;
- security and conditions of tenure;
- removal rules;
- institutional control over election administration; and
- practical capacity.

The safeguard architecture is not identical for the Chief Election Commissioner and
every Election Commissioner. Avoid flattening distinct statutory and constitutional
rules into one formula.

### 3. Comptroller and Auditor-General

The CAG audits Union and State accounts and reports on the legality, regularity and, in
appropriate audit forms, propriety or performance of public expenditure.

The CAG does not personally “stop” expenditure or function as a criminal court.
Accountability works through a chain:

```text
Public money spent
      ↓
CAG audit and report
      ↓
Report laid before legislature
      ↓
Committee examination and executive response
      ↓
Political and administrative accountability
```

Independence is supported by removal protection comparable to that of a Supreme Court
judge, protected service conditions and expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund.
The body is powerful because it supplies authoritative information; consequences still
depend on legislatures and administration.

### 4. Union and State Public Service Commissions

UPSC and State PSCs support merit-based public recruitment and advise on specified
service matters.

Their independence reduces patronage, but their advice is not equivalent to a binding
judicial order in every matter. Constitutional status, tenure, service conditions,
removal arrangements and post-office restrictions support impartiality.

The mechanism is preventive:

```text
Political demand for appointments
        ↓
Constitutionally structured examination/advice
        ↓
Transparent merit criteria
        ↓
Reduced scope for personal patronage
```

Its limit is that integrity also depends on examination security, administrative
capacity and reasoned government response.

### 5. Finance Commission

Article 280 provides for a Finance Commission constituted periodically by the President
to recommend specified aspects of Union–State fiscal distribution.

It differs from a permanently functioning regulator or adjudicatory court. Its
independence comes from constitutional status, expert composition, periodic constitution
and a reasoned recommendatory process.

Its recommendations carry major constitutional and political weight, but should not be
described as judicially binding decrees.

### 6. Safeguards are not interchangeable

The phrase “security of tenure, fixed service conditions and charged expenditure”
cannot be copied across all bodies without checking the relevant provision.

| Incorrect shortcut | Correct method |
|---|---|
| Every body has identical removal protection | State the rule body by body |
| Every body's expenditure is charged in the same way | Verify the applicable constitutional text |
| Independence means no accountability | Independence protects function; reporting and reasons preserve accountability |
| Recommendation means binding order | Distinguish recommendatory, administrative and adjudicatory effects |

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Constitutional status can create unelected institutions insufficiently
answerable to democratic government.

**Reply:** Their mandates are limited to integrity functions such as elections, audit,
recruitment and fiscal recommendation. Reporting, judicial review, legislative scrutiny
and reasoned procedure preserve accountability.

**Residual:** Formal safeguards cannot cure politicised appointments, vacancies, weak
capacity or ignored reports. Effective independence requires transparent selection,
resources and institutional compliance.

### 8. UPSC application

**Prelims**

- match body with Article and function;
- distinguish constitutional, statutory and executive bodies;
- avoid assigning one body's safeguard to another;
- distinguish audit, recruitment, election administration and fiscal recommendation.

**Mains**

```text
Function needing insulation
        ↓
Threat to credibility
        ↓
Body-specific safeguard
        ↓
Accountability channel
        ↓
Practical weakness and reform
```

> **Answer line:** Constitutional independence is not a single status conferred equally
> on all bodies; it is a function-specific design of insulation plus accountability.

### 9. Revision notes

1. Independent bodies protect functions vulnerable to incumbent control.
2. Article 324 concerns the Election Commission.
3. The ECI protects fair electoral administration, not politics from political conflict.
4. The CAG supplies legislative financial accountability through audit reports.
5. CAG audit does not itself impose criminal or budgetary punishment.
6. UPSC and SPSC reduce patronage in public recruitment.
7. Commission advice is not automatically a judicially binding order.
8. Article 280 provides for the Finance Commission.
9. The Finance Commission is periodic and recommendatory.
10. Appointment, removal, finance and reporting safeguards differ across bodies.
11. Independence does not mean absence of accountability.
12. Formal status needs transparent appointments, capacity and compliance.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it unsafe to prove the independence of every constitutional body by repeating the same list of tenure, removal and charged-expenditure safeguards?

**Model answer:** The bodies perform different functions and their constitutional designs are not identical. A valid answer must connect the particular threat to the body's actual appointment, tenure, removal, financial and reporting provisions. Generic repetition may assign a safeguard to a body that does not possess it in that form.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat “constitutional body” as a uniform legal category with identical powers, safeguards or binding effects.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original 10-mark prompt — 150 words:** “Institutional independence in the Constitution
is function-specific, not formulaic.” Explain with examples.

**Model (within 150 words):**

The Constitution insulates selected functions because direct incumbent control would
undermine democratic credibility. The method of insulation varies with the function.

The Election Commission requires autonomy in administering electoral competition under
Article 324. The CAG needs removal, financial and reporting safeguards to audit executive
expenditure and inform legislatures. UPSC and State PSCs use constitutionally protected
service arrangements to reduce patronage in recruitment. The Finance Commission,
periodically constituted under Article 280, structures expert recommendations on fiscal
distribution rather than exercising permanent adjudicatory power.

These bodies therefore differ in appointment, tenure, removal, expenditure, reporting
and legal effect. Independence also does not mean immunity from accountability: reports,
reasons, legislative scrutiny and judicial review remain relevant.

Formal constitutional status is insufficient where appointments are opaque, vacancies
persist or reports are ignored. The correct test is whether each body's actual
safeguards protect its assigned function while preserving answerability.

**Scoring guidance — 10 marks:** governing principle 2; four differentiated examples 4;
independence–accountability distinction 2; implementation qualification 2.

## Lesson 9 — How does democracy extend below States and into co-operation?

Progress: 9/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Three-tier government, Parts IX and IX-A, and the qualified status of co-operatives

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Book context: Checked — 73rd, 74th and 97th Amendments and the co-operative limitation

CA search: "Not run: local-government and co-operative doctrine is taught from settled constitutional and judicial material"

CA found: None used

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Visual: constitutional deepening

```text
                    UNION
                      │
                    STATES
          ┌───────────┴───────────┐
          ▼                       ▼
   RURAL LOCAL GOVERNMENT   URBAN LOCAL GOVERNMENT
   Part IX                  Part IX-A
   Eleventh Schedule        Twelfth Schedule
   Panchayats               Municipalities

CO-OPERATIVE CONSTITUTIONALISATION
Art 19(1)(c) right ── Art 43B directive ── Part IX-B
                                           │
                                           ▼
                    valid nationally for multi-State co-operatives;
                    State-co-operative application limited by 2021 ruling
```

*The Constitution moved beyond a Union–State duality, but constitutional recognition is
not the same as complete operational devolution.*

### 1. Why local government required constitutional status

Before the 73rd and 74th Amendments, local bodies depended heavily on State legislation
and political choice. Elections could be delayed, bodies superseded and powers withheld.

The constitutional response sought to create continuity:

```text
Irregular local institutions
        ↓
Constitutional status and periodic elections
        ↓
Representation, planning and local accountability
        ↓
Potential democratic devolution
```

The word **potential** matters. The Constitution creates a framework; States still shape
substantial details and must devolve real functions, finances and staff.

### 2. The 73rd Amendment: Panchayats

The **73rd Amendment Act, 1992** inserted:

- **Part IX**;
- constitutional status for Panchayats; and
- the **Eleventh Schedule**.

The broad design supports village, intermediate and district-level institutions, subject
to constitutional and State-specific conditions.

The Eleventh Schedule identifies fields that may be devolved. Listing a subject does not
automatically transfer complete legislative or administrative control. Effective
devolution requires State law, finance, personnel and administrative capacity.

### 3. The 74th Amendment: Municipalities

The **74th Amendment Act, 1992** inserted:

- **Part IX-A**;
- constitutional status for urban local bodies; and
- the **Twelfth Schedule**.

It recognises forms suited to transitional, smaller urban and larger urban areas.

Urban constitutionalisation responds to different problems from rural local government:
dense service networks, planning, land use, transport, sanitation and metropolitan
coordination. Treating rural and urban local government as one interchangeable template
misses these functional differences.

### 4. Why “third tier” needs qualification

Local government is commonly called the third tier. The expression captures democratic
depth, but local bodies do not possess the same constitutional status as States in every
respect.

| Union–State federalism | Local government |
|---|---|
| Legislative competence directly divided by the Constitution | Functions depend substantially on State devolution |
| States participate in specified constitutional amendments | Local bodies do not possess an equivalent ratification role |
| Constitutionally protected governmental level | Constitutionally recognised institutions with State-shaped powers |

The safe claim is that India constitutionally recognises rural and urban local
government, not that it created an exact third copy of Union–State federalism or a
globally unique local-government system.

### 5. The 97th Amendment and co-operative societies

The **97th Amendment Act, 2011** made three major changes:

1. added the right to form co-operative societies to Article 19(1)(c);
2. inserted **Article 43B**, a DPSP promoting voluntary formation, autonomous functioning,
   democratic control and professional management of co-operatives; and
3. inserted **Part IX-B**, Articles 243ZH–243ZT.

This created a rights–directive–institutional framework for co-operation.

### 6. Federal-competence problem and *Rajendra N. Shah*

Co-operative societies within one State fall primarily within State legislative
competence. The constitutional amendment provisions applying Part IX-B to State
co-operatives had not received the State ratification required for altering the federal
field.

In **Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021)**, the Supreme Court invalidated Part
IX-B insofar as it governed State co-operative societies for want of ratification. The
multi-State co-operative portion survived through severability.

```text
97th Amendment
      │
      ├── Article 19(1)(c) addition: stands
      ├── Article 43B: stands
      └── Part IX-B
             ├── multi-State co-operatives: survives
             └── State co-operatives: invalidated for lack of ratification
```

Do not say that the whole 97th Amendment was struck down.

### 7. Mechanism, promise and limits

Local government and co-operatives share a democratic aspiration:

- participation close to the citizen;
- accountability through smaller institutions;
- collective management of local resources; and
- inclusion of groups excluded from higher institutions.

Their limits also resemble each other:

- elite capture;
- inadequate finances;
- weak professional capacity;
- dependence on higher governments;
- delayed elections or supersession; and
- formal autonomy without functional control.

The remedy is not merely more constitutional text. It requires predictable finance,
professional staff, transparency, elections and real assignment of functions.

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Constitutionalising local bodies and co-operatives crowds State autonomy
and creates uniform rules for highly varied local conditions.

**Reply:** Minimum constitutional guarantees protect elections, representation and
institutional continuity against complete State-level discretion. Significant design
space remains with States.

**Residual:** *Rajendra N. Shah* demonstrates that even desirable reform must respect
the federal amendment procedure. Democratic purpose does not cure a competence defect.

### 9. UPSC application

*No feature is self-sufficient. Constitutional morality keeps each institution within
its role while allowing the whole system to work.*

### 1. What constitutional morality means

Constitutional morality is disciplined fidelity to the Constitution's:

- text;
- procedures;
- institutional roles;
- rights and values;
- accountability requirements; and
- methods of resolving disagreement.

It is not whatever judges, ministers or citizens personally consider moral. Nor is it a
free-standing power to disregard text.

Its rights-based anchors include **Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 and 25**, read with the
Preamble and the Constitution's democratic, federal and accountability structure.

The idea is associated in Indian constitutional discussion with Ambedkar's use of George
Grote's formulation: democratic forms survive only when political actors respect
constitutional methods, even when immediate power makes evasion tempting.

### 2. Why written rules need an operating ethic

No constitutional text can specify every situation. Consider:

- whether a constitutional head exercises formal power to frustrate an elected
  government;
- whether a majority allows meaningful legislative scrutiny;
- whether an independent body uses autonomy with reasons and fairness;
- whether courts design remedies mindful of institutional limits; and
- whether governments comply promptly with adverse judgments.

In each case, literal power may exist, but constitutional purpose constrains its use.

```text
Formal authority
      +
constitutional role
      +
reasoned self-restraint
      =
legitimate constitutional action
```

### 3. Essential facets

| Facet | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| Rule of law | Power is exercised through general, reviewable and reasoned law |
| Fidelity to procedure | Ends do not justify unconstitutional methods |
| Institutional role morality | Each office respects the limits and duties attached to it |
| Rights and dignity | Majorities cannot erase equal citizenship |
| Accountability | Public power must answer through constitutional channels |
| Federal respect | Union and States honour the distribution and spirit of co-operation |
| Tolerance of disagreement | Opposition and criticism are legitimate democratic functions |

These facets are derived from the Constitution's text and structure, especially the
Preamble, rights, parliamentary responsibility, federalism and judicial review.

### 4. Judicial illustrations and their limits

In **Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018)**, constitutional morality was
linked to democratic accountability, federal balance and respect for institutional
roles.

In **Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018)**, constitutional values were contrasted
with exclusionary social morality in protecting equal citizenship and dignity.

The Sabarimala litigation also used constitutional morality in a contested field of
equality, religious freedom and institutional competence.

These cases show the doctrine's potential. They also show its risk: if detached from
text, structure and reasons, “constitutional morality” can become a label for judicial
preference. A strong answer must include this limitation.

### 5. Constitutional morality applies to courts too

Judicial independence protects impartial decision-making. Judicial accountability
protects reasoned, ethical and institutionally bounded exercise of that independence.

The relationship is not:

```text
independence OR accountability
```

It is:

```text
independence from improper pressure
             +
accountability through reasons, ethics and constitutional process
             =
legitimate judicial authority
```

Accountability cannot become executive control over outcomes. Independence cannot
become immunity from standards.

### 7. Five central constitutional tensions

#### Liberty versus welfare

Rights restrain coercion; directives require positive action. Harmony, not annihilation
of either side, is the constitutional solution.

#### Responsibility versus stability

Parliamentary government allows removal through loss of confidence, accepting some
instability to gain accountability.

#### Union capacity versus State autonomy

Centralising mechanisms enable coordination, but federalism places a constitutional
floor beneath State power.

#### Democratic majority versus constitutional limits

Elections authorise government; rights, review and basic structure prevent electoral
victory from becoming unlimited legal power.

#### Independence versus accountability

Courts and constitutional bodies need insulation from incumbents, but remain answerable
through constitutional procedures, reasons and public scrutiny.

### 8. A failure-chain diagnostic

```text
Majority controls executive and legislature
        ↓
Deliberation weakens
        ↓
Independent bodies are pressured
        ↓
Federal and rights limits are treated as obstacles
        ↓
Courts either fail to intervene or intervene without restraint
        ↓
Formal Constitution survives, constitutionalism weakens
```

The reverse chain is constitutional resilience:

```text
competitive elections + legislative scrutiny + federal negotiation
+ independent review + reasoned institutions + civic vigilance
        ↓
constitutional power remains limited and legitimate
```

### 9. Strongest criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Constitutional morality is vague, elitist and capable of displacing
popular morality through judicial power.

**Reply:** Popular morality cannot determine the rights of unpopular minorities or the
limits of public office. Constitutional morality supplies a common public standard based
on adopted text, structure and democratic procedure.

**Residual:** The doctrine is legitimate only when the decision-maker identifies the
constitutional source, explains institutional competence, considers countervailing
values and adopts a proportionate remedy.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — the design test

**Question:** A feature is called “federal,” “parliamentary” or “independent.” What three
questions should you ask before accepting the label?

**Model answer:** Ask what constitutional problem the feature solves, what legal
mechanism makes it operate, and what limitation prevents it from becoming absolute. The
label must follow institutional evidence and practical qualification.

## Check 2 — source versus operation

**Question:** Why can the same borrowed institution operate differently in India and its
source country?

**Model answer:** Institutions interact with different executive forms, federal
arrangements, rights, courts, amendment rules and political conventions. Borrowing
identifies ancestry; operation depends on the receiving constitutional system.

## Check 3 — power and limits

**Question:** How do parliamentary fusion, judicial review and federalism coexist without
contradiction?

**Model answer:** They answer different risks. Fusion makes the executive politically
responsible; judicial review enforces constitutional limits; federalism territorially
distributes authority. Conflict is managed by constitutional supremacy and
institutional role boundaries.

## Check 4 — formal and effective independence

**Question:** What additional evidence is needed after establishing that an institution
has constitutional status?

**Model answer:** Examine appointment, tenure, removal, finance, jurisdiction, reporting,
capacity and compliance. Constitutional status is a foundation, not proof of effective
independence.

## Check 5 — integrated verdict

**Question:** Why is “federal in form but unitary in spirit” an inadequate final
description of India?

**Model answer:** It treats two interacting dimensions as a slogan. India has
constitutionally protected State competence and a powerful Union. The stronger verdict
identifies federal guarantees, centralising mechanisms, judicial limits and variation
between normal, co-operative and emergency operation.

**Model (within 150 words):**

The Constitution borrowed devices because established constitutional experience offered
tested solutions, but the framers did not import complete foreign systems.

India adopted Britain's parliamentary executive while rejecting hereditary monarchy
and parliamentary sovereignty. It drew rights and judicial review from the United
States but rejected presidential government, strict separation and dual citizenship.
Canadian centralising federal elements were placed within a Union of constitutionally
protected States. Irish Directive Principles became part of a larger programme of
social transformation, while South African amendment influence was combined with three
Indian routes and the judicially developed basic-structure limit.

The Government of India Act, 1935 supplied administrative continuity, but popular
sovereignty, universal franchise, republican government and enforceable rights changed
the authority and purpose of the inherited machinery.

Borrowing therefore explains ancestry. Selection, rejection, combination and adaptation
explain Indian constitutional identity.

**Rubric — 10:** definition/thesis 2; three named adaptations 4; 1935 Act qualification
2; precise conclusion 2.

## Final 15-mark question — 250 words

**Prompt:** The salient features of the Constitution are best understood as mechanisms
for managing constitutional tensions rather than as a descriptive list. Analyse.

**Model (within 250 words):**

The Constitution's features address recurring tensions in democratic government.

Federal distribution accommodates diversity, while Union residuary and emergency powers
preserve coordinating capacity. Parliamentary fusion makes the executive continuously
responsible, while judicial review prevents majority control of the legislature and
executive from becoming legally unlimited. Differentiated amendment balances
adaptability with stability; basic structure distinguishes constitutional change from
destruction.

Fundamental Rights protect political liberty, whereas Directive Principles direct social
and economic transformation. Their basic-structure harmony rejects both welfare without
freedom and formal rights without material capacity. Secularism similarly combines
religious liberty with principled State engagement, qualified by equality and the risk
of majoritarian capture.

Independent constitutional bodies insulate elections, audit, recruitment and fiscal
recommendation from direct incumbent control, yet body-specific reporting and review
preserve accountability. Local-government amendments deepen participation but leave
effective devolution dependent on functions, finances and personnel.

The same design can fail. Union strength may become coercion; parliamentary
responsibility may become cabinet dominance; review may become judicial overreach; and
constitutional status may coexist with weak institutional capacity.

Thus, the features should be explained as connected mechanisms—each created to solve a
problem, each producing a counter-risk, and each restrained by another part of the
constitutional order.

**Rubric — 15:** tension-based thesis 2; four interacting mechanisms 6; limitations 3;
cross-feature integration 2; qualified verdict 2.

## Final 20-mark question — 250 words

**Prompt:** “The Indian Constitution is an architecture of controlled power: democratic
enough to authorise government, strong enough to govern, and limited enough to preserve
liberty and diversity.” Critically evaluate.

**Model (within 250 words):**

The Constitution begins with popular sovereignty and universal franchise, authorising
representative government. Parliamentary responsibility links executive survival to the
Lok Sabha, while detailed Union and State institutions supply governing capacity.

Power is also distributed. Federal lists, elected States, partial rigidity and an
independent umpire protect diversity. The Union nevertheless holds residuary power,
coordinating institutions and emergency authority. This assists security and common
policy but creates risks of central overreach.

Liberty is protected through Fundamental Rights, remedies and judicial review. Parliament
may legislate and amend, but constitutional supremacy and basic structure prevent
ordinary or constituent majority from becoming unlimited. Courts themselves remain
constituted authorities, so review must respect text, evidence and institutional
competence.

The architecture is transformative, not merely restraining. Directive Principles pursue
welfare and social democracy; Duties add civic responsibility; secularism combines
conscience with equality; and local-government amendments deepen participation.
Independent bodies protect elections, public audit, recruitment and fiscal mediation,
although their safeguards and legal effects differ.

The design's weaknesses arise in operation. Party discipline may weaken Parliament;
formal federalism may coexist with fiscal dependence; rights may be inaccessible; local
bodies may lack resources; and constitutional morality may become an indeterminate
label.

The Constitution therefore creates controlled rather than weak power. Its success
depends on preserving the interaction of democratic authority, institutional capacity,
rights, federal negotiation, review and self-restraint. No single feature can carry that
burden alone.

**Rubric — 20:** opening thesis 2; democratic/governing authority 4; federal mechanism 3;
rights/review 3; transformative and integrity features 3; critical limitations 3;
integrated verdict 2.

# REMEDIATION

## Error 1 — treating the seventeen features as unrelated facts

**Why it fails:** It produces a descriptive answer with no constitutional reasoning.

**Repair:** For every feature, state:

```text
problem → mechanism → interaction → risk → constitutional limit
```

Example: parliamentary government solves political responsibility; fusion creates
majority dominance risk; legislative scrutiny, courts and constitutional limits provide
checks.

## Error 2 — calling India purely federal or purely unitary

**Why it fails:** It ignores constitutionally protected State competence or the Union
tilt.

**Repair:** Prove both sides, then classify:

- federal distribution and judicial protection;
- Union residuary, territorial and emergency strength;
- *Bommai* as a constitutional floor; and
- co-operative/coercive variation in practice.

## Error 3 — saying Parliament and the Supreme Court are both “supreme”

**Why it fails:** Two legally supreme institutions cannot operate under one supreme
Constitution without contradiction.

**Repair:** Parliament is democratically authoritative within competence; courts possess
review within judicial competence; the Constitution is legally supreme.

## Error 4 — reducing separation of powers to three separate boxes

**Why it fails:** India uses responsible-government fusion and multiple functional
overlaps.

**Repair:** Distinguish institutional existence, core-function differentiation, overlap
and checks. Use *Ram Jawaya* and at least two constitutional examples.

## Error 5 — “borrowed” means “copied”

**Why it fails:** It ignores rejection and interaction.

**Repair:** Give one four-part illustration:

```text
source → borrowed device → Indian adaptation → associated feature rejected
```

## Error 6 — exact contemporary article count without qualification

**Why it fails:** Inserted and omitted articles are counted differently.

**Repair:** Use the exact original count; describe the contemporary textbook figure as
“about 470,” while treating 25 Parts and 12 Schedules as safer.

## Error 7 — positive secularism as unlimited State intervention

**Why it fails:** It removes equality, conscience, non-discrimination and neutrality.

**Repair:** Write “principled engagement” and state both its reform rationale and
majoritarian-capture risk.

## Error 8 — every constitutional body has identical independence safeguards

**Why it fails:** Appointment, removal, finance, reporting and legal effect differ.

**Repair:** Connect the specific function, danger, actual safeguard and accountability
channel.

## Error 9 — third tier means complete federal equivalence

**Why it fails:** Local functions depend heavily on State devolution.

**Repair:** State the constitutional floor, then test functions, finances and
functionaries.

## Error 10 — the whole 97th Amendment was invalidated

**Why it fails:** It ignores severability and the exact federal defect.

**Repair:** Article 19(1)(c) and Article 43B stand; Part IX-B survives for multi-State
co-operatives but not its State-co-operative application.

## Error 11 — constitutional morality is personal moral preference

**Why it fails:** It grants indeterminate power to the decision-maker.

**Repair:** Identify text or structural principle, institutional role, competing value,
reason and proportionate remedy.

## Error 12 — revealing or assuming a PYQ key

**Why it fails:** Several older official objective keys are not held locally.

**Repair:** Keep the question and options answer-neutral. Teach the governing concept,
demand and elimination framework without claiming an official answer.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## The governing idea

- The Constitution **creates, distributes, limits and directs** public power.
- Constitutionalism means power is effectively limited, accountable and governed by law.
- India's distinctiveness lies in the **interaction** of features, not the global
  uniqueness of every individual device.

## Constitution and rule of law

- Political State: population + territory + government + sovereignty.
- Government changes; the State continues.
- Chief purpose: constitute and limit government.
- Rule of law: limited power, equality before law, protected liberty, known procedure,
  independent adjudication and remedies.
- Law may secure ordered liberty; arbitrary law may destroy it.

## Length and sources

- Original: Preamble + 395 Articles + 22 Parts + 8 Schedules.
- Contemporary textbook snapshot: about 470 Articles; exact count varies by method.
- Stable reference: 25 Parts and 12 Schedules.
- Reasons for length: diversity; 1935 Act; one Union–State framework; dominance of legal
  luminaries in the Constituent Assembly.
- Matters left elsewhere to ordinary law or convention were also placed in the
  constitutional text.
- By the book's 2019 snapshot: about 20 Articles and Part VII deleted; about 95 Articles,
  Parts IV-A/IX-A/IX-B/XIV-A and Schedules 9–12 added.
- Largest material source: Government of India Act, 1935.
- Political part: British influence.
- Philosophical rights/directives: US and Irish influences.
- Always write: **source → adaptation → rejected associated feature**.

## High-yield source pairs

- British: parliamentary government, rule of law, cabinet, single citizenship, writs,
  privileges, bicameralism.
- US: Fundamental Rights, judicial review, judicial independence, impeachment,
  Vice-President.
- Ireland: DPSPs, Rajya Sabha nomination, Presidential election method.
- Canada: strong-Centre federation, residuary power and Governors.
- Advisory jurisdiction has a preserved source conflict: the canonical table associates
  it with Canada, while the local eighth-edition table also attributes it to the
  Government of India Act, 1935. Do not present a single-source attribution as settled.
- Australia: Concurrent List, trade freedom, joint sitting.
- Weimar Germany: emergency rights-suspension influence.
- Former USSR: Fundamental Duties and justice ideals.
- France: republic; liberty, equality, fraternity.
- South Africa: amendment elements and Rajya Sabha election.
- Japan: procedure established by law.

## Six criticisms

1. borrowed/patchwork;
2. carbon copy of the 1935 Act;
3. un-Indian;
4. un-Gandhian;
5. elephantine;
6. lawyer's paradise.

Use the strongest reply and retain the residual concern. Do not dismiss criticism with
one Ambedkar quotation.

## Amendment design

- Mixed rigidity/flexibility.
- Route 1: Constitution-affecting ordinary legislation outside Article 368.
- Route 2: majority of total House membership + two-thirds present and voting.
- Route 3: above + ratification by not less than half the State legislatures for
  specified federal matters.
- Ratification is by simple majority.
- No joint sitting.
- President shall assent after valid passage.
- Basic structure: amendment is not destruction.
- Part XX contains Article 368.

## Federal design

- Federal features: dual polity, written and supreme Constitution, divided competence,
  partial rigidity, independent judiciary, bicameralism.
- Union tilt: residuary power, Article 3, specified State-list powers, Governors,
  All-India Services, integrated institutions, emergencies and single citizenship.
- One Constitution governs the Union and States; Jammu and Kashmir's former
  own-Constitution exception ended in 2019.
- Article 1: “Union of States”; no compact-based secession right.
- “Union” does not erase constitutional federalism.
- *S.R. Bommai*: federalism is a basic feature.
- Asymmetry accommodates different regional conditions.
- Emergency centralisation occurs within, not outside, the Constitution.

## Parliamentary government and separation

- Responsible government: executive survives through Lok Sabha confidence.
- President = constitutional head; PM and Council = real political executive.
- Article 75(3): collective responsibility to Lok Sabha.
- India accepts functional differentiation, not airtight organ isolation.
- Articles 121/211, 122/212, 50 and 361 are standard separation anchors.
- Overlaps: ministers in legislature, ordinances, delegated legislation, judicial
  rule-making/contempt, constituent power.
- *Ram Jawaya*: no rigid separation.
- *Kesavananda Bharati* and *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*: separation and structural
  limits cannot be displaced through constituent power.
- Main risk: majority discipline can become cabinet dominance.

## Constitutional supremacy and judiciary

- Parliament is not sovereign in the British sense.
- Courts are not sovereign over the Constitution.
- Articles 13, 32 and 226 support judicial review.
- Article 21 text: procedure established by law.
- *Maneka Gandhi*: procedure must be fair, just and reasonable.
- Integrated judiciary: one hierarchy applying Union and State law.
- Independent judiciary: tenure, service, removal, finance, adjudicatory autonomy and
  constitutional separation safeguards.
- Independence and accountability are complementary.
- Post-retirement practice restrictions differ under Articles 124(7) and 220.

## Rights, directives and duties

- Part III: six groups of Fundamental Rights.
- Rights justiciable but not absolute.
- Property: Article 300A, not a Fundamental Right.
- Part IV: DPSPs, non-justiciable but fundamental in governance.
- *Minerva Mills*: rights–directives balance is structurally protected.
- Swaran Singh Committee recommended adding Fundamental Duties.
- Part IV-A: Article 51A, 11 non-justiciable Fundamental Duties addressed to citizens only.
- Duties do not automatically override rights.

## Secularism, franchise and republic

- No official State religion.
- Equality and religious-freedom provisions were part of the original scheme.
- Socialist, Secular and Integrity were inserted into the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment,
  1976; none was part of the original 1950 wording.
- Use “principled engagement,” qualified by equality, conscience, non-discrimination and
  neutrality.
- Separate electorates differ from remedial reservations.
- Universal adult franchise = equal formal political worth.
- Voting age: 21 to 18 through 61st Amendment Act, 1988.
- Republic = non-hereditary head; it does not mean presidential government.

## Independent constitutional bodies

- ECI: electoral administration under Article 324.
- CAG: public audit and legislative accountability.
- UPSC/SPSC: merit recruitment and service advice.
- Finance Commission: periodic fiscal recommendations under Article 280.
- Never homogenise appointment, tenure, removal, finance, reporting or binding effect.
- Constitutional status is not proof of effective independence.

## Emergencies

- Article 352: National Emergency.
- Article 356: failure of constitutional machinery; Article 365 may be relevant.
- Article 360: Financial Emergency.
- Crisis authority may centralise the federation temporarily.
- Do not claim the Constitution or federal structure simply disappears.

## Local government

- 73rd Amendment: Part IX + Eleventh Schedule + Panchayats.
- 74th Amendment: Part IX-A + Twelfth Schedule + Municipalities.
- Constitutional recognition does not automatically transfer functions, finances and
  functionaries.
- Local bodies are not exact federal equivalents of States.

## Co-operative societies

- 97th Amendment:
  - Article 19(1)(c);
  - Article 43B;
  - Part IX-B.
- *Rajendra N. Shah* (2021):
  - State-co-operative application of Part IX-B invalid for lack of ratification;
  - multi-State portion survives;
  - whole 97th Amendment was not struck down.

## Constitutional morality

- Fidelity to constitutional text, procedure, roles, rights, accountability and values.
- Rights anchors include Articles 14, 15, 19, 21 and 25, read with the Preamble and
  constitutional structure.
- Applies to elected institutions, constitutional heads, courts, bodies and citizens.
- *NCT of Delhi*: democratic accountability and federal role.
- *Navtej Johar*: dignity and equal citizenship against exclusionary social morality.
- Main criticism: indeterminacy and judicial preference.
- Safeguards: textual/structural anchor, institutional competence, reasons, competing
  values and proportionate remedy.

## Ready answer spine

```text
Define integrated constitutional design
        ↓
Authority: franchise + parliamentary government
        ↓
Distribution: federalism + local government
        ↓
Limits: rights + review + basic structure
        ↓
Direction: DPSPs + secularism + duties
        ↓
Integrity: courts + constitutional bodies
        ↓
Adaptability: mixed amendment + emergency capacity
        ↓
Criticism and operational gaps
        ↓
Verdict: controlled power sustained by constitutional morality
```
