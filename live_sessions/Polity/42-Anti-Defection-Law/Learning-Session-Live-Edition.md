# Anti-Defection Law — Live Session Edition

An MLA wins on one party's ticket, joins the rival party's government three months later, and keeps the seat for two years because nobody decides the petition against him. A different MLA votes against her own party on a minor amendment she believes is unconstitutional and is told she could lose her seat for it. Twenty legislators announce that their party has "merged" with another party, although the party's own president says no merger ever happened. A Speaker facing a notice for his own removal is asked to decide whether the members who signed that notice should be disqualified. Every one of these situations is governed by the same short constitutional text — the Tenth Schedule — and versions of every one of them have reached the Supreme Court.

This session teaches that text the way an examiner tests it: first the problem the law was built to solve, then the exact paragraphs, then the people who decide and the courts that review them, then the loopholes that the law created while closing others, and finally the reform debate and the answer-writing method. By the end you should be able to take any defection fact pattern and walk it through four questions in order — who is the member, what did the member do, is any defence available, and who decides how fast — without confusing the Tenth Schedule with the other disqualification routes that sit right next to it in the Constitution.

### The learner's problem

Why should a democracy punish a legislator for changing her mind? In most of the world's parliaments a member can cross the floor and keep the seat; the voters decide at the next election whether the switch was principled or opportunistic. India chose differently. To understand every later lesson, you first need to see the specific harm India was trying to stop and the price it agreed to pay for stopping it.

### Visual: the problem and the bargain

```text
1967 GENERAL ELECTION -> fragmented State assemblies
        |
        v
LEGISLATORS CROSS THE FLOOR, OFTEN FOR OFFICE
("Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram")
        |
        v
MINISTRIES RISE AND FALL WITHOUT ANY NEW VOTE BY CITIZENS
        |
        v
POLITICAL RESPONSE: Lok Sabha resolution (8 Dec 1967) -> Committee on Defections (Y.B. Chavan) -> report (1969)
        |
        v
CONSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE (1985): defection becomes a ground of DISQUALIFICATION
        |
        v
THE BARGAIN
GAIN: stable majorities, a mandate that cannot be traded
COST: less freedom for the individual legislator to vote by conscience
```

*Caption: the law buys stability with the legislator's independence — every later controversy is an argument about whether the price is too high or the purchase incomplete.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of a general election as a collective hiring decision. Voters in a parliamentary system mostly choose a party's programme and its likely government, using the individual candidate as the vehicle. If, after the election, enough vehicles change owners, the government the voters chose can be replaced by one they never chose — without any vote by citizens at all. The anti-defection law treats the party ticket as part of what the voter actually bought. Leave the party (or defy it on a whipped vote) and you lose the seat you won on its ticket.

The intuition has a limit, and the limit is the whole debate. A voter also chooses a person — someone expected to deliberate, scrutinise and sometimes disagree. A rule that makes every party direction a seat-losing matter can turn that person into a reliable vote and nothing more.

### Technical doctrine: what kind of rule this is

Three features define the rule before you learn any paragraph:

1. **It is a disqualification rule.** The consequence is loss of membership of the House (and, after 2003, certain office disabilities). It does not create a crime and does not punish anyone outside the House.
2. **It is constitutional, not merely statutory.** It sits in the Constitution itself — the **Tenth Schedule**, inserted by the **Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985** — and operates through Articles **102(2)** (Parliament) and **191(2)** (State Legislatures).
3. **It is party-centred.** The legal question is always about a member's relationship to a *political party*: the party that set the member up as a candidate, or the party whose direction the member defied.

| Feature | What it means for an answer |
|---|---|
| Disqualification, not crime | Never write "defectors are prosecuted" or "fined" |
| Constitutional text | Never call it "a law passed by Parliament" without saying it is a constitutional amendment inserting a Schedule |
| Party-centred | Always identify the party relationship before discussing the conduct |

### Step-by-step argument: why constitutionalise it?

The reasoning that led to 1985 runs like this:

1. **Premise:** parliamentary government depends on a stable relationship between a ministry and a majority in the House.
2. **Premise:** after 1967, that majority was repeatedly purchased or reshaped through individual floor-crossing, often rewarded with ministerial office.
3. **Inference:** ordinary political sanctions (party expulsion, voter memory) were too slow and too weak to deter such crossings.
4. **Inference:** a legal consequence that bites immediately — loss of the seat — was needed, and because it concerns membership of Parliament and State Legislatures, it belonged in the constitutional rules on disqualification.
5. **Conclusion:** the 52nd Amendment added defection as a constitutional ground of disqualification.
6. **Cost:** the same consequence that deters opportunistic crossing also deters principled dissent, unless the law draws its lines carefully. Lessons 5, 16 and 17 return to this cost.

When the Bill passed both Houses unanimously, the then Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi described it as the "first step towards cleaning-up public life" — a description worth quoting briefly in an introduction, never as a substitute for analysis.

### Example and its limit

"Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" is the political shorthand associated with Gaya Lal, a Haryana legislator who changed sides repeatedly in 1967. It is useful as a one-line opening hook because it captures the mischief vividly. Its limit: it is a memory device, not a legal source. An answer that spends three lines on the anecdote and none on Articles 102(2)/191(2) has confused colour with content.

### Comparison: India against the floor-crossing tradition

| Question | Westminster floor-crossing tradition | Indian Tenth Schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Can a member switch parties mid-term? | Yes; the seat is kept | Seat lost (subject to defences) |
| Who sanctions the switch? | Voters at the next election | The Constitution, through a presiding officer's decision |
| Underlying theory of representation | Member as independent trustee | Member as carrier of a party mandate |
| Main risk | Opportunistic switching between elections | Suppression of legitimate dissent |

The difference is not that one system values democracy and the other does not. They weigh two democratic goods — stable, mandate-faithful government and deliberative, independent representation — differently, because India's post-1967 experience made instability the more pressing danger.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Voters, not a constitutional rule, should punish defectors. A disqualification law substitutes legal machinery for democratic judgment."

**Reply:** The harm defection causes is immediate — a government falls *now* — while the voters' sanction arrives years later, after the mandate has already been rewritten. Disqualification does not prevent the defector from seeking the voters' judgment; it only forces the defector to seek it again before enjoying the benefits of the switch. In that sense the law returns the question to the voters rather than taking it away from them.

**Qualified residual:** That reply works well for genuine switching of sides. It works badly for dissent on a single vote, where the member has not changed sides at all. The law's design must therefore distinguish defection from dissent — and whether it does so adequately is the central evaluative question of this topic.

### UPSC application

An introduction to almost any anti-defection answer can use this lesson in two sentences: name the mischief (office-driven floor-crossing after 1967) and name the bargain (stability purchased at some cost to legislative independence). Then move immediately to the constitutional text. Examiners reward the bargain framing because it sets up the "critically examine" balance that most defection questions demand.

### Revision notes

1. The anti-defection law responds to frequent, office-driven floor-crossing that destabilised ministries after the 1967 general election.
2. A Lok Sabha resolution of 8 December 1967 led to a Committee on Defections chaired by Y.B. Chavan, which reported in 1969.
3. "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" (Gaya Lal, Haryana, 1967) is a memory hook, not a legal source.
4. The law was enacted as the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985, inserting the Tenth Schedule.
5. Its consequence is disqualification from the House, not criminal punishment.
6. It operates through Articles 102(2) and 191(2).
7. It treats the party ticket as part of the voter's mandate.
8. Its central cost is reduced freedom for the individual legislator to dissent.
9. Westminster-style floor-crossing leaves the sanction to voters; India imposes an immediate constitutional sanction.
10. The strongest defence of the law is that it returns the question of switching sides to the voters before the defector can benefit.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to describe the anti-defection law as "a criminal law against changing parties"?

**Model answer:** Because the Tenth Schedule creates only a disqualification from membership of the House (with linked office disabilities added in 2003); it imposes no criminal offence, fine or imprisonment, and it does not forbid anyone from changing parties — it attaches a constitutional consequence to doing so while holding a seat won on another party's ticket.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "disqualification" as if it were a penalty imposed on the person generally; it concerns the person's membership of a particular House, and the person remains free to contest again if otherwise qualified.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "The anti-defection law trades the independence of the legislator for the stability of the government." Explain the problem the law was designed to solve and the trade-off it accepted.

**Model solution:** After the 1967 general election, frequent floor-crossing — often rewarded with ministerial office and captured in the phrase "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" — repeatedly toppled State ministries without any fresh vote by citizens. A Lok Sabha resolution in December 1967 led to the Committee on Defections, but political remedies proved too slow. The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 therefore inserted the Tenth Schedule, making defection a ground of disqualification under Articles 102(2) and 191(2). The gain is stability: a mandate expressed through a party ticket cannot be traded mid-term without returning to the voters. The cost is legislative independence: because the same rule reaches votes contrary to party direction, it can deter principled dissent as well as opportunistic switching. The law is therefore best understood as a deliberate bargain whose fairness depends on how narrowly it separates defection from dissent.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Mischief correctly described with historical anchor — 3; constitutional response correctly identified (52nd Amendment, Tenth Schedule, Articles 102(2)/191(2)) — 3; trade-off explained on both sides — 3; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### The learner's problem

Students often know "Tenth Schedule, 52nd Amendment, 1985" and nothing more. That is enough for a one-line Prelims recall and not enough for anything else. A strong answer must be able to say which Article creates the ground, which Article makes the seat vacant, and which paragraph of the Schedule does what. This lesson builds that map once, so every later lesson can point to it.

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine the Constitution's disqualification rules as a building with one front door for each House. Before 1985, the door listed the familiar reasons a person cannot sit — holding an office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, loss of citizenship, statutory disqualifications. The 52nd Amendment added a second door marked "disqualified under the Tenth Schedule" and built an entire annexe — the Schedule — behind it. The annexe has its own entrance rules (grounds), its own exits (defences), its own judge (the presiding officer) and its own rulebook.

### Technical doctrine: the amending text and its dates

- The **Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985** amended **Articles 101, 102, 190 and 191** and inserted the **Tenth Schedule**.
- The Act is dated **15 February 1985**; the amendment was brought into force on **1 March 1985**. Use "1985" for the Act; know that the Act's date and its commencement are different days.
- **Article 102(2)** provides that a person is disqualified for being a member of either House of Parliament if so disqualified under the Tenth Schedule; **Article 191(2)** says the same for a State Legislature.
- **Articles 101(3)(a) and 190(3)(a)** connect disqualification to **vacation of the seat**.
- The Schedule today has **paragraphs 1, 2 and 4 to 8**. **Paragraph 3 was omitted** by the Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 (Lesson 13). **Paragraph 7 is still printed** but was held invalid in *Kihoto Hollohan* (Lesson 10).
- **Paragraph 2(4)** is a transitional clause: members sitting when the 52nd Amendment commenced were deemed party-set-up candidates if they were party members immediately before commencement, and otherwise deemed independent or nominated members, so that the new rules could apply to the House as it then stood.

### Step-by-step argument: why a Schedule and not a few extra Articles?

1. The grounds, defences, definitions, decider and rule-making powers form a self-contained scheme.
2. Writing all of them into Articles 102 and 191 would have duplicated the same detail twice, once for Parliament and once for the States.
3. A single Schedule referenced by both Articles keeps one uniform scheme for every legislature in India.
4. The cost: a single scheme means a defect (such as paragraph 7's court bar) affects all legislatures at once — which is exactly why its validity became a question about the whole federal amending process (Lesson 10).

### Example and its limit

Suppose an MLA in Karnataka is alleged to have defected. The petition is not decided under some Karnataka statute; it is decided under the same Tenth Schedule that governs a Lok Sabha MP, routed through Article 191(2) instead of 102(2). The limit: although the substantive scheme is uniform, each House makes its own procedural rules under paragraph 8, so the detailed petition procedure can differ from House to House.

### Comparison: the two disqualification doors

| Feature | Article 102(1) / 191(1) door | Article 102(2) / 191(2) door |
|---|---|---|
| Typical grounds | Office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, citizenship, disqualification by law | Defection under the Tenth Schedule |
| Decided under | Articles 103 / 192 (taught in Lesson 15) | Paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule |
| Added in | Original Constitution (with later changes) | 1985 |

Keep the two doors separate from the very beginning: Lesson 15 shows how often exam options deliberately swap them.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If the law is so important, why is it hidden in a Schedule at the back of the Constitution rather than in the main text?"

**Reply:** Schedules are not lesser parts of the Constitution. They carry the same constitutional force and are amended by the same procedures as the Articles that point to them. Placement is a drafting choice for a detailed, self-contained scheme; it does not reduce legal status.

**Qualified residual:** Placement does have one practical effect on learners: because the Schedule is long and detailed, its paragraph numbers matter in exams, and confusing paragraph 2 (grounds) with paragraph 4 (merger) or paragraph 6 (decision) is a frequent source of wrong options.

### UPSC application

Prelims tests the architecture through "which Schedule / which Amendment / which Articles" statements. Mains rewards a two-line constitutional anchor at the start of the body: "The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985), makes defection a ground of disqualification under Articles 102(2) and 191(2), with seats falling vacant under Articles 101 and 190." The common trap is placing the law in the Ninth Schedule, which concerns protected laws and has nothing to do with defection.

### Revision notes

1. The 52nd Amendment Act, 1985 amended Articles 101, 102, 190 and 191 and inserted the Tenth Schedule.
2. Act dated 15 February 1985; in force from 1 March 1985.
3. Articles 102(2) and 191(2) create the ground; Articles 101(3)(a) and 190(3)(a) vacate the seat.
4. The Schedule now contains paragraphs 1, 2 and 4-8; paragraph 3 was omitted in 2003.
5. Paragraph 7 remains printed but is invalid after *Kihoto Hollohan*.
6. Paragraph 2(4) was a transitional clause applying the scheme to members sitting in 1985.
7. One uniform scheme governs Parliament and every State Legislature.
8. Procedural rules under paragraph 8 can differ between Houses.
9. Tenth Schedule, not Ninth Schedule.
10. Defection disqualification is a separate door from the Article 102(1)/191(1) grounds.

### Concept check

**Question:** If the Tenth Schedule supplies all the grounds of defection, why did the 52nd Amendment also need to amend Articles 101 and 190?

**Model answer:** Because a disqualification ground alone does not say what happens to the seat. Articles 101 and 190 govern vacation of seats; amending them ensured that a member who becomes disqualified under Articles 102(2)/191(2) actually vacates the seat, connecting the Schedule's ground to its practical consequence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the Schedule is self-executing in isolation; it works only because the Articles on disqualification and vacation of seats were amended to point to it.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how the 52nd Amendment integrated the Tenth Schedule into the Constitution's existing rules on membership of legislatures.

**Model solution:** The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 did not simply append a free-standing Schedule; it wired the new scheme into four existing Articles. Articles 102(2) and 191(2) declare that a person disqualified under the Tenth Schedule is disqualified for membership of Parliament or a State Legislature respectively, creating a second category of disqualification beside the older grounds in Articles 102(1) and 191(1). Articles 101 and 190, which govern vacation of seats, were amended so that such disqualification actually empties the seat. The Schedule itself then supplies definitions (paragraph 1), grounds (paragraph 2), defences (paragraphs 4 and 5), the decider (paragraph 6) and rule-making (paragraph 8), with a transitional clause applying it to members already sitting. The design produces one uniform scheme for every legislature, although the court-ouster in paragraph 7 was later held invalid and paragraph 3 was omitted in 2003.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Four amended Articles correctly identified with functions — 4; Schedule's internal structure correctly summarised — 3; uniformity and later changes (para 3 omitted, para 7 invalid) noted — 2; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### The learner's problem

When twenty MLAs of a forty-member group say "we are the party", are they? The answer depends on which "party" the Constitution means at each step. The Tenth Schedule uses three different expressions, and nearly every modern defection dispute — from the Uttar Pradesh split of 2003 to the Maharashtra crisis of 2022 — has turned on keeping them apart.

### Visual: three levels of "party"

```text
POLITICAL PARTY  (outside the House)
  organisation with its own constitution, office-bearers and members
  - sets up candidates for election
  - issues (or authorises someone to issue) voting directions
        |
        | its members who sit in a particular House form...
        v
LEGISLATURE PARTY  (inside one House)
  "the group consisting of all the members of that House
   for the time being belonging to that political party"
        |
        | for each individual member, the party he or she belongs to is...
        v
ORIGINAL POLITICAL PARTY  (member-specific)
  the political party to which the member belongs
  for the purposes of paragraph 2(1)
```

*Caption: the political party is the organisation; the legislature party is its group inside one House; the original political party is the reference point fixed for each individual member.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of a national cricket board, the eleven players it fields in one match, and the board a particular player is contracted to. The board selects players and sets team instructions. The eleven on the field are not the board; if seven of them decide to walk out, the board does not thereby move to a new address. And each player's contract is with a specific board, which is the reference point for judging whether that player has broken it. The Tenth Schedule's three expressions map onto the same three ideas: the political party (the board), the legislature party (the eleven), the original political party (the contracting board for each player).

The analogy stops working in one place: legislators are elected by citizens, not contracted by parties. The Schedule's choice to tie the elected member to the party is precisely the bargain from Lesson 1 — and it is a constitutional choice, not a natural fact.

### Technical doctrine: the three definitions and deemed belonging

**Paragraph 1** defines:

- **"House"** — either House of Parliament, or a State Legislative Assembly, or either House of a State Legislature;
- **"legislature party"** — in relation to a member belonging to a political party, the group of all members of that House for the time being belonging to that political party;
- **"original political party"** — in relation to a member, the political party to which the member belongs for the purposes of paragraph 2(1).

The **Explanation to paragraph 2(1)** then fixes *which* party a member belongs to:

| Member | Deemed to belong to |
|---|---|
| Elected member | The political party, if any, by which the member was **set up as a candidate** |
| Nominated member who was a party member on the date of nomination | That political party |
| Any other nominated member | The party the member joins (or first joins) **before six months** from taking the seat (Lesson 6) |

Belonging is therefore **deemed by law** from the election ticket, not from the member's present feelings or later statements.

### Step-by-step argument: why the Schedule anchors on the political party

1. The mischief (Lesson 1) was the trading of a party mandate.
2. The mandate was given to the party that set the candidate up, not to whatever group the member later forms inside the House.
3. If the legislature party could redefine itself by headcount, a group of members could simply declare itself "the party" and escape.
4. So the Schedule ties each member to the **political party** through the ticket, and uses the legislature party mainly as a *counting* unit (for example, in the merger threshold of paragraph 4).
5. In ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra*** (Constitution Bench, 11 May 2023), the Supreme Court drew the consequence: the **political party**, not the legislature party, appoints the Whip and the Leader of the party in the House, and the Speaker must identify the authorised party position by reference to the party's own constitution and structure, not merely by counting legislators.

### Examples and their limits

- An MLA elected on Party A's ticket is deemed to belong to Party A — even if he later says he always supported Party B. His later declarations are evidence about his *conduct* (Lesson 4), not about his deemed belonging.
- A faction claiming a majority of a legislature party cannot, by majority vote inside the House alone, replace the whip appointed by the political party (*Subhash Desai*).

**Limit:** *Subhash Desai* did not say that numbers are irrelevant. Legislative support can be evidence in other proceedings (for example before the Election Commission in a symbol dispute, Lesson 12), and the merger rule in paragraph 4 explicitly counts the legislature party. The point is narrower: headcount inside the House cannot by itself rewrite who the political party is.

### Comparison: political party versus legislature party

| Dimension | Political party | Legislature party |
|---|---|---|
| Where it exists | Outside the House, as an organisation | Inside one particular House |
| Role in paragraph 2 | Membership and voting direction | — |
| Role in paragraph 4 | The "original political party" that must merge | The group whose two-thirds agreement is counted |
| Who appoints the whip | The political party (*Subhash Desai*) | Cannot replace the party's choice by factional vote |
| Can it change by internal vote of legislators? | No | Its composition changes as members join or leave |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Legislators are the people's representatives; party office-bearers are often unelected. Isn't it undemocratic to let the organisation, rather than the elected members, decide who the whip is?"

**Reply:** The Schedule's premise is that voters chose a party-linked mandate. Allowing elected members to capture the party's voice inside the House by internal headcount would let the very people whose loyalty is in question decide whether they have been disloyal. Anchoring authority in the political party prevents that circularity.

**Qualified residual:** The reply is only as good as the party's own internal democracy. If the political party's organisation is itself controlled by a narrow leadership, "the party decides" can mean "a few leaders decide". This is why internal party democracy appears in the reform debate (Lesson 17).

### UPSC application

The distinction between the political party and the legislature party is now central to answers on party splits, whip authority and mergers. The legislature party (paragraph 1) is the group of that party's members in a particular House at a given time. The distinction matters because a faction inside the legislature may claim to be "the party". ### The learner's problem

A legislator who wants to switch sides has learned not to write a resignation letter to the party. He simply begins behaving like a member of the other party — sharing its platform, accepting its offices, even contesting an election on its ticket — while insisting on paper that he never left. Can the Constitution see through that? This lesson answers yes, explains how, and then tests the answer against a ruling delivered only days before this session.

### Visual: the conduct-inference ladder

```text
STRONGEST EVIDENCE
  written resignation from the party
        |
  publicly joining, or contesting on the ticket of, another party
        |
  sustained, public alignment with a rival party (e.g. in forming a government against one's own party)
        |
  sharp criticism of one's party leadership
        |
  a single dissenting speech or internal disagreement
WEAKEST EVIDENCE -> ordinarily NOT enough by itself
```

*Caption: "voluntarily giving up membership" is proved by the overall direction of conduct, not by any single act; the higher rungs make the inference strong, the lower rungs ordinarily cannot carry it alone.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of someone who says he still works for Company A while wearing Company B's uniform, sitting at Company B's desk and signing Company B's contracts. No court would accept that he still works for Company A merely because he never handed in a resignation letter. Paragraph 2(1)(a) asks the same practical question about party membership: what does the member's conduct, taken as a whole, show about where his allegiance actually lies?

The analogy has a limit. Employees do not usually have a constitutional freedom to criticise their employer in public debate; legislators do. So the inference must be drawn carefully — criticism is not departure.

### Technical doctrine: the text and its interpretation

**Paragraph 2(1)(a):** a member of a House belonging to a political party is disqualified "if he has voluntarily given up his membership of such political party".

Three settled interpretive points:

1. **Wider than resignation.** In ***Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India*** (1994), the Supreme Court held that "voluntarily giving up membership" is not synonymous with formal resignation; it can be **inferred from conduct**.
2. **Belonging survives expulsion.** In ***G. Viswanathan v. Speaker, Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly*** (1996), the Court held that a member expelled by his party continues, for Tenth Schedule purposes, to belong to the party that set him up; if he then joins another party, he is treated as having voluntarily given up membership of his original party. Expulsion cannot be used as a doorway out of the Schedule.
3. **Ground (a) has no condonation clause.** The fifteen-day condonation in paragraph 2(1)(b) is written into ground (b) only (Lesson 5). Nothing in ground (a) lets the party forgive an act of giving up membership after the event.

### Step-by-step argument: how the inference is drawn

1. **Start from deemed belonging.** The member belongs to the party that set him up (Lesson 3).
2. **Collect the conduct.** Statements, actions in the House, actions outside it, candidature, acceptance of office.
3. **Ask whether the conduct, read as a whole, objectively shows abandonment** of that party — not mere disagreement within it.
4. **Fix the date.** If abandonment is proved, the disqualification relates to the date of the disqualifying conduct, not the date of the decision (this matters for resignation and office bars, Lessons 13-14).
5. **Give a hearing.** The inference is drawn by the presiding officer acting as a tribunal, after notice and hearing (Lessons 9-10).

The cost of this approach is uncertainty at the lower rungs of the ladder: because there is no exhaustive checklist, a partisan decider can stretch weak evidence or ignore strong evidence. That cost is why review (Lessons 10-11) matters so much for ground (a).

The difference is about *whose* act it is. Ground (a) punishes the member's voluntary act of leaving, not the party's act of throwing the member out.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Inferring departure from conduct gives the decider enormous discretion. A legislator who criticises the leadership could be declared to have 'given up membership'."

**Reply:** The inference is not drawn from criticism alone. *Ravi S. Naik* widens the ground beyond paperwork, but the conduct must objectively show abandonment of the party, and the decider must act as a tribunal — giving notice, hearing the member and recording reasons — with the decision open to review for perversity or mala fides. The Khairatabad case shows the review working in both directions: it can correct an unwarranted acquittal as well as an unwarranted disqualification.

**Qualified residual:** At the lower rungs of the ladder the line remains genuinely uncertain, and Laxmikant records a related gap: the law does not itself provide for a member's expulsion from his party for activities outside the legislature. Party discipline and constitutional disqualification remain different instruments.

### The learner's problem

Ground (a) catches the member who leaves. Ground (b) catches the member who stays but votes against the party line. Is that defection at all? Many legislators who vote against a whip have no intention of leaving their party. This lesson teaches the exact four elements of ground (b), and then the hardest question in the topic: where party discipline ends and conscience begins.

### Visual: the four-element whip test

```text
ELEMENT 1  A DIRECTION was issued
           by the political party, or by a person/authority it authorised
                 |
ELEMENT 2  The member VOTED or ABSTAINED in the House
           contrary to that direction
                 |
ELEMENT 3  WITHOUT PRIOR PERMISSION
           of the party, person or authority
                 |
ELEMENT 4  NOT CONDONED within FIFTEEN DAYS
           of the vote or abstention
                 |
                 v
     ground (b) is made out -> petition -> decision (Lessons 9-10)

Missing ANY element -> ground (b) fails
```

*Caption: the whip ground is a cumulative test; each element is a separate door the petitioner must pass through.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of a cricket captain's instruction to a batsman to defend for the last over. If the batsman swings and is caught, the team may be angry — but whether he has "betrayed the team" depends on whether the instruction was actually given by the captain, whether he had permission to play his shots, and whether the team, after the match, chose to forgive him. Ground (b) builds exactly these three checks — authority, permission, forgiveness — around a single act of voting.

The limit of the analogy: a cricket instruction concerns one match. A party direction in a legislature can concern a law that binds a billion people, and the legislator has a constitutional role in deliberating on it. That is why the scope of ground (b) is so contested.

### Technical doctrine: the text, element by element

**Paragraph 2(1)(b):** a party member is disqualified "if he votes or abstains from voting in such House contrary to any direction issued by the political party to which he belongs or by any person or authority authorised by it in this behalf, without obtaining, in either case, the prior permission of such political party, person or authority and such voting or abstention has not been condoned by such political party, person or authority within fifteen days from the date of such voting or abstention."

| Element | What it requires | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Issued by the political party or someone it authorised | Assuming any faction leader inside the House can issue it (*Subhash Desai*, Lesson 12) |
| Vote/abstention | Voting or abstaining **in the House** | Treating speeches, interviews or committee remarks as breaches |
| Prior permission | Absence of permission before the vote | Forgetting that permission is an express protection |
| Condonation | Not forgiven within **15 days** | Writing 30 days, or applying condonation to ground (a) |

Two further points complete the doctrine:

- **Abstention counts.** Walking out or staying silent on a whipped vote is "abstaining contrary to direction" if the direction required a vote.
- **Paragraph 8(1)(b)** allows the rules to require the leader of the legislature party to report any condonation, so that the presiding officer has a record of what the party forgave.

### Step-by-step argument: why the law reaches votes, not words

1. The mischief was the changing of governments through changed votes.
2. A vote is a discrete, recorded act in the House; a speech is not.
3. Tying the ground to recorded votes keeps the inquiry objective and leaves debate free.
4. Building in permission and condonation lets the party itself decide whether a deviation was tolerable.
5. **Cost:** the text does not limit *which* votes may be whipped. A party may issue a direction on any vote, so every whipped vote — not only votes that decide the government's survival — can become a seat-losing matter.

### Examples and their limits

- An MP criticises the government's Bill in a powerful speech but votes with the party. **No ground (b) issue** — speech is not a vote.
- An MLA abstains on a no-confidence motion despite a direction to vote against it, without permission, and the party does not condone within fifteen days. **All four elements present.**
- An MLA votes against a direction on a minor amendment, but the party condones the vote within ten days. **Ground (b) fails** at element 4.

**Limit:** these examples assume the direction itself was validly issued. Where two rival groups each claim to have issued "the" direction, the decisive question is which one the political party authorised — the issue resolved in *Subhash Desai* (Lesson 12).

### Comparison: dissent versus defection

| Conduct | Party consequence | Tenth Schedule consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Critical speech, interview or article | Possible party discipline | None by itself under ground (b) |
| Contrary vote with prior permission | None | None |
| Contrary vote later condoned within 15 days | None | None |
| Contrary vote, no permission, not condoned | Possible party discipline | Ground (b) made out |
| Joining another party | Possible expulsion | Ground (a), not ground (b) (Lesson 4) |

Party discipline is wider than constitutional disqualification. A party may reprimand, suspend or expel a member for many things; the Constitution disqualifies only for the two grounds in paragraph 2(1).

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Ground (b) converts legislators into vote-machines. A member who votes his conscience on a single Bill is treated exactly like a member who sells his vote to topple a government."

**Reply:** The text gives the party two safety valves — prior permission and condonation — precisely so that conscientious deviations need not cost the seat. Parties that value internal debate can grant permission (as conscience votes sometimes do) or condone afterwards. The law does not force a party to treat every deviation as defection; it lets the party decide.

**Qualified residual:** The safety valves depend entirely on the party's willingness to use them, and in practice the incentive runs the other way: a party leadership can use whips to suppress dissent on ordinary legislation. This is why the most widely supported reform proposal is to confine whip-based disqualification to votes that affect the survival of the government (Lesson 17). Laxmikant's criticism — that the law "does not make a differentiation between dissent and defection" — targets exactly this residual.

### UPSC application

For Mains, this lesson supplies the "freedom of conscience" dimension of any critical evaluation: state the four elements precisely, acknowledge the two safety valves, then explain why breadth of the whip is still the core criticism. For Prelims, the checkable details are: voting **or abstaining**; direction by the party **or a person/authority it authorised**; **prior** permission; condonation **within fifteen days**.

### Revision notes

1. Paragraph 2(1)(b) has four cumulative elements: direction, contrary vote/abstention, no prior permission, no condonation within fifteen days.
2. The direction must come from the political party or a person/authority it authorised.
3. Abstaining contrary to direction counts as well as voting contrary to it.
4. The ground concerns votes in the House, not speeches or public criticism.
5. Prior permission and condonation are express protections.
6. Condonation must occur within fifteen days of the vote or abstention.
7. Condonation applies to ground (b) only, not to ground (a).
8. Paragraph 8(1)(b) lets the rules require reports of condonation by the legislature-party leader.
9. The text does not limit which votes may be whipped — the root of the dissent criticism.
10. Party discipline is broader than constitutional disqualification.

### Concept check

**Question:** An MLA abstains on a Money Bill despite a direction to vote for it. Before the vote, the party's authorised chief whip had told him in writing he could abstain. Is ground (b) made out?

**Model answer:** No. Element 3 fails: the member obtained the prior permission of a person authorised by the party, which the text expressly makes a protection, so the abstention is not "without obtaining ... the prior permission" and no question of condonation even arises.

**Misconception to avoid:** Thinking that every vote or abstention against a direction is automatically disqualifying; permission and condonation are part of the ground itself, not optional mercy.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Examine whether paragraph 2(1)(b) of the Tenth Schedule adequately distinguishes legislative dissent from defection.

**Model solution:** Paragraph 2(1)(b) disqualifies a party member who votes or abstains in the House contrary to a direction issued by his political party or its authorised person, without prior permission, unless the party condones the act within fifteen days. In one sense it does distinguish dissent from defection: it reaches only recorded votes, not speeches or criticism, and its permission and condonation clauses let a party tolerate conscientious deviation. In another sense it does not: nothing in the text limits which votes may be whipped, so a contrary vote on an ordinary amendment carries the same constitutional consequence as crossing over on a confidence motion. Laxmikant therefore records the criticism that the law does not differentiate dissent from defection, and reform bodies such as the Dinesh Goswami Committee proposed confining whip-based disqualification to votes affecting the government's survival. The distinction exists in form but depends on party restraint in practice.

```text
  election -----------------------------------------------> end of term
  joins ANY political party at ANY time after election  ==> disqualified (para 2(2))
  (no window at all)

NOMINATED MEMBER
  takes seat (after oath under Art 99 / Art 188)
  |<---------------- six months ---------------->|
  joining a party in this window: no disqualification;
  member is then deemed to belong to that party (Explanation (b)(ii))
                                                 |------------------> end of term
                                  joining a party after six months ==> disqualified (para 2(3))

  [if already a party member on the date of nomination:
   deemed to belong to that party from the start (Explanation (b)(i))]
```

*Caption: the independent has no window; the nominated member has a six-month window that starts when the seat is taken, not when the nomination is made.*

### Plain-language intuition

The independent asked voters for support precisely *as* an independent. Joining a party afterwards changes the very thing the voters chose — so the Schedule treats it like defection. A nominated member was never chosen by voters at all; nomination recognises expertise or distinction. Such a person may genuinely discover a political home once inside the House, so the Schedule allows a short period to make that choice, after which the member must stay where he has settled.

The limit: the logic about the independent's mandate is strong; the logic about a nominated member's window is more contestable — why six months, and why any window at all? That is the criticism discussed below.

### Technical doctrine: the exact text

| Provision | Rule |
|---|---|
| Paragraph 2(2) | An elected member who was elected otherwise than as a candidate set up by any political party is disqualified **if he joins any political party after such election** |
| Paragraph 2(3) | A nominated member is disqualified **if he joins any political party after the expiry of six months from the date on which he takes his seat** after complying with Article 99 (Parliament) or Article 188 (State Legislature) |
| Explanation (b)(i) | A nominated member who was a member of a political party on the date of nomination is deemed to belong to that party |
| Explanation (b)(ii) | Any other nominated member is deemed to belong to the party he becomes (or first becomes) a member of before six months from taking the seat |

Two consequences follow:

1. A nominated member who joins a party within the window becomes a **party member for the rest of the Schedule** — grounds (a) and (b) then apply to him like anyone else.
2. Articles 99 and 188 concern the **oath or affirmation**; the six months therefore run from taking the seat after the oath, not from the date of the notification of nomination.

### Step-by-step argument: why the asymmetry exists

1. The Schedule's premise is that a mandate given through a party ticket must not be traded (Lesson 1).
2. The independent's mandate was given *against* any party ticket. Joining a party betrays that mandate in the same way that a party member's switch betrays his.
3. The nominated member had no electoral mandate. There is no voter choice to betray by joining a party.
4. But unlimited freedom to join and leave would let a nominated seat be used as a floating vote. So the Schedule allows one choice, within a limited time, and then freezes it.

### Examples and their limits

- An independent MLA who openly attends a party's legislative meetings and campaigns for it may face a petition alleging that he has "joined" that party. Whether he has joined is a question of evidence, just as voluntarily giving up membership is (Lesson 4).
- A nominated Rajya Sabha member who formally joins a party four months after taking the seat is not disqualified, and from then on is bound by that party's directions.
- The same member joining a party eight months after taking the seat is disqualified under paragraph 2(3).

**Limit:** "joining" an independent's case may be inferred from conduct as well as from a formal membership form; how much conduct is enough is fact-specific, and there is no exhaustive test in the text.

### Comparison: three entry positions

| Entry position | Starting belonging | What triggers disqualification |
|---|---|---|
| Elected on a party ticket | The party that set him up | Ground (a) or ground (b) |
| Elected independent | None | Joining any political party after election |
| Nominated, already a party member at nomination | That party | Ground (a) or ground (b) |
| Nominated, not a party member at nomination | None, until a choice within six months | Joining a party after six months; once joined within the window, grounds (a) and (b) |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection (recorded by Laxmikant):** the discrimination between an independent and a nominated member is illogical — if the independent is disqualified for joining a party, why is the nominated member allowed to do the same?

**Reply:** The distinction tracks the source of the seat. The independent's seat came from voters who chose a non-party candidate; the nominated member's seat came from the President or Governor, with no voter choice involved. Treating unlike sources of mandate differently is not illogical.

**Qualified residual:** The reply explains the difference in principle but not the precise length of the window. Six months is a policy choice, and a nominated member who joins a party within it can still alter party strength in a closely divided House — especially in the Rajya Sabha, where nominated members sit. The criticism is weaker than it first sounds but not empty.

### UPSC application

This lesson's rules are compact and examinable: who has a window, how long it is, when it starts and what happens after. A verified 2022 Prelims question tested the nominated-member rule together with a second statement on the decision process; its exact wording and demand are in the final PYQ section for you to resolve from the text taught here and in Lesson 11. For Mains, use the "source of the seat" argument to evaluate the asymmetry rather than merely restating it.

### Concept check

**Question:** A nominated member was a member of Party X when nominated. Three months after taking his seat he resigns from Party X and joins Party Y. Does the six-month window protect him?

**Model answer:** No. Under Explanation (b)(i) he was deemed to belong to Party X from the start because he was its member on the date of nomination; the six-month window in Explanation (b)(ii) applies only to nominated members who were not party members when nominated. Leaving Party X for Party Y is therefore voluntarily giving up membership under ground (a).

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming every nominated member enjoys a six-month freedom to switch; the window exists only for a nominated member without a party on the date of nomination, and it is a window to *join*, not to switch.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why the Tenth Schedule treats independent and nominated legislators differently, and assess whether the distinction is defensible.

**Model solution:** Under paragraph 2(2), an independent elected otherwise than as a party candidate is disqualified if he joins any political party after election; there is no grace period. Under paragraph 2(3), a nominated member is disqualified only if he joins a party after six months from taking his seat following the oath under Article 99 or 188; a nominated member already in a party at nomination is deemed to belong to it from the start. The difference tracks the source of the seat. The independent's mandate was given by voters who chose a non-party candidate, so joining a party betrays that choice. A nominated member has no electoral mandate to betray, so one bounded choice is permitted and then frozen. The distinction is defensible in principle, although the six-month figure is a policy judgment and a late-joining nominated member can still affect party strength in a closely divided House.

### The learner's problem

Individual defection became costly in 1985. Group defection did not — for nearly two decades, a third of a legislature party could claim a "split" and walk away safely. The 91st Amendment closed that door in 2003. But a second door, marked "merger", was left open, and in 2026 the Supreme Court was again asked what exactly that door requires. This lesson teaches both doors and the live dispute about the second.

### Visual: split versus merger

```text
                     FORMER PARAGRAPH 3              CURRENT PARAGRAPH 4
                     "SPLIT"                         "MERGER"
Claim               a faction arose from a split    the ORIGINAL POLITICAL PARTY
                    in the original party           merges with another party
Threshold           not less than ONE-THIRD of      deemed to have merged if, and only if,
                    the legislature party           not less than TWO-THIRDS of the
                                                    legislature party agree
Protected           members of the faction          members who join the merged/new party
                                                    AND members who reject the merger and
                                                    function as a separate group
Status              OMITTED w.e.f. 1 Jan 2004       SURVIVES

```

*Caption: the split was a one-third exit for factions; the merger is a two-thirds deeming rule attached to a party-level event — the controversy is about how those two parts of paragraph 4 fit together.*

### Plain-language intuition

Picture two companies agreeing to merge. The employees of the old company either move to the merged company or, if they object, form their own unit — nobody is punished for the corporate decision. That is the intended logic of paragraph 4: when a *party* merges, its legislators should not be disqualified for following (or refusing to follow) the party's own decision.

Now picture something different: two-thirds of one company's employees in one branch office announce that "the company has merged" with a rival, although the company's board never agreed. Should their announcement be treated as a merger? That is the question the text leaves open, and it is why "merger" is often described as a doorway for organised group defection.

### Technical doctrine: the two paragraphs precisely

**Former paragraph 3 (omitted):** where a member claimed that he and other members of his legislature party constituted a group representing a faction that had arisen as a result of a **split in his original political party**, and the group consisted of **not less than one-third** of the members of the legislature party, he was not disqualified under ground (a) or ground (b). The **Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003** omitted it, with effect from **1 January 2004**.

**Paragraph 4(1):** a member is not disqualified under paragraph 2(1) where **his original political party merges with another political party** and he claims that he and other members of his original political party either (a) have become members of the other party or of a new party formed by the merger, or (b) **have not accepted the merger and opted to function as a separate group**. From the merger, that party or group is deemed to be his political party and original political party.

**Paragraph 4(2):** for paragraph 4(1), the merger of the original political party is **deemed to have taken place if, and only if, not less than two-thirds of the members of the legislature party concerned have agreed to such merger**.

### Step-by-step argument: the interpretive dispute

The text pulls in two directions:

1. **Organisational reading.** Paragraph 4(1) begins with the *original political party* merging. The two-thirds rule in 4(2) is a condition that must *also* be met. On this reading, a legislature-level two-thirds is necessary but not sufficient — there must be a genuine party-level merger.
2. **Headcount reading.** Paragraph 4(2) says merger is *deemed* to have taken place if two-thirds of the legislature party agree. On this reading, two-thirds agreement inside the House is itself the merger for Tenth Schedule purposes, regardless of what the organisation outside does.
3. **What the case law suggests.** In ***Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya*** (2007), interpreting the former split rule, the Supreme Court refused to accept a split merely because legislators claimed it; a split in the *original political party* had to be shown. *Subhash Desai* (2023) emphasised the political party over the legislature party for whip authority. Both point towards the organisational reading — but neither is a final ruling on paragraph 4 itself.
4. **Where matters stand.** On 27 July 2026 the Supreme Court issued notice on a writ petition asking it to settle the interpretation of paragraph 4, tagging it with connected pending matters, without expressing a view on the merits. The safe position is therefore: state both readings, state that they are under the Court's consideration, and do not declare a winner.

### Examples and their limits

- A party's national organisation formally merges with another party, and 22 of its 30 MLAs accept the merger while 8 refuse and sit as a separate group. Under paragraph 4, **both groups are protected** — the 22 because they joined the merged party, the 8 because they opted to function separately.
- Eighteen of a party's 25 MLAs announce a merger with a rival; the party organisation denies any merger. Whether paragraph 4 protects them is **exactly the contested question** described above.

**Limit:** the second example is deliberately left unresolved. Writing "two-thirds of MLAs can always merge with another party" as settled law would overstate the text; writing "a party-level merger is definitely required" would overstate the case law.

### Comparison: why the split was deleted but the merger kept

| Question | Split (deleted) | Merger (kept) |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold | One-third — easy to assemble | Two-thirds — harder |
| Underlying event | A faction claims the party split | The party merges |
| Defence of keeping it | Protected genuine ideological ruptures | Protects genuine realignment of parties |
| Experience | Widely used to legitimise organised defections | Increasingly used the same way, hence the 2026 petition |
| Status | Omitted 2003 | In force |

Laxmikant lists among the law's *advantages* that it "facilitates democratic realignment of parties in the legislature by way of merger" — and among its *criticisms* that it "banned only retail defections and legalised wholesale defections". Both descriptions concern the same paragraph; which is more accurate depends on how paragraph 4 is read.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Delete the merger exception too. If individual defection is wrong, group defection is worse."

**Reply:** Parties do genuinely merge — and legislators who follow their party into a merger have not betrayed the party that set them up; they have obeyed it. Without paragraph 4, a lawful organisational merger would disqualify every legislator of the merging party, punishing members for their party's own decision.

**Qualified residual:** The reply defends merger as an organisational event, not merger as a legislators' headcount. If paragraph 4 is read as protecting any two-thirds group inside the House, the reply loses most of its force, and the "wholesale defection" criticism becomes accurate. The strength of the exception therefore depends on the unresolved interpretive question.

### UPSC application

The single most common Prelims trap in this topic is "split and merger are both valid defences" — only merger survives. The second is "one-third" as the current threshold — it is two-thirds, and only for merger. For Mains, this lesson supplies the "wholesale defection" paragraph of any critical evaluation; present the two readings, cite *Rajendra Singh Rana* and *Subhash Desai* as pointers, note the pending 2026 petition as a status fact, and reach a qualified conclusion.

2. The 91st Amendment, 2003 omitted paragraph 3 with effect from 1 January 2004.
3. Paragraph 4 protects members where the original political party merges with another party.
4. Merger is deemed if, and only if, not less than two-thirds of the legislature party agree.
5. Both members who join the merged party and members who refuse the merger and function as a separate group are protected.

7. Headcount reading: two-thirds legislative agreement is itself the deemed merger.

### Concept check

**Question:** In a party-level merger that satisfies paragraph 4, ten of a party's thirty MLAs refuse to join the merged party and sit separately. Are those ten disqualified for refusing to follow their party?

**Model answer:** No. Paragraph 4(1)(b) expressly protects members who have not accepted the merger and opted to function as a separate group; from the merger, that group is deemed to be their political party for the Schedule, so neither those who join nor those who refuse are disqualified on account of the merger.

**Misconception to avoid:** Thinking paragraph 4 protects only those who move into the merged party; dissenters from a qualifying merger are equally protected.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "Deleting the split exception narrowed one door but left another open." Critically examine this statement with reference to the Tenth Schedule's treatment of group defection.

**Model solution:** Before 2004, former paragraph 3 protected any faction of at least one-third of a legislature party that claimed a split in its original party. Because one-third was easy to assemble, the exception became a routine cover for organised defection, and the Goswami Committee, the Law Commission's 170th Report and the NCRWC recommended its removal. The Ninety-first Amendment Act, 2003 omitted it with effect from 1 January 2004. Paragraph 4, however, survives. It protects members where their original political party merges with another party, and paragraph 4(2) deems the merger to have occurred if, and only if, at least two-thirds of the legislature party agree. Both those who join the merged party and those who refuse and sit separately are protected. The statement is accurate to the extent that paragraph 4 is read as a headcount rule: a two-thirds group could then achieve what one-third could before, which is why critics say the law "legalised wholesale defections". It is less accurate on an organisational reading, under which a genuine party-level merger is required in addition to two-thirds agreement — a reading supported by *Rajendra Singh Rana*'s insistence on a real split in the original party under the old rule and by *Subhash Desai*'s emphasis on the political party. On 27 July 2026 the Supreme Court issued notice on a petition seeking to settle this interpretation, without a merits view. Whether the second door is wide open or narrow therefore remains a live constitutional question.

### The learner's problem

A legislator elected Speaker may wish to step away from party politics to preside impartially. But stepping away means giving up party membership — which, under ground (a), is exactly what the Tenth Schedule punishes. Without a special rule, the law would penalise the very gesture of impartiality it should encourage. Paragraph 5 solves that problem, and only that problem.

### Visual: the paragraph 5 gate

```text
IS THE PERSON ELECTED TO A LISTED OFFICE?
  Lok Sabha: Speaker, Deputy Speaker
  Rajya Sabha: Deputy Chairman
  State Legislative Assembly: Speaker, Deputy Speaker
  State Legislative Council: Chairman, Deputy Chairman

        v

        | yes
        v

JOINS A DIFFERENT PARTY (in office, or treated outside 5(b))   -> paragraph 5 does not cover it
```

*Caption: paragraph 5 protects one specific gesture — leaving one's party because of election to the chair — and the return to that same party afterwards; it is not a general licence.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of a referee chosen from among the players of one team. To referee fairly, she takes off her team jersey for the season. The rulebook should not then punish her for "leaving the team". But if, while still refereeing, she puts on the *other* team's jersey, the gesture is no longer impartiality — it is a switch. And when her refereeing term ends, putting her *old* jersey back on is simply going home. Paragraph 5 encodes exactly these three positions.

The analogy's limit: a referee's fairness depends on how she referees, not on what she wears. Paragraph 5 deals only with the legal consequence of leaving the party on taking the chair; it says nothing about how the chair is then conducted.

### Technical doctrine: the text

**Paragraph 5 (Exemption):** notwithstanding anything in the Schedule, a person elected to the office of **Speaker or Deputy Speaker of the House of the People**, **Deputy Chairman of the Council of States**, **Chairman or Deputy Chairman of the Legislative Council of a State**, or **Speaker or Deputy Speaker of the Legislative Assembly of a State** shall not be disqualified:

- **(a)** if he, by reason of his election to such office, voluntarily gives up the membership of the political party to which he belonged immediately before such election and does not, so long as he continues to hold such office, rejoin that party or become a member of another party; or
- **(b)** if he, having given up membership by reason of such election, rejoins that political party after he ceases to hold such office.

Two textual details matter:

1. **The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha is not listed.** The Chairman is the Vice-President, who is not a member of the Rajya Sabha; the Tenth Schedule's membership-based scheme does not apply to that office in the same way. The Deputy Chairman, who *is* a member, is listed.
2. **Return is allowed only to the same party.** Paragraph 5(b) speaks of rejoining "such political party" — the one left on election to the chair.

### Step-by-step argument: why so narrow?

1. The aim is to protect the dignity and impartiality of the presiding office (Laxmikant's explanation).
2. Impartiality is served by allowing a presiding officer to sever party ties without penalty.
3. Impartiality is *not* served by allowing a presiding officer to acquire a new party while presiding — that would be a switch, not a severance.
4. Allowing a return to the same party after office prevents the protection from becoming a trap that permanently exiles the officer from political life.
5. Extending the exemption to ministers, whips or other office-holders would serve none of these purposes and would reopen the door to defection.

### Examples and their limits

- A Speaker elected in 2024 resigns from her party on election and remains unaffiliated throughout her term. **Protected under 5(a).**
- After her term she rejoins the same party. **Protected under 5(b).**
- A Deputy Speaker gives up membership on election and, two years later while still in office, joins a different party. **Paragraph 5 does not cover it**; the question of disqualification would then be decided under paragraph 6.
- A Minister resigns from his party claiming the "dignity of office". **Paragraph 5 is unavailable**; ministers are not listed.

**Limit:** whether presiding officers *should* be expected to sever party ties at all — a convention debated in Indian parliamentary practice — is a question about the presiding office, studied with the Parliament topic. Paragraph 5 only says what happens if they do.

### Comparison: paragraph 4 versus paragraph 5

| Feature | Paragraph 4 (merger) | Paragraph 5 (presiding office) |
|---|---|---|
| Who benefits | Groups of members following a party merger | One individual elected to a listed office |
| Triggering event | Merger of the original political party | Election to the presiding office |
| What it excuses | Joining the merged party or forming a separate group | Giving up party membership by reason of that election |
| Rejoining rule | — | May rejoin only the former party, after leaving office |
| Purpose | Protect genuine party realignment | Protect the dignity and impartiality of the chair |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Paragraph 5 is symbolic. Giving up a party card does not make a presiding officer impartial; loyalties outlast membership."

**Reply:** A disqualification scheme cannot regulate loyalty; it can only decide what legal consequence follows from an act. Paragraph 5 ensures that one act associated with impartiality — leaving the party on election to the chair — carries no penalty. Expecting more from a disqualification provision would be expecting it to do the work of conventions and conduct.

**Qualified residual:** The objection still has bite in the context of Lessons 9-12: presiding officers are elected from among the House's members, who ordinarily come from political parties, so the person who decides defection petitions under paragraph 6 is often connected with one of the contending sides. Paragraph 5 does not address that structural tension.

### UPSC application

Prelims statements on paragraph 5 typically test the exact list of offices (note the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, and the absence of the Chairman), the "by reason of election" condition, and the "same party" rejoining rule. A Mains answer on the Speaker's role can use paragraph 5 to show exactly what the Schedule does about a presiding officer's party ties — it removes the penalty for leaving the party on election to the chair — and then connect that to the adjudicator problem in Lesson 9.

### Revision notes

1. Paragraph 5 covers the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of a State Legislative Council, and the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of a State Legislative Assembly.
2. The Chairman of the Rajya Sabha is not listed.
3. Protection applies when membership is given up by reason of election to the office.
4. While in office, the person must not rejoin the old party or join another party.
5. After leaving office, the person may rejoin the same former party.
6. The exemption concerns the legal consequence of leaving the party; it does not regulate conduct in the chair.

### Concept check

**Question:** A Deputy Speaker of a State Assembly gives up party membership on election, and after her term ends she joins a party different from the one she left. Does paragraph 5 protect her?

**Model answer:** Paragraph 5(b) protects only a person who, after ceasing to hold the office, rejoins "such political party" — the party she left by reason of election. Joining a different party falls outside 5(b), so paragraph 5 gives her no protection for that act; whether any consequence follows depends on her status in the House at that time, which paragraph 6 would determine.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading paragraph 5 as a general freedom for former presiding officers to join any party; the rejoining protection is tied to the same party.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Examine the conditions under which paragraph 5 of the Tenth Schedule protects a presiding officer, and explain why its protection is deliberately narrow.

**Model solution:** Paragraph 5 protects a person elected Speaker or Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha or a State Assembly, Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, or Chairman or Deputy Chairman of a State Legislative Council. Protection has three conditions: membership of the party must be given up by reason of election to that office; while in office the person must not rejoin that party or join another; and after leaving office the person may rejoin only the same party. The Rajya Sabha Chairman, being the Vice-President, is not listed. The narrowness follows the purpose Laxmikant identifies — dignity and impartiality of the chair. Severing party ties serves impartiality; acquiring a new party while presiding would be a switch, and extending the exemption to ministers or whips would reopen the door to defection. The exemption removes a penalty for severance; it does not itself secure impartial conduct in the chair.

### The learner's problem

Defection does not vacate a seat by itself. Someone has to decide that it happened, and someone has to decide how that decision is made. The Tenth Schedule gives the first task to the presiding officer of the House and the second to rules that the presiding officer frames. This lesson shows exactly how that machinery works — and why the identity of the decider is the most argued-about choice in the entire scheme.

### Visual: from petition to order

```text
A MEMBER OF THE HOUSE FILES A WRITTEN PETITION
(facts + supporting documents, as the House's rules require)
        |
        v
PRESIDING OFFICER CHECKS THE PETITION FOR COMPLIANCE WITH THE RULES
        |
        v
COPY TO THE MEMBER CONCERNED -> written comments / explanation
        |
        v
PRESIDING OFFICER DECIDES  OR  REFERS FOR INQUIRY (e.g. Committee of Privileges)
        |
        v
REASONABLE OPPORTUNITY + HEARING  (natural justice)
        |
        v
WRITTEN ORDER: petition dismissed  OR  member declared disqualified
        |
        v
SEAT VACANT (Articles 101/190) -> judicial review available (Lesson 10)

SPECIAL CASE: if the question concerns the Speaker/Chairman personally
-> the House elects one of its members to decide (proviso to para 6(1))
```

*Caption: defection has no automatic effect — a petition, a hearing and a reasoned decision stand between the alleged act and the loss of the seat.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of a club's disciplinary committee. A complaint must be lodged by someone, the accused must be told the case against him, he must be heard, and a decision must be recorded. Making the club's president the sole judge keeps the matter in-house and quick — but if the president belongs to one faction and the accused to another, the in-house judge's fairness will always be doubted. Paragraph 6 makes exactly this trade: in-house adjudication for speed and respect for the House's autonomy, at the price of a decider who is often a party member.

### Technical doctrine: paragraph 6

- **Paragraph 6(1):** if any question arises whether a member has become subject to disqualification under the Schedule, it is referred for the decision of the **Chairman** or, as the case may be, the **Speaker** of the House, and **his decision shall be final**.
- **Proviso:** where the question is whether the **Chairman or Speaker himself** has become subject to disqualification, it is referred to **such member of the House as the House may elect** in this behalf.
- **Paragraph 6(2):** proceedings under paragraph 6(1) are **deemed to be proceedings in Parliament** within Article 122, or **proceedings in the Legislature of a State** within Article 212.

What paragraph 6 does *not* do: it gives **no role to the Election Commission**, and none to the President or Governor. Those authorities decide *other* disqualification questions under Articles 103 and 192 (Lesson 15).

### Technical doctrine: paragraph 8 and the House rules

- **Paragraph 8(1)** lets the Chairman or Speaker make rules, including on registers of members' party affiliations, reports by legislature-party leaders on condonation, reports by parties on admitting members, and **the procedure for deciding questions under paragraph 6**, including any inquiry.
- **Paragraph 8(2):** the rules are laid before the House for **thirty days** and take effect unless approved with modifications or disapproved.
- **Paragraph 8(3):** the presiding officer may direct that **wilful contravention** of the rules be dealt with **as a breach of privilege**.
- Under the rules, as Laxmikant summarises them, a case is taken up **only on a petition (complaint) by a member**; the member concerned must be given a chance to explain; the matter may be referred to the **Committee of Privileges** for inquiry. Hence "defection has no immediate and automatic effect".

### Step-by-step argument: why the presiding officer?

1. Questions about membership of the House were historically regarded as matters for the House itself.
2. A presiding officer already rules on many questions of House procedure and privilege.
3. Keeping adjudication in-house avoids routine first-instance litigation over the composition of the legislature.
4. Paragraph 6(2) gives the proceedings the character of House proceedings, protecting them from challenge for mere procedural irregularity.
5. **Cost:** presiding officers are elected from among the House's members, who ordinarily belong to parties, and a defection decision can decide whether the presiding officer's own side stays in power. The decider can therefore be suspected — and sometimes shown — to act strategically, especially through delay (Lesson 11).

### Examples and their limits

- An MP petitions the Lok Sabha Speaker to disqualify a member of another party for joining a rival. The Speaker forwards the petition, receives comments, hears both sides and passes a written order. **Paragraph 6 working as designed.**
- A petition alleges that the Speaker himself has defected. The House elects a member to decide it. **Proviso to paragraph 6(1).**

**Limit:** "his decision shall be final" sounds like the end of the matter. It is not. *Kihoto Hollohan* (Lesson 10) read finality as excluding appeals, not judicial review; and paragraph 6(2)'s "deemed proceedings" protects only against challenge for procedural irregularity, not against illegality or mala fides.

### Comparison: who does what in a defection dispute

| Institution | Role in a Tenth Schedule question |
|---|---|
| Member of the House | Files the petition |
| Speaker/Chairman | Decides as an adjudicator (paragraph 6) |
| Member elected by the House | Decides if the presiding officer is personally concerned |
| Committee of Privileges | May inquire on reference |
| High Courts / Supreme Court | Judicial review (Lesson 10) |
| Election Commission | No paragraph 6 role; decides symbol disputes and advises on other disqualifications (Lessons 12, 15) |
| President / Governor | No paragraph 6 role |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "A party member cannot be a fair judge of whether members of rival parties should lose their seats. Two Lok Sabha Speakers — Rabi Ray (1991) and Shivraj Patil (1993) — themselves doubted their suitability for this role."

**Reply:** The presiding officer is a constitutional office bound to act judicially in this capacity: notice, hearing, reasons and judicial review are all required. *Kihoto Hollohan*'s majority refused to hold that vesting the power in the Speaker was invalid merely because of possible political bias, observing that these functionaries hold a pivotal position and are expected to take far-reaching decisions.

**Qualified residual:** Review can correct a biased decision after it is made, but it cannot easily prevent a biased decider from *not* deciding. That residual — timing — is the subject of Lesson 11, and it is the reason why alternative deciders are among the most discussed reform proposals (Lesson 17).

### Concept check

**Question:** A newspaper reports that an MLA has joined a rival party. No member files a petition. Is the MLA's seat vacated?

**Model answer:** No. Under the rules framed under paragraph 8, the presiding officer takes up a defection question on a petition by a member, and disqualification follows only after a decision under paragraph 6 with an opportunity to be heard; without a petition and decision, there is no disqualification and the seat is not vacated, however clear the newspaper report.

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing that defection disqualifies a member automatically the moment it occurs; the scheme requires adjudication before the consequence follows.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "The Tenth Schedule makes the presiding officer an adjudicator but leaves much of the procedure to House rules." Examine the procedural architecture of anti-defection adjudication and the safeguards it contains.

**Model solution:** Paragraph 6(1) refers any question of disqualification to the Chairman or Speaker, whose decision is declared final; where the presiding officer is personally concerned, the House elects a member to decide. Paragraph 6(2) deems these proceedings to be proceedings of the House under Articles 122 and 212. Beyond this, the Schedule delegates procedure: paragraph 8 empowers the presiding officer to frame rules on party-affiliation registers, condonation and admission reports, and the procedure and inquiry for deciding paragraph 6 questions. The rules are laid for thirty days and may be modified or disapproved by the House, and wilful contravention may be treated as breach of privilege. Under such rules, a case begins only on a member's petition, the member concerned is given a chance to explain, and the matter may be referred to the Committee of Privileges — so defection has no automatic effect. Three safeguards follow. First, natural justice is built into the rules. Second, the House retains control over the rules themselves. Third, *Kihoto Hollohan* treats the presiding officer as a tribunal, so finality excludes appeal but not judicial review, and paragraph 6(2) shields only procedural irregularity. The architecture's weakness is structural: the adjudicator ordinarily comes from one of the contending sides, and strategic delay can defeat the procedure — a gap the courts have had to address.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Paragraph 6(1), proviso and 6(2) correctly explained — 4; paragraph 8 rule-making and laying — 3; petition-hearing-reference sequence — 3; safeguards including *Kihoto* limits — 3; structural weakness identified — 1; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

"Final" in paragraph 6 and "no court shall have any jurisdiction" in paragraph 7 suggest that the decider's word is the end. The Supreme Court decided otherwise — and how it did so is the most important single case in this topic.

### The learner's problem

Open the Constitution and paragraph 7 of the Tenth Schedule still says that no court shall have any jurisdiction in any matter connected with disqualification under the Schedule. Yet courts review Speakers' defection decisions all the time — the Telangana High Court did so in September 2026. How can printed text and legal reality diverge so completely? The answer lies in *how* the 52nd Amendment was passed.

### Visual: why paragraph 7 fell

```text
PARAGRAPH 7: "no court shall have any jurisdiction ..."
        |
        v
EFFECT: it would curtail the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts
(Articles 136, 226, 227)
        |
        v
PROVISO TO ARTICLE 368(2): an amendment making such a change must be
RATIFIED by the legislatures of not less than one-half of the States
        |
        v
52nd AMENDMENT BILL: passed by Parliament, but NOT sent for State ratification
        |
        v
KIHOTO HOLLOHAN (1992, 3:2): paragraph 7 INVALID; it is SEVERABLE;
rest of the Tenth Schedule UPHELD
        |
        v
CONSEQUENCE: presiding officer acts as a TRIBUNAL -> decision reviewable
```

*Caption: paragraph 7 failed on procedure, not on policy — it needed State ratification it never received — and its severance left the rest of the Schedule standing.*

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine a housing society that changes its bye-laws by majority of the managing committee, including a clause that no member may ever take a society dispute to court. Even if most bye-law changes need only the committee's vote, a clause that alters members' access to the courts may need the approval of the general body. If the committee skipped that step, only the court-ouster clause fails; the rest of the bye-laws survive. *Kihoto Hollohan* is that reasoning applied to a constitutional amendment.

### Technical doctrine: what *Kihoto Hollohan* decided

***Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu*** (Constitution Bench, judgment of 18 February 1992; majority 3:2):

1. **Paragraph 7 invalid.** It affected the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts, so the amendment required ratification under the **proviso to Article 368(2)**; without ratification, paragraph 7 was invalid.
2. **Severability.** Paragraph 7 was severable; the rest of the Tenth Schedule was **upheld** as valid.
3. **Tribunal.** The Speaker or Chairman, deciding a paragraph 6 question, functions as a **tribunal** exercising adjudicatory power.
4. **Finality.** "Final" in paragraph 6(1) excludes an appeal but **does not exclude judicial review**.
5. **Grounds of review.** Review is confined to jurisdictional errors such as **violation of the constitutional mandate, mala fides, non-compliance with natural justice and perversity** — not a re-hearing on the merits.
6. **Timing.** Review is ordinarily available only **after** the decision; no *quia timet* action (anticipatory proceedings) lies before it, and courts should not interfere at an interlocutory stage except where an interlocutory disqualification or suspension may have grave, immediate and irreversible consequences.
7. **Bias contention rejected.** The majority rejected the argument that vesting adjudicatory power in the presiding officer was itself invalid on the ground of likely political bias (the passage Laxmikant quotes about these functionaries holding "a pivotal position").

**The dissent (two judges):** the minority would have held that paragraph 7 could not be severed, so the whole amendment failed for want of ratification, and expressed doubt that a presiding officer whose tenure depends on the House majority could be an independent adjudicator.

### Step-by-step argument: why the majority's route matters

1. Striking down the whole Schedule would have abandoned the anti-defection project entirely.
2. Upholding paragraph 7 would have made a partisan decider unreviewable.
3. Severing paragraph 7 and classifying the decider as a tribunal achieved both goals: the law survives, and its decider is answerable to the courts.
4. Limiting review grounds and timing preserved the House's primary role.
5. **Cost:** review after the decision cannot help when there is no decision. That gap produced the delay jurisprudence of Lesson 11.

### Examples and their limits

- A Speaker disqualifies a member without giving her a hearing. **Reviewable** — breach of natural justice.
- A Speaker's order is reasoned and procedurally fair, but the member argues the Speaker should have weighed the evidence differently. **Ordinarily not a ground** — review is not a merits appeal.
- A member asks the High Court to stop the Speaker from even hearing the petition. **Ordinarily not maintainable** before the decision under the *Kihoto* timing rule.
- The 2026 Khairatabad ruling (Lesson 4): the Speaker's dismissal of the petitions was set aside on review and the Supreme Court declined to interfere — review operating against an order that *refused* to disqualify.

**Limit:** the grounds are stated generally; how strictly a court applies "perversity" in a given case varies, and the Khairatabad High Court's choice to declare the disqualification itself should not be generalised into a rule that courts routinely decide petitions on the merits.

### Comparison: printed text versus legal effect

| Provision | Printed text | Legal effect today |
|---|---|---|
| Paragraph 6(1) | "his decision shall be final" | No appeal; judicial review on limited grounds |
| Paragraph 6(2) | Deemed House proceedings (Arts 122/212) | Shields only procedural irregularity |
| Paragraph 7 | No court has jurisdiction | Invalid for want of ratification; severed |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection (the minority's concern):** "Calling a partisan Speaker a 'tribunal' does not make him impartial. The Court should have struck down the decision-making arrangement itself."

**Reply:** The majority did not assume impartiality; it supplied a check on its absence. By subjecting the tribunal to review for mala fides, perversity and breach of natural justice, it ensured that partisanship could be corrected by courts without discarding the entire scheme that Parliament had unanimously adopted.

**Qualified residual:** The check is reactive. A Speaker who simply sits on a petition has made no decision to review, and a decision made after the political moment has passed — a government already toppled or saved — may be reviewable but practically irreversible. The dissent's concern therefore survives in a new form: not bias in deciding, but bias in *timing*.

### UPSC application

*Kihoto Hollohan* is the anchor case for every Mains answer on the adjudicator. State its four propositions compactly — paragraph 7 invalid for want of ratification; the rest upheld; Speaker as tribunal; limited review after decision — and then use the timing residual to move to *Rajendra Singh Rana* and *Keisham*. Prelims traps: "paragraph 7 was deleted by amendment" (it is still printed; it was held invalid); "the Speaker's decision cannot be challenged in any court" (it can, on limited grounds); "*Kihoto* struck down the whole Tenth Schedule" (it upheld everything except paragraph 7).

### Revision notes

1. Paragraph 7 purports to bar all courts from Tenth Schedule disqualification matters.
2. *Kihoto Hollohan* (18 February 1992, 3:2) held paragraph 7 invalid for want of State ratification under the proviso to Article 368(2).
3. Paragraph 7 was severed; the rest of the Schedule was upheld.
4. The presiding officer acts as a tribunal under paragraph 6.
5. "Final" excludes appeal, not judicial review.
6. Review grounds: violation of constitutional mandate, mala fides, breach of natural justice, perversity.
7. Review ordinarily follows the decision; no quia timet action; interlocutory interference only in grave, irreversible cases.
8. The majority rejected the claim that the arrangement was invalid merely because of possible bias.
9. The two dissenting judges would have invalidated the whole amendment and doubted the Speaker's independence.
10. Review cannot cure non-decision — the gap addressed in Lesson 11.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did paragraph 7 fail while paragraph 6's "final" decision survived?

**Model answer:** Paragraph 7 sought to remove the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts altogether, which required State ratification under the proviso to Article 368(2) that was never obtained, so it was invalid; paragraph 6's finality, by contrast, was read as excluding only an appeal, leaving judicial review intact, so it raised no ratification problem and survived.

**Misconception to avoid:** Thinking *Kihoto* struck down paragraph 7 because ousting courts is substantively wrong in itself; the decisive reason was the missing ratification required for amendments affecting the higher judiciary's jurisdiction.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "*Kihoto Hollohan* saved the Tenth Schedule by narrowing it." Examine.

**Model solution:** The 52nd Amendment gave the presiding officer a "final" decision under paragraph 6 and, through paragraph 7, barred every court from disqualification matters. Because paragraph 7 curtailed the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts, it needed ratification by half the States under the proviso to Article 368(2), which the Bill never received. In *Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* (1992), a 3:2 majority held paragraph 7 invalid but severable and upheld the rest of the Schedule. It saved the scheme in two ways. First, it avoided the dissent's conclusion that the entire amendment fell with paragraph 7. Second, it answered the strongest objection to the scheme — a partisan, unreviewable decider — by classifying the Speaker as a tribunal whose decision, though final against appeal, is reviewable for violation of the constitutional mandate, mala fides, breach of natural justice and perversity. The narrowing is equally clear. The House lost its claimed immunity from courts, and paragraph 6(2)'s deemed-proceedings shield was confined to procedural irregularity. Yet the Court also limited its own role: review ordinarily follows the decision, with no quia timet action and only exceptional interlocutory interference. That self-restraint left a gap — a Speaker who does not decide escapes review — which later cases on delay had to fill. The statement is therefore accurate, with the qualification that *Kihoto*'s narrowing protected legality more effectively than timeliness.

### The learner's problem

Suppose a member defects in the first year of a five-year term, a petition is filed at once, and the Speaker — from the party that gained the defector — simply never lists it. Under *Kihoto*, the courts review decisions after they are made. If no decision is ever made, the member serves the full term, and the law has been defeated without anyone breaking its text. This lesson shows how the courts closed that gap — partially.

### Visual: the time-control hierarchy

*Caption: the time limit you can cite depends on the source — text, rules, general judicial norm, case-specific order or mere proposal — and an answer must never blur them.*

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine a referee who is supposed to rule on a disputed goal but simply walks off the field until the match ends. The result stands not because the goal was valid but because nobody ruled. A rule that only allows appeals *against rulings* is useless against a referee who never rules. Courts dealing with Tenth Schedule delay had to invent the equivalent of a rule that says: refusing to rule is itself a reviewable failure.

### Technical doctrine: the case-law sequence

1. ***Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya*** (2007). The Uttar Pradesh Speaker accepted a claimed "split" of a party's MLAs while not deciding the disqualification petition already pending against some of them. The Supreme Court held that the Speaker's failure to decide the petition was a failure to exercise jurisdiction — inaction could not be used to defeat the Schedule — and, in the circumstances of that case, itself declared the MLAs concerned disqualified.
2. ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly*** (21 January 2020). The Court held that the Speaker, as a tribunal, must decide disqualification petitions within a **reasonable period**, which, absent exceptional circumstances for which there is good reason, should ordinarily be **three months from the date of filing**. It also urged Parliament to consider replacing the Speaker as decider with a **permanent tribunal** headed by a retired Supreme Court judge or retired High Court Chief Justice, or another outside independent mechanism.
3. ***Subhash Desai*** (2023) reiterated that disqualification petitions must be decided within a reasonable period (Lesson 12).
4. **Telangana (31 July 2025).** After a High Court Division Bench had set aside a single judge's direction to fix a hearing schedule, the Supreme Court set the Division Bench order aside and directed the Telangana Speaker to adjudicate the petitions against ten MLAs within three months. The Speaker later dismissed the petitions; the sequel for one of those MLAs is the 2026 ruling in Lesson 4.

### Step-by-step argument: why courts moved from decisions to non-decisions

1. *Kihoto* allowed review only after a decision, to respect the House's primary role.
2. A decider who never decides makes that limitation an escape route.
3. A tribunal that refuses to exercise its jurisdiction commits a jurisdictional error — the same category of error *Kihoto* already made reviewable.
4. Therefore courts may direct a decision within a reasonable time and, in exceptional cases, act themselves.
5. **Cost:** courts must now decide what "reasonable" means without any text to guide them, which is why *Keisham* stated a general three-month norm and urged Parliament to legislate a lasting solution.

### Examples and their limits

- A petition filed in January is still unheard in December, with no reason recorded. **Keisham's ordinary three-month norm** has been exceeded; a court may direct a decision.
- A petition involving complex questions of party identity, with genuine hearings ongoing, takes five months. **Exceptional circumstances** may justify departure from the ordinary norm if reasons exist.

**Limit:** the three-month figure is a judicially stated *ordinary outer limit*, not text inserted by constitutional amendment and not a statutory deadline. Cite it as *Keisham*'s norm, and cite case-specific periods (such as the Telangana order) as directions in those cases.

### Comparison: three ways a court can respond to delay

| Response | Example | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declare inaction a jurisdictional failure and decide | *Rajendra Singh Rana* (2007) | Ends the delay decisively | Court acts as first-instance decider, in tension with *Kihoto*'s design |
| State a general reasonable-time norm | *Keisham* (2020) | Guides every Speaker | No automatic sanction if ignored |
| Issue a case-specific deadline | Telangana (2025) | Binding in that case | Must be litigated case by case |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If Speakers can be told when to decide, the House has lost its autonomy over its own membership."

**Reply:** The courts have not told Speakers *how* to decide, only that they must decide. A constitutional duty to adjudicate is meaningless if it can be performed by not performing it; insisting on timely decision protects the Schedule that Parliament itself enacted.

**Qualified residual:** Timeliness is only half the problem. The Telangana sequence shows that a Speaker directed to decide can decide promptly *and* in a way later set aside on review. Courts can compel speed, and they can review substance afterwards, but neither guarantees an impartial first decision — which is why the identity of the decider remains in the reform debate.

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how the Supreme Court has responded when presiding officers do not decide disqualification petitions promptly.

**Model solution:** *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992) made the presiding officer's decision reviewable but only after it was made, so non-decision became an escape route. In *Rajendra Singh Rana* (2007), the Court treated the Uttar Pradesh Speaker's failure to decide a pending petition, while accepting a split claim, as a failure to exercise jurisdiction and itself declared the MLAs disqualified. *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020) generalised the duty: the Speaker must decide within a reasonable period, ordinarily three months from filing absent exceptional circumstances, and Parliament should consider an independent tribunal. *Subhash Desai* (2023) reiterated the reasonable-period requirement, and on 31 July 2025 the Court directed the Telangana Speaker to decide petitions against ten MLAs within three months. These responses compel decision without dictating outcomes; they remain judicial norms and case-specific orders rather than constitutional text.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** *Kihoto* gap identified — 2; *Rana* correctly used — 2; *Keisham* norm and tribunal suggestion — 3; later reiteration/Telangana direction — 2; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

Delay is one way to manipulate the decider. Two more manipulations remain: disabling the Speaker through a removal notice, and capturing the party's voice inside the House. Both reached the Constitution Bench in the Maharashtra crisis.

## Lesson 12

### The learner's problem

Members facing disqualification have discovered two more ways to defeat the Tenth Schedule without breaking its words. One is to give notice of a resolution to remove the Speaker, then argue that a Speaker under such a cloud cannot decide their petitions. The other is to claim that *they* are the party — appointing their own whip and treating the original party's members as the rebels. The 2022 Maharashtra crisis raised both at once, and the Constitution Bench's answer in 2023 is now the most cited modern authority on the Schedule.

### Plain-language intuition

Think of a company dispute in which a breakaway group of directors claims to be the real board. Who is a validly appointed officer is one question (company law), who owns the brand name is another (trademark law), and whether the company can still trade is a third (the market). A good legal system lets each forum answer its own question without pretending that one answer settles the others. *Subhash Desai* applies that discipline to a party split.

### Technical doctrine: *Nabam Rebia* and the pending reference

- ***Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker, Arunachal Pradesh Legislative Assembly*** (Constitution Bench, 2016) held that a Speaker should not adjudicate disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule while a notice of intention to move a resolution for the Speaker's own removal is pending.
- **The risk it addressed:** a Speaker might disqualify the very members seeking his removal, deciding a dispute in which he has a personal stake.
- **The risk it created:** members facing disqualification can give a removal notice precisely to freeze the Speaker's power to decide.
- In ***Subhash Desai*** (2023), the Constitution Bench **referred the correctness of *Nabam Rebia* to a larger bench of seven judges**.
- **Status:** as recorded in a legal analysis published on 21 August 2026, the reference remained pending and had not been taken up for hearing; no later merits ruling has been found. *Nabam Rebia* therefore remains the operative precedent until the larger bench decides.

### Technical doctrine: what *Subhash Desai* decided (11 May 2023)

| Issue | Holding |
|---|---|
| Who appoints the Whip and Leader | The **political party**, not the legislature party; the Speaker must recognise the party's authorised appointees |
| How the Speaker identifies the party | By reference to the party's constitution and organisational structure, after inquiry — not merely by counting legislators |
| Split as a defence | Not available after the omission of paragraph 3 |
| Time | Disqualification petitions must be decided within a reasonable period |
| Election Commission | May decide the Symbols Order dispute **concurrently**; pending disqualification petitions do not bar it |
| Governor | The call for a floor test in the circumstances lacked objective material; but the earlier government could not be restored because the Chief Minister had resigned without facing the floor test |
| *Nabam Rebia* | Referred to a seven-judge bench |

What the Court did **not** do: it did not itself disqualify any member, did not restore the former government, and did not decide the merits of the symbol dispute.

### Step-by-step argument: why separate tracks?

1. The Tenth Schedule asks whether an individual member is disqualified; the Symbols Order asks which group is entitled to a party's name and symbol; a floor test asks whether a ministry commands confidence.
2. Each question has a different decider, different evidence and a different legal source.
3. If a decision on one track automatically settled another, the fastest forum would effectively decide all three — inviting forum manipulation.
4. Keeping them concurrent but distinct prevents that, and anchoring whip authority in the political party prevents a legislative faction from rewriting the Tenth Schedule question from inside the House.
5. **Cost:** concurrent tracks can produce apparently inconsistent results, and the timing of each can still influence the political outcome.

### Examples and their limits

- A faction persuades a Speaker to recognise its nominee as whip, then seeks to disqualify the original party's members for defying "the whip". After *Subhash Desai*, the Speaker must first identify which whip the **political party** authorised; the factional whip cannot be assumed valid merely because it has legislative numbers.
- The Election Commission awards a party's symbol to one group while disqualification petitions are pending before the Speaker. **Permissible** — the tracks are concurrent; the symbol decision does not by itself decide any member's disqualification.

**Limit:** *Subhash Desai* settled where whip authority lies and how the Speaker must approach party identity; it did not settle every factual dispute about which group is the political party in a particular case. That inquiry remains fact-specific and reviewable.

### Comparison: two manipulations and two answers

| Factional capture of the whip | *Subhash Desai*: political party appoints the whip | Settled by the Constitution Bench |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "*Nabam Rebia* protects members against a Speaker who would judge his own cause. Overruling it would let a Speaker facing removal disqualify his accusers."

**Reply:** The opposing view is that removal notices can be given strategically, and that a rule allowing a notice alone to freeze adjudication hands members a simple tool to suspend the Tenth Schedule whenever they need time. Neither risk is imaginary, which is precisely why the question was referred to a larger bench rather than resolved by a smaller one.

**Qualified residual:** Until the seven-judge bench decides, the safe answer is to state *Nabam Rebia*'s rule, state the 2023 reference, and present both risks — partisan self-protection by the Speaker and strategic paralysis by members — without predicting the outcome.

**Question:** Why does the Election Commission's decision on which group gets a party's symbol not automatically decide whether particular legislators are disqualified?

**Model answer:** Because the two questions arise on different legal tracks: the symbol dispute is decided by the ECI under the Symbols Order, while disqualification is decided by the presiding officer under paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule on the member's own conduct; *Subhash Desai* held the two may proceed concurrently, and neither forum's decision substitutes for the other's.

## Lesson 13

### The learner's problem

By the late 1990s two defects were obvious. Group defections were being dressed up as one-third "splits", and defectors were being rewarded with ministerial berths in oversized ministries. The 91st Amendment attacked both at once — the loophole and the reward. Learners often remember only "15 per cent" and miss the architecture; this lesson builds the whole of it.

### Visual: two attacks in one amendment

```text
CONSTITUTION (NINETY-FIRST AMENDMENT) ACT, 2003  (Tenth Schedule changes effective 1 January 2004)
        |
        +--> ATTACK ON THE LOOPHOLE
        |       Tenth Schedule paragraph 3 (one-third split) OMITTED
        |
        +--> ATTACK ON THE REWARD
                |
                +--> SIZE CAP
                |      Art 75(1A): Union Council of Ministers, incl. PM, <= 15% of Lok Sabha strength
                |      Art 164(1A): State Council of Ministers, incl. CM, <= 15% of Assembly strength,
                |                   but not fewer than 12
                |

                       Art 75(1B): cannot be appointed a Union Minister
                       Art 164(1B): cannot be appointed a State Minister

                       DURATION: from disqualification until the term would have expired,
                                 or until re-elected, whichever is earlier
```

*Caption: the amendment closed the easiest group exit and shrank the prizes that made defection profitable.*

### Plain-language intuition

If people keep jumping a fence to reach a reward on the other side, you can raise the fence, shrink the reward, or both. The 52nd Amendment built the fence. By 2003, a gate in it (the one-third split) was being used constantly, and the reward behind it (ministerial office) had grown, because governments formed by defection often expanded their ministries to accommodate defectors. The 91st Amendment locked the gate and shrank the reward.

### Technical doctrine: the provisions

| Provision | Content |
|---|---|
| Tenth Schedule, para 3 | Omitted; consequential changes in paragraphs 1 and 2 |
| Article 75(1A) | Total number of Ministers, including the Prime Minister, not to exceed **15 per cent** of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha |
| Article 75(1B) | A member of either House of Parliament belonging to a political party who is disqualified under paragraph 2 is also disqualified to be appointed a Minister for the specified period |
| Article 164(1A) | Total number of Ministers, including the Chief Minister, not to exceed **15 per cent** of the total number of members of the Legislative Assembly, provided the number is **not less than twelve** |
| Article 164(1B) | Corresponding bar on appointment as a State Minister |
| Article 361B | A member disqualified under paragraph 2 is disqualified to hold any **remunerative political post** for the specified period |

**Remunerative political post** (as Laxmikant summarises Article 361B): any office under the Central or a State Government whose salary or remuneration is paid out of that government's public revenue, or any office under a body wholly or partly owned by the Central or a State Government whose salary or remuneration is paid by that body — **except** where the salary or remuneration is compensatory in nature.

**Duration of the bars:** from the date of disqualification until the date on which the member's term would have expired, or, if the member contests an election to a House before then, until the date on which he is declared elected — **whichever is earlier**.

### Step-by-step argument: the reform lineage

1. The **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)**, the **Law Commission's 170th Report (1999)** and the **NCRWC (2002)** each recommended omitting the split exception, according to the reasons recorded for the amendment.
2. The **NCRWC** also proposed that a defector be debarred from ministerial office or any remunerative political post for at least the remaining term or until fresh election — the origin of Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B.
3. The NCRWC further observed that abnormally large ministries were being formed and proposed a ceiling of **10 per cent** of the popular House.
4. Parliament adopted the idea of a ceiling but fixed it at **15 per cent**, with a floor of **12** for States so that small States could still form functioning ministries.

The gap between the recommended 10 per cent and the enacted 15 per cent is a favourite Prelims trap — know which number belongs to which source.

### Examples and their limits

- A State Assembly has 60 members. Fifteen per cent is 9, but the floor is 12, so the ministry may have **up to 12** members, including the Chief Minister.
- A State Assembly has 200 members. The ministry may have **up to 30**.
- An MLA disqualified for defection in year two of a five-year term cannot be appointed a minister — unless he contests a by-election and is declared elected, at which point the bar ends.

**Limit:** the bars attach to a member "disqualified under paragraph 2" — they follow from an actual disqualification decision. A member who resigns *before* any disqualification (Lesson 14) is not automatically caught by them, which is why the resignation route remains attractive.

### Comparison: 52nd versus 91st

| Question | 52nd Amendment (1985) | 91st Amendment (2003) |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Created the anti-defection scheme | Tightened it |
| Schedule effect | Inserted the Tenth Schedule | Omitted paragraph 3 |
| Articles touched | 101, 102, 190, 191 | 75, 164; new 361B; Tenth Schedule |
| Group exception | One-third split and two-thirds merger | Only two-thirds merger |
| Reward controls | None specific | Ministry caps; ministerial and remunerative-post bars |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "A 15 per cent cap is arbitrary and interferes with a Prime Minister's or Chief Minister's freedom to form a government."

**Reply:** The cap targets a specific abuse identified by the NCRWC — oversized ministries used to reward defectors and allies. A ceiling linked to House strength still leaves ample room to govern, and the floor of 12 protects small States. The restriction is proportionate to the mischief.

**Qualified residual:** The cap limits *ministerial* posts, but governments can create other rewards. Article 361B reaches remunerative political posts only for members actually disqualified under paragraph 2; it does not prevent the appointment of non-disqualified allies to other positions, and it does not reach a member who resigned before disqualification.

### UPSC application

For Prelims, keep five numbers and labels exact: **2003** Act, effective **1 January 2004** for the Tenth Schedule changes; **15 per cent**; **minimum 12** for States; **Article 361B** for remunerative political posts; and the "whichever is earlier" duration rule. Do not attribute the 10 per cent figure to the enacted text. For Mains, use the "loophole and reward" framing to show that the 2003 reform attacked the incentives behind defection, not only its form.

### Revision notes

1. The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 omitted paragraph 3 (split) with effect from 1 January 2004.
2. Article 75(1A): Union ministry, including the PM, at most 15 per cent of Lok Sabha strength.
3. Article 164(1A): State ministry, including the CM, at most 15 per cent of Assembly strength, not fewer than 12.
4. Articles 75(1B) and 164(1B): a member disqualified under paragraph 2 cannot be appointed a minister for the specified period.
5. Article 361B: such a member cannot hold a remunerative political post for the same period.
6. Remunerative political post: government-paid office or office in a government-owned body, excluding compensatory remuneration.
7. Duration: until the term would have expired or until re-election, whichever is earlier.
8. Goswami (1990), Law Commission 170th Report (1999) and NCRWC (2002) recommended omitting the split exception.
9. NCRWC proposed a 10 per cent ministry ceiling; the enacted figure is 15 per cent.
10. The bars follow actual disqualification; resignation before disqualification escapes them.

### Concept check

**Question:** A member disqualified for defection in 2024 contests a by-election in 2025 and wins. Can he be appointed a minister in 2025, although the original term of the House runs until 2028?

**Model answer:** Yes. Under Articles 75(1B)/164(1B), the bar runs from disqualification until the original term would expire or until he is declared elected in an election contested before then, whichever is earlier; his 2025 victory ends the bar, so he may be appointed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the office bar as lasting for the full remaining term in every case; re-election before the term ends terminates it.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how the 91st Amendment attacked both the loophole and the reward structure of defection.

**Model solution:** By 2003 two defects were evident: group defections were being presented as one-third "splits" under paragraph 3, and defectors were rewarded with posts in oversized ministries. The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 addressed both. It omitted paragraph 3, leaving the two-thirds merger as the only group defence — a change recommended by the Goswami Committee, the Law Commission's 170th Report and the NCRWC. It then attacked the reward. Articles 75(1A) and 164(1A) capped ministries at 15 per cent of the popular House, with a minimum of 12 in States. Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) and 361B barred a member disqualified under paragraph 2 from ministerial office and remunerative political posts until the term would have expired or re-election, whichever is earlier. The limitation is that the bars follow actual disqualification, so resignation before disqualification remained a route around them.

## Lesson 14

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

A group of legislators wants to bring down their own party's government. Defecting would invite disqualification and office bars. So instead they resign their seats. The House's strength falls, the government loses its majority on a floor test, a new government is formed, and the former legislators contest by-elections — often on the new ruling party's ticket — and some become ministers. Has the Tenth Schedule been defeated? This lesson answers with a precise "partly".

### Visual: the chronology test

```text
CASE 1: DEFECTION FIRST, RESIGNATION LATER
  act of defection (e.g. joining rival camp) ---> petition filed ---> resignation submitted
        |
        v
  Disqualification relates to the DATE OF DEFECTION.
  Resignation does NOT remove the Speaker's jurisdiction to decide the petition.
  (Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil, 2019)

CASE 2: RESIGNATION FIRST, NO PRIOR DISQUALIFYING ACT
  genuine, voluntary resignation accepted ---> seat vacant
        |
        v
  No Tenth Schedule disqualification; no 91st-Amendment office bar.
  Member may contest the by-election and, if elected, hold office.

IN BOTH CASES: the Speaker cannot impose an extra ban on contesting elections.
```

*Caption: whether resignation helps depends on what happened first — resignation cannot wash away an earlier act of defection, but a genuine resignation before any such act is simply a resignation.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of an employee who has already taken a rival's job offer and started working for the rival, and only then submits a resignation letter to escape a disciplinary inquiry. The inquiry can still decide what he did before resigning. But an employee who genuinely resigns before doing anything wrong has done nothing to be disciplined for. The Tenth Schedule draws the same line.

### Technical doctrine: *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil* (2019)

In ***Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly*** (judgment of 13 November 2019), arising from the 2019 Karnataka crisis:

1. **Resignation and disqualification are distinct.** A resignation is accepted under Article 190 (or Article 101 for Parliament) after the presiding officer is satisfied that it is voluntary and genuine; disqualification is adjudicated under the Tenth Schedule.
2. **Resignation does not erase prior defection.** Where a disqualification petition concerns conduct that occurred before the resignation, the Speaker retains jurisdiction to decide it; disqualification relates to the date of the disqualifying act.
3. **No extra punishment.** The Speaker has no power to bar a disqualified member from contesting elections until the end of the House's term. The Constitution supplies only the consequences it states — loss of the seat, and the 91st-Amendment office bars for the period specified.
4. **Disqualification of the Karnataka members was upheld**, but the ban on contesting until the end of the term was set aside, so they could contest by-elections.

### Technical doctrine: expulsion

- **Party expulsion** is not a ground of disqualification (Lesson 4). Under *G. Viswanathan*, an expelled member continues to belong to the original party for the Schedule and attracts ground (a) if he joins another party.
- **House expulsion** for breach of privilege is a separate power of the House, unrelated to defection.
- Laxmikant records the criticism that the Schedule does not provide for expelling a legislator from his party for activities outside the legislature — party discipline and constitutional disqualification remain distinct instruments.

### Step-by-step argument: why the resignation route still works

1. The Tenth Schedule punishes defection, not resignation.
2. A genuine resignation before any disqualifying act creates a vacancy without disqualification.
3. Without disqualification, the 91st-Amendment office bars never attach.
4. Resignations reduce the House's effective strength, lowering the majority needed on a floor test.
5. So a coordinated resignation can change a government without any member being disqualified, provided no prior act of defection can be proved.
6. **The law's answer is partial:** *Shrimanth* captures cases where defection came first, but cannot reach a genuinely prior resignation.

### Examples and their limits

- **Karnataka (2019):** members resigned after earlier conduct alleged to amount to defection; the Speaker disqualified them, *Shrimanth* upheld the disqualification but allowed them to contest by-elections.
- **Madhya Pradesh (2020):** a large group of MLAs resigned, reducing the House's strength before a floor test — the pattern critics call "resign-and-recontest".

**Limit:** describing a particular episode as an "engineered" collapse is political criticism, not a legal finding. In a Mains answer, present the resignation route as a structural weakness of the law and cite *Shrimanth* for the legal position, without asserting motives as facts.

### Comparison: consequences that exist and those that do not

| Consequence | Exists? | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Loss of seat on disqualification | Yes | Articles 101/190 with 102(2)/191(2) |
| Bar on appointment as minister | Yes, for the specified period | Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) |
| Bar on remunerative political post | Yes, for the specified period | Article 361B |
| Bar on contesting elections | No | *Shrimanth* rejected a Speaker-imposed ban |
| Criminal liability for defecting | No | Not created by the Schedule |
| Permanent disqualification | No | — |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Allowing disqualified defectors to contest by-elections immediately makes the law toothless. They lose their seats for a few months and come back as ministers."

**Reply:** Re-election means the voters have now endorsed the member on the new ticket — the very thing the Tenth Schedule was designed to require. The law's purpose was to force defectors back to the electorate, not to exclude them from politics. A Speaker-imposed ban would add a penalty the Constitution does not contain.

**Qualified residual:** The reply assumes by-elections are a genuine fresh mandate. Critics argue that by-elections held after a change of government favour the new ruling party, and that the resignation route lets members avoid even the temporary office bars. The Schedule controls defection inside the House; it cannot control coordinated exits from it.

### UPSC application

Mains answers on the "outer limits" of the anti-defection law should use this lesson's chronology test and *Shrimanth* to show what the law can and cannot reach. Prelims traps: "resignation automatically ends a pending disqualification petition" (not if the alleged defection came first); "a disqualified member cannot contest until the end of the term" (the Speaker cannot impose such a ban); "party expulsion disqualifies a member" (it does not).

1. Resignation (Articles 101/190) and disqualification (Articles 102(2)/191(2)) are distinct mechanisms.
2. *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil* (13 November 2019): resignation does not remove jurisdiction over a prior act of defection.
3. Disqualification relates to the date of the disqualifying act.
4. The Speaker cannot bar a disqualified member from contesting elections until the term ends.
5. The Karnataka members' disqualification was upheld but they could contest by-elections.
6. A genuine resignation before any disqualifying act attracts neither disqualification nor the office bars.
7. "Resign-and-recontest" (Karnataka 2019, Madhya Pradesh 2020) exploits this gap.
8. Party expulsion is not a ground; *G. Viswanathan* keeps the expelled member bound to the original party.
9. The Schedule creates no criminal liability and no permanent disqualification.
10. Describe engineered collapses as criticism, not as legal findings.

### Concept check

**Question:** An MLA resigns his seat on Monday. On Wednesday a petition is filed alleging that, the previous month, he had joined a rival party's government-formation talks and publicly declared support for it. Can the Speaker still decide the petition?

**Model answer:** Yes. Under *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil*, a later resignation does not remove the Speaker's jurisdiction where the alleged disqualifying conduct occurred earlier; the petition can be decided on the earlier conduct, and if disqualification is established it relates to the date of that conduct, with the 91st-Amendment office bars following.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that resignation always ends the matter; the chronology of the alleged defection and the resignation decides whether the petition survives.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish resignation from disqualification under the Constitution, and explain why resignation is not an escape from an antecedent act of defection.

**Model solution:** Resignation is a member's voluntary exit, accepted under Article 101 or 190 after the presiding officer is satisfied it is genuine; it creates a vacancy without any finding of wrongdoing. Disqualification under Articles 102(2)/191(2) and the Tenth Schedule follows an adjudication that the member gave up party membership, defied a whip, or, as an independent or late-joining nominated member, joined a party. In *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil* (2019), the Supreme Court held that where the alleged defection preceded the resignation, the Speaker retains jurisdiction and disqualification relates to the date of the act; resignation cannot wash it away. Disqualification also attracts the 91st Amendment's bars on ministerial and remunerative political office. The Court added a limit: the Speaker cannot bar the member from contesting elections. A genuine resignation before any disqualifying act, however, remains outside the Schedule's reach.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Resignation and disqualification correctly distinguished — 3; *Shrimanth* holding on antecedent defection — 3; office-bar link — 1; no-extra-ban limit — 1; remaining gap noted — 1; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

Many students confuse the Tenth Schedule with other routes by which a legislator can lose a seat. The next lesson draws those boundaries deliberately.

## Lesson 15

Progress: 15/18 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Neighbouring regimes — the disqualifications that are not anti-defection

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Laxmikant's note that the 52nd Amendment changed Articles 101, 102, 190 and 191 "which relate to the vacation of seats and disqualification"; paragraph 8(3) privilege link
CA search: "anti-defection law Tenth Schedule Supreme Court 2026 judgment Speaker disqualification" (session-wide search)
CA found: none specific to the comparative boundaries
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

A legislator can lose a seat for holding an office of profit, for a criminal conviction, for defection, by resigning, or by being expelled for contempt of the House. A party dispute can also go to the Election Commission over a symbol. Exam questions love to take a correct fact from one of these regimes and attach it to another — the right decider with the wrong ground, or the right ground with the wrong decider. This lesson builds a firewall between them.

### Visual: the disqualification sorting tree

```text
WHY MIGHT THE MEMBER LOSE THE SEAT?
        |
        +--> Office of profit / unsound mind / insolvency / citizenship / disqualification by law (e.g. RPA conviction)
        |        -> Article 102(1) or 191(1)
        |        -> question referred to the PRESIDENT / GOVERNOR
        |        -> who must obtain and act according to the ELECTION COMMISSION's opinion (Art 103 / Art 192)
        |
        +--> Defection under the Tenth Schedule
        |        -> Article 102(2) or 191(2)
        |        -> decided by the SPEAKER / CHAIRMAN (paragraph 6)
        |        -> no role for ECI, President or Governor
        |
        +--> Voluntary resignation -> Article 101 / 190 -> presiding officer accepts if voluntary and genuine
        |
        +--> Contempt / breach of privilege -> House's own privilege powers (Art 105 / 194)

SEPARATE QUESTION (not about the seat): WHICH GROUP GETS THE PARTY SYMBOL?
        -> Election Commission under the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968
```

*Caption: identify the ground first; the ground determines the Article, and the Article determines the decider.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of a hospital where different departments handle different conditions. A fracture goes to orthopaedics, a heart attack to cardiology. A patient with a fracture is not treated by cardiology merely because both are emergencies. Disqualification questions work the same way: two members may both lose their seats, but a defection goes to the Speaker and an office-of-profit question goes to the President on the Election Commission's opinion.

### Technical doctrine: the two disqualification routes compared

| Feature | Article 102(1)/191(1) grounds | Tenth Schedule (Article 102(2)/191(2)) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical grounds | Office of profit; unsound mind; undischarged insolvency; loss of citizenship; disqualification by a law of Parliament (including Representation of the People Act provisions) | Voluntarily giving up membership; defying a whip; independent joining a party; nominated member joining after six months |
| Who decides for a sitting member | President (Art 103) / Governor (Art 192) | Speaker/Chairman (paragraph 6) |
| Advisory role | Decider must obtain the **Election Commission's opinion** and act according to it | None |
| Finality | President's/Governor's decision final | Presiding officer's decision final, subject to *Kihoto* review |

The single most important sentence: **Articles 103 and 192 apply to the Article 102(1)/191(1) grounds; they do not apply to the Tenth Schedule.**

### Technical doctrine: the other neighbours

- **Symbols Order disputes.** Under paragraph 15 of the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968, the Election Commission decides which of rival groups of a recognised party is that party for the purpose of its name and reserved symbol. *Subhash Desai* (Lesson 12) held that this can proceed concurrently with Tenth Schedule petitions.
- **Privilege.** A House may punish contempt or breach of its privileges. Paragraph 8(3) allows wilful contravention of the defection *rules* to be dealt with as breach of privilege — but defection itself is not a breach of privilege, and privilege is not defection.
- **Floor test.** A vote of confidence decides whether a ministry survives; it does not decide whether any member is disqualified.
- **Party expulsion.** An internal party matter; not a ground of disqualification (Lessons 4, 14).

### Step-by-step argument: why the Tenth Schedule bypasses the Election Commission

1. Article 103 was designed for questions of status — office of profit, citizenship, insolvency — where an independent expert body's opinion helps.
2. The 52nd Amendment chose to keep defection questions inside the House, treating them as matters of the House's own composition (Lesson 9).
3. It therefore created a new decider (the presiding officer) rather than extending Article 103.
4. **Cost:** the more independent route (ECI opinion binding on the President/Governor) was not used for the most politically sensitive disqualification of all — which is why several reform bodies proposed applying an Article 103-type route to defection (Lesson 17).

### Examples and their limits

- An MLA is appointed chairman of a government corporation with a salary. The question is whether he holds an **office of profit** — Article 191(1), decided by the Governor on the Election Commission's opinion under Article 192.
- The same MLA later joins a rival party. That is a **Tenth Schedule** question — decided by the Speaker.
- Two factions claim the same party symbol. That is an **ECI Symbols Order** question — it does not decide either faction's members' disqualification.

**Limit:** some fact patterns raise several questions at once (an MLA might both hold an office of profit and defect). Each question must still be sent to its own decider; one decision does not dispose of the other.

### Comparison: the six-way distinction sheet

| Issue | Correct legal home |
|---|---|
| Joining another party / defying a whip | Tenth Schedule, paragraph 6 decider |
| Office of profit, insolvency, citizenship | Articles 102(1)/191(1); decided under Articles 103/192 with ECI opinion |
| Conviction-based disqualification | Representation of the People Act, read with Articles 102(1)(e)/191(1)(e) |
| Party symbol between rival groups | ECI, Symbols Order |
| Contempt of the House | Privilege powers |
| Leaving the seat voluntarily | Resignation |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "These boundaries are technicalities. All of them are about whether a legislator keeps a seat; the Constitution should have a single decider."

**Reply:** The boundaries reflect different questions requiring different expertise and different degrees of political insulation. Status questions benefit from the Election Commission's expertise; questions of party allegiance were deliberately kept within the House. A single decider would have to be designed for both, and the choice of who that should be is exactly the reform debate.

**Qualified residual:** The reply explains the design but not whether the design was wise for defection. The contrast with Article 103 is the strongest evidence for critics who argue that defection, too, should be decided by — or on the opinion of — an independent body.

### UPSC application

This lesson is the main defence against close-option traps in Prelims: a statement that mixes Article 103's decider with a Tenth Schedule ground, or the ECI's Symbols Order role with disqualification, should be read against the sorting tree before anything else. Several adjacent PYQs owned by other topics (office of profit, RPA disqualification, Speaker's office) sit on the other branches of this tree; they are listed by year in the final PYQ section so that you can see where the boundary falls.

### Revision notes

1. Articles 102(1)/191(1) list status-based grounds; Articles 102(2)/191(2) add the Tenth Schedule.
2. Articles 103/192: President/Governor decides Article 102(1)/191(1) questions after obtaining, and acting according to, the ECI's opinion.
3. Articles 103/192 do not apply to Tenth Schedule questions.
4. Tenth Schedule questions are decided by the Speaker/Chairman under paragraph 6.
5. Conviction-based disqualification arises under the RPA, read with Articles 102(1)(e)/191(1)(e).
6. Symbol disputes between rival groups go to the ECI under paragraph 15 of the Symbols Order, 1968.
7. Breach of privilege is a separate House power; paragraph 8(3) links only rule-breaches to privilege.
8. Floor tests decide confidence, not disqualification.
9. Identify the ground first; the ground fixes the Article and the decider.
10. Reform proposals borrow the Article 103 model for defection.

### Concept check

**Question:** An MP is alleged to hold an office of profit and, separately, to have defied his party's whip on a confidence motion. Who decides each question?

**Model answer:** The office-of-profit question falls under Article 102(1) and is decided by the President, who must obtain and act according to the Election Commission's opinion under Article 103; the whip question falls under Article 102(2) and the Tenth Schedule and is decided by the Speaker under paragraph 6. Each decision is independent of the other.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that the Election Commission has some role in every disqualification of a legislator; it has an advisory role only on the Article 102(1)/191(1) grounds.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "Not every disqualification of a legislator is an anti-defection disqualification." Explain with reference to the constitutional routes for deciding disqualification.

**Model solution:** The Constitution provides two routes. Articles 102(1) and 191(1) disqualify for status-based grounds — office of profit, unsound mind, undischarged insolvency, loss of citizenship, or disqualification under a law such as the Representation of the People Act. For a sitting member, Articles 103 and 192 refer such questions to the President or Governor, who must obtain the Election Commission's opinion and act according to it. Articles 102(2) and 191(2), added by the 52nd Amendment, disqualify under the Tenth Schedule for defection; paragraph 6 assigns these questions to the Speaker or Chairman, with no role for the Election Commission, President or Governor, subject to judicial review under *Kihoto Hollohan*. Separate again are resignation, privilege-based expulsion and the Election Commission's Symbols Order jurisdiction over rival groups. Identifying the ground first therefore determines both the Article and the decider.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Article 102(1)/103 route correctly explained — 3; Tenth Schedule route correctly explained — 3; ECI's limited role made explicit — 2; other neighbours noted — 1; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

The law's architecture, its deciders and its boundaries are now complete. The final block steps back to judge it.

### The learner's problem

"Critically examine the anti-defection law" is the most predictable Mains demand on this topic, and the most poorly answered — usually with a list of criticisms and no replies. A strong evaluation pairs every criticism with its best defence, uses named evidence for both, and reaches a graded verdict. This lesson assembles that evaluation from everything already taught.

### Visual: what the law achieved and what it displaced

```text
ACHIEVED                                      DISPLACED INTO
--------                                      --------------
individual floor-crossing became costly  -->  organised group action (merger claims)
party mandate given legal protection     -->  party leadership control over every whipped vote
defection made adjudicable, not automatic -->  delay and strategic timing by the decider
office rewards curbed (2003)             -->  resignation before disqualification
courts can review decisions (1992)       -->  review arrives after the political moment

NET EFFECT: the law changed the TECHNOLOGY of defection more than its INCENTIVES
```

*Caption: institutional rules redirect behaviour; the evaluation question is whether the redirected behaviour is less harmful than the original mischief.*

### Plain-language intuition

When a city bans parking on one street, cars move to the next street. The ban "worked" on the first street; whether it improved the city depends on what happened next door. The anti-defection law ended the old pattern of individual MLAs crossing for office — and pushed defection into mergers, resignations and delay. Evaluating it means judging both streets.

### Technical doctrine: advantages and criticisms (Laxmikant's framework)

**Advantages:**

1. Greater stability in the body politic by checking legislators' propensity to change parties.
2. It facilitates democratic realignment of parties in the legislature by way of merger.
3. It reduces political corruption and the non-developmental expenditure incurred on irregular elections.
4. It gives political parties a clear constitutional role in the legislative process.

**Criticisms:**

1. It does not distinguish **dissent** from **defection**, curbing the legislator's freedom of conscience — Soli J. Sorabjee's warning that it "puts party bossism on a pedestal".
2. Its distinction between individual and group defection is irrational — in Madhu Limaye's phrase, it "banned only retail defections and legalised wholesale defections".
3. It does not provide for the expulsion of a legislator from his party for activities outside the legislature.
4. Its distinction between independent and nominated members is said to be illogical.
5. Vesting the decision in the presiding officer is criticised because he may not act impartially due to political exigencies and may lack legal expertise — doubts voiced by Speakers Rabi Ray (1991) and Shivraj Patil (1993) themselves.

### Step-by-step argument: criticism paired with reply

| Criticism | Named evidence | Best reply | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dissent equals defection | Breadth of paragraph 2(1)(b) | Permission and condonation let parties tolerate dissent; speeches are untouched (Lesson 5) | No textual limit on which votes may be whipped |
| Retail banned, wholesale legalised | Former paragraph 3; paragraph 4's two-thirds rule | Split deleted in 2003; genuine party mergers deserve protection (Lessons 7, 13) | Headcount reading of paragraph 4 unresolved in 2026 |
| Partisan, slow decider | Rabi Ray, Shivraj Patil; *Keisham* | *Kihoto* review; *Rana* and *Keisham* compel decision (Lessons 10-11) | Review is after the event; first decision can still be partisan |
| Resignation route | Karnataka 2019, Madhya Pradesh 2020 | *Shrimanth* preserves jurisdiction over prior defection (Lesson 14) | Genuine prior resignation remains outside the law |
| Independent/nominated asymmetry | Paragraphs 2(2), 2(3) | Different sources of mandate (Lesson 6) | Six-month figure is a policy choice |

### Examples and their limits

- **Evidence of success:** the pre-1985 pattern of individual MLAs crossing repeatedly for office — the "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" era — is no longer the dominant mode of instability.
- **Evidence of displacement:** modern crises have run through merger claims (Lesson 7), contested whip authority (*Subhash Desai*, Lesson 12), strategic delay (*Keisham*, Telangana, Lesson 11) and resignations (Lesson 14).
- **Evidence that review works:** in September 2026 a Speaker's order refusing disqualification was set aside and the Supreme Court declined to interfere (Lesson 4).

**Limit:** "success" cannot be measured as a simple count of defections; the law changed the form in which instability appears, so before-and-after comparisons must compare forms, not just numbers.

### Comparison: three possible verdicts

| Verdict | Supporting argument | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| "The law has failed" | Defection continues through new routes | Ignores the decline of individual floor-crossing and the 2003 tightening |
| "The law has succeeded" | Retail defection curbed; parties strengthened | Ignores displacement and delay |
| "The law succeeded against its original mischief and generated new ones" | Fits both sets of evidence | Requires careful, evidence-backed balance |

The third verdict is the strongest because it explains both sets of facts; it is also the hardest to write well, because it requires named evidence on both sides.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "The law has made legislators servants of party high commands. Even its successes are bought at the cost of parliamentary deliberation."

**Reply:** Before 1985, deliberation was not the main casualty of floor-crossing; governments themselves were. Party cohesion is a precondition for parliamentary government, and the law's safety valves (permission, condonation) leave room for conscience if parties choose to use them.

**Qualified residual:** The reply is strongest for confidence and money votes and weakest for ordinary legislation, where whips serve party convenience rather than government survival. That asymmetry is the hinge of the reform debate in Lesson 17.

### UPSC application

A "critically examine" answer should move: purpose → achievements with evidence → criticisms with evidence → best replies → residuals → graded verdict. Quote no more than one phrase ("retail versus wholesale") and attribute it correctly. The commonest scoring loss is a list of criticisms with no replies; the second is a verdict that ignores the 2003 amendment and the post-1992 case law.

### Revision notes

1. Purpose: prevent defections motivated by the lure of office or material benefit (Laxmikant).
2. Advantages: stability; realignment by merger; reduced corruption and election costs; constitutional role for parties.
3. Criticism: no distinction between dissent and defection (Sorabjee: "party bossism").
4. Criticism: "banned only retail defections and legalised wholesale defections" (Madhu Limaye).
5. Criticism: no provision for expulsion for activities outside the legislature.
6. Criticism: independent/nominated asymmetry.
7. Criticism: presiding officer's partiality and lack of legal expertise (Rabi Ray 1991, Shivraj Patil 1993).
8. Replies: permission/condonation; 2003 deletion of split; *Kihoto*, *Rana*, *Keisham*; *Shrimanth*.
9. The law changed the technology of defection more than its incentives.
10. Best verdict: succeeded against retail defection; generated new forms of instability.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is "the number of defections has not fallen, so the law failed" a weak evaluative argument?

**Model answer:** Because the law was aimed at a particular form of defection — individual floor-crossing for office — and the evidence suggests that form declined while instability shifted into mergers, resignations and delayed adjudication; evaluation must compare forms and harms, not a raw count, and must credit the 2003 tightening and judicial controls.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating continued political instability as proof that every part of the law is ineffective; displacement is evidence of partial success as well as of new gaps.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess the claim that the Tenth Schedule changed the form of defection rather than its frequency.

**Model solution:** The Tenth Schedule, inserted in 1985, aimed at the post-1967 pattern of individual legislators crossing over for office. On that target it largely worked: once leaving a party or defying a whip cost the seat, individual floor-crossing became costly, and the 91st Amendment (2003) further removed rewards by capping ministries at 15 per cent and barring disqualified defectors from ministerial and remunerative political posts. Defection did not disappear, however; it changed form. Groups used the one-third split until 2003 and now invoke the two-thirds merger rule — whose headcount reading was still before the Supreme Court in 2026, validating Madhu Limaye's "retail versus wholesale" criticism. Members also resigned rather than defected, as in Karnataka (2019) and Madhya Pradesh (2020); *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil* preserved jurisdiction only over defection that preceded resignation. Presiding officers delayed petitions, prompting *Rajendra Singh Rana*, *Keisham Meghachandra*'s three-month norm and case-specific directions such as Telangana's in 2025. Yet these new forms are themselves more constrained than the old one: merger requires a two-thirds threshold, resignation forces a return to voters, and review increasingly corrects partisan decisions, as the 2026 Khairatabad ruling shows. The claim is therefore largely accurate but incomplete — the law changed both form and cost, curbing individual defection while leaving organised and procedural routes partly open.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Original target and success with 2003 reinforcement — 3; merger displacement with status — 3; resignation displacement with *Shrimanth* — 2; delay displacement with case law — 3; counterpoint that new forms are constrained — 2; graded verdict — 1; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

If the law has these gaps, what would fix them — and what would each fix cost?

## Lesson 17

Book context: queried — Laxmikant's account of the Goswami Committee (1990), Law Commission 170th Report (1999) and NCRWC (2002) recommendations recorded in the reasons for the 91st Amendment

CA found: no reform proposal has been enacted since 2003; the only 2026 development touching reform is the pending paragraph 4 petition (Lesson 7), a judicial rather than legislative route
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

Reform answers usually fail in one of two ways: they present a wish-list of proposals as if they were law, or they recommend one fix without noticing what it breaks. This lesson teaches each proposal by its source, its status, the problem it targets and the new problem it creates.

### Visual: the reform menu by target

```text
PROBLEM                         PROPOSAL                                   SOURCE / STATUS
Whip too broad            -->   confine whip-disqualification to           Goswami (1990); Law Commission
                                government-survival votes                   170th (1999)       -> proposal
Split loophole            -->   delete the split exception                 Goswami, LC 170th, NCRWC
                                                                           -> ENACTED (91st Amdt, 2003)
Merger loophole           -->   require a genuine party-level merger,      reform debate; pending
                                or redesign/delete paragraph 4             2026 petition    -> not law
Reward of office          -->   ministry cap; office bars                  NCRWC (2002)
                                                                           -> ENACTED (2003, at 15%)
Partisan or slow decider  -->   (i) President/Governor on ECI advice       Goswami; LC 255th (2015) -> proposal
                                (ii) permanent independent tribunal        Keisham (2020) -> suggestion
                                (iii) fixed deadline                        judicial norm only -> not text
Opaque party commands     -->   internal party democracy                   LC 170th; wider debate -> proposal
```

*Caption: only two reform strands — deleting the split and curbing office rewards — have been enacted; everything else is proposal or judicial norm.*

### Plain-language intuition

Reforming the anti-defection law is like adjusting a dam with several leaks. Plugging one leak raises pressure elsewhere: narrow the whip and defection on ordinary votes becomes free; tighten mergers and genuine party realignments become harder; move the decision away from the Speaker and you must trust some other institution with intensely political questions. Good reform proposals say which leak they plug and where the pressure goes.

### Technical doctrine: the proposals precisely

1. **Narrowing the whip.** The **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)** recommended confining disqualification for defying a whip to high-stakes votes — votes of confidence and no-confidence, and, as commonly reported from its recommendations, Money Bills and the motion of thanks on the President's Address. The **Law Commission's 170th Report (1999)** similarly favoured limiting whip-based disqualification to votes integral to the government's survival or programme. **Status:** proposal.
2. **Deleting the split exception.** Recommended by Goswami, the Law Commission's 170th Report and the NCRWC. **Status:** enacted by the 91st Amendment, 2003.
3. **Curbing rewards.** The NCRWC proposed barring defectors from ministerial office and remunerative political posts and a 10 per cent ministry ceiling. **Status:** enacted with a 15 per cent ceiling (Lesson 13).
4. **Changing the decider.** The Goswami Committee and the **Law Commission's 255th Report (2015)** proposed that the President or Governor decide defection questions on the Election Commission's advice — the Article 103 model (Lesson 15). *Keisham Meghachandra* (2020) urged Parliament to consider a permanent tribunal headed by a retired Supreme Court judge or retired High Court Chief Justice, or another independent mechanism. **Status:** proposals; no amendment.
5. **Time limits.** *Keisham*'s ordinary three-month norm and case-specific directions exist as judicial doctrine; a fixed constitutional or statutory deadline does not. **Status:** proposal.
6. **Merger redesign.** Requiring a genuine organisational merger, or removing paragraph 4, has been argued in reform debate; the 2026 petition seeks a judicial interpretation rather than an amendment. **Status:** not law.
7. **Internal party democracy.** Making party directions more legitimate through democratic party organisation is a complementary proposal. **Status:** proposal.

### Step-by-step argument: evaluating reforms by their trade-offs

| Reform | Problem solved | New risk created |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow whip to survival votes | Restores deliberation on ordinary legislation | More fluid majorities on non-confidence votes; lobbying of individual members |
| Merger redesign | Closes group-defection route | May obstruct genuine party realignments |
| Decider on ECI advice | Moves the decision outside the House's partisan majority | Shifts political pressure onto the ECI and the formal decider |
| Independent tribunal | Expertise and impartiality | Appointment design, legitimacy and House autonomy questions |
| Fixed deadline | Prevents strategic delay | Complex cases may need more time; deadline needs a sanction |
| Internal party democracy | Makes whips more legitimate | Hard to define and enforce without state control of parties |

### Examples and their limits

- If the whip were confined to confidence and money votes, an MLA could vote against her party on a land-acquisition amendment without risk to her seat — a gain for deliberation — but her party could no longer use the Tenth Schedule to hold together a coalition on ordinary legislation.
- If defection questions went to the Governor on the ECI's advice, the decision would leave the Speaker's hands — but the Governor's own role in floor tests (Lesson 12) is itself frequently contested, so the change would relocate rather than abolish suspicion of partisanship.

**Limit:** these examples illustrate trade-offs; they do not predict how any reform would work in practice.

### Comparison: which decider?

Which decider is best is a genuinely contested design choice. Weigh the options against four criteria rather than assuming an answer:

| Criterion | Presiding officer (present) | President/Governor on ECI advice | Independent tribunal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independence from the House majority | Low | Higher (depends on ECI and formal decider) | Potentially high (depends on appointment design) |
| Legal expertise | Variable | Moderate | High |
| Speed | Depends on judicial pressure | Depends on design | Depends on design and mandate |
| Respect for House autonomy | High | Lower | Lowest |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Narrowing the whip would bring back the instability of the 1960s."

**Reply:** Survival votes — confidence, no-confidence and money — would still be protected by disqualification, so governments could not be toppled by individual defections. What would change is the freedom to vote differently on ordinary legislation, which does not threaten government survival.

**Qualified residual:** In coalition governments, ordinary legislation can carry political weight close to a confidence issue; drawing the line between survival votes and ordinary votes is itself a design problem, which is one reason no Parliament has yet adopted the narrow-whip proposal.

### UPSC application

A reform paragraph earns marks when every proposal carries its source and status ("recommended by the Law Commission's 255th Report; not enacted") and its trade-off. Prelims traps: "the Goswami Committee's recommendations were implemented in full" (only the split deletion was implemented); "*Keisham* created an independent tribunal" (it urged Parliament to consider one); "the NCRWC recommended a 15 per cent cap" (it proposed 10 per cent; Parliament enacted 15).

### Revision notes

1. Goswami Committee (1990): confine whip-disqualification to high-stakes votes; decision by President/Governor on ECI advice; delete split.
2. Law Commission 170th Report (1999): delete split; limit whip-based disqualification; strengthen internal party democracy.
3. NCRWC (2002): delete split; bar defectors from office; 10 per cent ministry ceiling.
4. 91st Amendment (2003) enacted the split deletion and office curbs (15 per cent cap).
5. Law Commission 255th Report (2015): President/Governor to decide on ECI's binding advice.
6. *Keisham* (2020): urged a permanent independent tribunal; ordinary three-month norm.
7. Only the split deletion and office curbs are law; all other proposals remain proposals.
8. Every reform relocates pressure: deliberation versus stability, impartiality versus House autonomy.
9. The 2026 paragraph 4 petition seeks judicial interpretation, not legislative reform.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can confining whip-based disqualification to confidence and money votes be defended without reviving the "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" problem?

**Model answer:** Because the mischief of 1967 was the toppling of governments through changed votes, and the votes that topple governments — confidence, no-confidence and money votes — would remain protected by disqualification; only votes on ordinary legislation, which do not decide a ministry's survival, would be freed, so deliberation gains without reopening the main route to instability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming every relaxation of the whip is a return to unrestricted floor-crossing; the reform is targeted precisely at votes that do not decide government survival.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Compare narrowing the whip and redesigning the merger exception as reforms to the anti-defection law, identifying the trade-off each creates.

**Model solution:** The two reforms address opposite defects. Narrowing the whip targets over-discipline: under paragraph 2(1)(b) a party direction can make any vote a seat-losing matter, so legislators cannot dissent even on ordinary legislation. The Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990) and the Law Commission's 170th Report (1999) proposed confining whip-based disqualification to votes affecting the government's survival, such as confidence and no-confidence motions and money matters. Its benefit is restored deliberation; its trade-off is looser cohesion on ordinary votes, which in coalition settings can carry near-confidence weight. Redesigning the merger exception targets under-discipline: paragraph 4 deems a merger when two-thirds of a legislature party agree, and if read as a headcount rule it permits organised group defection, the "wholesale" route Madhu Limaye criticised. Requiring a genuine party-level merger would close that route; its trade-off is that genuine realignments of parties could become harder, and courts would have to judge organisational events. The reforms are complementary rather than rival: one loosens the law where it suppresses dissent, the other tightens it where it tolerates defection. Neither has been enacted — only the 2003 deletion of the split and the office curbs are law — and the merger question was before the Supreme Court in 2026 as a matter of interpretation. A balanced position would pursue both, while defining "survival votes" and "genuine merger" carefully enough to prevent each reform from creating a new loophole.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Whip reform with source and benefit — 3; its trade-off — 2; merger reform with textual basis — 3; its trade-off — 2; complementarity explained — 2; status accuracy (enacted versus proposed; 2026 interpretation) — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

Everything is now in place. The final lesson turns the whole topic into a reusable answer-writing method.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Templates make answers mechanical; examiners recognise and penalise them."

**Reply:** The template does not supply content; it tells you where analysis and qualification belong. An answer built on the spine but filled with precise text, named cases and genuine trade-offs reads as argument, not formula.

**Qualified residual:** The structure should become invisible with practice. If an examiner can see the scaffolding before the argument, the answer has not yet internalised it.

### Original Mains question

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** "The Tenth Schedule protects the party's mandate more securely than it protects either the voter's choice of a representative or the representative's own judgment." Critically evaluate.

**Model solution:** The Tenth Schedule, inserted in 1985, ties each member to the political party that set him up and disqualifies him for voluntarily giving up its membership or defying its direction without permission or condonation. *Subhash Desai* (2023) placed whip authority in the political party rather than a legislative faction, and *Ravi S. Naik* and the 2026 Khairatabad ruling show conduct-based departure being enforced. The party's mandate is clearly protected. The representative's judgment is protected far less. Paragraph 2(1)(b) sets no limit on which votes may be whipped, so dissent on ordinary legislation risks the seat — the gap the Goswami Committee and the Law Commission's 170th Report sought to narrow. The voter's choice stands in between. The law forces defectors back to the electorate, the 91st Amendment removed the split and denied defectors office, and *Shrimanth* (2019) prevents resignation from erasing prior defection. Yet voters' choices remain exposed to group realignment under paragraph 4, whose two-thirds rule was still being interpreted in 2026, to genuine-resignation strategies, and to delayed adjudication despite *Kihoto*, *Rajendra Singh Rana* and *Keisham*. The statement is largely sound: party interest is the Schedule's organising principle. It needs one qualification — party protection is itself incomplete where merger claims and delay let organised groups override the party that set members up. The law protects the party against individuals more securely than against organised groups.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** Party-mandate protection with text and *Subhash Desai* — 4; representative's judgment and whip breadth with reform lineage — 4; voter's choice with 91st Amendment and *Shrimanth* — 4; residual gaps (merger, resignation, delay) with case law — 4; qualification on incomplete party protection — 2; graded verdict and coherent 250-word structure — 2.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks connect several lessons. Attempt each before reading the model answer.

### Cross-lesson check 1 (Lessons 3, 5, 12)

**Question:** A legislature-party faction appoints its own whip and issues a direction to vote for a rival's no-confidence motion. Members loyal to the party's national leadership vote against the motion. Can the faction seek their disqualification under paragraph 2(1)(b)?

**Model answer:** Only if its whip's direction was "issued by the political party ... or by any person or authority authorised by it". After *Subhash Desai*, whip authority belongs to the political party, identified through its constitution and organisation, not to a legislative faction by headcount; a factional whip that the political party did not authorise cannot found a ground (b) petition against members who followed the party's authorised direction.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that whoever commands a majority of the legislature party can issue binding directions under the Tenth Schedule.

### Cross-lesson check 2 (Lessons 4, 10, 11)

**Question:** How does the 2026 Khairatabad ruling fit into the line of cases from *Kihoto Hollohan* to *Keisham Meghachandra*?

**Model answer:** *Kihoto* made the Speaker's decision reviewable after it is made; *Rajendra Singh Rana* and *Keisham* addressed the failure to decide at all, and the Supreme Court's 31 July 2025 order applied that pressure to the Telangana Speaker. Once the Speaker decided — dismissing the petition against Danam Nagender on 11 March 2026 — the *Kihoto* review route opened, and the High Court set the order aside on 18 September 2026, with the Supreme Court declining to interfere on 24 September 2026. The sequence shows the delay doctrine and the review doctrine working in succession.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the Khairatabad ruling as a new doctrine; it applies existing principles (conduct-based giving up of membership and *Kihoto* review) to a fresh set of facts.

### Cross-lesson check 3 (Lessons 7, 13, 16)

**Question:** Why did deleting the split exception in 2003 not end group defection?

**Model answer:** Because paragraph 4's merger defence survived, and if its two-thirds deeming rule is read as a legislative headcount rule it allows a larger group to achieve what the one-third split permitted before; the 91st Amendment closed one route and curbed office rewards, but the merger route — whose interpretation was before the Supreme Court in 2026 — and the resignation route remained.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that the 91st Amendment removed every group defence; only the split was removed.

### Cross-lesson check 4 (Lessons 9, 15, 17)

**Question:** Why do reform proposals for the defection decider so often borrow the Article 103 model?

**Model answer:** Because Article 103 already provides, for the Article 102(1) grounds, a decider (the President, or the Governor under Article 192) who must act according to the Election Commission's opinion — an arrangement that places the decision outside the House's partisan majority. Reformers such as the Goswami Committee and the Law Commission's 255th Report proposed extending that model to defection, which paragraph 6 instead leaves with the presiding officer.

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing that the Article 103 model already applies to the Tenth Schedule; it applies only to the Article 102(1)/191(1) grounds.

### Cross-lesson check 5 (Lessons 6, 13, 14)

**Question:** A nominated member who joined a party within six months later votes against its whip without permission, is not condoned, and is disqualified. Can he be appointed a minister the following month?

**Model answer:** No. Having joined within the window, he is deemed to belong to that party, so ground (b) applies to him like any party member; once disqualified under paragraph 2, Articles 75(1B)/164(1B) bar his appointment as a minister until the term would have expired or until he is re-elected, whichever is earlier.

**Misconception to avoid:** Thinking that a nominated member's status shields him from the ordinary grounds and office bars after he has joined a party.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These three questions are new and distinct from the lesson questions and from every previous-year question listed above.

### Synthesis Mains — 10 marks, strict ceiling 150 words

**Question:** Explain why the Tenth Schedule allows a political party to condone a member's vote against its direction but not the member's giving up of party membership.

**Model solution:** The two grounds in paragraph 2(1) protect different things. Ground (b) concerns a single act — voting or abstaining in the House contrary to the party's direction — and the text expressly allows prior permission and condonation within fifteen days, so the party itself decides whether a deviation was tolerable. This preserves room for conscience on particular votes. Ground (a) concerns the relationship itself: a member who has voluntarily given up membership, whether by resignation or by conduct as in *Ravi S. Naik*, has abandoned the party that set him up, and the text contains no condonation clause. The difference was illustrated in the 2026 Khairatabad matter, where, as reported, the Supreme Court bench noted that condonation belongs to the whip ground, not to giving up membership. The design distinguishes a disagreement within the party from a departure from it.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Ground (b) with permission/condonation — 3; ground (a) and absence of condonation — 3; rationale distinguishing deviation from departure — 2; 2026 illustration correctly limited — 1; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 15 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** Discuss the role of the judiciary in shaping the working of the anti-defection law since 1992.

**Model solution:** The judiciary has turned a text designed to exclude courts into a scheme supervised by them. *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992) held paragraph 7's court bar invalid for want of State ratification, upheld the rest of the Schedule, and classified the presiding officer as a tribunal reviewable for mala fides, perversity and breach of natural justice. Later cases filled in the grounds: *Ravi S. Naik* (1994) allowed giving up membership to be inferred from conduct, and *G. Viswanathan* (1996) kept expelled members bound to their original party. When review after decision proved powerless against non-decision, *Rajendra Singh Rana* (2007) treated inaction as jurisdictional failure and *Keisham Meghachandra* (2020) set an ordinary three-month norm, urging an independent tribunal. *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil* (2019) prevented resignation from erasing prior defection while rejecting extra bans on contesting. *Subhash Desai* (2023) located whip authority in the political party and kept the Speaker's, Election Commission's and Governor's tracks separate, while referring *Nabam Rebia*'s removal-notice rule to seven judges. In 2026 the Telangana High Court set aside a Speaker's refusal to disqualify, and the Supreme Court declined to interfere. The judiciary has therefore supplied accountability, timeliness and interpretive clarity. Its limits are equally clear: it intervenes after events, has left the merger question and the *Nabam Rebia* reference pending, and cannot itself redesign the decider — a choice that remains with Parliament.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** *Kihoto* as foundation — 3; interpretation of grounds (*Ravi S. Naik*, *G. Viswanathan*) — 2; delay jurisprudence (*Rana*, *Keisham*) — 3; *Shrimanth* and *Subhash Desai* — 3; 2026 application — 1; limits of the judicial role — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 20 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "The anti-defection law is repeatedly outmanoeuvred because it regulates conduct inside the House, while political realignment increasingly happens outside it." Critically evaluate.

**Model solution:** The Tenth Schedule acts on members' conduct relative to their party: giving up membership, defying a direction in the House, or joining a party as an independent or late-joining nominated member. Inside that field it works. Individual floor-crossing became costly, the 91st Amendment removed the split and denied disqualified defectors office, and courts have enforced the grounds, most recently in the 2026 Khairatabad ruling. The statement correctly identifies three external routes. Resignations reduce a House's strength without any act of defection, and *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil* can reach only defection that preceded resignation. Merger claims convert organisational or factional realignment into a paragraph 4 defence whose two-thirds rule was still before the Supreme Court in 2026. Party splits move to the Election Commission's Symbols Order forum and the Governor's floor-test decisions, which *Subhash Desai* kept separate from the Speaker's adjudication. The statement overstates, however, in one respect: some outmanoeuvring happens inside the House — through delayed adjudication and factional whips — and the courts have answered it through *Rajendra Singh Rana*, *Keisham* and *Subhash Desai*. The deeper limitation is structural: a disqualification rule can raise the price of changing sides but cannot govern every lawful exit or every institution that responds to a realignment. The statement is therefore broadly valid, with the qualification that the law's internal gaps are being narrowed while its external ones remain matters for constitutional redesign rather than interpretation.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** Internal reach and achievements — 4; resignation route with *Shrimanth* — 3; merger route with 2026 status — 3; parallel tracks with *Subhash Desai* — 3; internal manipulation and judicial responses as counterpoint — 4; structural limitation and graded verdict — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

### Master Map 1 — solving any defection fact pattern

```text
STEP 1  WHO IS THE MEMBER?
        party-set-up elected member | independent | nominated (party member at nomination? joined within six months?)
            |
STEP 2  WHAT HAPPENED?
        party member: (a) gave up membership (conduct; expulsion is not enough)
                      (b) contrary vote/abstention + no prior permission + not condoned in 15 days
        independent:  joined any party after election
        nominated:    joined a party after six months from taking the seat
            |
STEP 3  IS A DEFENCE AVAILABLE?
        merger of the original political party with two-thirds agreement (para 4) -> both joiners and separate group protected
        listed presiding office, by reason of election (para 5)
        split -> NOT available (omitted 2004)
            |
STEP 4  WHO DECIDES, AND HOW?
        member's petition -> Speaker/Chairman (or member elected by House if presiding officer concerned)
        -> notice, comments, hearing, written order (para 8 rules)
            |
STEP 5  WHAT CONTROLS THE DECIDER?
        Kihoto review (after decision) | Rana/Keisham (inaction; ordinary three months) | Nabam Rebia (pending reference)
            |
STEP 6  WHAT FOLLOWS?
        seat vacated | office bars under Arts 75(1B), 164(1B), 361B until term ends or re-election | may contest again
```

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### 1. The mischief and the bargain (Lesson 1)

- Post-1967 floor-crossing for office destabilised ministries; Lok Sabha resolution 8 December 1967 → Committee on Defections (Y.B. Chavan) → report 1969.
- "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" (Gaya Lal, Haryana, 1967) is a hook, not a source.
- Bargain: stability and a protected party mandate, at a cost to legislative independence.
- Consequence is disqualification, not criminal liability.

### 2. Constitutional architecture (Lesson 2)

- 52nd Amendment Act, 1985 (dated 15 February 1985; in force 1 March 1985): amended Articles 101, 102, 190, 191; inserted the Tenth Schedule.
- Articles 102(2)/191(2) create the ground; Articles 101(3)(a)/190(3)(a) vacate the seat.
- Paragraphs today: 1, 2, 4-8; paragraph 3 omitted (2003); paragraph 7 printed but invalid.
- Paragraph 2(4): transitional application to members sitting in 1985.

### 3. Party vocabulary (Lesson 3)

- Paragraph 1: "House", "legislature party", "original political party".
- Elected member belongs to the party that set him up; nominated member as per Explanation (b).
- *Subhash Desai* (11 May 2023): political party appoints Whip and Leader; party identity from party constitution and structure, not headcount.

### 4. Giving up membership (Lesson 4)

- Paragraph 2(1)(a); wider than resignation (*Ravi S. Naik*, 1994).
- Expelled member still belongs to the original party; joining another party triggers ground (a) (*G. Viswanathan*, 1996).
- No condonation clause for ground (a).
- Telangana High Court, 18 September 2026: Danam Nagender disqualified with effect from 23 April 2024; Speaker's dismissal of 11 March 2026 set aside. Supreme Court, 24 September 2026: no interference.

### 5. The whip (Lesson 5)

- Four elements: direction by party or authorised person; contrary vote or abstention in the House; no prior permission; not condoned within fifteen days.
- Speeches and criticism are not breaches by themselves.
- Paragraph 8(1)(b): condonation reports by the legislature-party leader.
- Core criticism: no limit on which votes may be whipped.

### 6. Independent and nominated members (Lesson 6)

- Independent: disqualified on joining any party after election; no window.
- Nominated: disqualified on joining a party after six months from taking the seat (oath under Article 99/188).
- Nominated member already in a party at nomination belongs to it from the start; joining within the window makes grounds (a) and (b) applicable.
- Asymmetry justified by different sources of mandate; six months is a policy choice.

### 7. Split and merger (Lesson 7)

- Former paragraph 3: one-third faction claiming a split; omitted with effect from 1 January 2004.
- Paragraph 4: original political party merges; deemed merger if, and only if, not less than two-thirds of the legislature party agree; joiners and separate-group members both protected.
- Organisational versus headcount readings; *Rajendra Singh Rana* and *Subhash Desai* as pointers.
- Notice issued on 27 July 2026 on a petition seeking an authoritative reading; no merits view.

### 8. Presiding-officer exemption (Lesson 8)

- Listed offices: Lok Sabha Speaker and Deputy Speaker; Rajya Sabha Deputy Chairman; Legislative Council Chairman and Deputy Chairman; Legislative Assembly Speaker and Deputy Speaker.
- Conditions: membership given up by reason of election; no rejoining or joining while in office; may rejoin the same party afterwards.
- Rajya Sabha Chairman not listed; the exemption does not regulate conduct in the chair.

### 9. Decider and procedure (Lesson 9)

- Paragraph 6(1): Chairman/Speaker decides; decision "final"; proviso — member elected by the House if the presiding officer is concerned.
- Paragraph 6(2): deemed House proceedings (Articles 122/212).
- Paragraph 8: rules on records, condonation and admission reports, procedure; laid thirty days; wilful contravention as breach of privilege.
- Petition by a member → comments → hearing → possible Committee of Privileges reference → written order; no automatic effect.

### 10. *Kihoto Hollohan* (Lesson 10)

- 18 February 1992, 3:2: paragraph 7 invalid for want of ratification under the proviso to Article 368(2); severable; rest upheld.
- Presiding officer is a tribunal; finality excludes appeal, not review.
- Grounds: constitutional mandate, mala fides, natural justice, perversity; review after decision; no quia timet.
- Dissent: whole amendment invalid; doubts about Speaker's independence.

### 11. Delay and inaction (Lesson 11)

- *Rajendra Singh Rana* (2007): inaction as jurisdictional failure; Court declared MLAs disqualified.
- *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (21 January 2020): reasonable period, ordinarily three months from filing; independent tribunal urged.
- Supreme Court, 31 July 2025: Telangana Speaker to decide within three months.
- Separate text, rules, judicial norms, case-specific orders and proposals.

### 12. *Nabam Rebia* and *Subhash Desai* (Lesson 12)

- *Nabam Rebia* (2016): no adjudication while a removal notice against the Speaker is pending; referred to seven judges in 2023; pending as of August 2026.
- *Subhash Desai*: political party controls whip; split unavailable; reasonable period; ECI may proceed concurrently; Governor's floor-test call lacked objective material; no restoration.
- Four tracks: Speaker, ECI, Governor, courts.

### 13. The 91st Amendment (Lesson 13)

- 2003 Act; Tenth Schedule changes effective 1 January 2004; paragraph 3 omitted.
- Articles 75(1A)/164(1A): ministry at most 15 per cent of the popular House; States at least 12.
- Articles 75(1B)/164(1B)/361B: disqualified member barred from ministerial and remunerative political posts until term would expire or re-election, whichever is earlier.
- NCRWC proposed 10 per cent; enacted figure 15 per cent.

### 14. Resignation, expulsion and consequences (Lesson 14)

- *Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil* (13 November 2019): resignation does not erase prior defection; no Speaker-imposed ban on contesting.
- Genuine prior resignation lies outside the Schedule — the resign-and-recontest route (Karnataka 2019, Madhya Pradesh 2020).
- Party expulsion, House expulsion and disqualification are distinct.

### 15. Neighbouring regimes (Lesson 15)

- Articles 102(1)/191(1) grounds → Articles 103/192: President/Governor acting according to ECI's opinion.
- Tenth Schedule → paragraph 6: Speaker/Chairman; no ECI role.
- Symbols Order (paragraph 15, 1968): ECI decides rival-group claims; privilege and floor tests are separate.

### 16. Evaluation (Lesson 16)

- Advantages: stability; realignment by merger; reduced corruption and election costs; constitutional role for parties.
- Criticisms: dissent versus defection (Sorabjee); retail versus wholesale (Madhu Limaye); no provision for expulsion for outside conduct; independent/nominated asymmetry; Speaker's partiality and expertise (Rabi Ray 1991, Shivraj Patil 1993).
- Verdict: succeeded against retail defection; displaced instability into mergers, resignations and delay.

### 17. Reform designs (Lesson 17)

- Goswami (1990), Law Commission 170th (1999), NCRWC (2002), Law Commission 255th (2015), *Keisham* (2020).
- Enacted: split deletion and office curbs (2003). Proposals only: narrow whip, ECI-advised decider, independent tribunal, fixed deadline, merger redesign, internal party democracy.
- Each reform relocates pressure; state source, status and trade-off.

### 18. Answer architecture (Lesson 18)

- Claim → named evidence → analysis → qualification.
- Directive decoder and mark-scaled architecture (10/15/20).
- Topic map from purpose to reform; select by directive.
- Twenty-four-item trap register for final revision.
