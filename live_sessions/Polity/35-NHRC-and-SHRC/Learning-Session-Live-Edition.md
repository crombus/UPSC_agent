# NHRC and SHRC — Live Session Edition

A country can write the finest rights into its Constitution and still fail the person standing outside a police station at 2 a.m. with nowhere to complain. India's answer to that gap is a watchdog body with no power to punish anyone — and understanding exactly why it was built that way, and what that choice costs and buys, is the whole of this topic. By the end of this session you will be able to explain the National Human Rights Commission and the State Human Rights Commissions precisely enough to answer any UPSC question on their design, powers, limits, case law, international accreditation and reform — without quietly assuming they are stronger, or weaker, than the statute actually makes them.

## 🖼️ Visual: The gap a Human Rights Commission is built to close

```text
RIGHTS WRITTEN INTO THE CONSTITUTION
                |
    violated by a PUBLIC SERVANT
    (police excess, custodial death, jail neglect...)
                |
victim cannot always reach a court quickly, afford one,
or survive the violation to reach one at all
                |
            THE GAP
                |
a body that can INQUIRE FAST, VISIT, SUMMON EVIDENCE
and PUBLICISE — without needing a full trial first
                |
            NHRC / SHRC
```

*A commission is not a substitute for courts; it is a fast, visible, fact-finding layer that sits in front of them.*

### 1. The problem before the solution

Imagine a person dies in police custody. A criminal trial could eventually establish guilt — but trials take years, depend on the same police machinery being investigated, and offer no quick, visible response. Parliament's answer, in 1993, was not a new court. It was a specialised, faster, more visible **inquiry body** that could summon witnesses like a civil court, visit the jail where it happened, and put the facts on public record long before — or even regardless of whether — a criminal case concludes.

That is the entire logic of the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) and the State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs): **fast fact-finding and publicity, not adjudication or punishment.**

### 2. The international idea behind it: Paris Principles, in plain language

Before the technical name, hold the plain idea. In 1991, a UN-sponsored meeting in Paris asked: if every country is going to have a national body watching over human rights, what must that body look like to be credible rather than decorative? The answer — adopted by the UN General Assembly through Resolution 48/134 (OHCHR's own instrument text cites **19 December 1993**, while the official UN resolution record places adoption at the 85th plenary meeting on **20 December 1993** — sources diverge on the exact day) — became known as the **Paris Principles**. Four things matter most:

```text
BROAD, LEGALLY-ANCHORED MANDATE  — defined by statute/constitution, not an office circular
PLURALIST COMPOSITION            — not just retired judges and bureaucrats
INDEPENDENCE                     — own staff, own funds, own investigators
ADEQUATE POWERS AND RESOURCES    — real ability to summon, visit, question
```

India's Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 was drafted squarely inside this global moment — the Act and the Paris Principles share the same year because India was consciously building a Paris-Principles-style institution. You will meet the full six-headed Paris framework formally in Lesson 11, once you have seen what the Indian design actually does; for now, keep this plain intuition: **a national human-rights body earns international credibility by being independent, plural and well-resourced, not merely by existing.**

### 3. What kind of body is the NHRC — constitutional or statutory?

The NHRC and SHRCs are **statutory** bodies, created by an ordinary Act of Parliament — the **Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993** — not by the Constitution itself.

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL BODY                    STATUTORY BODY
created by/named in the Constitution   created by an ordinary Act of Parliament
example: Election Commission,          example: NHRC, SHRC, NCW, NCM,
Union Public Service Commission,       National Commission for Protection
NCSC (Art 338), NCST (Art 338A),       of Child Rights
NCBC (Art 338B)
amendable only by constitutional       amendable by ordinary legislation
amendment procedure
```

This single classification line is worth more exam marks than almost anything else in this topic, because UPSC habitually tests exactly this distinction — and because it determines an argument you will build fully in Lesson 12: constitutional commissions cannot be casually merged into a statutory one.

### 4. Why "shall" for the NHRC but "may" for an SHRC?

Look closely at the statutory language. Section 3 of the Act says the Central Government **shall constitute** the NHRC — it is mandatory; India has exactly one NHRC, permanently. Section 21 says a State Government **may constitute** an SHRC — it is enabling, not automatic. A State can choose whether to set one up at all, and the pace and quality of State-level human-rights protection consequently varies by State political choice, not by uniform statutory command.

```text
CENTRAL GOVERNMENT                      STATE GOVERNMENT
section 3: "shall constitute"           section 21: "may constitute"
        |                                         |
        v                                         v
       NHRC                            SHRC only after State action
 (mandatory, single, permanent)        (enabling; coverage is uneven)
```

**Why UPSC can ask this:** examiners often test whether a student notices that the Act does not guarantee a functioning SHRC in every State — an "every State must have one" answer is simply wrong on the text.

### 5. What does "human rights" even mean under this Act?

The Act does not use "human rights" loosely. Section 2(1)(d) gives a precise formula:

```text
LIFE + LIBERTY + EQUALITY + DIGNITY
                 |
      guaranteed by the Constitution
                 OR
      embodied in "International Covenants"
                 |
      enforceable by courts in India
                 =
      HUMAN RIGHTS UNDER SECTION 2(1)(d)
```

Section 2(1)(f) defines "International Covenants" narrowly: the **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)**, the **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR)** — both adopted by the UN General Assembly on 16 December 1966 — and any other UN General Assembly covenant or convention that the Central Government specifically notifies.

| Instrument | Automatically covered? | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| ICCPR | yes | expressly named in section 2(1)(f) |
| ICESCR | yes | expressly named in section 2(1)(f) |
| another UN General Assembly covenant/convention | only if the Centre notifies it | statutory notification gate |
| the Paris Principles | no, not as an "International Covenant" | it is an institutional design standard, not a rights covenant |
| an NGO declaration or commentary | no | not a notified covenant or convention |

A frequent misconception is that any international human-rights document automatically becomes enforceable through this Act — it does not. The gate is deliberately narrow: named covenants, or Centre-notified ones, and only where the right is also independently enforceable by Indian courts.

### 6. The Act's own evolution

```text
28 Sep 1993         8 Jan 1994          2006 Amendment              2019 Amendment
deemed commence --> presidential --> procedure widened: court- --> design updated:
                     assent            reference route, revised     broader chair eligibility,
                     (Act No. 10       visits, complaint transfer,  three experts, deemed
                     of 1994)          State-level provisions       members, 3-year terms
```

No later enacted amendment changing the provisions taught in this session has been located in the current India Code, Legislative Department, Ministry of Home Affairs or NHRC texts as of 2 October 2026, the review date for this session. The controlling architecture remains the 1993 Act as reshaped principally by the 2006 and 2019 amendments — hold that timeline; every later lesson's "current law" claims rest on it.

### 7. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** statutory-versus-constitutional classification; "shall" versus "may"; which covenants are automatically covered.

**Mains angle:** any answer on the NHRC's design or limitations should open by fixing its statutory (not constitutional) status, because several structural limitations discussed later in this session — merge-ability, ordinary-legislation amendability, no constitutional entrenchment — trace directly back to this one fact.

**Common trap:** writing "the NHRC is a constitutional body" or "every State has a functioning SHRC" — both are direct text-level errors, not matters of interpretation.

## Revision notes

1. NHRC and SHRC are statutory bodies under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 — not constitutional bodies.
2. The Act was deemed to come into force on 28 September 1993; it received presidential assent on 8 January 1994, as Act No. 10 of 1994.
3. Section 3: the Centre **shall** constitute the NHRC (mandatory, single, permanent).
4. Section 21: a State **may** constitute an SHRC (enabling; coverage across States is uneven).
5. The Paris Principles (UNGA Resolution 48/134, 19-20 December 1993 — OHCHR's instrument text and the official resolution record diverge by one day) require a broad legal mandate, pluralism, independence and adequate powers/resources for a credible national human-rights body.
6. India's Act and the Paris Principles share 1993 — a deliberate contemporaneous design choice.
7. Section 2(1)(d): human rights = life + liberty + equality + dignity, guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in International Covenants, and enforceable by Indian courts.
8. Section 2(1)(f): "International Covenants" = ICCPR + ICESCR + any further UNGA covenant/convention the Centre notifies.
9. Not every international rights document is automatically covered — only named or Centre-notified covenants, filtered further by domestic enforceability.
10. The 2006 Amendment widened procedure: court-reference inquiry, revised institution visits, complaint transfer and State-level provisions.
11. The 2019 Amendment updated design: broader chairperson eligibility, three expert members, deemed members and three-year terms (detailed in Lesson 2).
12. No later enacted amendment to this architecture has been located as of 2 October 2026.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State has not constituted an SHRC. A citizen in that State complains that this violates the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. Is the complaint textually correct?

**Model answer:** No. Section 21 uses "may constitute," making SHRC creation enabling rather than mandatory. The absence of an SHRC in a State is not, by itself, a violation of the Act's text — though it is a fair ground for criticising uneven State-level human-rights protection as a policy matter.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "may constitute" as if it were "shall constitute." Only the NHRC's creation is mandatory under the Act; SHRC creation is a State choice the Act merely enables.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "The NHRC's authority rests on an ordinary statute, not the Constitution." Explain what this classification means in practice and why it matters for evaluating the Commission's design.

**Model solution (148 words):** The NHRC is a **statutory** body under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, created by ordinary parliamentary legislation rather than the Constitution. This has three practical consequences. First, Parliament can alter its composition, powers or procedure through ordinary amendment — as it did in 2006 and 2019 — without the higher threshold a constitutional amendment requires. Second, unlike constitutional commissions such as the NCSC (Article 338) or NCST (Article 338A), the NHRC has no entrenched constitutional status protecting its existence or core design from simple legislative change. Third, and most consequentially for exam answers, this classification explains why a "merge every commission into one umbrella body" proposal cannot treat the NHRC and the constitutional commissions alike: statutory bodies can in principle be restructured by law, while constitutional commissions require constitutional amendment first. Classification is therefore the analytical hinge for every later limitation and reform argument.

**Scoring note:** award full marks only if the answer names the Act, explicitly contrasts statutory with constitutional status, and connects the classification to at least one concrete consequence (amendability or non-entrenchment) rather than merely restating the label.

## 🖼️ Visual: NHRC's standing composition

```text
CHAIRPERSON
a person who has been CJI OR a Judge of the Supreme Court
        |
        +-- Judicial member 1: serving/former Supreme Court judge
        |
        +-- Judicial member 2: serving/former High Court Chief Justice
        |
        +-- Expert 1
        +-- Expert 2
        +-- Expert 3
               |
          at least one of the three experts must be a woman
```

*Six persons form the Commission's ordinary working body — a judicial core plus a human-rights expert layer.*

### 1. Why mix judges with non-judges at all?

A commission that is only retired judges risks becoming a quiet extension of the judiciary, disconnected from grassroots human-rights work. A commission that is only activists risks lacking the authority and procedural discipline that quasi-judicial fact-finding needs. Section 3(2)'s design deliberately blends both: judicial gravitas for credible inquiry, plus three human-rights experts for wider social engagement. This is also, not coincidentally, a partial response to the Paris Principles' demand for pluralist composition — partial because, as you will see in Lesson 11, three fixed expert seats are a floor, not a guarantee of true social diversity.

### 2. The standing composition, precisely

Section 3(2) fixes a Chairperson and five ordinary members:

1. **Chairperson** — a person who has been Chief Justice of India **or** a Judge of the Supreme Court. This "or" is new since 2019; earlier, only a former CJI qualified.
2. One member who is or has been a Judge of the Supreme Court.
3. One member who is or has been the Chief Justice of a High Court.
4. Three members with knowledge of, or practical experience in, human-rights matters — **at least one of whom must be a woman**.

| Dimension | Before 2019 | After 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Chair eligibility | former CJI only | former CJI **or** former Supreme Court judge |
| human-rights experts | two | three |
| woman requirement | no express minimum in this clause | at least one among the three experts |
| ordinary term | five years | three years |

**Common trap:** stating that the NHRC Chairperson must still be a former CJI. After 2019, a former Supreme Court judge who was never CJI also qualifies — examiners test exactly this widening.

### 3. The seven deemed members — and their precise boundary

Section 3(3) adds seven officeholders as **deemed members**: the Chairpersons of the National Commissions for Backward Classes, Minorities, Protection of Child Rights, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Women, plus the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities.

```text
NCBC   NCM   NCPCR   NCSC   NCST   NCW   Chief Commissioner for PwD
  \     |      |       |     |     |          /
   \    |      |       |     |     |         /
            DEEMED NHRC MEMBERS (7)
                     |
           participate in section 12(b) to (j)
                     X
           do NOT participate in section 12(a)
           (ordinary complaint inquiry)
```

This is the single most commonly mistested line in NHRC composition: the seven deemed members **do not sit on ordinary human-rights complaint inquiries**. Their participation is confined to the Commission's other functions — intervening in court proceedings, reviewing safeguards, studying treaties, promoting literacy and the like (the full list is Lesson 5's subject). Think of them as specialist advisers folded into the Commission's promotional and coordination work, not as additional judges on every case file.

| Layer | How many | Where they act |
|---|---:|---|
| Chairperson + five ordinary members (section 3(2)) | 6 | All Commission work, including section 12(a) complaint inquiry |
| Deemed members (section 3(3)) | 7 | Section 12(b)-(j) only, never section 12(a) |

### 4. Why this design matters for the "umbrella commission" debate later

Notice something important for Lesson 12: the NHRC is **already** a partial coordination hub. By making the chairs of six specialised commissions and the Chief Commissioner for PwD deemed members, the 2019 Amendment built integration into the existing structure — without dissolving any of those specialised bodies. Keep this fact in reserve for Lesson 12's umbrella-body debate, where it is evidence you can weigh on either side, not a settled conclusion.

### 5. Comparative snapshot: before and after

```text
PRE-2019 NHRC                          POST-2019 NHRC
Chair: former CJI only                 Chair: former CJI OR former SC judge
2 human-rights experts                 3 human-rights experts (>=1 woman)
narrower deemed-member set             NCBC, NCPCR and Chief Commissioner
                                        for PwD newly added as deemed members

```

### 6. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** exact composition numbers (Chairperson + 5 + 7 deemed), who qualifies as Chairperson post-2019, the woman-requirement clause, and the 12(a)/12(b)-(j) deemed-member boundary.

**Mains angle:** composition questions usually appear embedded inside a "design versus independence" or "pluralism" argument — cite the exact numbers, then use them as evidence for or against adequate Paris-Principles pluralism (Lesson 11).

**Common trap:** assuming "at least one woman" applies across the whole six-person Commission. It applies specifically to the three human-rights-expert seats, not to the judicial seats or to gender balance generally.

## Revision notes

1. Section 3(2): Chairperson + five ordinary members form the NHRC's standing composition.
2. Chairperson: a person who has been CJI **or** a Judge of the Supreme Court (widened by the 2019 Amendment).
3. One ordinary member: serving/former Supreme Court judge.
4. One ordinary member: serving/former High Court Chief Justice.
5. Three ordinary members: human-rights experts, at least one of whom must be a woman.
6. Section 3(3): seven deemed members — chairs of NCBC, NCM, NCPCR, NCSC, NCST, NCW, plus the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities.
7. Deemed members act only for functions under section 12(b) to (j) — never section 12(a) complaint inquiry.
8. Pre-2019: Chair had to be a former CJI; only two human-rights experts; no express woman requirement in this clause.
9. Post-2019: Chair eligibility widened; three experts with a woman minimum; NCBC, NCPCR and Chief Commissioner for PwD newly added as deemed members.
10. The deemed-member design already partially integrates specialised commissions into the NHRC without merging them — relevant evidence for Lesson 12's umbrella-body debate.
11. Current officeholder names are deliberately not taught; the examinable content is the durable statutory structure.

### Concept check

**Question:** Can a deemed member — say, the Chairperson of the National Commission for Women — sit on the inquiry into an individual human-rights complaint under section 12(a)?

**Model answer:** No. Deemed members under section 3(3) participate only in functions listed under section 12(b) to (j) — such as court intervention, safeguard review or literacy promotion. Ordinary complaint inquiry under section 12(a) remains the function of the Chairperson and the five ordinary members under section 3(2).

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming all thirteen persons (six ordinary plus seven deemed) jointly decide every complaint. The deemed-member layer is functionally restricted, not a general expansion of the inquiry bench.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the purpose and precise statutory limit of the NHRC's seven deemed members.

**Model solution (147 words):** Section 3(3) of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 makes the Chairpersons of the National Commissions for Backward Classes, Minorities, Protection of Child Rights, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Women, along with the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities, deemed members of the NHRC. The purpose is institutional integration: it links the NHRC with specialised bodies that already possess group-specific expertise, enabling coordinated action on overlapping human-rights concerns without dissolving those specialised commissions. The precise limit is functional: deemed members participate only in functions under section 12(b) to (j) — court intervention, safeguard review, treaty study, research, literacy promotion and NGO encouragement — and never in section 12(a), the ordinary complaint-inquiry function reserved for the Chairperson and the five section 3(2) members. This design shows one concrete way the NHRC coordinates with specialised bodies without absorbing their statutory functions.

**Scoring note:** full marks require naming the seven deemed offices (or stating "six commission chairs plus the Chief Commissioner for PwD"), the exact functional limit (12(b)-(j), not 12(a)), and the coordination-without-absorption analytical point.

## 🖼️ Visual: NHRC appointment chain

```text
SIX-MEMBER COMMITTEE
Prime Minister (head) + Speaker, Lok Sabha + Union Home Minister
+ Leader of Opposition, Lok Sabha + Leader of Opposition, Rajya Sabha
+ Deputy Chairman, Rajya Sabha
                    |
             recommends names
                    |
                    v
                PRESIDENT
         warrant under hand and seal
                    |
                    v
          Chairperson / Member appointed
```

*Six political and parliamentary offices, not the judiciary, choose who sits on the Commission — the President merely formalises the appointment.*

### 1. Who actually decides, and who merely signs?

A recurring misconception is that the President "chooses" NHRC members in any independent sense. In practice, the committee recommends; the President appoints by warrant under hand and seal, giving legal effect to the committee's recommendation. The real decision-making power sits with the six-member committee — a mix of the executive (PM, Home Minister) and parliamentary offices (Speaker, two Leaders of Opposition, Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha).

### 2. The exact committee, and the exact trap list

| Included in the committee | Commonly assumed but **not** included |
|---|---|
| Prime Minister (head) | Chief Justice of India |
| Speaker of the Lok Sabha | President (in committee capacity) |
| Union Home Minister | Vice-President |
| Leader of Opposition, Lok Sabha | Cabinet Secretary |
| Leader of Opposition, Rajya Sabha | Attorney-General |
| Deputy Chairman, Rajya Sabha | — |

UPSC frequently tests the right column — candidates assume the CJI or the Attorney-General must be involved because the Commission deals with rights and has judicial members; neither sits on the selection committee.

**Qualification:** a sitting Supreme Court judge or a sitting High Court Chief Justice cannot be appointed Chairperson or member without consulting the Chief Justice of India first — so the judiciary does have a narrow consultative role, just not a seat on the committee itself. Section 4(2) also provides that a vacancy in the selection committee does not, by itself, invalidate an appointment — a procedural safeguard against deadlock, not a licence to bypass the committee altogether.

### 3. Tenure: how long, and until when?

```text
CHAIRPERSON: entry + 3 years OR age 70, whichever earlier -> reappointment eligible
MEMBER:      entry + 3 years -> reappointment eligible -> absolute age ceiling of 70
AFTER OFFICE: no further employment under the Union or a State government
```

The Chairperson holds office for three years from entry, or until age seventy, whichever comes first, and is eligible for reappointment. An ordinary member holds office for three years and is eligible for reappointment, but cannot continue in any case after turning seventy. The 2019 Amendment removed the earlier wording that fixed a further five-year reappointment term; the Act does not now state a maximum number of reappointments, only the three-year-or-seventy ceiling per term.

**Trap to avoid:** the term is **three years**, not five. The 2019 Amendment reduced it from five years — a direction that UPSC likes to test precisely because the intuitive assumption (longer terms protect independence) runs the opposite way from the actual amendment, which shortened the term while widening eligibility.

### 4. Why "no further government employment" after office?

This "cooling-off" style rule — a former Chairperson or member cannot take up further Union or State government employment — exists for the same reason judicial cooling-off periods exist elsewhere in the Constitution: to reduce the incentive for a sitting member to decide cases, or shape recommendations, with an eye toward a future government post. It is a quiet but real independence safeguard, worth citing whenever an answer discusses the Commission's autonomy.

### 5. Removal — two separate tracks, not one

A very common error is to say "NHRC members are removed only after a Supreme Court inquiry." That is true for only one of two statutory removal tracks.

```text
TRACK A: PROVED MISBEHAVIOUR OR INCAPACITY
President refers the matter -> Supreme Court inquiry -> Court reports the
person ought to be removed -> President removes

TRACK B: DIRECT STATUTORY GROUNDS (no Supreme Court inquiry needed)
adjudged insolvent
OR engaged in paid employment outside official duties during the term
OR unfit to continue by reason of infirmity of mind or body
OR declared of unsound mind by a competent court
OR convicted and sentenced to imprisonment for an offence the President
   considers to involve moral turpitude
            |
            v
     President orders removal directly
```

Track A protects against an arbitrary or politically motivated removal for something as serious and contestable as "misbehaviour" by inserting judicial scrutiny. Track B covers objective, largely self-evident disqualifying facts — insolvency, a declared unsound mind, a disqualifying conviction — where a full Supreme Court inquiry would add delay without adding real protection, since the underlying fact is already judicially or officially established.

| Removal ground | Supreme Court inquiry required? |
|---|---:|
| Proved misbehaviour or incapacity | Yes |
| Adjudged insolvent | No |
| Paid outside employment during term | No |
| Infirmity of mind or body | No |
| Declared of unsound mind by a competent court | No |
| Conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude | No |

### 6. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** exact committee composition, tenure duration (three years), the age ceiling (seventy), and the two-track removal structure.

**Mains angle:** independence arguments should cite (a) the six-member committee's political composition as a possible appointment-pluralism concern, and (b) the Supreme-Court-inquiry removal track as a genuine independence safeguard against arbitrary dismissal — both points belong in Lesson 11's Paris Principles discussion.

**Common trap:** collapsing all five non-judicial removal grounds into "proved misbehaviour," or stating that the CJI sits on the selection committee.

## Revision notes

1. The NHRC Chairperson and members are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a six-member committee.
2. Committee: Prime Minister (head), Speaker of the Lok Sabha, Union Home Minister, Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, Leader of Opposition in the Rajya Sabha, and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
3. Neither the Chief Justice of India nor the Attorney-General sits on this committee.
4. A sitting Supreme Court judge or sitting High Court Chief Justice cannot be appointed without prior consultation with the CJI.
5. A vacancy in the selection committee does not, by itself, invalidate an appointment (section 4(2)).
6. Chairperson's tenure: three years from entry, or age seventy, whichever is earlier; reappointment eligible.
7. Member's tenure: three years; reappointment eligible; absolute ceiling of age seventy.
8. The 2019 Amendment reduced the term from five years to three years while widening chairperson eligibility.
9. Neither the Chairperson nor a member may take further Union or State government employment after leaving office.
10. Removal Track A: proved misbehaviour/incapacity — President refers to the Supreme Court; removal follows the Court's report.
11. Removal Track B: direct statutory grounds — insolvency, paid outside employment, infirmity, declared unsoundness of mind, or a moral-turpitude conviction — removable by the President without a Supreme Court inquiry.
12. Do not describe all removal grounds as requiring a Supreme Court inquiry; only Track A does.

### Concept check

**Question:** A sitting NHRC member is declared insolvent by a competent court. Must the President refer this to the Supreme Court before removing the member?

**Model answer:** No. Adjudged insolvency is a direct statutory removal ground under Track B. The President may remove the member directly on this ground without the Supreme-Court-inquiry procedure, which applies only to the separate "proved misbehaviour or incapacity" ground (Track A).

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming every NHRC removal requires a Supreme Court inquiry. Only the misbehaviour/incapacity ground does; the five other statutory grounds permit direct presidential removal.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish the two statutory routes for removing an NHRC Chairperson or member, and explain the reasoning behind treating them differently.

**Model solution (149 words):** The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 provides two distinct removal routes. For proved misbehaviour or incapacity, the President refers the matter to the Supreme Court; removal follows only if the Court, after inquiry, reports that the person ought to be removed. The President may otherwise remove directly, without a Supreme Court inquiry, on specific statutory grounds: adjudged insolvency, paid outside employment during the term, infirmity of mind or body, a judicial declaration of unsoundness of mind, or conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude. The reasoning is proportionality: "misbehaviour" is a contestable, serious charge that could otherwise be misused for arbitrary or politically motivated removal, so it receives judicial scrutiny as a safeguard. The other grounds rest on facts already established independently — by a court, a medical finding, or a conviction — so an additional inquiry would add delay without adding real protection.

**Scoring note:** full marks require naming both tracks accurately, listing at least three of the five Track B grounds, and articulating the proportionality reasoning rather than merely listing the grounds.

## 🖼️ Visual: SHRC's standing composition

```text
CHAIRPERSON
a person who has been Chief Justice OR a Judge of a High Court
         |
         +-- Judicial member:
         |   serving/former High Court judge
         |   OR a District Judge with at least 7 years as District Judge
         |
         +-- Human-rights expert member
```

*Three persons, deliberately smaller than the NHRC's six — and with no deemed-member layer at all.*

### 1. A smaller, leaner design — and why

The SHRC has only a Chairperson and two members — no seven-strong deemed layer corresponding to NHRC's section 3(3). This is not an oversight; it reflects the Act's narrower State-level mandate (confined to State and Concurrent List subjects, taught fully in Lesson 8) and the practical reality that most specialised national commissions (NCW, NCM, NCSC and so on) do not have State-level counterparts to integrate in the same way.

1. **Chairperson** — a person who has been Chief Justice **or** a Judge of a High Court (widened in the same 2019 Amendment style as the NHRC chair clause).
2. One member who is or has been a High Court judge, **or** a District Judge within the State with at least seven years' experience as District Judge.
3. One member with knowledge of, or practical experience in, human-rights matters.

**Common trap:** assuming the SHRC judicial member must always be a High Court judge. A sufficiently senior District Judge (at least seven years as District Judge) also qualifies — a deliberately wider eligibility pool for States where identifying a willing former High Court judge may be harder.

### 2. Appointment: who recommends, who appoints?

```text
UNICAMERAL STATE: Chief Minister (head) + Assembly Speaker
                  + State Home Minister + Assembly Leader of Opposition
BICAMERAL ADD-ON: + Legislative Council Chairman + Council Leader of Opposition
                              |
                       recommends names
                              |
                              v
                          GOVERNOR
                 warrant under hand and seal
```

The Governor appoints, on the recommendation of a committee headed by the Chief Minister, mirroring the NHRC's structure one level down. In a bicameral State, the Legislative Council's Chairman and Leader of Opposition also join the committee — a parallel to how the Rajya Sabha's Deputy Chairman and Leader of Opposition join the central committee. A sitting High Court judge or sitting District Judge cannot be appointed without consultation with the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court — the same judiciary-consultation safeguard you saw at the national level.

### 3. The appointment-removal asymmetry — the single most tested SHRC fact

Here is the fact UPSC returns to again and again: the Governor **appoints** an SHRC Chairperson or member, and the Governor also receives that person's **resignation** — but removal is by the **President**, not the Governor.

```text
appoints              -> GOVERNOR
receives resignation  -> GOVERNOR
removes               -> PRESIDENT (after the same two-track procedure as the NHRC)
```

**Why design it this way?** Think about what would happen if a State Government could both appoint and remove the very commission meant to scrutinise its own police and administration. Removal power in State hands would let an unhappy State executive dismiss an inconvenient Chairperson or member at will. Vesting removal in the President — a different, more insulated authority — is a deliberate federal safeguard: it protects the SHRC's independence from the very government it is meant to watch, even though that same government appoints it in the first place.

| Stage | Authority | Why this authority |
|---|---|---|
| Appointment | Governor | State-level institution; State-level political process selects it |
| Resignation received | Governor | Routine administrative formality tied to appointment |
| Removal (both tracks) | President | Federal insulation from the State executive being scrutinised |
| Acting Chairperson during vacancy | Governor | Operational continuity at State level |

### 4. Side-by-side: NHRC versus SHRC design

| Dimension | NHRC | SHRC |
|---|---|---|
| Creating command | Centre **shall** constitute | State **may** constitute |
| Appointing authority | President | Governor |
| Removing authority | President | President |
| Chair eligibility | former CJI or SC judge | former HC Chief Justice or HC judge |
| Ordinary judicial members | SC judge + HC Chief Justice | HC judge or District Judge (>=7 years) |
| Expert members | three, at least one woman | one |
| Deemed members | seven, for section 12(b)-(j) | none |
| Headquarters | Delhi (other offices only with prior Central approval) | place notified by the State |
| Tenure | 3 years or age 70 | 3 years or age 70 (same ceiling) |

### 5. Two further design details worth remembering

Two or more State Governments may, with mutual consent and after the required committee recommendations in each State, appoint a **common Chairperson or member** — a cost-sharing and capacity device for smaller States. Separately, the Central Government may confer Union-territory human-rights functions on an SHRC, **except** that functions relating to the Union Territory of Delhi are handled by the NHRC itself, not by any SHRC.

### 6. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** the appointment/removal asymmetry (Governor appoints, President removes) is a perennial close-option trap; also test the District Judge eligibility route and the common-Chairperson provision.

**Mains angle:** the asymmetry is a ready-made example of a "federal safeguard built into institutional design" whenever a question asks about Centre-State balance in constitutional or statutory bodies — use it exactly as you would use All-India Services discipline or Governor's discretionary powers as federalism evidence.

**Common trap:** writing "the Governor removes SHRC members" — this is one of the single most frequently tested factual errors in this entire topic.

## Revision notes

1. SHRC composition: Chairperson (former HC Chief Justice or HC judge) + one judicial member (HC judge or District Judge with >=7 years) + one human-rights expert.
2. No deemed-member layer exists for SHRCs under the Act.
3. Governor appoints, on recommendation of a committee headed by the Chief Minister (plus Assembly Speaker, State Home Minister, Assembly Leader of Opposition).
4. Bicameral States add the Legislative Council Chairman and Council Leader of Opposition to the committee.
5. A sitting High Court judge or District Judge needs prior consultation with the Chief Justice of the relevant High Court before appointment.
6. The Governor receives resignation, but the **President removes** — the single most tested SHRC fact.
7. Removal follows the same two-track structure as the NHRC (misbehaviour/incapacity via Supreme Court inquiry; direct statutory grounds otherwise).
8. Tenure parallels the NHRC: three years or age seventy, whichever is earlier; reappointment eligible.
9. Two or more States may appoint a common Chairperson or member by mutual consent.
10. The NHRC, not any SHRC, handles human-rights functions for the Union Territory of Delhi.
11. The appointment-removal asymmetry is a deliberate federal safeguard protecting the SHRC from the State executive it scrutinises.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State Chief Minister, displeased with an SHRC Chairperson's findings against the State police, orders the Chairperson's removal. Is this order valid?

**Model answer:** No. The Governor appoints an SHRC Chairperson, but only the President may remove one, following the same two-track procedure that applies to NHRC removals. A State Government — including through the Chief Minister or Governor — has no power to remove an SHRC Chairperson or member.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the authority that appoints also removes. For SHRCs, appointment and removal are deliberately split between the Governor and the President.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** The SHRC is appointed by the Governor but can be removed only by the President. Analyse the purpose of this asymmetry and its limits as an independence safeguard.

**Model solution (248 words):** The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 deliberately splits SHRC appointment and removal between two different authorities. The Governor appoints the Chairperson and members on the recommendation of a State-level committee headed by the Chief Minister, and also receives their resignation. Removal, however — whether on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity (after a Supreme Court inquiry) or on direct statutory grounds such as insolvency or a disqualifying conviction — rests exclusively with the President.

The purpose is federal insulation. An SHRC exists principally to scrutinise human-rights conduct within a State, including by State police and administration. If the same State Government that appoints the Commission could also remove it, an aggrieved or unhappy State executive could dismiss an inconvenient Chairperson or member at will, hollowing out the Commission's independence precisely where it matters most. Vesting removal in the President — an authority institutionally distant from day-to-day State administration — denies the State executive that lever.

The safeguard has real limits. The State executive still controls appointment, budget allocation and administrative support, each of which can shape an SHRC's effectiveness short of outright removal. A State can also simply decline to fill vacancies promptly, achieving practical incapacitation without ever triggering the removal question. The asymmetry therefore protects against the most direct form of executive interference — arbitrary dismissal — while leaving subtler forms of State-level pressure largely unaddressed, a gap genuine reform should target alongside, not instead of, the existing removal safeguard.

**Scoring note:** full marks require explaining both who appoints and who removes, the federalism rationale, and at least one concrete limitation of the safeguard (appointment control, budget, or vacancy delay) for the "limits" component of the directive.

*The Commission is not only a complaints office — it also has an independent mandate to study, educate and strengthen the surrounding system.*

### 1. The ten functions, clause by clause

Section 12 lists what the Commission is authorised to do. It helps to see the whole wheel before studying any one spoke:

| Clause | Function | Exact limit |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Inquire into violation/abetment or public-servant negligence in prevention | complaint jurisdiction, subject to sections 19, 21 and 36 |
| (b) | Intervene in a pending court proceeding involving a human-rights allegation | requires the approval of that court |
| (c) | Visit a jail or other State-controlled institution where persons are detained or lodged for treatment, reformation or protection, to study conditions | confined to such institutions; visits to study conditions, not to conduct trials |
| (d) | Review constitutional/legal safeguards for human rights | recommend implementation measures |
| (e) | Review factors, including terrorism, that inhibit the enjoyment of human rights | recommend remedies |
| (f) | Study treaties and other international human-rights instruments | NHRC function only — SHRCs do not receive this clause |
| (g) | Undertake and promote research in the human-rights field | promotional, not adjudicative |
| (h) | Spread human-rights literacy | through publications, media, seminars and other means |
| (i) | Encourage the efforts of NGOs and institutions working in the human-rights field | cooperation function |
| (j) | Perform other functions necessary for the promotion of human rights | must remain tied to human-rights promotion |

### 2. How does a case actually reach the Commission?

```text
SUO MOTU              VICTIM PETITION
    \                       /
     \                     /
      +--> COMMISSION <--+
     /                     \
person on victim's behalf   court direction or order
               |
               v
       section 12(a) inquiry
```

Four entry routes exist: the Commission can act **suo motu** (on its own initiative, for example after a newspaper report of a custodial death); on a **victim's own petition**; on a petition filed by **another person on the victim's behalf** (useful where the victim cannot file personally — detention, death, incapacity); or on a **court's direction or order**. This breadth of entry is itself a Paris-Principles-aligned accessibility feature — a victim does not need to personally draft and file a formal petition for the Commission to act.

### 3. Court intervention is conditional, not automatic

```text
pending court proceeding
          +
human-rights allegation
          +
approval of that court
          =
NHRC/SHRC may intervene
```

A common misconception treats intervention as a right the Commission can simply exercise. It cannot: section 12(b) explicitly requires the **approval of the very court** where the proceeding is pending. The Commission adds expertise and a human-rights perspective to litigation already underway; it does not override judicial control of that litigation.

### 4. The jail-visit function, precisely bounded

Section 12(c), as currently worded after the 2006 Amendment, permits visits — despite any other law — to a jail or other State-controlled institution where persons are detained or lodged for treatment, reformation or protection, to study living conditions and recommend changes. This function is about **studying conditions and recommending improvements**, not conducting criminal investigations inside the institution or adjudicating individual detainee grievances on the spot.

### 5. What SHRCs do *not* get: the section 29 function delta

Section 29 extends section 12 to SHRCs — but **expressly omits clause (f)**. Treaty and international-instrument study is therefore an NHRC-only function; it is not imported into the SHRC's mandate through section 29.

| Function | NHRC | SHRC |
|---|---:|---:|
| Complaint inquiry | yes | yes, within State/Concurrent field |
| Court intervention (with approval) | yes | yes |
| Jail/institution visit | yes | yes |
| Safeguard/factor review | yes | yes |
| Treaty/instrument study (12(f)) | yes | **omitted by section 29** |
| Research/literacy/NGO encouragement | yes | yes |

**Why UPSC can ask this:** this single omission is a clean, text-based distinguishing fact between the two bodies' mandates — exactly the kind of precise detail a well-prepared candidate should know and a hurried one will miss.

### 6. Protection versus promotion — why the distinction matters analytically

The Act gives the Commission a **dual identity**: a protection arm built around complaints, inquiries and court involvement, and a promotion arm built around research, review, education and institutional cooperation. Exam answers that discuss the Commission's "role" often collapse into only the protection arm (complaints) and miss the promotion arm entirely — yet the promotion functions (literacy, NGO encouragement, safeguard review) are exactly where the Commission can have durable, systemic effect even without resolving a single individual case.

**Limit:** neither arm makes the Commission a substitute for police investigation under ordinary criminal procedure, State prosecution, criminal trial, or constitutional adjudication by the Supreme Court and High Courts. It supplements these; it does not replace them.

### 7. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** matching each section-12 clause to its exact function and limit; the section 29 omission of clause (f); the four complaint-entry routes.

**Mains angle:** a complete "functions" answer should explicitly separate the protection and promotion arms rather than listing all ten clauses as an undifferentiated list — differentiation itself earns analytical marks.

**Common trap:** stating that NHRC/SHRC "investigate and prosecute" human-rights violations — they inquire and recommend; prosecution, where recommended, is for the ordinary criminal justice system to carry out.

## Revision notes

1. Section 12(a): inquiry into violation/abetment or public-servant negligence — the Commission's complaint-handling function.
2. Section 12(b): court intervention, but only with that court's approval.
3. Section 12(c): jail/institution visits to study conditions and recommend changes (current wording since the 2006 Amendment).
4. Section 12(d)-(e): review of constitutional/legal safeguards and of inhibiting factors, including terrorism.
5. Section 12(f): study of treaties and international instruments — an NHRC-only function.
6. Section 12(g)-(j): research, literacy promotion, NGO encouragement, and other promotion-linked functions.
7. Four complaint-entry routes: suo motu, victim's petition, petition by another on the victim's behalf, or court direction/order.
8. Section 29 applies section 12 to SHRCs but omits clause (f) — SHRCs do not have the treaty/instrument-study function.
9. The Commission has a dual identity: a protection arm (complaints, court intervention, visits) and a promotion arm (review, research, literacy, NGO cooperation).
10. The Commission supplements, but does not replace, police investigation, prosecution, criminal trial or constitutional adjudication.
11. Jail visits are about studying conditions and recommending change, not conducting on-the-spot adjudication.

### Concept check

**Question:** An SHRC wishes to study India's obligations under an international human-rights covenant as part of its own promotional work. Does section 29 permit this?

**Model answer:** No. Section 29 applies section 12 to SHRCs but expressly omits clause (f), the treaty/international-instrument study function. That function remains an NHRC-only power under the current statutory scheme.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming section 29 imports every section-12 function unchanged into the SHRC's mandate. One specific clause — (f) — is deliberately excluded.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why describing the NHRC/SHRC mandate only in terms of "handling complaints" is incomplete.

**Model solution (146 words):** Describing the Commission purely as a complaints-handling body captures only its protection arm under section 12(a): inquiry into violations or public-servant negligence, entered through suo motu action, victim petitions, representative petitions, or court direction. It omits an equally statutory promotion arm under section 12(b) to (j): intervening in court proceedings with approval, visiting jails and institutions to study conditions, reviewing constitutional and legal safeguards, studying treaties (for the NHRC), undertaking research, spreading human-rights literacy, and encouraging non-governmental organisations working in the field. These promotional functions do not depend on an individual complaint ever being filed, yet they can produce durable systemic change — safeguard reviews and literacy work address root causes, while complaint inquiries address individual instances after the fact. A complete answer on the Commission's role must therefore present both arms, not reduce institutional purpose to grievance redressal alone.

**Scoring note:** full marks require naming at least three promotion-arm functions specifically (not just "promotion work" generically) and explicitly contrasting them with the complaint-handling function.

## 🖼️ Visual: The civil-court power kit

```text
SUMMON -> EXAMINE ON OATH -> REQUIRE DOCUMENTS -> RECEIVE AFFIDAVITS
   -> REQUISITION RECORDS -> ISSUE COMMISSIONS -> BUILD EVIDENCE RECORD
```

*Everything in this kit is about gathering and recording evidence — none of it is about convicting or sentencing anyone.*

### 1. Section 13: strong evidence powers, purpose-specific

While inquiring into complaints, the Commission has the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, for:

- summoning witnesses and enforcing their attendance, and examining them on oath;
- discovery and production of documents;
- receiving evidence on affidavit;
- requisitioning public records or copies from any court or office;
- issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses or documents; and
- any other matter prescribed.

It may also require information subject to lawful privilege, and an authorised gazetted officer may enter a building or place believed to contain relevant documents and seize, copy or extract them, subject to applicable criminal-procedure safeguards. For specified offences committed in its view or presence, the Commission is deemed a civil court for forwarding the matter to a Magistrate, and its proceedings are deemed judicial proceedings for specified purposes under the Penal Code and criminal procedure.

| Proposition | Correct? | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| The Commission may summon a witness | yes | section 13 |
| The Commission is a civil court for every legal purpose | no | the deeming is purpose-specific |
| A Commission proceeding has specified judicial-proceeding status | yes | for the specified purposes only |
| The Commission can itself convict the violator | no | trial power remains with the criminal court |
| A Commission recommendation is executable like a decree | no | section 18 uses recommendatory language (Lesson 7) |

**Why this distinction matters:** "civil-court powers" is one of this topic's most seductive half-truths. It is entirely accurate for evidence-gathering, and entirely wrong if stretched into "the Commission can try and punish" — a leap examiners specifically probe for.

### 2. Who actually investigates — and the independence question it raises

Section 14 lets the Commission use an officer or investigation agency of the Union or a State, with that government's concurrence. The officer or agency acts under the Commission's direction and control and submits a report; the Commission must independently satisfy itself about the correctness of the facts and conclusions in that report, and may examine the investigating persons itself.

```text
COMMISSION decides investigation is needed
                 |
        government concurrence required
                 |
officer/agency services utilised (often drawn from police)
                 |
     acts under Commission's direction and control
                 |
             investigation report
                 |
Commission independently checks facts/conclusions
```

Formally, the Commission retains direction and control over the investigators. But notice the practical tension: the people doing the fieldwork are frequently drawn from the very police forces whose conduct is most often the subject of a human-rights complaint. This single structural feature — government-supplied investigators — is the most repeated independence criticism in this entire topic, and you will see it again as a specific GANHRI concern in Lesson 11.

### 3. A fairness safeguard you must not skip: section 16

Section 16 requires a reasonable opportunity of hearing, and the right to present a defence, wherever the Commission's inquiry examines a person's conduct or may prejudicially affect that person's reputation.

```text
Will the inquiry examine conduct,
OR could it prejudice someone's reputation?
           |
          YES
           |
notice + reasonable hearing + opportunity to present a defence
```

This matters for a balanced exam answer: the Commission is not a one-sided accusation machine against public servants; the Act itself builds in natural-justice protection for the person whose conduct is under examination, consistent with any fair quasi-judicial process.

### 4. How a complaint actually moves through the system

Section 17 lets the Commission call for a report from the concerned government or authority within a specified time. If no report arrives in time, the Commission may inquire into the matter itself. If a report shows that satisfactory action has already been taken, the Commission may close the matter and inform the complainant. In any case, it retains the discretion to initiate an inquiry directly where necessary.

```text
COMPLAINT / SUO MOTU / COURT ROUTE
                 |
        preliminary jurisdiction check
                 |
     call for report OR initiate inquiry
         |                    |
 report adequate          report absent/inadequate
 action taken?                 |
   |                           v
 close/inform             Commission inquiry
         \                    /
          \                  /
            findings stage
```

### 5. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** the exact civil-court powers list, the purpose-specific nature of the deeming provisions, and the distinction between section 13 (evidence) and section 14 (investigation machinery).

**Mains angle:** any "effectiveness" or "limitation" answer should cite section 14's government-investigator dependence as the central practical independence concern — it is more concrete and more frequently rewarded than a vague "lack of independence" claim.

**Common trap:** describing the Commission as having "full investigative independence" — it depends on government-concurred officers/agencies for fieldwork, even though it formally directs and controls them.

## Revision notes

1. Section 13(1): civil-court powers for summoning witnesses, document discovery, affidavit evidence, record requisition and issuing commissions.
2. These powers and the "judicial proceeding" deeming are purpose-specific — they do not make the Commission a court for all purposes.
3. The Commission cannot itself convict; criminal-trial power remains with the criminal courts.
4. Section 14: the Commission may use government-concurred officers/agencies, acting under its direction and control, for investigation.
5. The Commission must independently verify the facts and conclusions in an investigation report, and may examine the investigators.
6. Dependence on government-supplied (often police) investigators is the central structural independence concern raised against the Commission.
7. Section 16: a reasonable opportunity of hearing and defence is required wherever conduct is examined or reputation may be prejudiced.
8. Section 17: the Commission may call for a government report; if none arrives in time, or the report is inadequate, it may inquire itself.
9. If satisfactory action is already shown to have been taken, the Commission may close the matter and inform the complainant.
10. "Civil-court powers" is accurate for evidence-gathering; it is not accurate for adjudication, conviction or decree-style enforcement.

### Concept check

**Question:** The Commission directs a State police officer, with the State Government's concurrence, to investigate a custodial-death complaint. Does this investigation proceed independently of the Commission once assigned?

**Model answer:** No. Under section 14, the officer or agency acts under the Commission's direction and control throughout, and the Commission must independently satisfy itself about the correctness of the facts and conclusions in the resulting report — it may also examine the investigating officer itself. The Commission retains formal oversight even though it does not itself field the investigators.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating section 14 investigation as either fully independent of the Commission or fully independent of the government supplying the officers. Formally, the Commission controls; practically, the investigators are often government/police personnel — both facts matter for a complete answer.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why dependence on government-supplied investigators is considered the Commission's central practical independence concern, even though section 14 formally preserves Commission control.

**Model solution (149 words):** Section 14 of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 allows the Commission to use an officer or investigation agency of the Union or a State, with that government's concurrence, while formally requiring the officer to act under the Commission's direction and control and the Commission to independently verify the report's facts and conclusions. The practical concern arises because these investigators are frequently drawn from the very police forces whose conduct — custodial violence, encounter deaths, or detention practices — forms the most common category of complaint. An officer investigating colleagues from the same force faces an inherent conflict of interest that formal "direction and control" cannot fully neutralise, since fieldwork, evidence access and witness cooperation still run through the investigator's own institutional loyalties. Genuine reform would require an independent, multidisciplinary investigative cadre answerable only to the Commission, rather than continued reliance on government-concurred police personnel for sensitive inquiries.

**Scoring note:** full marks require citing section 14's mechanism accurately, identifying the police-investigating-police conflict specifically (not a vague independence complaint), and naming the independent-cadre reform direction.

```text
        +-- publish the report with government comments and action taken/proposed
```

*Notice every branch begins with "recommend" or "approach" — never "order," "direct" or "impose."*

### 1. The single word that decides most exam answers here: "recommend"

Once an inquiry establishes a violation, section 18 lets the Commission:

1. recommend compensation or damages to the victim or family;
2. recommend prosecution or other suitable action against the concerned public servant;
3. recommend such further action as it thinks fit;
4. approach the Supreme Court or the concerned High Court for directions, orders or writs;
5. recommend immediate interim relief at any stage of the inquiry;
6. make the inquiry report available to the petitioner or representative, subject to the statutory sequence;
7. send the report and recommendations to the concerned government or authority; and
8. publish the report, together with the government's comments and the action taken or proposed.

The government or authority concerned must, within **one month** — or further time the Commission allows — send its comments, including any action taken or proposed.

**Limit:** the legal duty created here is a duty to **respond** with comments and action-taken information. Section 18 does not say every recommendation automatically binds the government to comply. This is the statutory root of the "toothless tiger" criticism you will evaluate in full in Lesson 13 — but notice it is not toothlessness in fact-finding; it is toothlessness in compulsory compliance.

### 2. Evidence power against remedy power, side by side

| Stage | Commission's strength | Ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence gathering | coercive civil-court powers for listed matters (Lesson 6) | limited to the inquiry itself |
| Fact finding | a reasoned inquiry report | reviewable by constitutional courts |
| Victim relief | may recommend compensation or interim relief | not a self-executing award |
| Accountability | may recommend prosecution or other action | a prosecutor, department or court must still act |
| Constitutional enforcement | may approach the Supreme Court or High Court | the court decides the actual relief |
| Publicity | publishes the report and the government's response | reputational pressure, not a coercive sanction |

### 3. The ordinary response timetable

```text
Commission sends report + recommendations
                 |
       one month by default
        (or further time allowed)
                 |
government/authority sends comments:
action taken OR action proposed
                 |
Commission publishes report + response
```

Hold this one-month default alongside Lesson 9's three-month armed-forces timetable — the contrast between the two is a frequently tested comparison.

### 4. Where does the report finally go, and who is accountable to whom?

```text
NHRC REPORT -> Centre/concerned State Government -> laid before Parliament/State Legislature
SHRC REPORT -> State Government                   -> laid before the State Legislature
                         |
             action-taken/proposed memorandum
             + reasons for non-acceptance, if any
```

NHRC annual and special reports go to the Central Government and the concerned State Government, and must be laid before Parliament or the State Legislature, with an action-taken memorandum and reasons for any non-acceptance. SHRC reports go to the State Government and are laid before the State Legislature on the same pattern.

### 5. Does publication without compulsion actually achieve anything?

Publication, legislative laying and a reasoned non-acceptance requirement create real political and reputational accountability even where legal compulsion is weak — a government that must publicly explain why it is rejecting a human-rights recommendation faces a cost quite different from simply ignoring a private letter. But delayed laying of reports, or generic, boilerplate responses, can blunt that accountability considerably. A sound reform answer should therefore focus on strengthening the **follow-up** mechanism — time-bound responses, detailed reasoned rejection, judicial escalation on default — rather than mischaracterising the current recommendations as already binding.

### 6. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** the exact section-18 remedy list, the one-month response default, and the reporting chain (NHRC to Parliament, SHRC to State Legislature).

**Mains angle:** this is the statutory core of almost every "effectiveness" or "toothless tiger" question — pair the recommendatory limit with a specific, named reform (comply-or-reasoned-reject duty with court escalation) rather than a generic call for "more powers."

**Common trap:** describing section 18 outputs as "orders" or stating that government compliance is mandatory — the Act's own language is consistently recommendatory.

## Revision notes

1. Section 18 lets the Commission recommend compensation/damages, recommend prosecution/other action, recommend further action, approach the SC/HC, recommend interim relief, and publish its report.
2. The government/authority must respond with comments and action taken/proposed within one month, or further time the Commission allows.
3. The legal duty is to respond, not to comply — recommendations are not self-executing decrees.
4. Evidence-gathering power (section 13) is coercive; remedy power (section 18) is recommendatory — do not confuse the two.
5. NHRC reports go to the Centre/concerned State and must be laid before Parliament/State Legislature with an action-taken memorandum and reasons for non-acceptance (section 20).
6. SHRC reports go to the State Government and are laid before the State Legislature on the same pattern (section 28).
7. Publication and legislative laying create political/reputational accountability even without legal compulsion.
8. Delayed laying or generic responses can weaken this accountability in practice.
9. The reform direction favoured by most serious analysis is a comply-or-reasoned-reject duty with judicial escalation, not converting the Commission into a court.
10. Compare the one-month ordinary response default against Lesson 9's three-month armed-forces default.

### Concept check

**Question:** The Commission recommends that a State Government pay compensation to a custodial-torture victim. The State Government declines, citing budget constraints, and takes no further action. Has the State Government violated section 18?

**Model answer:** Not necessarily on the compliance point itself, but it may be in breach of its response duty if it fails to send its comments, including reasons for declining, within one month or the further time the Commission allows. Section 18 obliges the government to respond with action taken or proposed; it does not oblige the government to comply with the recommendation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating "the government declined" with "the government broke the law." Only a failure to respond within the statutory/extended time, not a reasoned refusal to comply, is a clear breach of the Act's own text.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "Section 18 gives the NHRC strong recommendatory powers but no compulsory remedy." Examine this statement with reference to the statutory text.

**Model solution (150 words):** Section 18 of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 authorises the Commission, after a finding of violation, to recommend compensation or damages, recommend prosecution or other suitable action, recommend further action, recommend interim relief at any inquiry stage, and approach the Supreme Court or High Court for directions. It also requires the government or authority concerned to respond with comments, including action taken or proposed, within one month or further allowed time, and empowers the Commission to publish the report together with that response. The statement is accurate: every remedy is framed as a recommendation or a court approach, never a direct order. The Commission cannot itself award compensation, prosecute, or compel compliance; it depends on the government's voluntary action, judicial intervention through the courts it approaches, or the reputational pressure of publication and legislative scrutiny to convert a recommendation into an actual remedy.

**Scoring note:** full marks require citing at least three distinct section-18 outputs by name and explicitly stating the recommendatory, non-compulsory character rather than merely asserting "it is weak."

## 🖼️ Visual: Section 36's double gate

```text
GATE 1: Is the matter pending before an SHRC or another duly
        constituted commission?
        YES -> NHRC does not inquire
        NO  -> move to Gate 2

GATE 2: Has more than one year passed since the alleged
        violative act?
        YES -> NHRC/SHRC statutory inquiry is barred
        NO  -> other jurisdiction checks continue
```

*Two separate fences, not one — do not collapse them into a single "one-year rule."*

### 1. Why would a statute deliberately limit its own watchdog's reach?

It can feel counterintuitive that a human-rights body would willingly fence off its own jurisdiction. But every fence here serves a specific institutional purpose: preventing duplicate inquiries into the same matter (Gate 1), preventing stale, hard-to-verify complaints from clogging a fast-moving inquiry body (Gate 2), keeping the SHRC's scrutiny within the State's own constitutional competence (the List II/III boundary below), and requiring a public-servant nexus so the Commission is not turned into a general civil-dispute forum (below). None of these fences is accidental; each trades some reach for institutional focus and credibility.

### 2. Gate 1 — no duplicate inquiry

Section 36(1) bars the NHRC from inquiring into a matter that is already pending before an SHRC or another duly constituted commission. The logic is simple: two bodies investigating the identical matter risks contradictory findings, wasted effort and forum-shopping by complainants.

### 3. Gate 2 — the one-year limitation, and why it is strict

Section 36(2) bars the NHRC or an SHRC from inquiring into a matter more than **one year** after the date of the alleged violative act. This is not a flexible guideline; the governing Supreme Court authority (studied fully in Lesson 10) has treated it as a strict **jurisdictional bar**, rejected a generic "continuing wrong until reparation" theory for a detention that had already ended, and noted that the Act gives the Commission no power to extend this period on its own.

| Situation | Relevant inquiry question |
|---|---|
| Completed illegal detention | When did the unlawful detention actually end? |
| Recurring but separate acts | Is each later act independently alleged, with its own one-year clock? |
| A continuing harmful *effect* | The effect alone does not automatically extend the "act" for limitation purposes |

**Why this matters for reform answers:** proposals to relax the one-year bar for grave, concealed, or vulnerable-complainant cases are genuine and defensible — but they remain **proposals**, not enacted law, and must be labelled accordingly.

### 4. The SHRC's federal boundary: State List and Concurrent List only

Section 21(5) confines an SHRC to violations connected with entries in the **State List** and the **Concurrent List**. If the NHRC or another duly constituted commission is already inquiring into the matter, the SHRC cannot take it up.

```text
human-rights allegation
        |
State List or Concurrent List connection?
        | YES
already being inquired by NHRC/another commission?
        | NO
one-year limit satisfied?
        | YES
SHRC may proceed
```

This confinement is the structural reason an SHRC cannot, for example, independently inquire into a matter that sits squarely within a Union List subject — that falls to the NHRC or another appropriate national body instead.

### 5. The public-servant nexus — why private disputes mostly stay out

The complaint function under section 12(a) is framed around violation, abetment, or negligent failure to prevent, **by a public servant**. A purely private assault, discrimination or exploitation dispute generally sits outside the Commission's complaint jurisdiction unless a public servant is alleged to have violated or abetted the right, or negligently failed to prevent the violation.

```text
PRIVATE ACTOR ALLEGATION
         |
public servant directly involved or abetted?
         OR
public servant negligently failed to prevent?
         |
        YES -> possible section 12(a) route
        NO  -> use police/court/specialised civil or statutory remedies instead
```

**Limit:** this is not a loophole that excuses State inaction — a public servant's **negligent failure to prevent** a violation (for example, police inaction during a mob attack) itself brings the matter within the public-servant nexus; it is not only direct perpetration that counts.

### 6. One more fence worth remembering: Delhi

As noted in Lesson 4, the Central Government may confer Union-territory human-rights functions on an SHRC, except that functions relating to the Union Territory of Delhi are handled by the NHRC itself.

### 7. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** exact wording of the one-year rule ("from the date of the act," not "from the date of the complaint" or "from the date of discovery"); the SHRC's List II/III confinement; the public-servant nexus requirement.

**Mains angle:** a "limitations" answer should name both section 36 gates separately, since examiners frequently reward candidates who do not collapse the overlap bar and the time bar into one undifferentiated "jurisdiction problem."

**Common trap:** stating that the Commission can inquire into "any old human-rights violation" or that the one-year clock resets whenever new evidence of an old violation surfaces — both contradict the settled case-law reading in Lesson 10.

## Revision notes

1. Section 36(1): NHRC may not inquire into a matter already pending before an SHRC or another duly constituted commission.
2. Section 36(2): neither the NHRC nor an SHRC may inquire more than one year after the alleged violative act.
3. The one-year bar has been judicially treated as a strict jurisdictional bar, not a flexible procedural guideline.
4. A generic "continuing wrong until reparation" theory does not automatically extend the one-year clock for a completed act.
5. The Act gives the Commission no statutory power to extend the one-year period itself.
6. Section 21(5): an SHRC's jurisdiction is confined to State List and Concurrent List subjects.
7. An SHRC cannot inquire into a matter already being inquired into by the NHRC or another duly constituted commission.
8. The complaint function requires a public-servant nexus — direct violation/abetment, or negligent failure to prevent.
9. Negligent failure to prevent a violation (not only direct perpetration) brings a matter within the public-servant nexus.
10. The NHRC, not an SHRC, handles human-rights functions for the Union Territory of Delhi.
11. Relaxing the one-year bar for grave or concealed violations remains a reform proposal, not enacted law.

### Concept check

**Question:** A complainant alleges a custodial assault that took place fourteen months before filing the complaint, arguing that the psychological trauma from the assault is a "continuing wrong" that keeps the matter within time. Is this argument likely to succeed under section 36(2)?

**Model answer:** It is unlikely to succeed on the settled reading of section 36(2). The one-year period runs from the date of the alleged violative act itself. A continuing *effect* of a completed act — such as ongoing psychological trauma — does not, by itself, convert a completed past act into a continuing wrong that resets or extends the limitation clock.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that any lingering harm from an old violation automatically qualifies as a "continuing wrong" that defeats the one-year bar. The bar is measured from the date of the act, not the duration of its consequences.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Distinguish the two jurisdictional gates under section 36 of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, and evaluate whether the one-year limitation strikes a reasonable balance.

**Model solution (249 words):** Section 36 creates two distinct jurisdictional fences. Section 36(1) bars the NHRC from inquiring into a matter already pending before an SHRC or another duly constituted commission, preventing duplicate inquiries, contradictory findings and forum-shopping. Section 36(2) separately bars the NHRC or an SHRC from inquiring into a matter more than one year after the date of the alleged violative act, treated judicially as a strict jurisdictional bar rather than a flexible limitation period that courts may extend case by case.

The one-year rule balances two legitimate concerns. In its favour: human-rights inquiries depend heavily on witness memory, physical evidence and institutional records that deteriorate quickly, so a time limit encourages prompt complaints and protects the Commission's fact-finding credibility; it also prevents stale, difficult-to-verify allegations from consuming an already resource-constrained institution's limited capacity.

Against it: victims of custodial violence, sexual violence, or violations by powerful actors often face genuine barriers to filing promptly — fear of reprisal, lack of awareness, physical incapacitation, or active concealment by the violator — meaning the bar can shield precisely the gravest and most power-asymmetric violations from scrutiny. The absence of any statutory power to extend the period, even for demonstrably concealed violations, makes the fence unusually rigid compared with ordinary limitation statutes, which typically allow condonation of delay for sufficient cause.

A more defensible design would preserve the one-year default while creating a narrow, reasoned exception for concealed or trauma-linked delay — balancing evidentiary freshness against access to justice for the most vulnerable complainants.

**Scoring note:** full marks require distinguishing both gates by section number, giving at least one argument on each side of the balance evaluation, and a specific reform suggestion rather than a generic call to "relax the rule."

```text
complaint / suo motu
```

*Compare this carefully against Lesson 7's ordinary one-month response default — the timetable itself is different, and so is the fact-finding method.*

### 1. Why does the armed forces get a separate procedure at all?

Section 2(1)(a) defines "armed forces" as the naval, military and air forces, and any other armed forces of the Union. For complaints against members of the armed forces, section 19 **overrides** the ordinary sections 13-18 procedure: the Commission may act suo motu or on petition, but instead of conducting its own direct inquiry with civil-court powers, it may only **seek a report from the Central Government**. After receiving that report, the Commission may decide not to proceed further, or it may make recommendations. The Central Government must inform the Commission of action taken within **three months**, or further time the Commission allows. The Commission then publishes its report, recommendations and the government's response, and a copy reaches the petitioner or representative.

| Dimension | Ordinary sections 13-18 route | Armed-forces section 19 route |
|---|---|---|
| Body | NHRC/SHRC as applicable | NHRC only |
| Fact finding | direct inquiry with civil-court powers | report sought from the Central Government |
| Response default | one month (section 18) | three months (section 19) |
| Output | recommendations, court approach, publication | recommendations and publication |
| Concern raised | non-binding follow-up | reduced independent fact-finding |

### 2. The genuine trade-off, honestly stated

The special route reflects a real operational and security concern — ordinary civil-court-style inquiry procedures (summoning serving officers, demanding operational documents) could be disruptive or sensitive in an armed-forces context. But the trade-off is real too: a report-based procedure, where the Commission depends on the very institution under scrutiny to supply the facts, weakens perceived independence precisely where allegations (custodial deaths in counter-insurgency operations, excessive force) tend to be most serious.

**Limit, and a frequently misapplied trap:** State police are **not** automatically "armed forces" under section 2(1)(a). Do not apply section 19's special procedure to an ordinary State-police custodial-death or encounter allegation — those remain governed by the ordinary sections 13-18 route (or, where applicable, the special facts of *EEVFAM*-type litigation studied in Lesson 10).

### 3. Human Rights Courts: a criminal-trial forum, not a Commission function

A State Government **may**, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, notify a Court of Session for each district as a **Human Rights Court**, for the speedy trial of offences arising out of human-rights violations. The provision does not apply where a Court of Session is already specified as a Special Court, or where a Special Court already exists for those offences under another law. For every Human Rights Court, section 31 requires either a notified Public Prosecutor or the appointment of an advocate with at least **seven years' practice** as Special Public Prosecutor.

```text
STATE GOVERNMENT proposal
          +
Chief Justice of the High Court's concurrence
          |
notification of a Court of Session
          |
HUMAN RIGHTS COURT for speedy trial
          |
Special Public Prosecutor:
notified PP OR advocate with >=7 years' practice
```

**Limit:** the Act does not create a new offence called "human-rights violation," does not prescribe a complete separate criminal procedure for every such case, and does not automatically activate a dedicated court in every district. Operationalisation depends on actual State notification, an underlying offence-creating law, available prosecutors, and functioning procedural machinery — a gap genuinely worth noting rather than papering over with an invented nationwide count.

### 4. How the Commission and a Human Rights Court differ fundamentally

| Institution | Main role | Binding output |
|---|---|---|
| NHRC/SHRC | inquire, review, recommend, publish | recommendations generally not binding |
| Human Rights Court | try an offence within the ordinary criminal-law framework | judgment/order, binding subject to appeal |
| Supreme Court/High Court | constitutional adjudication and writs | binding orders and judgments |
| Police/investigation agency | investigate cognisable offences under law | investigation report, not a verdict |
| Prosecutor | conduct the prosecution | argument, not judgment |

A Human Rights Court tries and convicts within an ordinary criminal-law framework; the Commission inquires and recommends. Conflating the two — describing the Commission as able to "convict" offenders, or describing a Human Rights Court as a Commission function — is a structural error examiners watch for.

### 5. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** section 19's three-month default (versus the one-month ordinary default), the exact Human Rights Court notification chain, and the seven-years'-practice prosecutor qualification.

**Mains angle:** the armed-forces route is a strong, specific example of how a statutory design choice (operational-security concern) trades off against an institutional value (independent fact-finding) — use it as concrete evidence rather than a generic "accountability gap" claim.

**Common trap:** applying section 19 to ordinary State police complaints, or claiming Human Rights Courts function automatically and uniformly across all districts.

### Concept check

**Question:** A complainant alleges excessive force by a State police unit during a law-and-order operation. Should the NHRC apply the section 19 armed-forces procedure to this complaint?

## 🖼️ Visual: Three-case synthesis

```text
PARAMJIT KAUR  -> a constitutional assignment can place NHRC beyond ordinary s.36(2)
DHOUNDIAL      -> ordinary NHRC jurisdiction remains tightly statutory
EEVFAM         -> courts retain Article 32 oversight; NHRC is useful, not exclusive
```

*Read together, these three cases draw the exact outer edge of the Commission's jurisdiction — where it can stretch, where it cannot, and who stays in ultimate control.*

### 1. *Paramjit Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1999): NHRC as the Supreme Court's expert body

In the Punjab mass-cremation matter, the Supreme Court used the NHRC as an expert fact-finding body assisting its own Article 32 jurisdiction. The significance of this case lies less in its own facts than in how the later *Dhoundial* judgment explained it: when the NHRC investigates **pursuant to Supreme Court directions under Article 32**, the ordinary one-year bar under section 36(2) does not apply, because the NHRC is then acting as the Court's expert body rather than exercising its ordinary statutory jurisdiction under the PHRA.

```text
ORDINARY NHRC CASE                SUPREME COURT ARTICLE 32 ASSIGNMENT
acts under the PHRA                acts as the Court's expert body
section 36(2) applies              *Paramjit Kaur*/*Dhoundial* exception applies
```

**Limit:** this is not a general licence for the Commission to ignore section 36(2) whenever a case feels grave or important. The exception depends specifically on the Supreme Court's own constitutional assignment of the task to the NHRC — it is the Court's authority being exercised through the Commission, not the Commission expanding its own jurisdiction.

### 2. *N.C. Dhoundial v. Union of India* (11 December 2003): the one-year bar is jurisdictional

Here, an alleged illegal detention had ended in April 1994; the complaint reached the NHRC only years later. The NHRC itself had treated the continued non-reparation as a "continuing wrong," effectively trying to sidestep section 36(2). The Supreme Court rejected this approach outright, holding that the Commission is a **creature of statute**, bound strictly by its own statutory limits, and describing section 36(2) as a **jurisdictional embargo** measured from the date of the alleged violative act — not from the date of the complaint, and not from the date the harm was discovered or persisted.

```text
illegal detention ended
        |
complaint filed years later
        |
NHRC invokes a "continuing wrong" theory
        |
Supreme Court rejects the generic theory
        |
section 36(2) = a strict jurisdictional bar
```

The Court specifically noted that the completed detention did not somehow "repeat" daily after the person's lawful release, and that the Act contains no provision allowing the Commission to extend the one-year period on its own authority.

**Why this is the decisive authority for Lesson 8's limitation discussion:** whenever an exam question tests whether the Commission can revive an old complaint through a clever "continuing wrong" argument, *Dhoundial* is the direct, named precedent that answers it — no, not as a general matter.

### 3. *EEVFAM v. Union of India* (8 July 2016): Article 32 oversight continues

The Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association petition concerned alleged extra-judicial executions in Manipur. The Supreme Court held the Article 32 petition **maintainable**, reiterating that excessive or retaliatory force by police or Union armed forces is impermissible even in an area under the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, and that allegations of excessive force causing death must be thoroughly inquired into. The Court also observed that an internal armed-forces inquiry, or a State magisterial inquiry, would not necessarily preclude another lawful inquiry or investigation — recognising judicial inquiry, NHRC inquiry, or an inquiry under the Commissions of Inquiry Act as possible accountability routes in the context before it. The Court also recorded the NHRC's own "toothless tiger" grievance, and — importantly — kept open, at that stage, the specific question whether NHRC guidelines or recommendations were binding or merely advisory.

| Claim | Named evidence | What it proves | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security operations remain reviewable | *EEVFAM*, Article 32 | there is no rights-free zone, even in a disturbed area | operational context still matters to the analysis |
| Excessive/retaliatory force is impermissible | reiterated in *EEVFAM* | force is legally constrained | not every encounter is automatically unlawful |
| A death allegation needs thorough inquiry | *EEVFAM*'s conclusion | accountability requires established facts | the appropriate inquiry body/procedure depends on the law and the Court's own directions |
| NHRC has genuine institutional relevance | the Court sought NHRC assistance/information | specialist fact-finding has real value | not a substitute for Article 32 or a criminal court |

**Limit, and a trap to avoid:** do not cite *EEVFAM* as a holding that section 18 recommendations have generally become binding. The judgment kept that specific question open in the passage relevant here — citing it as settling the binding-versus-advisory debate overstates what it actually decided.

### 4. Reading the three cases together

```text
PARAMJIT KAUR -> a constitutional assignment can place the NHRC beyond ordinary s.36(2)
DHOUNDIAL     -> but ordinary NHRC jurisdiction remains tightly, strictly statutory
EEVFAM        -> and the Supreme Court retains ultimate Article 32 oversight regardless;
                 the NHRC is a useful specialist tool, never an exclusive remedy
```

The unifying thread: the judiciary consistently treats the Commission as a valuable, specialised fact-finding instrument that operates **within** and **under** constitutional courts, never as a body with freestanding power to expand its own jurisdiction or to convert its recommendations into binding decrees on its own authority.

### 5. UPSC application

**Prelims angle:** matching each case to its correct holding (expert-body exception, jurisdictional bar, Article 32 maintainability) — a classic case-attribution trap format.

**Mains angle:** cite *Dhoundial* by name whenever discussing the one-year limitation's strictness, and *EEVFAM* whenever discussing security-force accountability or the open binding-versus-advisory question — named case law consistently earns more marks than an unattributed general claim.

**Common trap:** citing *EEVFAM* as proof that NHRC recommendations are now binding, or treating the *Paramjit Kaur* exception as a general rule rather than a narrow, Court-assigned exception.

## Revision notes

1. *Paramjit Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1999): the NHRC acted as the Supreme Court's expert body in the Punjab mass-cremation matter.
2. *Dhoundial* later explained *Paramjit Kaur*: when the NHRC investigates under a Supreme Court Article 32 direction, section 36(2) does not apply, because the NHRC acts as the Court's expert body, not under ordinary PHRA jurisdiction.
3. This exception is narrow — it depends on a specific constitutional assignment, not on the general seriousness of a case.
4. *N.C. Dhoundial v. Union of India* (11 December 2003): the Supreme Court held section 36(2) to be a strict jurisdictional embargo.
5. The Court rejected a generic "continuing wrong until reparation" theory for a detention that had already ended.
6. The Act gives the Commission no power to extend the one-year period on its own authority.
7. *EEVFAM v. Union of India* (8 July 2016): the Supreme Court held the Article 32 petition on alleged Manipur extra-judicial executions maintainable.
8. *EEVFAM* reiterated that excessive/retaliatory force is impermissible even in an AFSPA-disturbed area, and that death allegations require thorough inquiry.
9. *EEVFAM* recognised judicial inquiry, NHRC inquiry, or a Commissions-of-Inquiry-Act inquiry as possible accountability routes, without making any one of them exclusive.
10. *EEVFAM* kept open, rather than settled, the question whether NHRC recommendations are binding or advisory.
11. Read together, the three cases show the judiciary treating the NHRC as a useful specialist instrument operating within and under constitutional courts, not as an independent jurisdiction-expanding authority.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes that *EEVFAM* (2016) established that NHRC recommendations are now legally binding on the government. Is this an accurate reading of the case?

**Model answer:** No. *EEVFAM* recorded the NHRC's "toothless tiger" grievance and discussed NHRC inquiry as one possible accountability route for the Manipur extra-judicial-execution allegations, but it explicitly kept open, rather than decided, the specific question whether NHRC recommendations are binding or merely advisory. Citing it as having settled that question overstates its actual holding.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating any Supreme Court judgment that discusses the NHRC favourably as having resolved the binding-versus-advisory debate. Only the precise holding of a case may be cited for a proposition; general favourable mentions are not the same as a decided point of law.

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Using named case law, explain how Indian courts have simultaneously expanded and constrained the National Human Rights Commission's jurisdiction.

**Model solution (238 words):** Indian case law has drawn a careful two-sided boundary around the NHRC's jurisdiction — enlarging it in one narrow context while firmly constraining it in the ordinary case.

The expansion comes from *Paramjit Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1999), as later explained in *N.C. Dhoundial v. Union of India* (2003). When the Supreme Court assigns an investigation to the NHRC under its own Article 32 jurisdiction — as in the Punjab mass-cremation matter — the NHRC then acts as the Court's expert fact-finding body rather than under its ordinary statutory jurisdiction, and section 36(2)'s ordinary one-year limitation does not apply. This is a genuine enlargement of the NHRC's practical reach, but one depending entirely on the Supreme Court's own assignment, not on the NHRC's independent authority.

The constraint comes from *Dhoundial* itself, decided on 11 December 2003: outside such an assignment, the Commission is "a creature of statute" bound strictly by section 36(2), a jurisdictional embargo it cannot extend on its own, and a generic "continuing wrong" argument cannot revive a time-barred complaint.

*EEVFAM v. Union of India* (2016) completes the picture: even where NHRC inquiry is relevant, as in the Manipur extra-judicial-execution allegations, the Supreme Court retains ultimate Article 32 oversight and treats NHRC inquiry as one accountability route among several, not an exclusive one. Together, the three cases confirm the NHRC's jurisdiction expands only when a constitutional court actively extends it, and otherwise remains tightly statutory.

**Scoring note:** full marks require naming all three cases correctly, stating the expansion-versus-constraint structure explicitly, and avoiding the overreading trap of treating *EEVFAM* as having made recommendations binding.

*An institution is judged against all six heads together — strength on one head cannot substitute for weakness on another.*

### 1. From intuition to the formal framework

Lesson 1 gave you the plain idea: a national human-rights institution earns international credibility by being independent, plural and well-resourced, not merely by existing. The UN General Assembly's Resolution 48/134 — OHCHR's instrument text cites **19 December 1993**, while the official UN resolution record places adoption at the 85th plenary meeting on **20 December 1993** — formalised that idea as the **Paris Principles**, calling for:

- the broadest possible promotion-and-protection mandate, fixed in constitutional or legislative text, not an administrative circular;
- pluralist representation of social forces in the institution's composition;
- adequate funding, staff and premises supporting genuine independence;
- stable, protected terms of office;
- freedom to consider issues, hear persons, obtain documents and publicise its views;
- regular meetings and working groups;
- cooperation with courts, ombuds institutions, civil society and vulnerable groups; and
- fair complaint, conciliation, referral and reform methods wherever the institution has quasi-jurisdictional competence.

### 2. Mapping India's design against each Paris head

| Paris head | Supporting feature in the PHRA | Tension or qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Legal mandate | a detailed parliamentary statute | mandate narrowed by the one-year bar and the armed-forces route |
| Stability | fixed statutory term and protected removal | three-year terms are comparatively short |
| Pluralism | three experts with a one-woman minimum, plus deemed specialised members | a floor, not a guarantee of broad social diversity or gender balance |
| Investigation | civil-court powers and statutory investigation provisions | government-provided police/officials create a genuine independence concern (Lesson 6) |
| Resources | statutory grants and staff | government provision of resources can itself affect perceived autonomy |
| Accessibility | petitions, representative filing, suo motu action | the one-year bar, geography, awareness and capacity still affect real access |

This table is the single most useful tool for a Paris-Principles exam answer: every "India's gap" claim should be tied to one specific head, not asserted vaguely.

### 3. GANHRI: who grades compliance, and how

Compliance with the Paris Principles is externally assessed by the **Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions (GANHRI)**, whose Sub-Committee on Accreditation (SCA) reviews national institutions and assigns a grade of **A, B or C**. A-status institutions may participate fully, including with speaking and voting rights, in UN human-rights forums; B-status institutions may only observe, without full participation rights — a real, consequential international downgrade, not a purely symbolic label.

The recommendation was not a single complaint but a cluster of concerns mapping directly onto the Paris heads above:

- weak **pluralism** in composition and staffing;

- the Commission's response to serious human-rights violations and its public positioning on them; and

1. The Paris Principles (UNGA Resolution 48/134, 19-20 December 1993 — sources diverge by one day) set the global benchmark for a credible national human-rights institution.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student writes, "GANHRI downgraded India's NHRC to B-status in 2025." Is this precisely accurate as of 2 October 2026?

**Model answer:** Not quite. In March 2025, the SCA *recommended* the downgrade; on 4 December 2025, the GANHRI Bureau *upheld* that recommendation after rejecting India's appeal — confirming the decision in principle. ### Mains practice (original)

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the Paris Principles as the benchmark for evaluating India's National Human Rights Commission, with reference to the current accreditation review.

**Model solution (250 words):** The Paris Principles, adopted by the UN General Assembly through Resolution 48/134 (19 December 1993 per OHCHR; 20 December 1993 per the official UN record), set the global benchmark for a credible national human-rights institution: a broad, legally-anchored mandate; pluralist composition; genuine independence; adequate resources; effective investigative methods and powers; and real accessibility to complainants. India's Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, enacted in the same year, was consciously built to meet this benchmark, earning the NHRC "A" accreditation from the Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions (GANHRI) since 1999, re-accredited in 2006, 2011 and 2017.

The episode shows accreditation functioning exactly as the Paris Principles intend: as an evidence-based external check rather than a formality. Durable reform — an independent investigative cadre, transparent appointments, and genuine pluralism — would do more to restore India's standing than contesting the Bureau's finding.

### Concept check

**Question:** A Mains answer lists five limitations of the NHRC but ends with the single remedy "grant it more powers." What is the main drafting weakness of this answer, independent of whether the five limitations are individually correct?

### Mains practice (original)

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** "The NHRC's problem is not that it lacks powers, but that none of its powers end in a binding remedy." Critically examine this statement and suggest a reform that preserves the Commission's existing institutional character.

**Model solution (249 words):** The statement is broadly accurate and usefully precise. Section 13 gives the NHRC genuine civil-court powers — summoning witnesses, requiring documents, receiving affidavit evidence — and section 12 authorises wide-ranging inquiry, court intervention and institutional visits. The Commission is therefore well-equipped to establish facts. Yet every output under section 18 — compensation, prosecution, interim relief, or an approach to the Supreme Court or High Court — is framed as a **recommendation**; the government must respond within one month but need not comply, and only an approaching court, not the Commission itself, can ultimately issue a binding remedy.

This precisely locates the weakness: not investigative incapacity, but the absence of a compulsory final step. *N.C. Dhoundial* (2003) confirms the Commission is bound by its own statutory limits rather than possessing inherent coercive authority, while *EEVFAM* (2016) shows courts, not the Commission, retaining ultimate constitutional oversight.

A reform preserving the Commission's existing recommendatory, non-judicial character would introduce a statutory **comply-or-reasoned-reject duty**: the government must, within a fixed period, either comply or publish detailed, specific reasons for declining, with an aggrieved complainant retaining an independent right to escalate a rejected recommendation to the relevant High Court. This converts a currently cost-free non-compliance into a reputationally and procedurally costly choice, without transforming the Commission itself into a tribunal or removing the appeal safeguards a genuine court provides — strengthening enforcement while respecting the Paris-Principles-aligned, inquiry-based design Parliament originally chose.

**Scoring note:** full marks require explicitly distinguishing investigative power from remedy power, citing at least one named case, and proposing a reform that is explicitly stated to preserve (not replace) the Commission's existing character.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks test connections **across** lessons, beyond any single lesson's local concept check.

### Cumulative check 1 — identity and composition

**Question:** Why can a reform proposal to "make the NHRC a constitutional body like the NCST" not be achieved through the same ordinary amendment process that changed its composition in 2019?

**Model answer:** The 2019 composition change was an ordinary statutory amendment to the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 — Parliament's normal legislative power over a statutory body. Making the NHRC a constitutional body, by contrast, would require inserting it into the Constitution itself (as Articles 338, 338A and 338B did for the NCSC, NCST and NCBC), which demands the higher threshold of a constitutional amendment under Article 368, not an ordinary Act.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating all "reform" as procedurally equivalent. Changing composition within a statutory framework and changing the body's fundamental constitutional status are different orders of legal change.

### Cumulative check 2 — appointment-removal asymmetry across both bodies

**Question:** Compare, in one sentence each, what the appointment-removal design of the NHRC and the SHRC reveals about where each body's independence is most at risk.

**Model answer:** For the NHRC, appointment and removal both ultimately trace to central political authorities (the six-member committee and the President), so its independence risk concerns central-government influence over an all-India mandate. For the SHRC, appointment by the Governor and removal by the President deliberately split State and central authority, precisely to prevent the State government being scrutinised from also controlling the Commission's removal.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the SHRC's design is simply a smaller copy of the NHRC's. The appointment-removal split is a distinct federal safeguard not needed, in the same form, at the national level.

### Cumulative check 3 — powers across the inquiry-to-remedy chain

**Question:** Trace the Commission's power from the moment a complaint is filed to the moment a government responds, naming the section governing each stage.

**Model answer:** A complaint enters under section 12(a) (suo motu, petition, representative petition, or court direction). The Commission gathers evidence under section 13's civil-court powers, and may use section 14's government-concurred investigation machinery, subject to the section 16 fairness safeguard. After inquiry, section 18 permits only recommendations — compensation, prosecution, interim relief, or an approach to a constitutional court — and the government must respond within one month under section 18, or three months under section 19 for armed-forces complaints.

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing the Commission's coercive civil-court powers (section 13) extend into its remedy stage (section 18). The two stages have fundamentally different legal character — coercive at the evidence stage, recommendatory at the remedy stage.

### Cumulative check 4 — jurisdiction, case law and current status, together

**Question:** A State human-rights activist argues that, because GANHRI has flagged India's NHRC for independence concerns, the one-year limitation under section 36(2) should simply be ignored by the Commission in genuinely serious cases. Evaluate this argument using the governing case law.

**Model answer:** The argument conflates two separate issues. GANHRI's accreditation concerns (Lesson 11) are an international, reputational assessment of institutional design; they create no domestic legal power for the Commission to disregard its own statute. *N.C. Dhoundial* (2003) establishes that section 36(2) is a strict jurisdictional bar that the Commission cannot extend on its own authority, regardless of a case's perceived gravity — the only recognised exception is the narrow *Paramjit Kaur* scenario, where a constitutional court itself assigns the investigation under Article 32. Reform of the one-year bar must come through statutory amendment, not through the Commission unilaterally reinterpreting its own jurisdiction.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating international accreditation pressure as a source of domestic legal authority. GANHRI's findings are evidence for a reform argument; they do not alter the Commission's current statutory jurisdiction.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These three questions are distinct from every lesson-local and cumulative question above, forming the session's final, standalone practice set.

## Final 10-mark question (150-word ceiling)

**Question:** Distinguish the NHRC's "civil-court powers" from an actual court's adjudicative powers, with one example of each.

**Model solution (149 words):** The NHRC's civil-court powers, under section 13 of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, are confined to **evidence-gathering** during an inquiry — for example, summoning a witness and examining them on oath to establish what happened during an alleged custodial assault. An actual court's adjudicative powers extend to **determining guilt or liability and issuing a binding, appealable judgment** — for example, a criminal court convicting and sentencing the officer responsible after a full trial. The Commission can build a credible, publicly reported factual record and recommend prosecution or compensation, but it cannot itself convict, sentence, or award compensation as of right; those outcomes depend on the ordinary criminal or civil justice system, or a constitutional court the Commission approaches, acting on its own independent adjudicative authority.

**Scoring note:** full marks require one concrete, named example on each side (evidence-gathering versus adjudication) rather than an abstract restatement of the distinction.

## Final 15-mark question (250-word ceiling)

**Question:** "The NHRC-SHRC relationship encodes a careful distribution of trust between the Centre and the States." Examine this statement with reference to composition, jurisdiction and removal.

**Model solution (248 words):** The statement is well supported across three dimensions of the NHRC-SHRC design. In **composition**, the NHRC draws its judicial members from the Supreme Court and High Courts, reflecting a national mandate, while the SHRC draws from High Courts and senior District Judges, reflecting a State-bounded mandate appropriately scaled to local institutional capacity. In **jurisdiction**, the SHRC is confined to State List and Concurrent List subjects under section 21(5), and cannot proceed where the NHRC or another lawful commission is already seized of the matter under section 36 — a deliberate allocation that prevents both jurisdictional vacuum and destructive overlap between two layers of government. In **removal**, the clearest trust-distribution signal appears: the Governor appoints an SHRC Chairperson or member, reflecting the State's constitutive role, but only the **President** may remove one, insulating the SHRC from the very State executive it is meant to scrutinise.

Together, these three dimensions show a design that neither fully centralises human-rights protection at the Centre nor fully devolves it to the States without safeguard. The Centre retains a federal insulation function specifically at the vulnerable point — removal — while leaving appointment, day-to-day jurisdiction and institutional scale to the State level. This is a considered trust distribution, not an accidental inconsistency, and it should be cited by name whenever a question tests Centre-State institutional design beyond the more familiar examples of Governor's powers or All-India Services discipline.

**Scoring note:** full marks require covering all three named dimensions (composition, jurisdiction, removal) with the specific supporting provision for each, not just the removal asymmetry alone.

## Final 20-mark question (250-word ceiling)

**Question:** Evaluate the National Human Rights Commission as an institution, integrating its statutory design, judicial interpretation and current international accreditation status.

**Model solution (250 words):** The NHRC's statutory design, under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, builds a genuinely capable fact-finding body: civil-court evidence powers (section 13), broad inquiry entry routes (section 12(a)), and visible reporting obligations (sections 18 and 20). Its central design limitation is equally clear — every remedial output is recommendatory, not binding, and government-supplied investigators create a structural independence concern wherever police conduct itself is under scrutiny.

Judicial interpretation has refined this design at its edges rather than expanding it generally. *N.C. Dhoundial* (2003) confirmed the one-year bar under section 36(2) as a strict jurisdictional embargo, rejecting attempts to stretch it through a generic "continuing wrong" theory. *Paramjit Kaur* (1999), as explained in *Dhoundial*, carved a narrow exception where the Supreme Court itself assigns an investigation under Article 32. *EEVFAM* (2016) confirmed continuing judicial oversight of security-force conduct while explicitly leaving the binding-versus-advisory question about NHRC recommendations open rather than settled.

The current international evidence sharpens, rather than contradicts, this picture: the GANHRI Bureau upheld, on 4 December 2025, a recommendation to downgrade India's accreditation from A to B status, citing police-investigator dependence, opaque appointments and weak pluralism — concerns this session has independently derived from the statute itself. With implementation deferred to the November 2026 SCA session, the honest verdict is that the NHRC remains a genuinely capable but structurally non-coercive institution, whose credible path forward is targeted independence and enforceability reform, not wholesale institutional redesign.

**Scoring note:** full marks require integrating all three dimensions — statutory design, at least two named cases, and the precise current GANHRI status with its correct date — into one connected evaluation rather than three separate, disconnected paragraphs.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Statutory identity and evolution

- NHRC and SHRC are statutory, not constitutional, bodies under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993.
- The Centre **shall** constitute the NHRC; a State **may** constitute an SHRC.
- The Act was deemed to commence on 28 September 1993; it received presidential assent on 8 January 1994, as Act No. 10 of 1994.
- Principal amendments: 2006 (procedure, court-reference route, revised visits, complaint transfer, State-level provisions) and 2019 (composition, eligibility, tenure, deemed members, UT arrangements).
- No later enacted change to the taught provisions has been located as of 2 October 2026.

## Human-rights definition

```text
life + liberty + equality + dignity
guaranteed by the Constitution OR embodied in International Covenants
AND enforceable by courts in India
```

- International Covenants = ICCPR + ICESCR + any other UN General Assembly covenant/convention the Centre notifies.
- Not every human-rights instrument is automatically covered by this definition.

## NHRC composition recall

```text
Chair: former CJI or former SC judge
Members: SC judge + HC Chief Justice + 3 human-rights experts
Woman rule: at least 1 among the 3 experts
Deemed: NCBC, NCM, NCPCR, NCSC, NCST, NCW, Chief Commissioner for PwD
Participation: section 12(b)-(j) only, never 12(a)
```

## NHRC appointment, tenure and removal

- President appoints, on the recommendation of a six-member committee: PM (head), LS Speaker, Union Home Minister, LS LoP, RS LoP, RS Deputy Chairman.
- A sitting Supreme Court judge or sitting High Court Chief Justice needs prior CJI consultation.
- A vacancy in the committee does not, by itself, invalidate an appointment.
- Chair: 3 years or age 70, whichever earlier; reappointment eligible. Member: 3 years; reappointment eligible; absolute ceiling of 70.
- No further Union/State government employment after office.
- Removal Track A (misbehaviour/incapacity): President refers to Supreme Court; removal follows the Court's report.
- Removal Track B (direct grounds): insolvency, paid outside employment, infirmity, declared unsoundness, or a moral-turpitude conviction — President removes directly.

## SHRC design and the appointment-removal asymmetry

```text
Governor appoints
Governor receives resignation
President removes
```

- Chair: former HC Chief Justice or HC judge. Judicial member: HC judge or District Judge (>=7 years). One human-rights expert.
- Appointment committee: CM (head), Assembly Speaker, State Home Minister, Assembly LoP; add Council Chairman/LoP in bicameral States.
- Two or more States may jointly appoint a common Chairperson or member.
- The NHRC, not any SHRC, handles Delhi's human-rights functions.


- Section 19 is a special report route, not ordinary direct inquiry; State police are not automatically "armed forces."
- Section 30: a State **may** notify a Sessions Court as a Human Rights Court, with the High Court Chief Justice's concurrence.
- Section 31: a notified Public Prosecutor, or an advocate with at least seven years' practice, as Special Public Prosecutor.

## Final rapid-recall traps

1. Statutory, not constitutional.
2. Chair eligibility includes a former Supreme Court judge, not only a former CJI.
3. Three experts; at least one woman.
4. Seven deemed members only for section 12(b)-(j).
5. President appoints the NHRC; the Governor appoints the SHRC.
6. The President removes both.
7. Three-year terms; age-seventy ceiling.
8. The SHRC's treaty-study clause (12(f)) is omitted.
9. Ordinary response default is one month; the armed-forces default is three months.
10. Section 36(2) remains a strict one-year bar.
11. Civil-court powers are evidence powers, not decree powers.
12. Human Rights Court notification is enabling, not automatically nationwide.
13. The 4 December 2025 Bureau decision confirmed the downgrade recommendation on appeal; implementation awaits the November 2026 SCA session.
14. No current officeholder name is examinable content in this session.
15. Keep "strong inquiry power" and "weak compulsory remedy" as separate points in any effectiveness answer, and do not let identifying limitations substitute for proposing your own matched remedies.
