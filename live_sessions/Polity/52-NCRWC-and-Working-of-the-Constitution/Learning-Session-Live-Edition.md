# NCRWC and Working of the Constitution — Complete Live Session

The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC) asked a question that remains central to Indian polity: when constitutional government underperforms, is the defect in the text, in institutions, or in the behaviour of those who operate them? Its answer was not a new Constitution. It was a wide reform menu bounded by parliamentary democracy and the basic structure.

The Commission could diagnose and recommend. It could not amend the Constitution, enact legislation or bind future governments.

### 1. Why was a review commission created?

After about fifty years of constitutional experience, the Union Government constituted the NCRWC to examine whether existing provisions could meet the needs of efficient, smooth and effective government and socio-economic development.

✅ **Fact:** It was created by a Government of India resolution dated **22 February 2000**.

✅ **Fact:** It was an **executive, temporary and advisory** body. It was neither a constitutional body nor a statutory body.

⚠️ **Inference:** Its authority was persuasive rather than coercive. Persuasion depended on the breadth of consultation, quality of diagnosis and precision of the recommended legal route.

### 2. Review was not rewriting

The terms of reference imposed two express limits:

1. recommendations had to remain within **parliamentary democracy**; and
2. they could not disturb the Constitution's **basic structure or basic features**.

This produced a decisive distinction:

| NCRWC could do | NCRWC could not do |
|---|---|
| examine institutional experience | exercise constituent power |
| recommend amendments | amend under Article 368 |
| propose statutes or executive measures | enact a statute or issue binding law |
| identify defects in practice | replace the constitutional order |

✅ **Fact:** The Commission itself clarified that its task was to review the **working** of the Constitution, not to rewrite it.

### 3. Composition

The eleven-member Commission included its Chairperson:

1. Justice M.N. Venkatachaliah;
2. Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy;
3. Justice R.S. Sarkaria;
4. Justice Kottapalli Punnayya;
5. P.A. Sangma;
6. Soli J. Sorabjee;
7. K. Parasaran;
8. Dr Subhash C. Kashyap;
9. C.R. Irani;
10. Dr Abid Hussain; and
11. Sumitra G. Kulkarni.

✅ **Fact:** P.A. Sangma resigned before submission of the report. Dr Raghbir Singh served as Secretary; he was not a twelfth member.

> **Composition trap:** Do not add the Secretary, advisory-panel members or consultees to the Commission's membership.

The membership brought judicial, parliamentary, legal, administrative, diplomatic and public-policy experience. That expertise strengthened the report's deliberative value but did not give it constitutional authority.

### 4. Consultation and report

The Commission used consultation papers, expert studies, public responses, advisory panels and thematic deliberation.

```text
problem identification
        ↓
consultation paper / expert study
        ↓
public and institutional responses
        ↓
thematic deliberation
        ↓
recommendation + proposed legal route
```

✅ **Fact:** The report was adopted and signed on **11 March 2002** and presented to the Prime Minister on **31 March 2002**.

✅ **Fact:** It made **249 recommendations**. The standard eighth-edition classification records **58** requiring constitutional amendment, **86** requiring legislation and **105** capable of executive action.

⚠️ **Qualification:** These figures classify proposed routes. They do not show how many recommendations were later accepted.

### 5. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** An executive-appointed commission lacks a democratic mandate to redesign constitutional institutions.

**Reply:** It did not claim constituent authority. It supplied expertise, consultation and reform options while leaving acceptance to Parliament, State legislatures, courts and governments acting within their own powers.

**Residual:** Even an advisory report can shape political debate disproportionately. Its recommendations therefore require democratic scrutiny, federal sensitivity and rights review before adoption.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** status, chairperson, membership count, dates and basic-structure limit.
- **Mains:** begin with the distinction between review and replacement.
- **Trap:** never call it the Venkatachaliah constituent assembly or a statutory commission.
- **Answer line:** "The NCRWC possessed diagnostic expertise, not constituent authority."

### Revision notes

1. NCRWC was created by executive resolution on 22 February 2000.
2. It was temporary, advisory and non-statutory.
3. M.N. Venkatachaliah chaired the eleven-member body.
4. Eleven includes the Chairperson.
5. P.A. Sangma resigned before report submission.
6. The Secretary was not a Commission member.
7. The mandate covered fifty years of constitutional working.
8. Parliamentary democracy and basic structure were express limits.
9. Review did not mean rewriting.
10. The report was adopted on 11 March 2002 and presented on 31 March 2002.
11. It made 249 recommendations: 58 constitutional, 86 legislative and 105 executive in the standard classification.
12. A recommendation had no legal effect without a separate competent instrument.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why could the NCRWC recommend an Article 368 amendment but not itself change the Constitution?

**Model answer:** The NCRWC was an executive advisory commission created by government resolution. Constituent power under Article 368 belongs to Parliament acting through the prescribed procedure, with State ratification where required and subject to basic structure. The Commission could only propose a route.

**Misconception to avoid:** Expertise and consultation do not convert an advisory commission into Parliament, a court or a constituent assembly.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the constitutional significance of the NCRWC's mandate boundary.

**Model answer (131 words):** The NCRWC was established by executive resolution in 2000 to review fifty years of constitutional working. Its mandate was significant because it separated constitutional repair from constitutional replacement. The Commission had to work within parliamentary democracy and without disturbing the basic structure. It could diagnose failures, consult stakeholders and recommend constitutional, legislative or executive measures, but it possessed neither Parliament's Article 368 power nor original constituent authority. Its 2002 report was therefore advisory: no proposal became law merely by being included in it. This self-limitation enhanced legitimacy by recognising that expertise cannot replace democratic enactment, federal ratification or judicial review. At the same time, it allowed comprehensive examination across rights, institutions, elections, federalism and administration. The mandate thus made the NCRWC an instrument of constitutional maintenance, not a rival constitution-making body.

**Unique quantified rubric (10):** origin and status 2; two mandate limits 2; advisory/legal-effect distinction 2; Article 368 and democratic route 2; maintenance verdict 2.

## Lesson 2 — What “Working of the Constitution” Means

### Visual: from text to constitutional outcome

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL TEXT
Articles | Parts | Schedules
          ↓
LEGAL INSTRUMENTS
statutes | rules | judgments
          ↓
OPERATING INSTITUTIONS
legislature | executive | courts | audit | commissions
          ↓
POLITICAL PRACTICE
parties | conventions | opposition | federal consultation
          ↓
ADMINISTRATIVE CAPACITY
staff | finance | records | expertise | time
          ↓

rights | accountability | trust | delivery | remedies
```

A constitution “works” only when the full chain converts authority into restrained and effective public action.

### 1. Text is necessary but insufficient

✅ **Fact:** The written Constitution allocates powers, protects rights, creates institutions and prescribes amendment and accountability mechanisms.

⚠️ **Inference:** Good text can coexist with poor outcomes when institutions lack capacity, conventions weaken, parties centralise control or remedies become too slow.

The opposite is also true: every performance failure does not prove textual failure. A vacancy problem may require timely appointments; weak legislative scrutiny may require committee practice; opaque administration may require records and transparency. Constitutional amendment should be the last, not the automatic, response.

### 2. Six dimensions of working

| Dimension | Core question | Typical failure | Likely response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text | Is authority or right clearly allocated? | ambiguity or constitutional gap | amendment if truly necessary |
| Institution | Can the authorised body act independently and competently? | capture, vacancies, delay | appointments, finance, procedure, statute |
| Convention | Are role restraints respected? | formally lawful but corrosive behaviour | political accountability or codification |
| Political practice | Do parties preserve debate and opposition? | opaque finance, broad whips, centralised agenda | party, electoral and parliamentary reform |
| Federal practice | Is consultation real? | unilateralism or mistrust | councils, negotiated rules, review |
| Rights enforcement | Can a person obtain an effective remedy? | cost, delay, weak compliance | legal aid, courts, administration, monitoring |

### 3. Constitutionalism and constitutional morality

**Constitutionalism** means effective limited government, not merely possession of a constitutional document.

**Constitutional morality** means fidelity to constitutional forms, roles, equal citizenship, reasoned procedure and restraints on power. It is not the personal morality of a judge or office-holder.

✅ **Fact:** *Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India* (2018) and *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) are important modern doctrinal anchors for constitutional morality. *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), *Minerva Mills* (1980) and *S.R. Bommai* (1994) supply structural limits involving basic structure, limited amending power, judicial review, federalism and secularism.

⚠️ **Qualification:** These judicial doctrines are current law developed by courts; they are not NCRWC recommendations.

### 4. The working test

Ask five sequential questions:

1. **Authority:** Is the actor empowered?
2. **Procedure:** Was the correct process followed?
3. **Restraint:** Were rights, federalism and role limits respected?
4. **Capacity:** Could the institution deliver a timely result?
5. **Correction:** Could failure be exposed and remedied?

**Example:** A legislature may validly pass a Bill, yet scrutiny may be weak if committees are bypassed and debate is curtailed. The law may remain formally valid, but constitutional working has suffered.

**Limit of example:** Weak debate does not by itself allow a court to invalidate every law. Political accountability and judicial invalidity are different questions.

### 5. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** “Working” is too vague and permits every political dissatisfaction to be constitutionalised.

**Reply:** The framework is disciplined when each complaint is tied to a named constitutional value, institution, procedure, evidence and remedy.

**Residual:** Some failures span law and politics. Courts may enforce legal limits, but conventions, opposition space and deliberative quality also require political correction.

### UPSC application

- Distinguish constitutional text from constitutional performance.
- Use constitutional morality only with a concrete institutional role or right.
- Do not turn every governance weakness into a demand for amendment.
- **Answer line:** "Constitutional working is the conversion of text into restrained power, accountable institutions, effective rights and cooperative practice."

### Revision notes

1. Constitutional working is wider than literal compliance with Articles.
2. Statutes, rules, judgments, institutions and conventions mediate the text.
3. Political parties are constitutional operators even when much of their organisation is statutory or informal.
4. Capacity failure is not automatically a textual defect.
5. Constitutionalism means effective limits on public power.
6. Constitutional morality concerns fidelity to roles, procedures and equal citizenship.
7. It is not free-floating personal morality.
8. Authority, procedure, restraint, capacity and correction form a useful working test.
9. Courts, legislatures, elections, audit and administration provide different correction routes.
10. Political accountability and legal invalidity must not be conflated.
11. Basic structure limits amendment; it does not make every desirable convention judicially enforceable.
12. Evaluate outcomes without ignoring institutional competence.

### Concept check

**Question:** A validly enacted law receives little legislative scrutiny. Is that solely a question of legal validity?

**Model answer:** No. Legal validity depends on competence, procedure and constitutional limits. Weak scrutiny may instead reveal a defect in constitutional working: committee use, debate, opposition space and executive dominance. It may justify political or procedural reform even when no court invalidates the law.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that every constitutional-performance defect produces a judicial remedy.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “The working of a Constitution depends as much on institutions and conventions as on text.” Discuss.

**Model answer (205 words):** Constitutional text creates offices, allocates competence, protects rights and supplies amendment and review procedures. Yet it does not operate itself. Statutes and rules give detail; legislatures, executives, courts, audit bodies and election authorities apply it; parties and conventions shape how formal powers are used; administrative capacity determines whether decisions become outcomes. Thus, a Governor's power, Parliament's scrutiny or a right to remedy may exist textually but work poorly because of delay, capture, weak consultation or inadequate resources.

This does not make text irrelevant. Text defines authority and places enforceable limits, while the basic-structure doctrine prevents constitutional identity from being destroyed through amendment. The point is that constitutional performance requires a complete chain: lawful authority, fair procedure, role restraint, institutional capacity and correction through courts, legislatures, audit, elections or political accountability.

Conventions are especially important in parliamentary government because not every restraint can be judicially codified. However, convention cannot override express law, and poor practice does not automatically make an act invalid. Reform must therefore match the defect: convention and procedure for behavioural failure, capacity and statute for institutional weakness, and Article 368 only where constitutional text must change. A working Constitution is consequently a lived system of limited and accountable power, not merely a document.

**Unique quantified rubric (15):** text functions 3; institutional/conventional chain 4; named Indian examples or doctrines 3; validity-performance distinction 2; calibrated reform route 2; balanced conclusion 1.

### Visual: diagnose before prescribing

```text

      |
      +-- behaviour / convention? ------> political or procedural repair
      |
      +-- capacity / administration? ---> staffing, finance, records, executive action
      |

      |
```

The legal intensity of the remedy should be proportionate to the source of failure.

### 1. Eleven fields of study

The Commission organised its work around:

1. parliamentary institutions and political stability;
2. electoral reform and standards in public life;
3. socio-economic change;
4. literacy, employment, social security and poverty;
5. Union-State relations;
6. decentralisation and local government;
7. enlargement of Fundamental Rights;
8. effectuation of Fundamental Duties;
9. DPSPs and Preambular objectives;
10. fiscal and monetary control and public audit; and
11. administration and standards in public life.

The list shows that “working” linked constitutional design to political integrity, administrative delivery and social transformation.

### 2. Historical diagnosis, not a 2026 data sheet

The 2002 report identified broad concerns:

| Cluster | Historical diagnosis | Constitutional significance |
|---|---|---|
| Democratic trust | distance between public authority and popular sovereignty | legitimacy depends on service and answerability |
| Political integrity | criminalisation, corruption, defections and opaque finance | representative institutions may be formally elected yet substantively weakened |
| Institutional performance | delay, cost, overlap and weak responsibility | authority without capacity cannot deliver constitutional purposes |
| Social transformation | under-realised education, health, welfare and dignity | rights and DPSPs require implementation institutions |
| Federal and local government | insufficient consultation and incomplete devolution | division of power requires cooperative practice |
| Justice delivery | delay, weak investigation and limited access | remedies are part of constitutional effectiveness |
| Fiscal accountability | waste, deficits and weak audit follow-up | public finance is a constitutional accountability question |

✅ **Fact:** These are the Commission's historical assessments around 2002.

⚠️ **Inference:** They remain analytically useful, but no historical statistic or description should be repeated as a verified condition on 3 October 2026 without fresh evidence.

### 3. Why comprehensive diagnosis matters

Single-issue reform can shift a problem rather than solve it. For example:

```text
government instability
      ↓
broader whip / stronger party control
      ↓
less legislator autonomy
      ↓
weaker deliberation and internal democracy
```

A constitutional review must therefore examine interaction effects. Stability, accountability, representation and federal voice may pull in different directions.

### 4. Reform-method ladder

```text
1. diagnose exact failure
          ↓
2. repair convention or internal procedure
          ↓ if insufficient
3. strengthen capacity or executive rules
          ↓ if legal authority is needed
4. enact ordinary legislation
          ↓ if constitutional text must change
5. use Article 368
          ↓ always
6. test basic structure, rights, federalism and feasibility
```

**Example:** Weak transparency may be improved through record-management rules, a statute such as the RTI Act, or constitutional amendment. The least intrusive route that can solve the actual problem should be preferred.

### 5. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A comprehensive commission produces an unmanageable catalogue rather than implementable reform.

**Reply:** Breadth reveals interdependence and permits recommendations to be classified by legal route and priority.

**Residual:** Breadth also produces proposals of uneven precision and political feasibility. A later government must sequence reforms rather than claim wholesale implementation.

### UPSC application

- Present concern clusters, not a random recommendation list.
- Date historical diagnosis and avoid treating it as current data.
- Match each proposal to a reform route.
- **Answer line:** "The NCRWC's strongest method was diagnosis before amendment; its weakness was that breadth exceeded implementation capacity."

### Revision notes

1. The Commission studied eleven broad fields.
2. Its scope linked institutions, rights, socio-economic development and public ethics.
3. Historical concern statements belong to 2002.
4. Democratic trust and institutional performance are related but distinct.
5. Political-integrity reform includes finance, criminalisation, defection and party organisation.
6. Social rights require delivery capacity as well as text.
7. Federal design requires consultation in practice.
8. Justice delay weakens the right-remedy relationship.
9. Reform must begin with the exact failure.
10. Convention, capacity, statute and amendment are different routes.
11. Article 368 is not the default reform instrument.
12. Every proposal must pass rights, federalism, basic-structure and feasibility review.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “amend the Constitution” an incomplete response to weak legislative scrutiny?

**Model answer:** Weak scrutiny may arise from low sitting time, committee bypass, executive agenda control or party whips. These are partly procedural and political failures. Amendment is justified only if the constitutional text itself blocks an adequate remedy; otherwise rules, conventions and institutional practice are more proportionate.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that constitutional importance necessarily requires constitutional amendment.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the NCRWC's reform-method logic.

**Model answer (130 words):** The NCRWC's subject was constitutional working, so reform had to begin by identifying whether failure lay in text, institutions, conventions, party behaviour or administrative capacity. A behavioural defect may need political accountability or procedural reform; a capacity deficit may require staffing, finance, records or executive rules; a statutory gap requires legislation; and only a genuine constitutional allocation problem justifies Article 368. This ladder prevents over-constitutionalisation. It also subjects every proposal to basic structure, rights, federalism, democratic accountability and feasibility. The Commission's classification of 249 recommendations into constitutional, legislative and executive routes reflects this logic. Its advantage is proportionality: the legal remedy matches the defect. Its limitation is implementation, because a broad report cannot itself prioritise reforms for changing political conditions. The sound approach is therefore selective sequencing, not wholesale adoption.

**Unique quantified rubric (10):** diagnosis dimensions 2; four reform routes 3; basic-structure/rights screen 2; 249-route illustration 1; selective-sequencing verdict 2.

The Commission treated rights, social purposes and duties as related but legally different.

### 1. Proposed Fundamental Rights

The NCRWC recommended express protection in areas including:

- wider express non-discrimination grounds covering ethnic or social origin, political or other opinion, property or birth;
- an express press/media and information dimension within speech and expression;
- freedom from torture, cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment;
- compensation for unlawful deprivation of life or liberty;
- the right to leave and return to India;
- privacy and family life;
- a proposed rural wage-employment guarantee for a stated minimum period;
- access to courts and speedy justice;
- equal justice and free legal aid;
- environmental necessities, safe drinking water and sustainable development;
- specified protection and assistance for children; and
- broader speech, media and information formulations.

🧾 **NCRWC proposal:** These were recommended constitutional formulations.

✅ **Current-law caution:** Some related protections have developed through Article 21 jurisprudence, statutes and later constitutional provisions. That does not insert the NCRWC's exact wording into Part III or prove NCRWC causation.

### 2. Education and emergency safeguards

The Commission proposed a broader education guarantee for specified groups. Current constitutional law must instead be stated from **Article 21A** and the enacted education framework.

It also proposed insulating additional rights from suspension during a national emergency beyond the existing protection of Articles 20 and 21.

> **Trap:** A proposed immunity from suspension is not part of Articles 358-359 merely because the Commission recommended it.

Other specific proposals included a six-month maximum for preventive detention with a serving-judge advisory board, separate constitutional treatment of Sikhism, Jainism and Buddhism rather than the inclusive explanation attached to Article 25, and restriction of Article 31B protection to specified reform fields. Each remained a proposal and must not be taught as enacted text.

### 3. Property

The Commission proposed recasting Article 300A to require:

1. authority of law and public purpose;
2. non-arbitrary deprivation or acquisition; and
3. rehabilitation protection in specified cases involving SC/ST agricultural, forest or non-urban homestead land.

🧾 **Proposal:** This was a reform design.

✅ **Current-law caution:** The text of Article 300A remains the controlling starting point, together with current judicial doctrine. Do not quote the proposed clauses as enacted text.

### 4. DPSPs

Recommendations included:

- renaming Part IV to emphasise State policy and action;
- adding a population-control principle;
- periodic National Education Commissions;
- an Inter-Faith Commission;
- a high-status implementation-review body;
- employment action; and
- implementation of statistical-system reforms.

⚠️ **Inference:** The underlying concern was the implementation gap: constitutional aspiration requires measurement, institutions and responsibility.

### 5. Fundamental Duties

The Commission urged operationalisation of the Justice J.S. Verma Committee recommendations and proposed additional duties relating to:

- voting and democratic participation;
- payment of taxes;
- responsible parenthood and children's welfare; and
- educational responsibility of industrial organisations.

🧾 **Proposal:** These are not additions to Article 51A.

### 6. Rights versus policy inflation

**Objection:** Adding many rights constitutionalises resource choices and invites courts to manage policy.

**Reply:** Express rights can protect vulnerable interests and force minimum accountability where ordinary politics repeatedly fails.

**Residual:** A right without institutions, finance, standards and remedies may remain symbolic. Conversely, excessive judicialisation can weaken democratic priority-setting. The solution is precise right design plus implementation architecture.

### UPSC application

- Separate proposed text from later law and case doctrine.
- Use rights-plus-remedy, not rights-only, analysis.
- Show the Part III-Part IV relationship without treating DPSPs as directly enforceable rights.
- **Answer line:** "The NCRWC sought to move from declaratory constitutionalism to rights backed by remedy, review and delivery."

### Revision notes

1. The NCRWC proposed several express liberty and remedy rights.
2. Torture, privacy and compensation recommendations were proposals, not automatic amendments.
3. Later judicial development does not prove causal implementation.
4. Article 21A, not the NCRWC draft, states the current education guarantee.
5. Articles 358-359 govern present emergency suspension rules.
6. Article 300A must not be replaced by the proposed wording.
7. The property proposal stressed public purpose, non-arbitrariness and rehabilitation.
8. DPSP recommendations focused on implementation institutions.
9. The Commission proposed new civic duties.
10. Proposed voting and tax duties are not current Article 51A clauses.
11. Rights require institutions, resources and effective remedies.
12. Judicial enforceability and political implementation remain distinct.

### Concept check

**Question:** If privacy later receives strong constitutional protection through judicial interpretation, can it be described as implementation of the NCRWC privacy proposal?

**Model answer:** Only if authoritative evidence establishes adoption or attribution. Similar subject matter and later chronology show convergence, not causation. Current privacy law must be stated from constitutional text and judgments; the NCRWC remains historical reform evidence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not convert thematic similarity into proof that a commission recommendation was implemented.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate the NCRWC's approach to rights and social transformation.

**Model answer (200 words):** The NCRWC approached rights through both textual enlargement and implementation capacity. It proposed express protection against torture, compensation for unlawful deprivation of life or liberty, privacy, speedy justice, legal aid and environmental necessities. It also suggested recasting Article 300A, broadening education protection and strengthening emergency safeguards. Alongside Part III, it sought DPSP implementation-review mechanisms and operationalisation of Fundamental Duties.

The approach recognised that constitutional dignity is weakened when a right exists without access to courts, timely remedy or administrative delivery. Its environmental, education and legal-aid proposals also linked liberty with social conditions. However, the report risked constitutionalising matters whose real deficit might be legislation, resources or administration. Resource-sensitive rights require standards, budgets and institutions; otherwise amendment may create symbolic entitlement without delivery. Wider judicial enforcement may also shift policy choices from elected institutions.

The recommendations should therefore be read selectively. Where a basic protection needs entrenched status, amendment may be justified. Where the need is implementation, legislation and capacity may be superior. Later privacy, education or transparency developments must be cited from their own constitutional, judicial or statutory sources rather than assumed to be NCRWC adoption. The enduring value of the report is its rights-plus-remedy perspective, qualified by institutional feasibility.

Proposed rights against torture, stronger remedies, reformed legislative scrutiny, constructive no-confidence, federal consultation, local fiscal domains, judicial accountability, party regulation and a different anti-defection decider could materially transform constitutional outcomes. The distinction lies in authority and method: reform remains within the existing settlement and passes through competent democratic institutions.

Calling the project simply transformative may obscure this self-limitation, while calling it conservative ignores its ambition. Its proposals also require selective evaluation. A commission lacks authority to settle conflicts among independence, accountability, stability, representation, federal autonomy and administrative feasibility. Parliament, State institutions, courts and governments must choose through the relevant constitutional routes.

The proper synthesis is “transformative maintenance”: preserve constitutional identity while repairing procedures and capacities that prevent rights, accountability and federalism from working. Such maintenance should be transparent and sequenced, with reasons for adoption or rejection, current-law verification and periodic review. NCRWC's enduring importance lies not in offering a rival Constitution but in showing how constitutional renewal can occur without constitutional refounding.

**Unique quantified rubric (20):** maintenance definition and mandate 4; transformative scope 4; authority/method distinction 4; selective-evaluation analysis 3; “transformative maintenance” synthesis 3; qualified conclusion 2.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish review of constitutional working from rewriting the Constitution, with reference to the NCRWC.

**Model answer (125 words):** Review of constitutional working asks whether constitutional text, institutions, conventions, parties and administration produce accountable government and effective rights. Rewriting claims authority to replace the founding settlement. The NCRWC, created by executive resolution in 2000, belonged to the first category. Its terms required it to operate within parliamentary democracy and without disturbing basic structure. It could recommend constitutional amendments, statutes or executive measures, but it could not exercise Parliament's Article 368 power or bind governments. Its 249 recommendations therefore formed an advisory reform menu. This distinction matters because many failures—weak scrutiny, appointment delay, opaque administration or incomplete devolution—may need procedural, statutory or capacity repair rather than constitutional replacement. The NCRWC represents constitutional maintenance: preserve constitutional identity while correcting operation through the least intrusive competent route.

**Quantified rubric (10):** two definitions 2; mandate facts 2; authority distinction 2; reform-route logic 2; maintenance conclusion 2.

## Original 15-mark question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “The central insight of the NCRWC was that constitutional deficits often arise from institutional practice rather than constitutional text.” Examine.

**Model answer (216 words):** The NCRWC reviewed fifty years of constitutional experience and treated many failures as problems of operation: executive dominance over legislatures, unstable coalitions, criminalisation, opaque party finance, arbitrary personnel administration, federal mistrust, incomplete devolution, judicial delay and weak remedies. These problems arise through institutions, conventions, incentives and capacity even where constitutional text is formally adequate.

Its recommendations therefore used different routes. Minimum legislative sittings, committee scrutiny and constructive no-confidence addressed parliamentary practice and stability. Civil-service boards, transparency and disclosure protection targeted administration. Party law, election reform and a different anti-defection decider addressed political incentives. Federal consultation and stronger local fiscal domains sought cooperative implementation. Rights and judicial proposals were reserved for areas where entrenched protection or institutional redesign was considered necessary.

The insight should not be overstated. Some reform demands genuinely concern constitutional allocation or protection and may require Article 368. Moreover, practice and text interact: a poorly designed institution can create incentives that convention alone cannot cure. Conversely, amendment without capacity may produce only symbolic change.

The NCRWC's enduring method is proportional diagnosis. Identify whether failure lies in authority, restraint, delivery or correction; choose convention, executive action, statute or amendment accordingly; and test the result against rights, federalism and basic structure. Its contribution is thus less a list of amendments than a framework for constitutional maintenance.

**Quantified rubric (15):** thesis 2; institutional-practice examples 4; route differentiation 3; text-practice counterpoint 3; framework and verdict 3.

## Original 20-mark question

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Critically evaluate the continuing relevance of the NCRWC recommendations to institutional reform in India.

**Model answer (233 words):** NCRWC remains relevant because it connected constitutional design with the behaviour and capacity of legislatures, executive, judiciary, parties, federal institutions, local bodies and audit. Its basic-structure self-limitation and classification of 249 proposals into constitutional, legislative and executive routes offer a disciplined method for reform.

Several concerns remain analytically important: legislative scrutiny, party finance and internal democracy, criminalisation, defection adjudication, judicial appointments and accountability, federal consultation, Governor-State relations, local fiscal capacity, transparency and justice delay. The report also anticipated a rights-plus-remedy approach by linking liberty with speedy justice, legal aid and implementation institutions.

However, continuing relevance does not mean current law or wholesale adoption. The 91st Amendment used a 15 per cent ministry ceiling rather than NCRWC's 10 per cent. RTI and Lokpal statutes possess independent designs. The proposed judicial commission was not the later NJAC or current collegium. Political-party law and ECI-based defection adjudication remain pending. On assent, the 2025 Supreme Court advisory opinion, not the proposed NCRWC periods, states the current position.

Some recommendations also risk over-constitutionalising administrative problems, creating overlapping bodies or trading independence for accountability. Reform must therefore be selective and dated. Each proposal should be tested for democratic legitimacy, federal impact, rights cost, basic structure, appointment design, capacity, reviewability and causal evidence of implementation.

NCRWC is most relevant as a diagnostic framework and reform menu. Its value lies in informed constitutional maintenance, not authority to prescribe a parallel Constitution.

**Quantified rubric (20):** continuing themes 5; method and strengths 4; status distinctions with examples 5; criticisms 3; evaluation criteria 2; qualified conclusion 1.

# REMEDIATION

## Error set 1 — Status inflation

**Faulty claim:** “The NCRWC was a constitutional commission whose recommendations amended the Constitution.”

**Repair:** It was an executive advisory commission. A recommendation acquired legal force only through a separate amendment, statute, rule, judgment or executive instrument issued by the competent authority.

**Retrieval cue:** **Resolution creates report; Article 368 creates amendment.**

## Error set 2 — Composition inflation

**Faulty claim:** “The NCRWC had twelve members because the Secretary also served on it.”

**Repair:** It had eleven members including the Chairperson. The Secretary and advisory participants were not additional Commission members.

**Retrieval cue:** **Eleven includes Chair; staff are not members.**

## Error set 3 — Recommendation-current law collapse

**Faulty claim:** “India has a 10 per cent constitutional ceiling on ministries.”

**Repair:** NCRWC proposed 10 per cent. The 91st Amendment enacted 15 per cent, with a minimum of 12 ministers in a State.

**Retrieval cue:** **NCRWC 10; Constitution 15.**

## Error set 4 — Judicial-body conflation

**Faulty claim:** “NCRWC created the NJAC, which now appoints judges.”

**Repair:** NCRWC proposed a distinct commission. NJAC arose from the 99th Amendment and 2014 Act and was invalidated in 2015. The collegium is the current system.

**Retrieval cue:** **Proposal → different NJAC → invalidation → collegium.**

## Error set 5 — Federal-commission conflation

**Faulty claim:** “Sarkaria, Punchhi and NCRWC are three names for the same Centre-State commission.”

**Repair:** Sarkaria and Punchhi are specialised federal commissions with different dates and reports. NCRWC was a comprehensive review containing a federalism chapter.

**Retrieval cue:** **Shared theme, separate mandate.**

## Error set 6 — Assent timeline error

**Faulty claim:** “Articles 200-201 impose the NCRWC's six-month and three-month limits.”

**Repair:** Those were recommendations. The 2025 advisory opinion rejects court-created rigid timelines and deemed assent while permitting limited mandamus against prolonged unexplained inaction.

**Retrieval cue:** **Proposed periods ≠ constitutional text.**

## Error set 7 — Causal overclaim

**Faulty claim:** “Every later transparency or welfare law implemented NCRWC.”

**Repair:** Compare exact wording, institution, route, legal effect and official attribution. Use “convergent” or “related” where causation is unproved.

**Retrieval cue:** **After does not mean because of.**

## Error set 8 — Amendment reflex

**Faulty claim:** “Every constitutional-working problem requires amendment.”

**Repair:** Use the ladder: convention, procedure, capacity, executive action, legislation, then Article 368 only where text must change.

**Retrieval cue:** **Diagnose first; amend last.**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Identity and mandate

- Government of India resolution: **22 February 2000**.
- Status: executive, temporary, advisory; neither constitutional nor statutory.
- Chair: former CJI **M.N. Venkatachaliah**.
- Composition: **11 members**, including Chairperson; P.A. Sangma resigned; Secretary not a member.
- Report: adopted/signed **11 March 2002**; presented **31 March 2002**.
- Mandate: review fifty years of working, not rewrite the Constitution.
- Limits: parliamentary democracy and basic structure/basic features.
- Legal effect: recommendation only; competent later instrument required.

## Study architecture and diagnosis

- Eleven fields: parliamentary institutions; elections; socio-economic change; literacy/employment/security/poverty; Union-State relations; decentralisation; rights; duties; DPSPs/Preamble; fiscal/monetary control and audit; administration/public standards.
- Historical concern clusters: democratic trust, political integrity, institutional performance, social transformation, federal/local design, justice delivery and fiscal accountability.
- Date historical diagnosis; do not present 2002 rhetoric or data as 2026 fact.

## Working-of-Constitution formula

```text
text
 + statutes/rules/judgments
 + institutions
 + conventions/parties
 + administrative capacity
 = rights, accountability, trust, delivery and remedies
```

- Working failure may exist without textual invalidity.
- Constitutionalism = effective limited government.
- Constitutional morality = fidelity to roles, procedure, equality and restraint; not personal morality.
- Five tests: authority, procedure, restraint, capacity, correction.

## Report scale and reform routes

- **249 recommendations**.
- Standard classification: **58 constitutional**, **86 legislative**, **105 executive**.
- Route ladder: convention/procedure → capacity/executive action → statute → Article 368.
- Every route remains bounded by rights, federalism, democratic accountability and basic structure.

- Criminal-case and election-petition speed.

- Exact adoption.
- Modified adoption.
- Related later reform without proven attribution.
- Judicial development in related field.
- Rejected or displaced.
- Pending or unimplemented.

- “After” does not mean “because of.”
