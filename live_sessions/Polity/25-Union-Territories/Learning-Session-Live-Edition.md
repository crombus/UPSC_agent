# Union Territories — Live Session Edition

India's Union Territories are not one uniform category. They range from small islands directly administered by the Union to territories with elected governments, distinct legislative fields and recurring constitutional disputes. This session therefore begins with classification, builds the Article 239 framework, studies the three legislature models, and only then compares institutions, finance and asymmetric federalism.

### Visual: one label, three governing forms

| Constitutional position | Democratic institution | Governing idea | Present examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| State | State legislature and responsible ministry | Constitutionally divided federal field | Twenty-eight States |
| UT without legislature | No territorial legislature or ministry | Direct Union administration with local bodies and advisory arrangements | A&N, Chandigarh, DNHDD, Ladakh, Lakshadweep |
| UT with legislature | Elected Assembly and responsible ministry, but Parliament retains plenary UT power | Local representation under continuing Union predominance | Delhi, Puducherry, J&K |

The expression “Union Territory” identifies a constitutional relationship with the Union; it does not describe one identical administrative model.

### 1. Start with the learner’s puzzle

Why does India retain territories that are neither ordinary States nor mere departments of the Union government? Geography, strategic exposure, capital-city functions, cultural history, small size and transitional political arrangements do not always fit a uniform State model. The Constitution therefore permits direct Union administration while Parliament may add representative institutions where local democratic accountability requires them.

Union Territories first became a systematic constitutional category in 1956 after the Seventh Amendment and the States Reorganisation Act replaced the old Part C and Part D classification. Their later history shows that UT status is flexible rather than permanent: some UTs became States; some territories were merged; and the former State of Jammu and Kashmir was reorganised into two UTs in 2019.

### 2. The current eight

```text
Eight Union Territories
├─ Islands: Andaman and Nicobar Islands; Lakshadweep
├─ National capital: Delhi
├─ Shared capital: Chandigarh
├─ Former colonial enclaves: Puducherry; DNH and Daman and Diu
└─ Himalayan territories: Jammu and Kashmir; Ladakh
```

The current list is:

1. Andaman and Nicobar Islands;
2. Chandigarh;
3. Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu;
4. National Capital Territory of Delhi;
5. Jammu and Kashmir;
6. Ladakh;
7. Lakshadweep; and
8. Puducherry.

DNH and Daman and Diu became one UT on 26 January 2020. Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh came into existence as separate UTs on 31 October 2019. Older editions that list nine UTs or treat Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu separately must be date-labelled.

#### A third limb most lists forget: acquired territories

Article 1(3) describes the territory of India in three limbs, and the third is easy to miss:

```text
Article 1(3): the territory of India comprises —
  (a) the territories of the States
  (b) the Union territories specified in the First Schedule
  (c) such other territories as may be acquired
```

Limb (c) matters for two reasons.

**It explains how enclaves entered India at all.** Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Goa, Daman and Diu and Puducherry joined by processes of acquisition, cession or merger rather than by carving up an existing State. Acquisition is a distinct constitutional route into Indian territory.

❌ **Do not add Sikkim to that list.** Sikkim did not enter as an Article 1(3)(c) acquired territory. It was **admitted as a State** under **Article 2** — “Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit” — by the **Constitution (Thirty-sixth Amendment) Act, 1975**, which omitted the associate-state arrangement, placed Sikkim in the First Schedule as a State and inserted Article 371F. Admission under Article 2 and acquisition under Article 1(3)(c) are different doors into the Union, and Sikkim used the first.

| Route | Provision | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Admission or establishment of a **new State** | Article 2 | Sikkim, 1975 |
| **Acquisition** of territory, later placed in the First Schedule or merged | Article 1(3)(c) | Goa, Daman and Diu; Dadra and Nagar Haveli; Puducherry |
| **Reorganisation** of existing Indian territory | Articles 3 and 4 | Creation of the UTs of J&K and Ladakh, 2019 |

**It does not create a fourth category of governance.** An acquired territory does not float outside the scheme. Once acquired, Parliament brings it into the First Schedule and it is governed as a Union territory under Part VIII, or is merged into a State.

✅ **Fact — and this is the clause that completes the logic:** **Article 366(30)** defines “Union territory” to mean “any Union territory specified in the First Schedule and includes any other territory comprised within the territory of India but not specified in that Schedule.” So a territory that has been acquired but not yet entered in the First Schedule is still, by definition, a Union territory.

```text
territory acquired under Art 1(3)(c)
            ↓
not yet named in the First Schedule
            ↓
Art 366(30) second limb:  still a "Union territory"
            ↓
so Part VIII (Arts 239–241) applies from the outset
```

⚠️ **Inference:** Article 366(30) prevents a constitutional gap. Without its second limb, newly acquired territory would sit inside India's borders with no provision telling anyone how to administer it until Parliament got round to amending the First Schedule. Article 1(3)(c) answers *how territory is obtained*; Article 366(30) ensures it is immediately classified; Articles 239 to 241 answer *how it is then administered*.

⚠️ **Trap:** “Acquired territory” is not a synonym for “Union territory.” It is the mode of entry, not the mode of government. An answer that lists acquired territories as a separate administrative class is wrong — and an answer that lists Sikkim among them is wrong twice.

### 3. Why create or retain a UT?

| Rationale | Illustration | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| National-capital governance | Delhi | Local democracy exists, but public order, police and land are reserved |
| Strategic location | A&N, Lakshadweep, Ladakh | Strategic importance does not eliminate the need for accountable administration |
| Small territorial or demographic scale | Lakshadweep, Chandigarh | Small size is a policy rationale, not an independent constitutional test |
| Distinct historical or cultural inheritance | Puducherry, DNHDD | Puducherry received a legislature; DNHDD did not |
| Shared-capital function | Chandigarh | It serves Punjab and Haryana while remaining a UT |
| Transitional security and political arrangement | J&K after 2019 | Statehood restoration remains a live constitutional-political commitment |

These are explanatory reasons, not closed legal categories. Parliament’s constitutional powers, rather than a judicially enforceable “smallness” or “strategic” test, determine territorial status.

### 4. State versus UT: the decisive distinction

A State participates in the federal distribution under Part XI. Its Governor is the constitutional head of a State and Parliament normally cannot legislate on State List matters unless a constitutional exception applies. A UT, by contrast, is administered under Part VIII. Its Administrator is the President’s agent, and Article 246(4) lets Parliament legislate on any matter for territory not included in a State.

A UT legislature consequently provides meaningful self-government but not State-equivalent legislative exclusivity. This is why Delhi, Puducherry and J&K remain UTs despite elected Assemblies.

### 5. Objection, reply and residual tension

**Objection:** If residents elect an Assembly, continuing Union predominance creates a democratic deficit.

**Reply:** National-capital security, strategic frontiers, small scale or transitional conditions can justify differentiated control. Local democracy and Union responsibility can coexist.

**Residual:** The justification is strongest for genuinely Union-wide functions and weakest when routine administration is shifted away from an elected government without clear constitutional accountability. Delhi’s services litigation and J&K’s statehood question reveal this unresolved edge.

### UPSC application

- Never equate every UT with direct bureaucratic rule.
- Separate the legal category from the policy reasons for creating it.
- Use the three-part classification before discussing any individual UT.
- In Mains answers, qualify the simplistic statement that UTs “have no autonomy”: three have functioning elected governments, but none enjoys State-equivalent exclusivity.

### Revision notes

1. Part VIII contains the principal UT framework.
2. India has eight UTs on 1 October 2026.
3. Delhi, Puducherry and J&K have legislatures.
4. Five UTs do not have territorial legislatures.
5. UT status is constitutionally flexible and has changed repeatedly.
6. Article 1(3)(c) recognises “such other territories as may be acquired”; acquisition is a mode of entry, not a mode of government.
7. Sikkim entered as a **State admitted under Article 2** by the Thirty-sixth Amendment, 1975; it is not an Article 1(3)(c) example.
8. Article 366(30) defines a Union territory as one specified in the First Schedule **and** any other territory comprised within India but not so specified.
9. DNH and Daman and Diu merged on 26 January 2020.
10. J&K and Ladakh became UTs on 31 October 2019.
11. A legislature does not convert a UT into a State.
12. Parliament’s continuing power is the central State–UT distinction.
13. Strategic or administrative rationales explain policy; they do not replace the constitutional text.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “UT with a legislature” not simply another name for a State?

**Model answer:** It has an elected Assembly and responsible ministry, but it remains under Part VIII: the Administrator is the President’s agent and Parliament retains power to legislate on every subject, including State List matters. Its local legislature therefore lacks State-equivalent exclusivity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the presence of an Assembly as the sole test of Statehood ignores the source and limits of legislative power.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Prompt:** “Union Territory is a constitutional category containing several administrative models.” Explain.

#### Model answer

Union Territory denotes a territory administered within Part VIII, not a uniform institutional design. Five UTs—A&N, Chandigarh, DNHDD, Ladakh and Lakshadweep—lack territorial legislatures and remain under direct Union administration, though local bodies and advisory institutions operate. Three possess elected governments through different routes: Delhi’s legislature is constitutional under Article 239AA; Puducherry’s is statutory under Article 239A and the 1963 Act; J&K’s is statutory under the 2019 Reorganisation Act. Their legislative fields also differ. Delhi excludes public order, police and land; J&K excludes public order and police; Puducherry has the broadest applicable State List field. Yet Article 246(4) preserves Parliament’s plenary competence for every UT. The category therefore combines direct administration, differentiated democracy and overriding Union responsibility rather than one fixed model.

**Rubric (10):** classification 2; reasons 1; three legislature routes 2; field variation 2; Article 246(4) 2; qualified conclusion 1.

### Why the next lesson follows

Classification tells us that UTs differ. The next question is what all of them share constitutionally: administration by the President through an Administrator and Parliament’s exceptional territorial competence.

### Visual: the constitutional chain

```text
Constitution
   │
   ├─ Article 239 ── President ── appointed Administrator ── UT administration
   │                                      │
   │                                      └─ agent, not Part VI State head
   │
   └─ Article 246(4) ── Parliament may legislate on any matter
                         for territory not included in a State
```

Article 239 supplies the executive chain; Article 246(4) supplies the legislative foundation of Union predominance.

### 1. Article 239 in plain language

Read the clause slowly, because its opening words are routinely misquoted:

> **Article 239(1):** “**Save as otherwise provided by Parliament by law**, every Union territory shall be administered by the President acting, to such extent as he thinks fit, through an administrator to be appointed by him with such designation as he may specify.”

Three things follow, and each is examinable.

**First, the default.** Absent a contrary parliamentary law, the President administers the territory through an appointed Administrator.

**Second, who may displace the default.** The saving clause names **Parliament by law** — not the President, not the Administrator and not the Union executive. Executive convenience cannot carve an exception out of Article 239; only a statute can. So when the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963 or the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 creates a ministry that aids and advises the Administrator, it is operating inside this saving clause rather than against the Article.

**Third — and this is the point most answers get wrong — displacing the *mode* of administration does not displace the *office*.** Delhi has its own Article, 239AA, yet Article 239AA(1) itself says that “the administrator thereof **appointed under article 239** shall be designated as the Lieutenant Governor.” Delhi's LG is therefore still an Article 239 appointee. Article 239AA re-describes and re-channels that office; it does not create a separate constitutional post outside Article 239.

```text
Article 239  → the office of Administrator exists, by presidential appointment
                              │
        ┌─────────────────────┼──────────────────────┐
        ↓                     ↓                      ↓
  no special law        Art 239AA (Delhi)      Parliament by law
  → plain presidential   → same Art 239         (1963 Act, 2019 Act)
    administration         appointee, newly     → Administrator advised
                           designated LG,          by a responsible ministry
                           with an Assembly
```

The Administrator may be designated Lieutenant Governor, Administrator or Chief Commissioner. The title does not by itself determine the office’s powers. Those powers must be traced to the Constitution, parliamentary legislation, rules and valid presidential directions applicable to that UT.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** Article 239 is displaced *as to manner*, by parliamentary law, never as to the existence of the office. Writing “Delhi’s LG is appointed under Article 239AA, not Article 239” is a textual error.

### 2. “Agent of the President” does real work

| Part VI Governor | UT Administrator |
|---|---|
| Constitutional head of a State | Constitutional channel of presidential administration |
| Office situated within federal State structure | Office situated within Part VIII Union administration |
| State executive power is constitutionally vested in Governor | UT administration is carried on by President through Administrator |
| Ordinary rule is aid and advice of State ministry, subject to limited discretion | Powers vary by Article and statute; elected-UT aid-and-advice rules are territory-specific |

Calling the Administrator an agent does not mean a private-law employee who can do anything instructed. Every action still requires lawful constitutional or statutory authority. Nor does it mean that the Union Home Ministry may casually substitute executive instructions for parliamentary legislation.

A practical point belongs here, because answers often leave the Union end of the chain unnamed. Union Territories are administered at the Centre through the **Ministry of Home Affairs**, which acts as the nodal ministry for UT administration: it handles the UT division's business, processes appointments and references concerning Administrators, and routes territorial matters that need Union decision. That is an administrative fact about *who in the Union government handles the file*. It is not an independent source of power. The MHA's involvement never converts an executive instruction into law, and it cannot displace the Assembly of a legislature UT within that Assembly's own field.

### 3. Article 239(2): adjoining State Governor

The President may appoint the Governor of a State as Administrator of an adjoining UT. When performing that UT role, the Governor acts independently of the State Council of Ministers.

Chandigarh demonstrates the principle: its Administrator has conventionally also been the Governor of Punjab. The two capacities must be separated:

```text
Punjab Governor capacity → State constitutional framework
Chandigarh Administrator capacity → President’s UT framework
```

The State ministry cannot control the Administrator’s Chandigarh functions merely because the same individual holds both offices.

### 4. Article 246(4): why Parliament remains supreme

Article 246(4) permits Parliament to legislate with respect to any matter for any part of India not included in a State, even if that matter appears in the State List. This is the constitutional foundation for Parliament’s plenary UT power.

Where a UT has an Assembly, Parliament does not lose this competence. Instead, the local legislature works within a concurrent territorial structure designed by Article 239AA or statute. Parliamentary law may prevail according to the relevant constitutional or statutory inconsistency rule.

### 5. Exact substitutions matter

The present Constitution uses names altered through territorial changes. Old commentary may refer separately to Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, or contain superseded UT lists. A current answer must use **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu** as one UT and must not insert Ladakh into Article 240’s textual list. Date correction is part of constitutional accuracy.

### 6. Objection and answer

**Objection:** Plenary parliamentary power means a UT legislature is merely decorative.

**Answer:** A legislature creates electoral accountability, debate, budget scrutiny, local lawmaking and a responsible ministry. These are substantial democratic functions.

**Qualification:** Its authority is constitutionally subordinate to continuing parliamentary power. The correct description is “real but non-exclusive self-government,” not full State autonomy and not decorative consultation.

### UPSC application

- Begin institutional answers with Articles 239 and 246(4).
- Quote the saving clause exactly: “Save as otherwise provided by Parliament by law.”
- Remember that Delhi’s LG remains an Article 239 appointee under Article 239AA(1).
- Do not infer powers from the title “Lieutenant Governor.”
- Distinguish the President’s formal constitutional role from day-to-day administration under valid rules.
- Use Article 239(2) for the dual-role Chandigarh trap.

### Revision notes

1. Article 239(1) opens with “Save as otherwise provided by Parliament by law” — only a statute may displace the default.
2. Subject to that saving, the President administers every UT through an Administrator appointed by the President.
3. The Administrator is the President’s agent, not a State Governor.
4. Delhi’s Lieutenant Governor is still an Article 239 appointee; Article 239AA(1) says so expressly.
5. Designation does not determine the exact power; powers must be traced to the applicable Constitution, statute and rules.
6. The Ministry of Home Affairs is the Union nodal ministry for UT administration, which is an administrative fact, not a source of power.
7. Article 239(2) allows an adjoining State Governor to be Administrator.
8. In that UT role, the Governor acts independently of the State ministry.
9. Article 246(4) gives Parliament plenary territorial competence.
10. Parliament’s State List power survives the creation of a UT Assembly.
11. A UT legislature therefore exercises real but non-exclusive authority.

### Concept check

**Question:** Does describing the Administrator as the President’s agent make every Administrator decision immune from judicial review?

**Model answer:** No. Agency identifies the constitutional chain of administration. The Administrator must still act within the Constitution, parliamentary statutes, rules and judicially enforceable limits; ultra vires, arbitrary or procedurally invalid action remains reviewable.

**Misconception to avoid:** “President’s agent” is not a source of unlimited personal discretion.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Prompt:** Explain how Articles 239 and 246(4) jointly distinguish Union Territories from States.

#### Model answer

Article 239 establishes the executive distinction: a Union Territory is administered by the President through an appointed Administrator, who is the President’s agent rather than the constitutional head of a State. Article 246(4) establishes the legislative distinction: Parliament may legislate on any subject for territory not included in a State, including State List subjects. This power continues even where Parliament or the Constitution creates a territorial Assembly. Delhi, Puducherry and J&K consequently possess meaningful local lawmaking and responsible ministries, but their Assemblies do not acquire the exclusive State List sphere enjoyed by States. Article 239(2) further shows the separate UT character: a State Governor serving as Administrator of an adjoining UT acts independently of the State ministry. Together the provisions combine direct Union responsibility with differentiated local self-government.

**Rubric (10):** Article 239 text 2; agency distinction 2; Article 239(2) 1; Article 246(4) 2; elected-UT consequence 2; synthesis 1.

### Why the next lesson follows

Once the default framework is clear, the next problem is how local democracy can be inserted without creating a State. Article 239A supplies Parliament’s statutory-legislature technique.

## Lesson 3: Article 239A and the Statutory Legislature Model

### Visual: Parliament adds democracy without creating Statehood

```text
Article 239 default
President → Administrator
          │
          └─ Article 239A authorises Parliament by law to add:
               ├─ legislature
               ├─ Council of Ministers
               └─ both
                       ↓
             statutory self-government
             under continuing Union power
```

Article 239A is an enabling constitutional provision. The actual institutions and their detailed powers arise from parliamentary law.

### 1. What Article 239A permits

Parliament may create for Puducherry:

- a body, whether elected or partly nominated and partly elected, to function as a legislature;
- a Council of Ministers; or
- both.

The law may specify their constitution, powers and functions. Article 239A(2) states that such a law is not treated as a constitutional amendment for Article 368 purposes even if it supplements or incidentally alters the constitutional arrangement.

### 2. Why “statutory legislature” matters

Delhi’s Assembly is directly constitutionalised by Article 239AA. Puducherry’s detailed Assembly structure comes from the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963. J&K’s legislature comes from the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019, with Section 13 applying Article 239A’s logic.

| Territory | Immediate source of legislature | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Delhi | Article 239AA plus GNCTD Act | Core field and institutional existence constitutionally entrenched |
| Puducherry | Article 239A plus 1963 Act | Parliament designs and can alter the statutory structure, subject to the Constitution |
| J&K | 2019 Reorganisation Act; Section 13 applies Article 239A | Statutory UT legislature with its own field and transitional statehood context |

“Statutory” does not mean legally weak in every daily decision. It identifies the institution’s source and Parliament’s greater design control.

### 3. Government of Union Territories Act, 1963

The Act supplies Puducherry’s institutional machinery:

- Legislative Assembly;
- legislative competence over State and Concurrent List matters insofar as applicable to UTs;
- parliamentary override and repugnancy rules;
- Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Assembly;
- appointment of Chief Minister and ministers by the President;
- budget, financial procedure and funds;
- assent/reservation of Bills; and
- a statutory breakdown route when the machinery fails.

Its importance is methodological: a learner cannot answer a Puducherry question from Articles 239 and 239A alone. The Constitution authorises; the statute operationalises.

#### What happens when the statutory machinery fails

A statutory legislature needs a statutory emergency route, and the 1963 Act supplies one.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 51** provides that where the President, on a report from the Administrator or otherwise, is satisfied either that a situation has arisen in which the administration of the Union territory cannot be carried on in accordance with the Act, or that it is necessary or expedient for the proper administration of the territory, the President may by order **suspend the operation of all or any of the provisions of the Act** for such period as the President thinks fit, and make incidental and consequential provisions for administering the territory **under Article 239**.

Notice where that sentence lands. Suspension does not create a legal vacuum; it returns the territory to the Article 239 default you learned in Lesson 2. The statutory superstructure is switched off and the constitutional floor reappears beneath it.

| | Delhi | Puducherry |
|---|---|---|
| Breakdown provision | Article 239AB — **constitutional** | Section 51, 1963 Act — **statutory** |
| What is suspended | Specified provisions of Article 239AA / supporting law | All or any provisions of the 1963 Act |
| Fall-back administration | Consequential presidential arrangements | Administration under Article 239 |
| How the provision itself could be changed | Constitutional amendment | Ordinary parliamentary legislation |

⚠️ **This is the entrenchment contrast again, now at its sharpest.** Delhi's breakdown mechanism is written into the Constitution; Puducherry's sits in a statute Parliament can amend by simple majority. Calling both of them "President's Rule in a UT" loses exactly the distinction the examiner is testing.

⚠️ **Where Article 239B fits.** Puducherry's recess-ordinance power is **not** supplied by the 1963 Act at all. It is conferred by **Article 239B of the Constitution**, which the Act then operates alongside. Lesson 7 teaches that power in full; the point to fix here is the source. Writing "the 1963 Act gives Puducherry's Administrator ordinance power" is a sourcing error.

### 4. Parliament does not surrender control

The local Assembly and Parliament may both legislate within the applicable field. Parliamentary law prevails in case of inconsistency, subject to the governing provisions. A locally enacted law reserved for and assented to by the President may receive protected operation within the territory, but Parliament can later add to, amend, vary or repeal it.

### 5. Nominated representation

Article 239A itself permits mixed composition. The 1963 Act allows the Central Government to nominate up to three members to Puducherry’s Assembly. Judicial decisions in *K. Lakshminarayanan v. Union of India* upheld the nomination route and recognised that nominated members enjoy voting rights under the governing law.

This creates a democratic criticism: centrally selected voting members can influence a closely divided Assembly. The defence is textual—Article 239A expressly contemplates a partly nominated legislature. The residual issue is legitimacy, not absence of legal authority.

### 6. Wider significance

Article 239A demonstrates constitutional asymmetry by delegation. It lets Parliament tailor self-government without using the full State model. This offers flexibility but also leaves institutional stability more exposed to ordinary parliamentary alteration than Delhi’s constitutional framework.

Keep the comparison exact, because it is easy to argue it badly. The asymmetry is about **what kind of instrument is required**, and therefore about **how hard the change is to make**:

| Change | Delhi needs | Puducherry needs | Practical difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alter the Assembly’s essential design | Constitutional amendment under Article 368 | Ordinary amendment of the 1963 Act | Special majority and a possible floor defeat, versus a simple majority |
| Add women’s reservation | Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 | Government of Union Territories (Amendment) Act, 2023 | Both passed, but by different routes |

⚠️ **A commencement notification does not prove the point.** S.O. 1930(E) of 17 April 2026 merely appointed the date on which the 2023 amendment came into force. Fixing a commencement date is an *executive* act performed under authority the statute already gave. It is not Parliament reshaping the statute, and citing it as evidence of parliamentary flexibility confuses the legislature with the government of the day. The parliamentary act was the 2023 amendment itself.

#### April 2026 showed exactly how different the two difficulties are

✅ **Fact:** On 16 April 2026 the Government introduced a package of three measures bearing on reservation and representation: the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026**, the **Delimitation Bill, 2026**, and the **Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026**, the last of which dealt with representation and delimitation arrangements for Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir.

✅ **Fact:** On 17 April 2026 the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill was **defeated in the Lok Sabha, 298 in favour to 230 against**. Because a constitutional amendment requires a special majority, a plurality of 298 was not enough.

✅ **Fact:** The two accompanying Bills were thereafter **withdrawn**, the constitutional foundation they depended on having failed.

```text
              UT Laws (Amdt) Bill ─┴→ WITHDRAWN
                      ↓
              NOTHING ENACTED · law unchanged
```

⚠️ **Do not overstate this.** No new law came into existence in April 2026 from this package. The 2011-census delimitation it proposed did not happen; UT representation rules were not altered; and nothing in it changed Delhi's, Puducherry's or J&K's existing reservation position. A Bill introduced is not a Bill passed; a Bill defeated is not a Bill in force.

⚠️ **Inference:** The episode is nevertheless the cleanest available illustration of this lesson's thesis. Puducherry's 2023 reservation amendment needed only an ordinary statute and is on the books. The 2026 attempt to move constitutional provisions needed a special majority, and it failed on the floor of the House. Entrenchment is not a label — it is the number of votes you must find, and that number is exactly what defeated the 131st Amendment Bill.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** “Statutory legislature” finally means that Parliament can redesign the institution by the same procedure it uses for any ordinary law — while a constitutionally entrenched institution can only be redesigned by a procedure that is capable of failing. The substantive Puducherry reservation scheme is taught in Lesson 8.

### UPSC application

- Use “enabling Constitution, operational statute.”
- Contrast constitutional entrenchment with statutory creation.
- Do not say Puducherry’s legislature was created by the Sixty-ninth Amendment.
- Do not attribute Article 239B ordinance power to the 1963 Act.
- Treat a commencement notification as an executive act, never as evidence of legislative flexibility.
- Keep Article 239A’s present textual focus on Puducherry while explaining its statutory application to J&K through Section 13 of the 2019 Act.

### Revision notes

1. Article 239A is an enabling provision.
2. It may produce a legislature, ministry or both.
3. The legislature may be elected or mixed.
4. Article 239A legislation is not an Article 368 amendment.
5. The 1963 Act operationalises Puducherry’s government.
6. Section 51 of the 1963 Act lets the President suspend the Act’s provisions and administer the territory under Article 239.
7. Article 239B, not the 1963 Act, is the source of Puducherry’s recess-ordinance power.
8. Puducherry’s legislature is statutory; Delhi’s core legislature is constitutional under Article 239AA.
9. Section 13 of the J&K Act applies Article 239A to J&K.
10. Parliament retains overriding competence.
11. Puducherry may have up to three nominated voting members.
12. Parliament amended the 1963 Act in 2023 by ordinary statute; S.O. 1930(E) merely appointed its commencement date, which is an executive act.
13. In April 2026 the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill was defeated 298–230 and the two accompanying Bills were withdrawn, so nothing from that package became law.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it insufficient to state only that Puducherry has an Assembly “under Article 239A”?

**Model answer:** Article 239A merely authorises Parliament to create institutions. The Assembly’s composition, legislative field, ministry, assent, budget and other operating rules are principally supplied by the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963.

**Misconception to avoid:** An enabling Article and the statute enacted under it perform different legal functions.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Prompt:** Compare constitutional and statutory routes for creating legislatures in Union Territories.

#### Model answer

A constitutional route places the territorial legislature’s essential design in the Constitution itself. Delhi’s Article 239AA directly establishes the NCT framework, Assembly field, Council of Ministers and Union–territorial relationship. A statutory route begins with constitutional authorisation but leaves detailed creation to Parliament. Article 239A enabled Puducherry’s legislature, operationalised through the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963. J&K’s Assembly similarly arises from the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019, with Section 13 applying Article 239A. Both routes permit local democracy without Statehood and preserve Parliament’s plenary UT competence. Their difference is institutional entrenchment: altering Delhi’s essential constitutional field requires constitutional change, while Parliament has wider ordinary-law control over statutory structures. Thus, the distinction concerns source and stability, not whether local representative institutions perform real functions.

**Rubric (10):** route definitions 2; Delhi 2; Puducherry 2; J&K 1; common parliamentary predominance 1; entrenchment consequence 2.

### Why the next lesson follows

Article 239A shows Parliament-created self-government. Delhi presents the stronger and more intricate alternative: a legislature whose field and executive relationship are written directly into Article 239AA.

## Lesson 4: Delhi’s Constitutional Design under Article 239AA

Progress: 4/15 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: NCT legislature, reserved entries, ministry and parliamentary override

Book context: Queried — Article 239AA, the GNCTD Act and standard textbook chapters on the special provisions for Delhi
CA search: "Official Gazette 16 April 2026 Constitution 106th Amendment Delhi Assembly women reservation commencement"
CA found: S.O. 1922(E), 16 April 2026, brought the 106th Amendment into force; seat reservation still awaits the constitutionally required census-linked delimitation
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: Delhi’s divided field

```text
DELHI ASSEMBLY
State List + Concurrent List, so far as applicable
                 │
                 ├─ EXCLUDED: Entry 1 public order
                 ├─ EXCLUDED: Entry 2 police
                 ├─ EXCLUDED: Entry 18 land
                 └─ EXCLUDED: Entries 64–66
                    only insofar as they relate to 1, 2 or 18

PARLIAMENT → may legislate on every subject for Delhi
```

Delhi’s constitutional design combines an elected government with reserved national-capital functions and continuing parliamentary supremacy.

### 1. Why Delhi received a special Article

The Sixty-ninth Amendment Act, 1991 inserted Articles 239AA and 239AB. Delhi was redesignated the National Capital Territory of Delhi, and its Administrator was designated Lieutenant Governor.

⚠️ **Three dates, not one — and candidates routinely merge them.** The Amendment was **enacted in 1991**; it **came into force on 1 February 1992**; and the Legislative Assembly and Council of Ministers it authorised began operating only after the **first Assembly election, held in 1993**. This is the same enactment–commencement–operation ladder that returns later in this lesson for the 2023 women's reservation and again in Lessons 8 and 10. Noticing it once here makes it automatic later.

```text
1991   Sixty-ninth Amendment enacted
  ↓
1 Feb 1992   Articles 239AA and 239AB come into force
  ↓
1993   first Assembly elected; ministry begins functioning
```

Delhi’s national-capital role makes its design different from both an ordinary State and an ordinary UT. The Union requires control over security, police and land connected with national institutions, while millions of residents require representative government for education, health, transport, welfare, local regulation and ordinary administration.

### 2. Assembly and legislative field

Article 239AA provides an Assembly elected from territorial constituencies. Its present strength is 70 elected members.

The Assembly may legislate on:

- State List matters applicable to UTs; and
- Concurrent List matters applicable to UTs;

but not:

- Entry 1: public order;
- Entry 2: police;
- Entry 18: land; or
- Entries 64, 65 and 66 insofar as they relate to Entries 1, 2 and 18.

The linked Entries 64–66 concern offences, jurisdiction and fees connected with listed subjects. Omitting them produces an incomplete account of Delhi’s reserved field.

#### Where Delhi’s seat numbers, SC reservation and elections actually come from

Two further provisions of the same clause carry marks and are routinely mis-sourced.

✅ **Fact — Article 239AA(2)(b):** the total number of seats in the Assembly, **the number of seats reserved for the Scheduled Castes**, the division of the NCT into territorial constituencies and all other matters relating to the functioning of the Assembly “shall be regulated by law made by Parliament.”

❌ **Common error:** citing **Article 332** for Delhi’s SC-reserved seats. Article 332 reserves seats in the Legislative Assemblies **of the States**. Delhi is not a State, so Article 332 does not apply to it. Delhi’s SC reservation flows from Article 239AA(2)(b) and the parliamentary law made under it.

✅ **Fact — Article 239AA(2)(c):** Articles 324 to 327 and 329 apply to the NCT, its Assembly and its members as they apply to a State, with references to “appropriate Legislature” in Articles 326 and 329 read as references to **Parliament**.

That single sub-clause does a lot of work. Because Article 324 is applied, the **Election Commission of India** superintends, directs and controls elections to the Delhi Legislative Assembly — exactly as it does for a State Assembly. There is no separate territorial election authority.

| Question about Delhi | Correct source | Wrong source to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| How many seats, and how many SC-reserved? | Article 239AA(2)(b) + parliamentary law | Article 332 |
| Who conducts Assembly elections? | Article 239AA(2)(c) applying Article 324 → Election Commission of India | A UT-specific or LG-run election machinery |
| Who fixes the franchise? | Article 326 as applied, with “appropriate Legislature” meaning Parliament | The Delhi Assembly itself |

⚠️ **Inference:** Notice the pattern. Delhi gets State-like *machinery* — the same Election Commission, the same electoral provisions — while the *authority to legislate about that machinery* stays with Parliament. That is the Article 239AA compromise expressed at the level of elections.

### 3. Parliament’s override

Parliament may legislate on any matter for Delhi. If a Delhi law conflicts with parliamentary law, parliamentary law prevails according to Article 239AA(3). A Delhi Bill reserved for and assented to by the President may operate despite an earlier parliamentary law, but Parliament retains power to enact later overriding legislation.

```text
Local competence
      +
Parliament’s plenary competence
      +
repugnancy rule
      =
concurrent but hierarchically Union-dominant lawmaking
```

### 4. Council of Ministers

Article 239AA(4) provides a Council of Ministers, with the Chief Minister at its head, to aid and advise the LG in matters where the Assembly can legislate, except where the LG is by or under any law required to act in his discretion.

- **Ministry size:** Article 239AA(4) itself says the Council shall consist of “not more than ten per cent. of the total number of members in the Legislative Assembly” — with 70 seats, seven including the CM. Note the location: for Delhi the cap sits inside Article 239AA(4), not in a separate clause and not in the GNCTD Act.
- **CM:** appointed by the President under Article 239AA(5).
- **Other ministers:** appointed by the President on the CM’s advice, under the same clause.
- **Tenure:** Article 239AA(5) ends with the words “and the Ministers shall hold office **during the pleasure of the President**.”
- **Collective responsibility:** Article 239AA(6) makes the Council collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly.

That last pair deserves attention, because the two halves pull in opposite directions and both are in the text.

```text
Art 239AA(5)  ministers hold office during the PLEASURE OF THE PRESIDENT
                                  ╳
Art 239AA(6)  Council is COLLECTIVELY RESPONSIBLE to the ASSEMBLY
                                  ↓
          the same tension that exists for a State under
          Articles 164(1) and 164(2) — resolved the same way
```

⚠️ **How the tension is actually resolved.** “Pleasure” is a formal constitutional expression of the appointment chain, not a licence for arbitrary dismissal. It is read alongside collective responsibility: the ministry exists so long as it holds the Assembly's confidence, and the appointing authority's formal power tracks that political reality rather than overriding it. The parallel with Article 164 for States is close, and saying so is a legitimate comparison.

❌ But do **not** flatten the difference. For a State the pleasure is the **Governor's**; for Delhi it is the **President's**. That single substitution is the whole Union-chain point, and an answer that writes “the Delhi ministers hold office during the pleasure of the LG” is wrong on the text.

### 5. Difference-of-opinion proviso

Read the operative verb carefully. The proviso to Article 239AA(4) says that in the case of a difference of opinion between the LG and his Ministers on any matter, the Lieutenant Governor **“shall refer it to the President for decision and act according to the decision given thereon by the President”** — and that pending such decision, where the matter is in his opinion so urgent that immediate action is necessary, he may take such action or give such direction as he deems necessary.

✅ **Fact:** the text is mandatory in form — **“shall refer,”** not “may refer.” Once the LG genuinely decides to differ, referral is the prescribed route; he does not get a third option of simply overruling the ministry and stopping there.

⚠️ **Why that cuts against the LG, not for him.** A casual reader treats “shall” as a grant of power. It is better read as a *channel*: the LG's own view does not prevail; the President's decision does, and the LG must then act according to it. The 2018 Constitution Bench used exactly this structure to hold that the proviso is an exception to be invoked on rare and genuinely substantial matters, not a general veto converting every disagreement into a referral.

So two errors must be avoided together:

| Error | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| “The LG *may* refer, so referral is optional” | Misquotes the text; the word is “shall” |
| “Because it says ‘shall’, the LG can block anything” | Confuses a mandatory channel with a discretionary power; 2018 confines it to exceptional matters |

### 6. Women’s reservation: commencement is not operation

The Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 inserted provisions for one-third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly. For Delhi the operative insertions are **Article 239AA(2)(ba), (bb) and (bc)**: seats reserved for women; roughly one-third of the SC-reserved seats reserved for women; and roughly one-third of the total directly elected seats — including those reserved for SC women — reserved for women, as Parliament may by law determine. S.O. 1922(E) brought the amendment into force on 16 April 2026.

However, **Article 334A** supplies the condition precedent. It provides that the reservation takes effect after a delimitation exercise undertaken on the basis of the figures of the **first census taken after the commencement** of the 106th Amendment; it runs for fifteen years from that commencement, extendable by Parliament; and the particular seats reserved for women are to be **rotated after each subsequent delimitation**.

| Stage | Status on 1 October 2026 |
|---|---|
| Constitutional amendment enacted | Complete |
| Commencement notification | Complete, 16 April 2026 |
| Attempt to remove the census condition by amendment | Made in April 2026 and **failed** |
| Required census-linked delimitation | Not completed |
| Reserved Delhi Assembly seats operating | No |

This is a classic UPSC status trap: enactment, commencement and implementation are separate.

#### The April 2026 attempt to unlock it — and why it changes nothing

The gap between commencement and operation was not left alone. Within days of the 106th Amendment's commencement, Parliament was asked to remove the obstacle.

✅ **Fact:** On **16 April 2026** the Government introduced three Bills — the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026**, the **Delimitation Bill, 2026**, and the **Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026** covering Delhi, Puducherry and J&K.

✅ **Fact:** The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill proposed to amend **Articles 81, 82 and 334A**. It would have raised the ceiling in Article 81 so that the House of the People consisted of not more than **815 members chosen from the States and not more than 35 members to represent the Union territories** — an **850**-member House — and would have removed the requirement to wait for a post-2026 census, allowing the delimitation on which Article 334A depends to proceed on the latest published figures.

✅ **Fact:** On **17 April 2026** it was **defeated in the Lok Sabha, 298 in favour to 230 against**, short of the special majority an amendment requires. The Delimitation Bill and the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill were thereafter **withdrawn**.

❌ **Nothing from that package became law.** No Article was amended; no UT statute was amended; the Delhi condition in Article 334A stands exactly as it did.

```text
16 Apr 2026   three Bills introduced
                    ↓
17 Apr 2026   Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill DEFEATED 298–230
                    ↓
              other two Bills WITHDRAWN
                    ↓
      Art 334A condition intact · no Delhi seat reserved
```

⚠️ **Inference — why this belongs in a Delhi answer and not only in a Parliament answer.** The episode shows the *cost* of the constitutional route. Delhi's reservation is entrenched in the Constitution, so the only way to accelerate it was a constitutional amendment, and that attempt needed a special majority it did not have. Lesson 8 and Lesson 10 will show that Puducherry's and J&K's equivalent conditions are statutory, so Parliament could in principle have relaxed those by ordinary law — which is precisely what the accompanying UT Laws Bill was for. When the constitutional step failed, the ordinary ones were withdrawn with it, because moving the UT conditions alone would have produced reservation in two Union territories and not in the national capital. Entrenchment protected the provision and also froze it.

⚠️ **The trap to avoid.** A withdrawn Bill's contents are not law, and a defeated amendment is not a "pending" amendment. Do not write that UT representation was raised to 35, that the House was expanded to 850, or that the census condition "has been relaxed." State the proposal, state the outcome, and move on.

⚠️ **Source the *condition*, not just the reservation.** For Delhi the delay is constitutionally prescribed — it comes from Article 334A in the Constitution itself. Lessons 8 and 10 will show that Puducherry and J&K reach a similar result through **statutory** provisions in their own Acts, not through Article 334A. The three Assemblies share an outcome, not a legal source, and the next line is the one that separates a precise answer from a vague one.

#### A historical echo worth one line

Delhi's present Assembly was not its first representative body. Under the Delhi Administration Act, 1966, Delhi had a **Metropolitan Council** together with an Executive Council. The Metropolitan Council was largely **advisory**: it discussed and recommended, but it could not enact laws for the territory and no Council of Ministers was collectively responsible to it. The Sixty-ninth Amendment replaced that arrangement with a genuine legislature.

```text
1966 Delhi Administration Act   →  METROPOLITAN COUNCIL (advisory)
                                   discusses · recommends · no lawmaking
                                           ↓
1991 Sixty-ninth Amendment      →  LEGISLATIVE ASSEMBLY (Art 239AA)
                                   legislates within a defined field
                                   sustains a responsible ministry
```

⚠️ **Why this is examinable:** it is the clearest Indian demonstration that an elected body is not automatically a legislature. The same distinction returns in Lesson 11 for Ladakh's hill councils. Deliberation is not legislation; recommendation is not responsibility.

### 7. Critique and defence

**Critique:** Dual accountability permits blame-shifting between Union and elected government.

**Defence:** A national capital requires Union control over security and land while representative institutions govern everyday civic life.

**Qualified verdict:** The model is workable only when reserved subjects remain precise, ordinary aid-and-advice government is respected, and exceptional referral is not transformed into routine obstruction.

### UPSC application

- Write the exact entries, including related portions of 64–66.
- Source Delhi’s SC seats to Article 239AA(2)(b), never to Article 332.
- Say that the Election Commission of India runs Delhi Assembly elections, via Article 239AA(2)(c).
- Quote the proviso as “shall refer,” then explain why that still does not create a veto.
- Note that Delhi ministers hold office during the pleasure of the **President**, not the LG.
- Distinguish legislative competence from executive control.
- State Parliament’s power before analysing conflict.
- Separate commencement of women’s reservation from its future operation, and attribute the condition to Article 334A.

1. Article 239AA was inserted by the Sixty-ninth Amendment; Delhi’s earlier Metropolitan Council under the 1966 Act was advisory, not a legislature.
2. Delhi is the NCT; its Administrator is designated LG but is still appointed under Article 239.
3. Assembly strength is 70; seats, SC reservation and constituencies are regulated by parliamentary law under Article 239AA(2)(b).
4. Article 332 does not apply to Delhi.
5. Article 239AA(2)(c) applies Articles 324–327 and 329, so the Election Commission of India conducts Assembly elections.
6. Entries 1, 2 and 18 are excluded, as are related parts of Entries 64–66.

9. Article 239AA(5): President appoints CM and ministers, and ministers hold office during the President’s pleasure.
10. Article 239AA(6): the Council is collectively responsible to the Assembly.
11. The proviso to Article 239AA(4) says the LG “shall refer” a difference to the President and act on that decision; 2018 confines it to exceptional matters.

13. Article 334A supplies the census-linked delimitation condition, a fifteen-year duration and rotation after each delimitation; no Delhi seat was reserved on 1 October 2026.

### Concept check

**Question:** Can Delhi legislate on an offence connected exclusively with police merely because criminal law generally appears in the Concurrent List?

**Model answer:** No. Article 239AA excludes Entry 2 and also excludes Entry 64 insofar as it relates to reserved Entries. The connected offence remains outside Delhi’s field despite the Assembly’s general Concurrent List competence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading Entries 1, 2 and 18 without their expressly linked Entries 64–66 understates the exclusion.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Prompt:** Explain the constitutional balance embedded in Article 239AA.

#### Model answer

Article 239AA balances democratic self-government with the Union’s responsibility for the national capital. It creates a 70-member elected Assembly and a Council of Ministers collectively responsible to it. The Assembly may legislate on applicable State and Concurrent List matters, but public order, police and land—Entries 1, 2 and 18—and related portions of Entries 64–66 remain excluded. Parliament nevertheless retains power over every subject and its laws ordinarily prevail over inconsistent Assembly laws. The ministry, capped at 10% of Assembly strength, aids and advises the LG within the elected sphere, while the difference-of-opinion proviso permits exceptional presidential reference. The design is neither federal Statehood nor direct administration. Its success depends on respecting representative authority in ordinary matters while reserving genuinely national-capital functions to the Union.

**Rubric (10):** institutions 2; exact field 2; Parliament/repugnancy 2; ministry 1; referral 1; balanced evaluation 2.

### Visual: rule and exception after 2018

```text
Matter inside Delhi’s legislative field?
          │
     ┌────┴────┐

 reserved      │
 Union/LG      ↓
 sphere   aid and advice is the rule
               │

               │

          used with restraint
```

The judgment did not turn Delhi into a State. It prevented an exceptional referral mechanism from swallowing representative government.

### 1. The dispute

Repeated disagreements arose over whether the LG could independently approve, delay or refer routine decisions of Delhi’s elected government. A literal reading of the proviso to Article 239AA(4), detached from collective responsibility, could make ordinary administration dependent on LG concurrence.

The 2018 Constitution Bench in *Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India* interpreted Article 239AA as a democratic constitutional arrangement rather than an administrative veto system.

### 2. Core propositions

1. Delhi remains a UT and does not possess Statehood.
2. Within the Assembly’s allotted field, the Council of Ministers’ aid and advice is ordinarily binding.
3. The LG has no general independent decision-making power.
4. The LG need not mechanically concur in every decision, but the difference-of-opinion proviso is exceptional.
5. Every disagreement cannot be referred to the President.
6. Constitutional morality, representative government, collaborative federalism and constitutional trust require institutional accommodation.
7. The Union retains reserved subjects and Parliament’s plenary power.

### 3. Why the referral power had to be narrowed

```text
Collective responsibility to Assembly
             ↓
elected ministry must be able to administer
             ↓
routine LG veto would sever power from accountability
             ↓
therefore referral must be exceptional, reasoned and proportionate
```

If the LG controlled ordinary decisions while ministers answered to voters and the Assembly, democratic responsibility would become incoherent. The Court harmonised the proviso with the main aid-and-advice structure.

### 4. What “no independent power” does not mean

It does not remove:

- Union control over public order, police and land;
- discretion expressly conferred by the Constitution or statute;
- the referral power in genuine exceptional cases;
- Parliament’s lawmaking power; or
- judicial review of elected-government action.

The safe formulation is: **the LG has no general independent decision-making power within the elected sphere**, not “the LG is powerless.”

### 5. What remained unresolved

The 2018 decision settled the governing principle but did not finally determine every subject-specific conflict. Control over “services” under State List Entry 41 proceeded through a 2019 split decision and later a 2023 Constitution Bench. This shows the difference between a constitutional principle and its application to a contested entry.

### Verified direct PYQ linkage

**2018 · GS-II · Question 11 · 15 marks · 250 words**

> “Whether the Supreme Court Judgement (July 2018) can settle the political tussle between the Lt. Governor and elected government of Delhi? Examine.”

**Directive:** Examine.

**Demand:** Explain the constitutional settlement, identify its limits and assess whether legal doctrine alone can end recurring institutional conflict.

**Answer approach:** Article 239AA structure → pre-2018 conflict → binding aid and advice/no general independent LG power → exceptional referral → reserved fields and parliamentary supremacy → issues left open, especially services → qualified verdict on political cooperation.

### 6. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Terms such as collaborative federalism and constitutional trust are too open-ended to resolve hard jurisdictional disputes.

**Reply:** The operative rules—aid and advice, absence of general independent power and exceptional referral—are concrete. Normative principles explain how ambiguous edges should be approached.

**Residual:** Statutes can reallocate authority within constitutional limits, and political incentives can still produce conflict. Judicial doctrine controls legality; it cannot compel cooperative political behaviour.

### UPSC application

- Separate the ratio from broad constitutional values.
- Avoid saying the Court gave Delhi complete control.
- For “settle the tussle,” assess law and political practice separately.
- Lead with a qualified thesis: the judgment settled the ordinary governing rule but not every jurisdictional dispute.

### Revision notes

1. The 2018 case was a Constitution-Bench decision.
2. Delhi remained a UT.
3. Aid and advice is the ordinary rule within the elected field.
4. LG lacks general independent decision-making power.
5. The referral proviso is exceptional.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did the Court not need to erase the difference-of-opinion proviso to protect representative government?

**Model answer:** It harmonised the proviso with the main aid-and-advice rule. Referral survives for genuine exceptional differences, but cannot become routine concurrence or a general veto that disconnects executive power from Assembly accountability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Constitutional protection of elected government does not require treating every LG power as invalid.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Prompt:** Did the 2018 Constitution Bench make the Lieutenant Governor of Delhi merely ceremonial?

#### Model answer

No. The Court rejected a general independent decision-making power, not the constitutional office itself. Within Delhi’s allotted legislative field, the elected Council of Ministers ordinarily governs through binding aid and advice because it is collectively responsible to the Assembly. The LG cannot demand concurrence in every decision or routinely refer disagreements to the President. Yet Delhi remains a Union Territory. Public order, police and land stay outside the Assembly’s field; Parliament retains plenary competence; the LG may act where the Constitution or statute grants discretion; and genuine exceptional differences may be referred to the President. The decision therefore transformed the LG from a potential parallel executive into a constitutional facilitator and exceptional safeguard, while preserving reserved Union functions. “Non-independent” is not the same as “ceremonial.”

**Rubric (10):** clear thesis 1; aid/advice 2; referral limit 2; retained functions 2; UT/Parliament context 2; precise conclusion 1.

```text
          ↓
W.P.(C) 678/2023 challenge
20 Jul 2023 Constitution-Bench referral
25 Aug 2023 amendment of petition after the Act
no final merits disposal located by 1 October 2026
```

The current rule is the operative statute under challenge, not the pre-statute judgment viewed in isolation.

### 1. Why services determine real government

Legislative power answers who may make a rule. Executive control over officers answers who implements it, prepares files, supervises departments and bears responsibility for failure.

The 2023 Constitution Bench described a chain:

```text
civil servants accountable to ministers
          ↓
ministers accountable to Assembly
          ↓
Assembly accountable to electorate
```

If officers implementing transferred subjects are controlled by another executive, electoral responsibility weakens.

### 2. What the 14 February 2019 split actually held

The post-2018 regular Bench did not speak with one voice on Entry 41. The official judgment in Civil Appeal No. 2357 of 2017 contains two materially different positions:

| Opinion | Legal position on services | Practical consequence stated |
|---|---|---|
| A.K. Sikri J. | Did not completely exclude Delhi from every level of service administration; instead adopted a differentiated working arrangement | Transfers/postings of Secretaries, Heads of Department and officers at Joint Secretary to Government of India level and above were to be done by the LG directly. Files for other levels, including DANICS, were to be routed through the Chief Minister to the LG; if they differed, the LG’s view would prevail. Existing delegated arrangements for DASS grades were retained, with Civil Services Boards suggested for transparency |
| Ashok Bhushan J. | Held that Entry 41 of List II was unavailable to the Delhi Legislative Assembly | Because Delhi lacked legislative competence over services, GNCTD had no co-extensive executive power over them except power conferred by parliamentary law or delegated to it |

The split was therefore not simply “Centre versus Delhi.” Sikri J. proposed divided administrative control by rank and route; Bhushan J. excluded Delhi’s general legislative and executive control over Entry 41 altogether. This disagreement required the later Constitution Bench.

### 3. The 11 May 2023 judgment

In *Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India*, the Constitution Bench held that Delhi’s elected government exercises legislative and executive authority over services under State List Entry 41 except insofar as services relate to public order, police and land.

The judgment reasoned that:

- executive power ordinarily follows legislative competence;
- Article 239AA creates a representative form of government;
- officers serving transferred departments must be accountable to elected ministers; and
- Delhi’s UT status does not erase the constitutional sphere granted to its Assembly.

The Court did not deny Parliament’s power to legislate for Delhi.

### 4. Ordinance and Act 19 of 2023

The Union promulgated the Government of NCT of Delhi (Amendment) Ordinance on 19 May 2023. Parliament replaced it with the Government of NCT of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2023, which received assent in August and operates from 19 May 2023.

The Act created the **National Capital Civil Services Authority (NCCSA)**:

| Member | Institutional position |
|---|---|
| Chief Minister | Chairperson |
| Chief Secretary | Member |
| Principal Home Secretary | Member-Secretary |

The Authority makes recommendations concerning transfers, postings, vigilance and disciplinary matters for covered Group A and DANICS officers. Decisions are by majority. Because the two senior bureaucratic members can form a majority, the CM may be outvoted.

### 5. LG’s statutory position

The NCCSA recommendation proceeds to the LG. The LG may:

- approve it;
- return it for reconsideration; and
- after reconsideration, take the final decision in case of continuing difference.

This is statutory primacy, not a statement that every Delhi administrative decision falls under the LG. The exact covered services, exclusions and statutory procedure matter.

### 6. Constitution-Bench referral, amendment order and current status

The original petition challenged the Ordinance. The official **20 July 2023 reportable order** referred the petition to a Constitution Bench and framed two questions: the contours of Parliament’s power under Article 239AA(7), and whether its exercise can abrogate constitutional principles of governance for the NCT. The Court also dismissed the interim stay application.

After Parliament replaced the Ordinance with Act 19 of 2023, the **25 August 2023 record-of-proceedings order** allowed the formal amendment of the petition so that the enacted statute, rather than only the superseded Ordinance, could be challenged. The later order did not make the Constitution-Bench referral; it updated the pleading after the legal instrument changed.

The constitutional issues include:

- whether Parliament may restructure executive control in this manner under Article 239AA;
- whether the scheme defeats representative government and collective responsibility;
- the scope of Parliament’s express plenary power; and
- whether the LG-finality design preserves or nullifies the elected sphere.

**Dated status:** No final merits judgment in W.P.(C) 678/2023 was located on the official Supreme Court record through 1 October 2026. The Act remains operative unless stayed, repealed or invalidated. This status must be docket-checked again before later publication.

### 7. Competing constitutional arguments

**Challenge argument:** The statute converts elected responsibility into formal accountability without administrative control, defeating Article 239AA’s representative structure and the 2023 judgment’s triple chain.

**Union argument:** Article 239AA expressly preserves Parliament’s power to legislate for Delhi, and national-capital administration justifies a special services mechanism.

**Balanced residual:** Parliament clearly possesses unusually wide competence, but its exercise remains subject to the basic constitutional design of Article 239AA. The unresolved question is how far ordinary law may alter the effective content of responsible government without formally abolishing it.

### UPSC application

- Always write the sequence: judgment → Ordinance → Act → pending challenge.
- Do not say the 2023 judgment is the complete current rule.
- Do not say the Act has already been upheld or struck down.
- Use the triple chain of accountability as analysis, not as a substitute for the statutory text.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the continued validity of the 11 May 2023 constitutional reasoning not mean that its practical allocation of services is the only current rule?

**Model answer:** Parliament subsequently enacted Act 19 of 2023 under its Delhi lawmaking power. The statute currently governs the services mechanism while its compatibility with Article 239AA and the judgment’s reasoning is pending judicial determination.

**Misconception to avoid:** A constitutional judgment prevents all later legislation; it instead constrains later legislation within the Constitution.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Prompt:** Analyse the constitutional conflict created by the Government of NCT of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2023.

#### Model answer

The conflict concerns whether electoral responsibility can remain effective without control over the officials implementing transferred subjects. On 11 May 2023, the Constitution Bench held that Delhi’s elected government controlled services under Entry 41 outside public order, police and land. It linked civil-service accountability to ministers, Assembly responsibility and electoral control. Parliament then enacted Act 19 of 2023, effective from 19 May, establishing the NCCSA. Although chaired by the Chief Minister, its two bureaucratic members can constitute a majority. Its recommendations go to the LG, who may return them and take the final decision after reconsideration.

The Union can rely on Parliament’s express plenary competence for Delhi and the national capital’s special needs. The challenge responds that ordinary law cannot hollow out Article 239AA’s responsible-government structure by separating ministers from administrative control. The issue is therefore not Parliament’s competence alone but the constitutional limit on its exercise.

On 1 October 2026, no final merits disposal of W.P.(C) 678/2023 was located; the Act remained operative. A balanced verdict must preserve Parliament’s national-capital role without making collective responsibility institutionally empty.

## Lesson 7: Breakdown, Ordinances and Regulations: Articles 239AB, 239B and 240

Progress: 7/15 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Three exceptional lawmaking and administration routes

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: do not merge the three powers

| Provision | Trigger | Lawmaker/actor | Territory | Legal function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Article 239AB | Failure of Delhi’s Article 239AA administration | President | Delhi | Suspends specified scheme and supplies consequential administration |
| Article 239B | Legislature in recess | Administrator with Presidential instructions | Puducherry; Delhi via 239AA(8) | Temporary ordinance within territorial legislative competence |
| Article 240 | Peace, progress and good government | President | Constitution’s named UTs; Puducherry conditionally | Regulation with force of parliamentary law |

Similar-looking Union interventions answer different problems.

### 1. Article 239AB: breakdown in Delhi

If the President, on the LG’s report or otherwise, is satisfied that Delhi’s administration cannot be carried on according to Article 239AA or the supporting law, the President may suspend the operation of specified provisions for a stated period and make incidental or consequential arrangements.

It resembles Article 356 functionally but is a distinct Part VIII provision. A precise answer should not simply call it “Article 356 in Delhi.”

### 2. Article 239B: ordinance during recess

Article 239B permits the Administrator of Puducherry, during recess of its legislature, to promulgate an ordinance after obtaining instructions from the President. Article 239AA(8) applies the provision to Delhi with necessary adaptations.

⚠️ **Source note:** Article 239B is a provision **of the Constitution**. The Government of Union Territories Act, 1963 does not confer this power and must not be cited as its source.

Key limits:

- the legislature must not be in session;
- prior Presidential instructions are required;
- the ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of the territorial legislature assented to by the Administrator;
- it must remain within the territorial legislature’s competence — to the extent it makes a provision that would have been invalid in an Act of that legislature, it is void; and
- an ordinance is not the route when the legislature is dissolved or its functioning is suspended.

#### How an ordinance ends: three different ways

Get this right, because the usual shorthand is wrong.

```text
Ordinance promulgated during recess
            │
            ├─ laid before the Legislature on reassembly
            │
            ├─ (i) LAPSE: ceases to operate at the expiry of
            │        SIX WEEKS from reassembly — automatically
            │
            ├─ (ii) DISAPPROVAL: ceases earlier, the moment the
            │        Legislature passes a resolution disapproving it
            │
            └─ (iii) WITHDRAWAL: the Administrator may withdraw it
                     at any time, after obtaining the President's
                     instructions
```

❌ **Do not write “it ceases six weeks after reassembly unless approved.”** That sentence smuggles in a rule that does not exist. The six-week clock runs regardless of whether the legislature approves of the ordinance; a mere resolution of approval does not extend its life. What preserves the content is a **replacing Act** passed by the legislature, not an approving vote. Approval is relevant only in the negative direction: a resolution **disapproving** the ordinance kills it *earlier* than six weeks.

The withdrawal route is the one most answers omit altogether. It is a separate, executive termination — and note that even to withdraw his own ordinance, the Administrator must again obtain the President's instructions. Presidential control attaches at both ends of the instrument's life.

The dissolution limit prevents confusion with Article 240’s conditional Puducherry regulation power.

### 3. The statutory twins: Jammu and Kashmir’s Sections 52 and 73

Article 239B is constitutional, and it reaches Puducherry directly and Delhi through Article 239AA(8). J&K is a Union territory with a legislature too — so where does *its* emergency lawmaking come from? Not from Article 239B. Parliament wrote J&K's equivalents into the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 itself. Setting the constitutional and statutory versions side by side is one of the cleanest ways to see what the topic is really about: the same governance problem solved twice, once above Parliament's reach and once inside it.

#### Section 52 — the LG’s own ordinance power

✅ **Fact — Section 52(1):** if at any time, except when the Legislative Assembly is in session, the Lieutenant Governor “is satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary for him to take immediate action, he may promulgate such Ordinances as the circumstances appear to him to require,” provided that the power “shall extend only to those matters with respect to which the Legislative Assembly has power to make laws.”

✅ **Fact — Section 52(2):** such an ordinance “shall have the same force and effect as an Act of the Legislative Assembly assented by the Lieutenant Governor,” but (a) shall be laid before the Assembly and shall cease to operate at the expiration of **six weeks from the re-assembly** of the Assembly, or earlier if a disapproving resolution is passed; and (b) **“may be withdrawn at any time by the Lieutenant Governor.”**

| Feature | Article 239B (Puducherry, and Delhi via 239AA(8)) | Section 52, J&K Act, 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Constitution | Ordinary parliamentary statute |
| Who promulgates | Administrator | Lieutenant Governor |
| Prior Presidential instructions to promulgate | **Required** | **Not required** |
| Competence limit | Only what the legislature could enact | Only what the Assembly could enact |
| Lapse | Six weeks from reassembly | Six weeks from reassembly |
| Earlier death by disapproving resolution | Yes | Yes |
| Withdrawal | By the Administrator, **again on Presidential instructions** | **By the LG alone**, at any time |

⚠️ **Inference — this is the sharpest contrast in the whole lesson, and it runs the opposite way to the one most answers expect.** On ordinances, the *statutory* J&K scheme is less Union-supervised than the *constitutional* Article 239B scheme. The J&K LG neither needs the President's instructions to promulgate nor the President's instructions to withdraw. The Union's grip on J&K was asserted elsewhere — through the reserved subjects in Section 32(1), the discretion heads in Section 53(2) and the business rules of Lesson 10 — and not through the ordinance clause. Never assume that a constitutional provision is always the more centralising one; read each instrument for what it actually says.

#### Section 73 — the breakdown provision, and what it is *not*

✅ **Fact — Section 73:** if the President, on receipt of a report from the LG of the Union territory of J&K “or otherwise,” is satisfied **(a)** that a situation has arisen in which the administration of the UT cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Act, or **(b)** that for the proper administration of the UT it is necessary or expedient so to do, the President may by order **suspend the operation of all or any of the provisions of the Act** for such period as he thinks fit and make such incidental and consequential provisions as appear necessary for administering the UT in accordance with the Act's provisions.

```text
   BREAKDOWN, THREE TIMES OVER — same device, three sources

   DELHI            PUDUCHERRY             JAMMU AND KASHMIR
   Art 239AB        s.51, 1963 Act         s.73, 2019 Act
   constitutional   statutory              statutory
        ↓                 ↓                        ↓
   President suspends operation of specified provisions
   and makes incidental and consequential arrangements
```

⚠️ **Distinguish the *function* from the *particular proclamation*, because Lesson 10 depends on it.** Section 73 is a standing power. The **Proclamation of 31 October 2019** was one exercise of it, issued when the UT came into existence and there was as yet no Assembly or Council of Ministers. That proclamation is the vehicle through which the President, rather than an LG acting on ministerial advice, made the rules amended by G.S.R. 386(E) in 2024. So:

| Statement | Verdict |
|---|---|
| “Section 73 is the J&K breakdown provision” | ✅ Correct |
| “The 2019 Proclamation under Section 73 was a response to a breakdown of an elected government” | ❌ Wrong — there was no elected UT government to break down; it accompanied the creation of the UT |
| “G.S.R. 386(E) was made under Section 73” | ❌ Imprecise — it was made under **Section 55**, *read with* the Section 73 Proclamation |
| “The Section 73 proclamation ended when the Assembly was constituted in 2024” | ⚠️ Do not assert; the Lesson 10 attribution turns on the proclamation having been in operation in July 2024, which the notification itself recites |

⚠️ **Why Parliament bothered.** A UT without a legislature needs no breakdown clause: there is nothing to suspend. The moment Parliament creates a territorial Assembly and ministry — by statute for Puducherry and J&K, by constitutional amendment for Delhi — it must also write the clause that switches them off. Breakdown provisions are therefore a reliable *marker* of a legislature UT. Finding one tells you an Assembly exists; its absence for A&N or Lakshadweep is not a gap but a consequence.

### 4. Article 240’s exact textual list

The President may make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of:

1. Andaman and Nicobar Islands;
2. Lakshadweep;
3. Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu; and
4. Puducherry, but only when its legislature is dissolved or its functioning remains suspended.

The Constitution has been amended over time to substitute current territorial names and remove territories that became States. **Ladakh is not named in Article 240.**

#### How Article 240’s list actually gets changed

Here is a point almost every answer gets wrong, in both directions.

❌ **Wrong:** “Article 240 is in the Constitution, so only an Article 368 amendment can alter its list.”

✅ **Fact:** When Parliament legislates under Article 2 or Article 3, **Article 4** allows that law to contain “such supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions… as Parliament may deem necessary,” and **Article 4(2)** declares that no such law “shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368.”

✅ **Fact:** That is exactly how Article 240’s list reached its present form. **Section 4 of the Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu (Merger of Union Territories) Act, 2019** — an ordinary Act of Parliament — substituted entry (c) of Article 240(1) with “Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu” and omitted entry (d). No constitutional amendment Bill was required.

```text
reorganisation under Art 2 / Art 3
            ↓
Art 4 permits supplemental, incidental and
consequential provisions in the SAME ordinary law
            ↓
Art 4(2): not an amendment for Art 368 purposes
            ↓
DNHDD Merger Act 2019, s.4  →  rewrote Art 240(1)(c)–(d)
```

⚠️ **Where the line really falls.** The distinction is not “Constitution versus statute.” It is **consequential versus standalone**:

| Proposed change | Correct route |
|---|---|
| Rename or merge Article 240 entries as a consequence of an Article 2/3 reorganisation | Ordinary law, using Article 4 |
| Insert a territory into Article 240 as a freestanding policy choice, with no reorganisation | Article 368 amendment |

So a Bill that merely declared “Ladakh is included in Article 240” without any accompanying reorganisation would need Article 368. A reorganisation law that restructured territories and consequentially adjusted the entry would not. And because Section 58(2) already carries Ladakh to the Article 240 power without naming it in the list, neither has been necessary.

### 5. Force and reach of a regulation

After promulgation, an Article 240 regulation has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament applicable to that territory. It may repeal or amend an existing parliamentary law in its territorial application.

This makes it stronger than a territorial ordinance:

```text
Article 239B ordinance → temporary Assembly-equivalent law
Article 240 regulation → Parliament-equivalent territorial law
```

### 6. Ladakh’s correct route: Article 240 reached through a statutory bridge

This is the single point on which more answers go wrong than any other in the lesson, and the error is now usually an *over*-correction. Having learned that Ladakh is not in Article 240's list, candidates conclude that Ladakh's regulation power is some separate, free-standing statutory device. It is not. Read the section.

✅ **Fact — Section 58(2), J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019:** “The President may make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of the Union territory of Ladakh **under article 240 of the Constitution of India**.”

The words “under article 240” are in the statute. The power exercised is the Article 240 power. What Section 58(2) supplies is the **bridge** that brings Ladakh within the operation of an Article that does not name it.

✅ **Fact — Section 58(3):** any regulation so made “may repeal or amend any Act made by Parliament or any other law which is for the time being applicable to the Union territory of Ladakh and, when promulgated by the President, shall have the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament which applies to the Union territory of Ladakh.”

⚠️ **Keep the two sub-sections apart.** Sub-section (2) supplies the **bridge to the power**; sub-section (3) supplies the **force and effect**, doing for Ladakh what Article 240(2) does for the listed territories. An answer that attributes the Act-of-Parliament force to Section 58(2) has cited the wrong sub-section.

```text
       THREE DISTINCT THINGS — do not collapse them

  Article 240(1)            the CONSTITUTIONAL SOURCE of the
  (Constitution)            regulation-making power
          ↑
          │ brought to bear on Ladakh by —
          │
  Section 58(2)             the STATUTORY BRIDGE: President may
  (2019 Act)                make regulations for Ladakh
                            "under article 240"
          │
          ↓
  Section 58(3)             the EFFECT: force of an Act of
  (2019 Act)                Parliament; may repeal or amend
                            existing law for Ladakh
```

✅ **Fact — the proof is in the instrument itself.** Regulation No. 10 of 2026 opens: “**In exercise of the powers conferred by article 240 of the Constitution read with sub-section (2) of section 58 of the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**, the President is pleased to promulgate the following Regulation made by her.” The enacting formula names Article 240 first and Section 58(2) as the provision read with it. That is the correct citation to reproduce.

⚠️ **So what exactly remains true about Ladakh's absence from the list?** Only this, and it is worth stating carefully:

| Proposition | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Ladakh appears in the text of Article 240(1) | ❌ No. The clause names A&N, Lakshadweep, DNHDD and conditionally Puducherry |
| Ladakh's regulations are made under Article 240 | ✅ Yes — through the bridge in Section 58(2) |
| Section 58(2) is a free-standing statutory power independent of Article 240 | ❌ No. The sub-section itself says “under article 240” |
| Adding Ladakh to Article 240(1)'s text would need a constitutional route | ✅ Yes — and this is the real significance of the absence |

⚠️ **Why Parliament chose a bridge instead of an amendment.** Amending the list in Article 240(1) as a standalone policy choice requires Article 368. Writing a sub-section into the reorganisation statute did not. Section 58(2) therefore achieved the same practical result through the legislative route Parliament already had in hand — which is exactly the pattern this whole topic keeps displaying: where the Constitution sets the design, change is hard; where Parliament sets it, change is ordinary.

#### Chandigarh, and the proposal that was not enacted

A live illustration of the same boundary surfaced in **November 2025**, when it was reported that a proposal was under consideration to bring **Chandigarh** under Article 240 and give it its own Administrator rather than continuing the Punjab-Governor arrangement of Lesson 2.

❌ **Do not record this as a change in the law.** The Ministry of Home Affairs stated that no Bill would be introduced in that session and that no final decision had been taken. Nothing was enacted, and Chandigarh remained outside Article 240 on the cut-off date.

⚠️ **Inference — why it is still worth one line in an answer.** Chandigarh is instructive precisely because it has no reorganisation vehicle running. There is no Article 2 or Article 3 law in progress into which an Article 4 consequential amendment could be folded, and there is no statute of its own carrying a Section 58(2)-style bridge. A Chandigarh entry in Article 240 would therefore be the **standalone constitutional amendment** case from the table above — the hard route. That is a substantial part of why a reported proposal did not become a Bill.

#### The 2026 regulation, named exactly

✅ **Fact:** the instrument is **Regulation No. 10 of 2026**, promulgated by the President on **27 August 2026** and published in the Gazette of India Extraordinary, Part II, Section 1, by the Ministry of Law and Justice.

✅ **Fact — the short title, as the Regulation writes it.** Section 1(1) reads: “This Regulation may be called the **Union territory of Ladakh (Sitting of Bench of the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh in Ladakh) Regulation, 2026**.”

Two things in that title repay attention:

- it uses the lower-case form **“Union territory”** — exactly as the Constitution and the 2019 Act do, and as the Regulation also does in Section 1(2) (“the whole of the Union territory of Ladakh”) and in Section 2(2); and
- it carries a **comma** before “and Ladakh” — “High Court of Jammu and Kashmir**,** and Ladakh.”

⚠️ **One qualification about capitals, so the point is not overstated.** The Gazette *heading* above the enacting text is set in capitals throughout — “THE UNION TERRITORY OF LADAKH (SITTING OF BENCH OF THE HIGH COURT OF JAMMU AND KASHMIR, AND LADAKH IN LADAKH) REGULATION, 2026” — as Gazette headings routinely are. That is a printing convention, not a second official title. The operative short title is the one in Section 1(1), and it is lower-case. Quote Section 1(1).

⚠️ **The comma is specific to this Regulation and must not be generalised.** Lesson 13 will show that the words substituted into Section 75(1)(a) by a 2021 order contain **no** comma, and that the Court's own ordinary usage is different again. There is no single “correct” punctuation to apply everywhere; each instrument must be quoted as that instrument writes it.

✅ **Fact — Section 1(3) and its Explanation:** the Regulation comes into force “on such date as the **Administrator** may, by notification in the Official Gazette, appoint,” and “Administrator” is defined for that sub-section as the Administrator of the Union territory of Ladakh **appointed by the President under article 239**. The Lieutenant Governor of Ladakh accordingly issued **S.O. 262**, appointing **5 September 2026** as the commencement date.

⚠️ **A quiet connection worth noticing.** The commencement clause of an Article 240 regulation routes through the Article 239 Administrator. Articles 239 and 240 are not alternatives; in a non-legislature UT they interlock, one supplying the executive head and the other the lawmaking device.

✅ **Fact — Section 2:** sub-section (1) keeps the principal seat of the common High Court where it was before commencement. Sub-section (2) provides that, notwithstanding that, “the **Judges and division courts** of the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh may also sit at such place in the Union territory of Ladakh as the **Chief Justice may, with the approval of the Lieutenant Governor of Ladakh, appoint**.” Sub-section (3) permits the Chief Justice, in his discretion, to order that any case or class of cases arising in Ladakh be heard at Srinagar or Jammu.

⚠️ **Use the Regulation's own noun.** It says **“division courts,”** not “division benches.” The two mean the same thing in practice, but when you are quoting a statutory provision whose entire examinable value is its precision, borrow its vocabulary rather than the newspaper's.

⚠️ **Inference:** The LG-approval requirement is a quiet but revealing detail. Even a judicial-sitting arrangement in a non-legislature UT routes through the Union-appointed Administrator. It is a precise illustration of the lesson's theme: exceptional Union instruments do not merely create institutions, they also keep a hand on how those institutions are located and used. Legal commencement enabled the next institutional steps; it did not by itself prove that a staffed bench had begun regular sittings.

### 7. Objection and response

**Objection:** Parliament-equivalent presidential regulations concentrate legislative power in the executive.

**Response:** The device serves territories without legislatures and permits territorially tailored law. The power originates in the Constitution or an express parliamentary statute and remains judicially reviewable.

**Residual:** Democratic consultation is institutionally weaker than legislation debated by elected representatives. Advisory committees and local bodies cannot fully substitute for a legislature.

### UPSC application

- Identify trigger, actor, territory and legal effect.
- Never include Chandigarh, Delhi or J&K in Article 240’s named list.
- Explain Puducherry’s condition.
- State Ladakh’s route exactly: regulations under **Article 240 read with Section 58(2)**, with Section 58(3) supplying the Act-of-Parliament force.
- Contrast Article 239B with J&K Section 52: no prior Presidential instructions there, and the LG may withdraw alone.
- Treat Section 73 as the J&K breakdown provision and the 31 October 2019 Proclamation as one exercise of it.

### Revision notes

1. Article 239AB concerns failure of Delhi’s governing scheme and is distinct from Article 356.
2. Article 239B is a constitutional provision, not a provision of the 1963 Act; Delhi receives it through Article 239AA(8).
3. It concerns ordinances during legislative recess, and Presidential instructions are necessary.
4. An ordinance has the force of an Act of the territorial legislature and is void so far as that legislature could not have enacted it.
5. It lapses six weeks after reassembly; a disapproving resolution kills it earlier; the Administrator may withdraw it earlier still, again on Presidential instructions. Approval does not extend it — only a replacing Act preserves its content.
6. J&K’s ordinance power is statutory, in Section 52: no prior Presidential instructions, the same six-week lapse, and withdrawal by the LG alone at any time.
7. J&K’s breakdown provision is Section 73; the Proclamation of 31 October 2019 was one exercise of it and later supported the Section 55 rule-making used in 2024.
8. Breakdown clauses exist only where a legislature exists — Article 239AB for Delhi, Section 51 for Puducherry, Section 73 for J&K.
9. Article 240 regulations are Parliament-equivalent and directly name A&N, Lakshadweep, DNHDD and conditionally Puducherry, the condition being dissolution or suspension of Puducherry’s legislature.
10. Ladakh is absent from Article 240’s text, but Section 58(2) says the President may make regulations for Ladakh **“under article 240”** — a statutory bridge to the constitutional power, not a separate power.
11. Section 58(3), not 58(2), gives a Ladakh regulation the force of an Act of Parliament and the power to repeal or amend existing law.
12. Regulation No. 10 of 2026 was made “in exercise of the powers conferred by article 240 of the Constitution read with sub-section (2) of section 58” of the 2019 Act.
13. Its Section 1(1) short title uses the lower-case “Union territory of Ladakh…” and a comma in “Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh”; the all-capital Gazette heading is a printing convention.
14. Section 1(3) left commencement to the Article 239 Administrator, who appointed 5 September 2026 by S.O. 262; Section 2(2) lets Judges and **division courts** sit in Ladakh at places the Chief Justice appoints with the Lieutenant Governor’s approval, the principal seat being unchanged.
15. A November 2025 proposal to bring Chandigarh under Article 240 was not enacted; the MHA said no Bill would be introduced and no final decision taken — the standalone Article 368 case.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes: “Ladakh is not an Article 240 territory, so its High Court regulation was made under a separate statutory power in Section 58(2).” Identify what is right and what is wrong.

**Model answer:** The first half is right: Ladakh is not named in Article 240(1), whose list is A&N, Lakshadweep, DNHDD and conditionally Puducherry. The second half is wrong. Section 58(2) does not create a separate power; it states that the President may make regulations for Ladakh **“under article 240.”** Regulation No. 10 of 2026 was accordingly made under Article 240 read with Section 58(2), and it is Section 58(3) that supplies the force of an Act of Parliament.

**Misconception to avoid:** Absence from a list is not absence from the power. Section 58(2) is a statutory bridge into Article 240, not a parallel device outside it.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Prompt:** Distinguish Articles 239AB, 239B and 240 and explain the special position of Ladakh.

#### Model answer

The three provisions differ in trigger, actor and legal effect. Article 239AB addresses failure of Delhi’s Article 239AA administration. The President may suspend specified provisions and make consequential arrangements; it is a Delhi-specific breakdown mechanism, not Article 356 itself. Article 239B concerns temporary lawmaking during the recess of a territorial legislature. The Administrator acts after Presidential instructions, and the ordinance ordinarily expires six weeks after reassembly. It applies to Puducherry and, through Article 239AA(8), Delhi. J&K’s statutory analogues are Section 52, where no Presidential instruction is required and the LG may withdraw alone, and Section 73 for breakdown.

Article 240 is a direct presidential regulation power for peace, progress and good government. It textually names Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, and Puducherry only while its legislature is dissolved or suspended. A regulation has the force of an Act of Parliament and may alter parliamentary law for the territory.

Ladakh’s position is distinctive but frequently misstated. It is absent from Article 240’s list, yet Section 58(2) of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 provides that the President may make regulations for Ladakh “under article 240,” with Section 58(3) supplying Act-of-Parliament force. Regulation No. 10 of 2026 recites exactly that combination. Ladakh therefore uses the constitutional power through a statutory bridge; it is neither listed in Article 240 nor governed by a power outside it.

**Rubric (15):** 239AB 2; 239B and J&K ss.52/73 contrast 3; Article 240 list and condition 3; legal-effect comparison 2; Section 58(2) bridge and 58(3) effect 4; clarity 1.

### Visual: Puducherry’s institutional circuit

```text
Article 239A
     ↓
Government of Union Territories Act, 1963
     ├─ 30 elected members
     ├─ seats reserved for Scheduled Castes under Section 3
     ├─ one-third women's reservation added by Act 39 of 2023
     ├─ up to 3 centrally nominated members
     ├─ Council of Ministers responsible to Assembly
     ├─ applicable State + Concurrent List field
     └─ Administrator/President assent and control mechanisms
```

Puducherry is the classic statutory-legislature UT: substantial self-government under an ordinary parliamentary framework.

### 1. Composition

The Assembly contains 30 elected members. The Central Government may nominate up to three persons. Puducherry’s dispersed geography—Puducherry and Karaikal in Tamil Nadu, Mahe in Kerala and Yanam in Andhra Pradesh—adds administrative complexity but does not alter the unitary legislative institution.

Nominated members are members of the Assembly with voting rights under the governing statute. In *K. Lakshminarayanan v. Union of India*, the nomination power and participation of nominees were upheld.

### 2. Legislative competence

Section 18 of the 1963 Act authorises laws on State and Concurrent List matters insofar as they are applicable to UTs. Puducherry does not have Delhi’s express exclusion of public order, police and land.

Yet three qualifications remain:

1. applicability to a UT must be established;
2. Parliament retains power over every subject; and
3. parliamentary law prevails according to the statutory inconsistency rule.

Puducherry therefore has the broadest formal local field among the three legislature UTs, but not exclusive State competence.

### 3. Ministry and appointments

The President appoints the Chief Minister. Other ministers are appointed by the President on the CM’s advice. The Council of Ministers aids and advises the Administrator except where law requires discretion and is collectively responsible to the Assembly.

The textbook sequence is easy to state but easy to get wrong in an answer, because four different legal moments are involved:

```text
election and result
      ↓
old ministry resigns; President accepts
      ↓
caretaker continuation until a new Council is sworn
      ↓
appointment order (names a minister)
      ↓
oath / assumption (the appointment takes effect)
      ↓
allocation of business (decides what each minister actually does)
```

#### The 2026 Puducherry cycle, stage by stage

The territory completed this entire sequence during 2026, which makes it the clearest available illustration of each stage.

Four distinctions carry marks.

First, **an appointment order is not an assumption of office.** This is not a one-off feature of the June appointments — it is how every appointment in the cycle was drafted. S.O. 2381(E) of 9 May appointed Rangasamy, but operated from his oath on 13 May. S.O. 2459(E) of 13 May appointed the first two ministers, again from the date of their oath. S.O. 2890(E) of 8 June named three more, and they took office only on 17 June. Exactly the same discipline governs Administrator appointments, where a presidential communiqué and the oath fall on different dates; Lesson 12 works through that chronology in detail.

```text
APPOINTMENT ORDER                OATH / ASSUMPTION
(who is appointed)               (when it starts operating)
   S.O. 2381(E)  9 May  ────────────→  13 May   Chief Minister
   S.O. 2459(E) 13 May  ────────────→  13 May   two ministers
   S.O. 2890(E)  8 June ────────────→  17 June  three ministers
                                           ↓
                              Council complete: six members
                                           ↓
                   5 Aug: G.O. Ms. No. 69 allocates business
```

⚠️ **Note that S.O. 2459(E) and the oath fall on the same day, 13 May.** That coincidence is exactly what makes the distinction hard to see — and exactly why the June pair, where the gap is nine days, is the better teaching example. Same-day execution does not merge two legally separate acts.

⚠️ **Note also the different advice formulas in the two May orders.** S.O. 2381(E) simply records that the President is pleased to appoint the Chief Minister. S.O. 2459(E) records appointment **“on the advice of the Chief Minister.”** That is Section 45 of the 1963 Act showing through the drafting: the head of government is chosen by the appointing authority, while the rest of the Council reaches office on the head of government’s advice. The same asymmetry appears in Article 164 for a State and in Section 54(1) of the J&K Act. Reading the two orders side by side is a quicker way to internalise it than memorising the section.

Second, **appointment is not allocation of business.** Being a minister and holding a defined portfolio are separate legal acts. Between 17 June and 5 August the Council was complete in membership while the distribution of subjects was still pending.

Third, **a caretaker Council is not a new Council.** S.O. 2348(E) directed the outgoing ministry to continue until a new Council was sworn in. Caretaker continuation is a device for avoiding an administrative vacuum; it confers no fresh mandate and ends automatically on the swearing-in of the successor.

Fourth, **a central notification and its local republication are one instrument seen twice.** Each of S.O. 2348(E), 2381(E) and 2459(E) was issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs and then republished in the Puducherry Gazette under its own G.O. Ms. number. The republication does not re-enact anything; it gives local public notice. Do not count the two dates as two decisions.

#### Who allocates business, and under what power

✅ **Fact:** The 5 August 2026 allocation was made by the **Lieutenant Governor in consultation with the Chief Minister**, in exercise of the power under **rule 4 of the Business of the Government of Puducherry (Allocation) Rules, 1963**, read with **Section 44 of the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963**.

That citation is worth memorising because it shows the hybrid character of the territory in one line. Section 44 is the aid-and-advise provision: the Council aids and advises the Administrator on matters within the Assembly’s competence. The allocation rules then require the Administrator to act *in consultation with* the Chief Minister. So the formal instrument issues from the Union-appointed Administrator, while the political choice of who gets what is made with the elected head of government. Neither a pure Union command nor a pure State-style Chief Ministerial prerogative describes it accurately.

⚠️ **Section 44 also contains the escape hatch.** Where a difference of opinion arises between the Administrator and his Ministers on any matter, the Administrator is to refer it to the President for decision and act according to that decision — with the same urgency power to act in the interim that you met in Delhi's Article 239AA(4) proviso. Compare the two deliberately:

| | Delhi | Puducherry |
|---|---|---|
| Difference-of-opinion provision | Proviso to Article 239AA(4) — **constitutional** | Proviso to Section 44, 1963 Act — **statutory** |
| Who decides | President | President |
| Interim action pending decision | Permitted on the Administrator’s view of urgency | Permitted on the Administrator’s view of urgency |
| How the mechanism could be narrowed | Constitutional amendment | Ordinary parliamentary amendment |

⚠️ **Inference:** The mechanics are near-identical; the entrenchment is not. This matters practically: the judicial reading that confined Delhi’s proviso to exceptional matters rests on constitutional structure, and its reasoning is persuasive rather than automatically binding when transplanted to a statutory proviso. An answer that simply says “Puducherry is the same as Delhi here” has skipped the step the examiner wants.

Three consequences follow:

1. **The residuary clause is not decorative.** In a six-member Council covering a long departmental list, “Other subjects which are not allocated to any other Ministers” is where a great deal of ordinary administration actually sits. Listing only the named portfolios understates the Chief Minister’s working field.
2. **Coordination and finance are centralised in the head of government.** Confidential & Cabinet, General Administration and Personnel & Administrative Reforms together control the secretariat’s machinery; adding Planning & Finance to them concentrates both the process and the purse.
3. **Small Councils force concentration.** With no numerical ceiling in the 1963 Act but only five ministers appointed, the arithmetic of a full departmental list has to land somewhere. That is a structural feature of small-territory government, not a personal arrangement.

### 4. Reserved seats: Scheduled Castes, and women’s reservation from 2023

Composition is not only about how many members sit in the House. It is also about which of the 30 directly elected seats are legally reserved, and for whom.

#### The statutory Scheduled Caste baseline

✅ **Fact:** Reservation of seats in the Puducherry Assembly is **statutory, not constitutional**. The Government of Union Territories Act, 1963 provides in **Section 3, sub-sections (4) and (5)**, that seats shall be reserved for the Scheduled Castes in the Legislative Assembly, and that the number so reserved shall bear, as nearly as may be, the same proportion to the total number of seats as the Scheduled Caste population of the Union territory bears to its total population.

✅ **Fact:** The Explanation to Section 3(5) defines “population” as the population ascertained at the last preceding census of which the relevant figures have been published — but its proviso directs that, **until the relevant figures for the first census taken after the year 2026 have been published, that reference is to be construed as a reference to the 2001 census**.

That proviso is doing something very specific, and it is worth pausing on because the same technique appears throughout this topic.

```text
Ordinary rule:  use the LAST PUBLISHED census
                              ↓
Proviso freezes it:  read "last published census" as  2001
                              ↓
Freeze lifts when:   figures for the first census taken
                     AFTER 2026 are published
```

⚠️ **Inference:** Parliament here deliberately **froze** a population figure so that seat arithmetic would not shift with every census. The same freezing technique explains the nationwide freeze on Lok Sabha seat readjustment and, as the next subsection shows, it is also why the 2023 women’s reservation cannot operate yet. Once you recognise the device, three separate-looking “why hasn’t this happened yet?” questions turn out to have one answer: a census-linked condition that has not yet been satisfied.

This is the structural contrast that UPSC can test directly:

| Question | States | Puducherry |
|---|---|---|
| Where does seat reservation for SCs come from? | Article 332 of the Constitution | Section 3(4)–(5) of the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963 |
| What fixes the number? | Census-based proportion, through delimitation | Census-based proportion, through delimitation |
| Which census is used at present? | Frozen by the corresponding constitutional proviso | Frozen at the 2001 census by the proviso to Section 3(5) |
| How is the rule changed? | Constitutional amendment | Ordinary parliamentary legislation |

⚠️ **Inference:** The proportionality formula is the same; the **source** is different. This is Lesson 3’s entrenchment point reappearing at the level of seats rather than institutions, and it is why the women’s reservation of 2023 reached Puducherry through a statute rather than through the 106th Amendment.

✅ **Fact — Section 3(6) closes the loop.** Notwithstanding sub-section (4), reservation of seats for the Scheduled Castes in the Union territory’s Legislative Assembly “shall cease to have effect on the same date on which the reservation of seats for the Scheduled Castes in the House of the People shall cease to have effect under **article 334**,” with a proviso protecting existing representation until the then-existing Assembly is dissolved.

⚠️ **Why that sub-section matters more than it looks.** Parliament could have given the Puducherry reservation its own independent sunset. Instead it **tethered** the statutory reservation to a constitutional clock. The practical consequence is that each time Parliament extends Article 334 for the Lok Sabha, Puducherry’s Assembly reservation is extended automatically, with no amendment of the 1963 Act at all. The same drafting technique appears in **Section 14(8)** of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 for the SC and ST seats in that Assembly.

```text
Article 334  (Constitution)     ← the single clock
      │
      ├── House of the People and State Assemblies: directly
      ├── Puducherry Assembly:  via GoUT Act s.3(6)
      └── J&K Assembly:         via J&K Act s.14(8)
```

⚠️ **Inference — a precise lesson about entrenchment.** It is not true that a statutory reservation is automatically more fragile than a constitutional one in every respect. The *source* of Puducherry's reservation is statutory and therefore easy for Parliament to alter; but its *duration* is pinned to a constitutional provision and therefore as durable as Article 334 itself. Separate the question “who can change the rule?” from “when does the rule expire?” Answers that merge them produce a confident-sounding but incorrect comparison.

#### Women’s reservation: the third instrument

Lesson 4 showed Delhi receiving one-third reservation through the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023. Lesson 10 will show J&K receiving it through the J&K Reorganisation (Second Amendment) Act, 2023. Puducherry needed its own third instrument, because its Assembly is a creature of the 1963 Act.

✅ **Fact:** The **Government of Union Territories (Amendment) Act, 2023 (Act 39 of 2023)** inserts **Sections 3A and 3B** into the 1963 Act. Section 3A reserves, as nearly as may be, **one-third of all seats filled by direct election** in the Puducherry Legislative Assembly for women, **including one-third of the seats reserved for the Scheduled Castes**.

✅ **Fact:** Section 3B supplies the timing and duration. The reservation takes effect after a delimitation exercise undertaken on the basis of the figures of the **first census taken after the commencement of that amendment**; it operates for **fifteen years** from commencement; and the particular seats reserved for women are to be **rotated after each subsequent delimitation**.

✅ **Fact:** It was brought into force by **S.O. 1930(E), dated 17 April 2026** — the day after the 106th Amendment’s own commencement and the same day as the J&K statutory commencement.

✅ **Fact:** Commencement is not operation. No Puducherry seat was reserved for women on 17 April 2026 by force of that notification alone, because the delimitation condition in Section 3B had not been satisfied.

⚠️ **Get the source of the delay right — this is where answers lose marks.** It is tempting to write that all three reservations “await the constitutionally prescribed delimitation.” That is accurate only for Delhi.

| Assembly | Reservation provision | Provision supplying the condition | Nature of that condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delhi | Article 239AA(2)(ba)–(bc) | **Article 334A** | Constitutional |
| J&K | Sections 14A–14B, J&K Reorganisation Act | Section 14B of that Act | Statutory |
| Puducherry | Sections 3A–3B, 1963 Act | Section 3B of that Act | Statutory |

```text
three Assemblies  ·  three different instruments  ·  one shared condition

Delhi       → Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023  → S.O. 1922(E), 16 Apr 2026
              condition: Article 334A (constitutional)

J&K         → J&K Reorganisation (Second Amendment)
              Act, 2023, ss. 14A–14B                    → S.O. 1929(E), 17 Apr 2026
              condition: statutory

Puducherry  → Government of Union Territories
              (Amendment) Act, 2023 (Act 39 of 2023),
              ss. 3A–3B                                 → S.O. 1930(E), 17 Apr 2026
              condition: statutory
                                   ↓
            all three await a census-linked delimitation
            — but only Delhi's condition is in the Constitution
                                   ↓
                   none of them operated on 1 October 2026
```

> 🔑 **Answer line:** The instrument differs with the source of each Assembly, and so does the provision imposing the condition. Write all three halves: same outcome, three instruments, two different kinds of legal source.

⚠️ **Note the SC overlap.** The one-third for women is not computed only on general seats. It expressly includes one-third of the SC-reserved seats, so a Scheduled Caste woman candidate is contemplated within the SC quota itself rather than being pushed into a separate category. This is exactly why the statutory SC baseline above had to be taught first: the women’s reservation operates *on* that baseline, not beside it. And because that baseline is itself frozen to the 2001 census, the SC component of the women’s quota cannot be computed until the same census-and-delimitation sequence unlocks both.

⚠️ **A live illustration from April 2026.** As Lesson 3 recorded, the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 would have altered representation and delimitation arrangements for Delhi, Puducherry and J&K, but it was withdrawn after the accompanying constitutional amendment was defeated. Nothing in Puducherry’s position therefore changed: Sections 3A–3B remain commenced and inoperative, on the original census-linked terms.

### 5. Administrator, Bills and Union supervision

The Administrator participates in:

- summoning, proroguing and dissolving the Assembly under the statutory framework;
- assent, withholding or reservation of Bills;
- specified discretionary functions, with a Section 44 reference to the President where a difference of opinion with ministers arises;
- ordinance action under Article 239B with Presidential instructions; and
- escalation of legally reserved matters.

Behind all of these sits the ultimate statutory lever taught in Lesson 3: under **Section 51** of the 1963 Act the President may suspend the operation of the Act’s provisions and administer the territory under Article 239. Union control is therefore embedded through appointments, assent, parliamentary override, exceptional lawmaking and a statutory breakdown route — not merely through informal administrative influence.

### 6. Nominated members: democratic controversy

**Critique:** Up to three centrally nominated voting members can affect government formation or confidence in a 30-elected-member House without direct electoral mandate.

**Legal defence:** Article 239A expressly permits a partly nominated legislature, and the 1963 Act confers membership and voting status.

**Institutional residual:** Legal validity does not end the debate over democratic legitimacy. Transparent criteria and restrained use are important where nomination can change political outcomes.

### 7. Comparison with Delhi

| Dimension | Puducherry | Delhi |

### Concept check

**Question:** Does Puducherry’s broader local legislative field make its Assembly constitutionally stronger than Delhi’s?

**Model answer:** It is broader regarding expressly excluded subjects, but the Assembly is created and structured by parliamentary statute under Article 239A, whereas Delhi’s essential scheme is constitutionally entrenched in Article 239AA. “Stronger” therefore depends on field breadth or institutional entrenchment.

**Misconception to avoid:** Comparing institutions on only one axis produces a false hierarchy.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Prompt:** Critically examine the nominated-member device in Puducherry’s Legislative Assembly.

#### Model answer

Article 239A permits Parliament to create a wholly elected or partly nominated territorial legislature. The Government of Union Territories Act, 1963 accordingly provides 30 elected members and allows the Central Government to nominate up to three members. *K. Lakshminarayanan v. Union of India* sustained the nomination framework and recognised nominees’ voting participation.

The device may supply expertise, minority voice or representation not secured electorally. Its constitutional and statutory basis is therefore clear. However, three nominated votes are politically significant in a small House. Where nominees influence confidence, government formation or closely divided legislation, central appointment can appear to dilute electoral equality and strengthen Union leverage over a locally responsible ministry.

The correct criticism is consequently not that nominees are legally non-members, but that valid nomination requires democratic restraint. Transparent selection standards, timely appointments and conventions against partisan manipulation would reconcile Article 239A’s flexibility with representative legitimacy. Puducherry demonstrates the central tension of statutory UT government: meaningful local democracy exists, but Parliament and the Union retain unusually direct institutional instruments.

**Rubric (15):** legal basis 3; structure/voting status 2; case law 2; benefits 2; democratic critique 3; reforms/verdict 3.

The J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019:

- reorganised the former State;
- created the UT of Jammu and Kashmir with a legislature;
- created the UT of Ladakh without a legislature;
- allocated parliamentary representation;
- continued a common High Court;
- adapted laws, services, assets and liabilities; and

The appointed day was 31 October 2019.

### 2. Why Section 13 matters

Section 13 applies Article 239A to J&K with the necessary modification that references operate for the new UT. The Assembly is therefore a statutory UT institution, not a revived Part VI State legislature.

This provides the correct comparison:

```text
J&K Assembly ≈ statutory UT legislature
J&K Assembly ≠ State legislature pending statehood restoration
```

### 3. Reorganisation and Article 370 are related but distinct

The constitutional changes concerning Article 370 and the Reorganisation Act occurred together politically, but this lesson concentrates on the resulting Union-Territory institutions. The full Article 370 doctrine requires separate treatment.

In *In Re: Article 370* on 11 December 2023, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional outcome relating to Article 370 and upheld Ladakh’s formation. It did not finally adjudicate the validity of converting J&K into a UT after the Union gave an assurance that statehood would be restored. The Court directed Assembly elections by 30 September 2024 and stated that statehood should be restored at the earliest.

Elections occurred and an elected government took office. This fulfilled the electoral component, not statehood restoration.

That is a sharper federalism point than simply noting that statehood is pending.

### 6. Federal criticism and response

**Criticism:** Converting a State into UTs reduces federal autonomy and creates a precedent for unilateral downgrading.

**Union rationale:** Security, cross-border terrorism, administrative integration and Ladakh’s long-standing separate-UT demand justified reorganisation.

**Balanced residual:** Territorial reorganisation power must be distinguished from democratic legitimacy. Elections restored representative government, but continuing UT status leaves Parliament and the LG with authority exceeding that of a State framework.

To understand the two present UT structures, it is enough here to distinguish them from:

- Article 371 special provisions;
- full Articles 1–4 doctrine; or
- a complete statehood litigation narrative.

Those questions remain relevant, but they do not alter the immediate distinction between J&K’s statutory legislature and Ladakh’s direct-administration model.

### 1. The 114–24–90 logic

The statutory Assembly figure was increased through delimitation to 114 territorial constituencies. Twenty-four seats remain vacant for areas under Pakistani occupation. Ninety seats were therefore directly contested in the 2024 election.

The empty 24 are not vacancies in the ordinary electoral sense and are excluded from specified calculations under the statute.

### 2. Legislative competence

Section 32 empowers the Assembly to legislate on:

- State List matters except public order and police; and
- Concurrent List matters applicable to UTs.

Unlike Delhi, **land is not generally excluded**. Parliament may legislate for J&K on every subject, and parliamentary enactments prevail under the UT framework.

| Field | Delhi | J&K |
|---|---:|---:|
| Public order | Excluded | Excluded |
| Police | Excluded | Excluded |
| Land | Excluded | Not generally excluded |

### 3. Functions of the Assembly

The Assembly:

- makes laws within its field;
- authorises taxation and expenditure where law permits;
- considers the annual financial statement and demands for grants;
- holds the ministry collectively accountable;
- debates administration and public policy;
- passes resolutions and conducts committee scrutiny; and
- participates in Rajya Sabha elections according to law.

It cannot command reserved police/public-order administration merely through a resolution.

### 4. Council of Ministers

Section 53(1) provides a Council of Ministers not exceeding ten per cent of the Assembly’s total strength, with the Chief Minister at the head. The CM is appointed by the LG; other ministers are appointed by the LG on CM advice. The ministry is collectively responsible to the Assembly.

⚠️ **Compare the three ministry-size rules now, while they are side by side.** Delhi’s 10% cap is in **Article 239AA(4)** — constitutional. J&K’s 10% cap is in **Section 53(1)** of the 2019 Act — statutory. Puducherry has **no numerical ceiling** in Sections 44–45 of the 1963 Act at all. Writing “all UTs with legislatures have a 10% ministry cap” is wrong on a third of the examples, and wrong about the source in another.

### 5. Nomination routes

The amended Act permits:

- nomination of **two women** under Section 15 if, in the LG’s opinion, women are not adequately represented;
- nomination of **not more than two Kashmiri migrants** under Section 15A, **one of whom shall be a woman**; and
- nomination of **one displaced person from Pakistan-occupied Jammu and Kashmir under Section 15B**, a distinct provision inserted by the 2023 amendment with its own definition of “displaced person” covering those displaced from the occupied area during 1947–48, 1965 and 1971, and their successors-in-interest.

These nominations are additional to the 90 directly elected seats; they do not convert the 24 statutorily kept-vacant seats into filled seats.

### 6. Women’s reservation

The J&K Reorganisation (Second Amendment) Act, 2023 inserted **Sections 14A and 14B** into the 2019 Act, creating constituency-reservation provisions aligned with the national framework: Section 14A reserves, as nearly as may be, one-third of the directly elected seats for women, including one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and Section 14B supplies the timing, duration and rotation. Its own commencement is separate from commencement of the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act:

| Legal instrument | Commencement | Provision imposing the condition | Nature of the condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 — Delhi | S.O. 1922(E), 16 April 2026 | **Article 334A** | Constitutional |
| J&K Reorganisation (Second Amendment) Act, 2023 — J&K | S.O. 1929(E), 17 April 2026 | **Section 14B** of the 2019 Act | Statutory |
| Government of Union Territories (Amendment) Act, 2023 — Puducherry | S.O. 1930(E), 17 April 2026 | **Section 3B** of the 1963 Act | Statutory |

Statutory commencement therefore does not mean that one-third of J&K Assembly constituencies were already reserved on 17 April 2026. Section 15 nomination of women and constituency reservation under Section 14A are also separate mechanisms: one is an LG nomination of up to two members, the other is a reservation of elected constituencies.

⚠️ **Three instruments, two kinds of condition.** Delhi’s reservation rides on a constitutional amendment and is held back by a constitutional provision. J&K’s and Puducherry’s ride on ordinary statutes and are held back by provisions in those same statutes. Each Assembly’s reservation instrument matches the source of that Assembly, and so does the brake. None of the three operated on 1 October 2026.

⚠️ **Why the distinction has practical bite.** Because J&K’s and Puducherry’s conditions are statutory, Parliament could in principle relax them by ordinary legislation; Delhi’s could not be relaxed without touching the Constitution. The April 2026 package taught in Lesson 3 attempted exactly that combination — a constitutional amendment plus ordinary UT legislation — and failed at the constitutional step, which is why none of the three positions changed.

### 7. LG discretion: the statutory baseline before any amendment

Most answers jump straight to the 2024 notification and therefore cannot say what it changed. Build the baseline first.

✅ **Fact — Section 53(1):** there shall be a Council of Ministers, not exceeding ten per cent of the total number of members in the Assembly, with the Chief Minister at the head, to aid and advise the Lieutenant Governor in the exercise of his functions in relation to matters **with respect to which the Legislative Assembly has power to make laws**, “except in so far as he is required by or under this Act to act in his discretion or by or under any law to exercise any judicial or quasi-judicial functions.”

That single sentence already contains the whole limit, and it repays slow reading. The duty to accept advice attaches only to the Assembly's own field. Outside it, there is nothing for the ministry to advise upon as of right. And note where the words **“quasi-judicial”** sit: they are in sub-section (1), as part of the exception carved out of the aid-and-advise duty. Keep them there.

✅ **Fact — Section 53(2):** the Lieutenant Governor “shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in his discretion in a matter —

> (i) which **falls outside the purview of the powers conferred on the Legislative Assembly**; or
> (ii) in which he is required by or under any law to act in his discretion **or to exercise any judicial functions**; or
> (iii) **related to All India Services and Anti Corruption Bureau**.”

⚠️ **Two over-corrections to resist, because both are common and both are wrong.**

| Tempting addition | What the text actually says |
|---|---|
| “Limb (i) applies only where the President has entrusted or delegated the matter to the LG” | There is no entrustment condition in limb (i). It is satisfied by the matter falling outside the Assembly's purview, full stop |
| “Limb (ii) covers judicial *and quasi-judicial* functions” | Limb (ii) says **judicial functions** only. “Quasi-judicial” appears in sub-section (1), not in (2)(ii) |

Why does the difference matter? Because each addition quietly enlarges or shrinks the discretion. Importing an entrustment requirement into limb (i) would make the LG's discretion over reserved subjects conditional on a separate presidential act that the statute never demanded. Importing “quasi-judicial” into limb (ii) would widen the discretionary head beyond the words Parliament chose. In an answer that turns on precision, quoting the limb is safer than paraphrasing it.

✅ **Fact — the proviso to Section 53(2):** if any question arises whether a matter is one as respects which the LG is required to act in his discretion, “the decision of the Lieutenant Governor in his discretion shall be final, and the validity of anything done by the Lieutenant Governor shall not be called in question on the ground that he ought or ought not to have acted in his discretion.”

✅ **Fact — Section 53(3):** “The question whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the Lieutenant Governor shall not be inquired into in any court.”

⚠️ **There is no Section 53(4).** The finality clause is a **proviso to sub-section (2)**, not a separate sub-section, and the non-justiciability of advice is sub-section (3). Citing a “Section 53(4)” is a quick way to lose the examiner's confidence in everything else on the page.

✅ **Fact — Section 54(2):** “The Ministers shall hold office during the pleasure of the Lieutenant Governor.” Section 54(1) has the LG appoint the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister's advice, the other Ministers; Section 54(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly.

⚠️ **Hold those two together.** Pleasure of the LG *and* collective responsibility to the Assembly sit in consecutive sub-sections, exactly as pleasure of the Governor and collective responsibility sit together in Article 164 for a State. The pleasure doctrine is a formal tenure rule; the political reality is supplied by the responsibility clause. Compare Delhi, where Article 239AA(5) places Ministers at the pleasure of the **President** rather than the Lieutenant Governor — a small wording difference that places the formal power one further step up the Union chain.


⚠️ **Inference:** Notice that limb (iii) is the interesting one. Limbs (i) and (ii) merely restate general principles — you cannot be bound by advice on something the advisers have no competence over, and an express statutory discretion must be honoured. Limb (iii) is different: All India Services and the Anti Corruption Bureau are carved out **by name**, even though a UT ministry would otherwise expect a say in how its own officers are deployed and how corruption in its own administration is investigated. That named carve-out, not the general structure, is what distinguishes J&K from an ordinary responsible government.

✅ **Fact — Section 55(1):** “The Lieutenant Governor **shall make rules on the advice of the Council of Ministers** —

> (a) for the **allocation of business to the Ministers**; and
> (b) for the **more convenient transaction of business with the Ministers**, including the procedure to be adopted in case of a **difference of opinion between the Lieutenant Governor and the Council of Ministers or a Minister**.”

Three features of that sub-section decide most examination answers about J&K.

First, the verb is **“shall make,”** not “may make.” Rule-making here is a duty.

Second, the rules are made **on the advice of the Council of Ministers**. Section 55 contains no carve-out for discretionary business; it does not say “in so far as it is not business in which he acts in his discretion.” The discretion lives in Section 53(2); the rule-making duty in Section 55 is not itself qualified by it.

Third — and this is the clause most answers miss — the **difference-of-opinion procedure is left to the rules themselves**, under clause (b). Section 53 contains **no mandatory referral of a disagreement to the President**. Compare Delhi, where the proviso to Article 239AA(4) places that referral in the Constitution and makes it obligatory in terms. J&K's equivalent safeguard is delegated to a rule-book that the LG must frame on ministerial advice.

⚠️ **Inference:** That contrast explains why the J&K rule-book carries so much political weight. In Delhi the escalation route is fixed above the parties' reach. In J&K the escalation route is written into rules — and whoever shapes those rules shapes how often, and on what, a disagreement travels upwards. The next section is about precisely such a rule change.

Section 55 is therefore the *ordinary* route, and it mirrors the ordinary route in a State: the formal head makes the rules, but the elected government supplies the advice on which they are made.

### 8. What G.S.R. 386(E) changed, and what it did not

With the baseline fixed, the amendment can be stated precisely. The applicable instrument is the **2019 Transaction of Business Rules as amended in 2024**. Use that full description every time; a bare “2024 Business Rules” hides the fact that the 2019 Rules remain the parent instrument and only specified rules were inserted into them.

✅ **Fact:** G.S.R. 386(E), dated 12 July 2024, amended those rules. It was made by the **President**, in exercise of the powers conferred by **Section 55** of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 **read with the Proclamation dated 31 October 2019 issued under Section 73** of that Act.

⚠️ **That attribution is the examinable point.** Section 55 ordinarily operates through the LG acting on ministerial advice. But while the Section 73 proclamation was in operation, the President was exercising the functions of the territorial government. So the 2024 amendment reached the rule-book through a route that **bypassed the ordinary advice requirement** — not because the LG claimed new authority, but because the proclamation had displaced the ordinary machinery.

```text
ORDINARY ROUTE (s.55)        ROUTE ACTUALLY USED IN JULY 2024
LG makes the rules           President makes the rules
on the advice of the         under s.55 read with the

          ↓                                ↓

elected government's view    territorial machinery was displaced
```

⚠️ **Do not over-read this.** The notification is not an order of the Lieutenant Governor, and it is not an independent assertion of LG authority. The LG is the officer *through whom* the specified files must travel; the rule itself came from above him. Equally, it did not abolish Section 53(1) — the aid-and-advise duty survives for everything the amendment does not touch.

The amendment is exact and limited. It requires:

1. proposals requiring prior Finance Department concurrence concerning **Police, Public Order, All India Service and Anti-Corruption Bureau**, where the LG is to exercise discretion under the Act, to be placed before the LG through the Chief Secretary before concurrence or rejection;
2. proposals for appointment of the **Advocate-General and other law officers assisting the Advocate-General** to go to the LG through the Chief Secretary and Chief Minister;
3. proposals granting or refusing **prosecution sanction**, or concerning **filing of an appeal**, to go to the LG through the Chief Secretary;
4. matters connected with **Prisons, the Directorate of Prosecution and the Forensic Science Laboratory** to go from the Administrative Secretary, Home Department, through the Chief Secretary to the LG; and
5. proposals concerning posting and transfer of **Administrative Secretaries and cadre posts of All India Services officers** to go from the Administrative Secretary, General Administration Department, through the Chief Secretary to the LG.

The notification does not create a general LG channel for every “senior posting,” every financial proposal or the ministry’s entire administrative field. Its specified categories must be stated rather than replaced by broad labels.

⚠️ **Map the five categories back onto Section 53(2), and be honest about how far the map actually goes.**

| Rule group | Does it track a discretion head named in the Act? |
|---|---|
| 1. Finance concurrence for Police, Public Order, All India Service, ACB | **Yes.** Police and Public Order are expressly excluded from the Assembly's field by Section 32(1), so they fall in limb (i); All India Services and the Anti Corruption Bureau are named in limb (iii) |
| 5. Administrative Secretaries and All India Services cadre postings | **Yes, in part.** The All India Services component is named in limb (iii) |
| 2. Advocate-General and assisting law officers | **Not a named discretion.** The LG's appointing power sits in **Section 79(1)**, and the Advocate-General holds office during the LG's pleasure under Section 79(4). Section 53(2) does not name it |
| 3. Prosecution sanction and filing of appeals | **Not a named discretion.** The connection to limb (ii) or to the reserved security field has to be argued, not read off the text |
| 4. Prisons, Directorate of Prosecution, Forensic Science Laboratory | **Not a named discretion, and the opposite is arguable.** “Prisons, reformatories, Borstal institutions…” is **Entry 4 of the State List**, which Section 32(1) places squarely *inside* the Assembly's field |

⚠️ **Analytical inference — two readings are defensible, and a careful answer states both.**

**Reading A (procedural).** The amendment builds a channel, not a new power. Two of the five groups plainly concern matters already outside ministerial control, and the remaining three concern the prosecution-and-custody chain, which is functionally entangled with the reserved security field even where the subject-heading is not reserved. On this reading the honest description is “it changed the route, within or close to an existing carve-out.”

**Reading B (substantive).** The amendment reaches further than Section 53(2)'s named heads. Prisons is a State List subject inside the Assembly's competence; the appointment of law officers is a Section 79 function, not a Section 53(2) discretion; and routing prosecution sanctions through the LG touches decisions an elected Home Minister would ordinarily own. On this reading the rules do more than redirect paperwork — they relocate practical influence over matters the statute left with the elected government.

State the five categories exactly, identify which track named statutory discretion and which do not, and label the evaluation as your inference.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Prompt:** Evaluate the balance between representative government and Lieutenant-Governor control in the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir.

#### Model answer

J&K has meaningful representative institutions. Its 90 directly elected seats form an Assembly that may legislate on applicable State and Concurrent List subjects except public order and police; unlike Delhi, land is not generally excluded. It authorises expenditure, debates policy and holds a Council of Ministers collectively responsible. The ministry is capped at 10% of Assembly strength.

However, this is a statutory UT legislature under the 2019 Act, not a State legislature. Parliament retains competence over every subject. Section 53(2) gives the LG discretion over matters outside the Assembly’s competence, matters where law requires discretion, and All India Services and the Anti-Corruption Bureau. G.S.R. 386(E) of 12 July 2024, made by the President under Section 55 read with the Section 73 Proclamation, then channelled exact categories through the LG: specified Finance-concurrence proposals concerning police/public order/AIS/ACB; law-officer appointments; prosecution sanction or appeals; prisons, prosecution and FSL matters; and postings or transfers of Administrative Secretaries and AIS cadre posts.

The Union can justify these safeguards through security and transitional needs. Yet excessive expansion into transferred subjects would weaken collective responsibility by separating ministers from administrative control. The sound balance is subject-specific: firm Union authority in expressly reserved fields, transparent and reasoned discretion, and ordinary ministerial primacy where the Assembly is competent. Statehood restoration remains the broader unresolved federal question.


The five non-legislature UTs have administration and representation, but no Assembly capable of sustaining a territorial Council of Ministers.

### 1. The five territories

- Andaman and Nicobar Islands;
- Chandigarh;
- Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu;
- Ladakh; and
- Lakshadweep.

They differ radically in geography and history. Their shared feature is the absence of a territorial legislature and responsible ministry.

### 2. How law is made

Parliament may legislate under Article 246(4). Delegated legislation and executive rules operate under parliamentary enactments. Article 240 additionally permits presidential regulations for A&N, Lakshadweep and DNHDD, which it names directly. It does not reach Chandigarh at all. Ladakh reaches the same Article 240 power through the statutory bridge in **Section 58(2)** of the 2019 Act, which expressly says the President may make regulations for Ladakh “under article 240”; **Section 58(3)** then gives such a regulation the force of an Act of Parliament.

| UT | Principal special route |
|---|---|
| A&N | Article 240, named directly in the text |
| Lakshadweep | Article 240, named directly in the text |
| DNHDD | Article 240, named directly in the text |
| Chandigarh | Parliamentary law and delegated administration; no Article 240 route at all |
| Ladakh | Article 240 **read with** Section 58(2) of the 2019 Act; not named in Article 240's list |

### 3. Ladakh’s institutional design

Ladakh has no Legislative Assembly. Its LG administers within the presidential/Union framework.

#### Districts: the figure changed in 2026

Most textbooks still say Ladakh has two districts. On the cut-off date that is out of date.

✅ **Fact:** On **27 April 2026** the Lieutenant Governor of Ladakh approved the issuance of a notification creating **five new districts** — **Sham, Nubra, Changthang, Zanskar and Drass** — taking the Union territory from two districts to **seven**.

✅ **Fact:** Nubra (headquarters Diskit), Sham (Khaltse) and Changthang (Nyoma) were carved out of **Leh**; Zanskar (Padum) and Drass (Drass-Ranbirpura) were carved out of **Kargil**. The notification also defined the territorial limits of all seven, distributing the revenue villages between them.

✅ **Fact:** The Ministry of Home Affairs had approved the formation of five new districts in **August 2024** and directed the UT administration to constitute a committee to examine the question; the committee consulted delegations, conducted field visits and submitted a report before the 2026 notification issued.

| Parent district | New districts carved out | Headquarters |
|---|---|---|
| Leh | Nubra · Sham · Changthang | Diskit · Khaltse · Nyoma |
| Kargil | Zanskar · Drass | Padum · Drass-Ranbirpura |

```text
       BEFORE 27 Apr 2026              AFTER 27 Apr 2026
            Leh                   Leh · Nubra · Sham · Changthang
            Kargil                Kargil · Zanskar · Drass
         = 2 districts                  = 7 districts
```

⚠️ **Inference — why a district is not an institution of self-government.** It is tempting to read this as Ladakh gaining political autonomy. It did not. A district is a unit of **administrative deconcentration**: it brings the Deputy Commissioner, the revenue machinery and service delivery closer to remote populations across a territory where travel distances can exceed 300 km. It creates no legislature, no ministry and no new elected body. Ladakh is exactly as far from a territorial Assembly on 28 April 2026 as it was on 26 April.

⚠️ **But do not dismiss it either.** The reform concedes the premise of Ladakh’s long-standing grievance — that remoteness makes centralised administration inadequate — while answering it with administrative rather than constitutional means. That is a genuine and examinable pattern in UT governance: **the Union’s preferred response to a demand for self-government is improved administration.** Lesson 11’s democratic-deficit debate below is precisely about whether that substitution is adequate.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** More districts means administration closer to the citizen; it does not mean government by the citizen. Use the 2026 reorganisation as evidence of responsiveness *and* as evidence of the limits of an administrative answer to a representative demand.

#### Hill councils

The Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Councils at Leh and Kargil are statutory regional institutions. They support planning and local participation in specified subjects. They are not:

- territorial legislatures;
- Councils of Ministers;
- constitutional Sixth Schedule autonomous district councils; or
- substitutes for Parliament’s lawmaking authority.

⚠️ **Note the mismatch the 2026 reorganisation creates.** The two hill councils are constituted for Leh and Kargil. Creating five further districts inside those two areas does not automatically multiply the councils; it multiplies the administrative units beneath them. The elected layer and the administrative layer have moved out of step, which is exactly the kind of detail a thoughtful Mains answer can use.

Demands for Statehood or Sixth Schedule protection are current political context. The detailed Sixth Schedule doctrine belongs to the scheduled-and-tribal-areas topic.

### 4. High Court bench regulation

The J&K Reorganisation Act continued a common High Court for J&K and Ladakh. Regulation No. 10 of 2026, published on 27 August 2026, creates a legal framework for sittings of a bench in Ladakh and authorises the Chief Justice to make relevant sitting arrangements according to its terms.

Ladakh notification **S.O. 262** appointed **5 September 2026** as the date on which the Regulation came into force. The official Ladakh Administration announcement carefully described commencement as an institutional step **towards operationalising** the bench and identified further consultation plus judicial, administrative, infrastructural, financial and logistical arrangements. Therefore:

```text
27 Aug 2026: Regulation promulgated and gazetted
                 ↓
5 Sep 2026: legal commencement under S.O. 262
                 ↓
later administrative and High Court arrangements
                 ↓
actual sittings/regular bench operations
```

Commencement is verified; a claim that regular sittings had already begun requires separate official operational evidence.

### 5. Advisory and local participation

Home Minister’s Advisory Committees or Administrator’s Advisory Committees may bring together MPs, local representatives and officials to discuss social and economic development. Municipalities, panchayats and specialised bodies also operate.

These institutions improve consultation but differ from an Assembly:

| Assembly | Advisory/local institution |
|---|---|
| Enacts territorial laws within a defined field | Exercises statutory local powers or advises |
| Sustains collectively responsible ministry | No territorial ministry responsible to it |
| Votes territorial budget/appropriation | Receives grants or manages assigned local funds |
| Can question ministers | Primarily administrative/local accountability |

### 6. Democratic-deficit debate

**Critique:** Parliament is geographically remote, and local bodies cannot scrutinise a territorial executive as an Assembly would.

**Response:** Small scale, strategic exposure or capital functions may favour direct Union responsibility; local institutions can provide participation without duplicating a full State apparatus.

**Residual:** The appropriate institutional depth should respond to population, distance, identity and administrative capacity. Ladakh’s demands show that direct administration may require stronger consultative guarantees even where a legislature is not created.

### Concept check

**Question:** Ladakh went from two districts to seven in April 2026. Does that reduce its democratic deficit?

**Model answer:** Only marginally, and not in the sense the question implies. Creating districts deconcentrates administration — it shortens the distance between citizens and the revenue and service machinery across very large areas. It creates no legislature, no Council of Ministers responsible to an elected body, and no new representative institution. The democratic deficit is about the absence of territorial responsible government, which a district reorganisation cannot supply.

**Misconception to avoid:** Administrative decentralisation and democratic devolution are different reforms; improving the first does not substitute for the second.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Prompt:** Explain how democratic participation is pursued in Union Territories without legislatures.

#### Model answer

Non-legislature UTs remain under presidential administration through an Administrator or LG, with Parliament exercising plenary lawmaking and budget control. Participation nevertheless occurs through Lok Sabha representation, municipalities, panchayats, advisory committees and territory-specific institutions. Ladakh’s elected hill development councils at Leh and Kargil, for example, support regional planning and local administration, though they are neither territorial legislatures nor Sixth Schedule councils. Home Minister’s or Administrator’s advisory committees may include MPs and local representatives. Courts and CAG scrutiny provide legal and financial accountability. These mechanisms reduce administrative distance but do not create responsible territorial government: no Assembly legislates across the UT, votes a territorial ministry into office or holds it collectively accountable. The model therefore supplies consultation and local self-government while retaining direct Union political responsibility.

**Chandigarh:** Union Territory and joint capital of Punjab and Haryana. Its Administrator is separately accountable in the UT capacity. It lacks Article 240 regulation power.

**DNHDD:** The 2020 merger reduced duplication while retaining two Lok Sabha allocations because parliamentary representation is statutorily allocated.

**Delhi:** Constitutional Assembly model with reserved national-capital subjects.

**J&K:** Statutory legislature, reserved police/public order, functioning elected ministry, an Assembly resolution of 28 September 2026 demanding statehood, and restoration still pending.

**Ladakh:** Direct administration, hill councils and a statutory regulation power under Section 58(2). Reorganised from two districts to seven on 27 April 2026 — the clearest recent example of the Union answering a demand for self-government with improved administration rather than representation.

**Lakshadweep:** Small island UT under an Administrator and direct Article 240 regulation power.

Allocation is a legal seat entitlement; it is not a claim that every allocated seat is occupied at every moment.

### 1. Lok Sabha representation

Every UT has at least one Lok Sabha allocation. Delhi has seven, J&K five and DNHDD two; each other UT has one. The aggregate is nineteen.

#### The constitutional ceiling behind that nineteen

The figure is not free-floating. It sits beneath an express constitutional cap.

✅ **Fact — Article 81(1)(b):** the House of the People shall consist of “not more than twenty members to represent the Union territories, chosen in such manner as Parliament may by law provide.”

```text
Article 81(1)(a)   ≤ 530 members from the States
Article 81(1)(b)   ≤  20 members to represent the UTs
                           ↑
        present UT allocation: 19   →   one seat of headroom
```

⚠️ **Inference — this is why the manner clause matters.** Article 81(1)(a) fixes State representation by direct election from territorial constituencies. Clause (b) does not: it leaves the *manner* of choosing UT members to Parliament by law. Historically that permitted methods other than ordinary direct election for very small territories. Today the allocations are filled by direct election under the Representation of the People Acts — but the constitutional flexibility remains, and it is a clean illustration of the topic's central theme. Even in Parliament's own composition, UT representation is something Parliament legislates about, while State representation is something the Constitution settles.

⚠️ **The ceiling is not merely tight — it is one seat from exhausted.** With nineteen allocated against a cap of twenty, a single further UT Lok Sabha seat would **exhaust** Article 81(1)(b) outright. There is no room for a second. Raising the ceiling would require amending Article 81 itself — which is exactly what the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 proposed, seeking not more than 815 members from the States and **not more than 35 to represent the Union territories**, and failed to carry, as Lessons 3 and 4 recorded.

⚠️ **Inference:** That arithmetic quietly constrains territorial policy. Any proposal to reorganise an existing UT into two, or to create a new UT with its own parliamentary seat, now runs into a constitutional ceiling rather than an administrative preference — and the April 2026 vote showed that the ceiling cannot be lifted casually. The 2019 reorganisation worked only because the five J&K seats and one Ladakh seat replaced the six seats of the former State, leaving the UT total within the cap.

Lok Sabha membership provides:

- voice in parliamentary legislation applicable to the UT;
- scrutiny of Union ministries administering UT affairs;
- participation in national budget voting; and
- constituency representation.

Yet a single MP cannot reproduce the continuous lawmaking and executive-accountability functions of a territorial Assembly.

The allocation source must be distinguished from candidate qualification:

- the **First Schedule to the Representation of the People Act, 1950**, as amended, supplies the Lok Sabha allocations;
- Sections 10 and 11 and the Schedules of the **J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019** supply and carry through the J&K–Ladakh allocation changes; and
- delimitation law determines the territorial constituencies within those allocations.

### 2. Rajya Sabha representation

Only Delhi, J&K and Puducherry have Rajya Sabha allocations:

- Delhi — 3;
- J&K — 4;
- Puducherry — 1.

Their elected Assembly members form the relevant electoral college according to law. Non-legislature UTs lack the territorial Assembly necessary for this route and have no Rajya Sabha seats.

The eight figure is allocation, not guaranteed present occupancy. Elections, vacancies or institutional transitions may temporarily affect sitting membership.

The allocation comes from the **Fourth Schedule to the Constitution**, read with Article 80 and amendments made by territorial-reorganisation law. Election from Delhi, J&K and Puducherry proceeds under the Representation of the People Act, 1951 and the applicable Assembly electoral-college provisions.

### 3. Who is qualified to enter either House?

Article 84 supplies the constitutional floor for every parliamentary candidate:

1. Indian citizenship and the prescribed oath or affirmation;
2. minimum age of **30 years for the Council of States** and **25 years for the House of the People**; and
3. other qualifications prescribed by Parliament.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 then supplies the principal statutory rules:

| House | Statutory rule | UT consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Rajya Sabha | Section 3: the candidate must be an elector for a parliamentary constituency in India | A candidate for a Delhi, J&K or Puducherry seat need not be enrolled only in that same UT |
| Lok Sabha, ordinary seat | Section 4(d): elector for any parliamentary constituency | Applies to ordinary UT seats |
| Lok Sabha, Lakshadweep ST-reserved seat | Section 4(cc): member of a Scheduled Tribe of Lakshadweep and elector for the Lakshadweep parliamentary constituency | This is a specific territorial qualification, not the general ordinary-seat rule |
| Other reserved seats | Section 4’s relevant SC/ST clauses | Category qualification and elector status must both be satisfied |

Allocation answers **how many seats the territory has**. Article 84 and RPA 1951 answer **who may be chosen**. Vacancy answers **whether an allocated seat is presently occupied**. These are three different questions.

### 4. Article 241

Article 241 authorises Parliament to:

- constitute a High Court for a UT; or
- declare an existing court to be the High Court for that territory.

Parliament may also extend or exclude a State High Court’s jurisdiction over a UT through law. Article 241 therefore permits judicial arrangements suited to geography and institutional scale without requiring every UT to maintain a separate High Court.

### 5. Complete High Court map

- Delhi has its own High Court under the **Delhi High Court Act, 1966**.
- J&K and Ladakh share the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh under **Section 75 of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019**.
- A&N falls under Calcutta High Court through the **Calcutta High Court (Extension of Jurisdiction) Act, 1953**.
- Chandigarh is served by the common Punjab and Haryana High Court under **Section 29 of the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966**.
- DNHDD falls under Bombay High Court; **Section 8 of the Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu (Merger of Union Territories) Act, 2019** continued/extended that jurisdiction for the merged UT.
- Lakshadweep falls under Kerala High Court under **Section 60 of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956**.
- Puducherry falls under Madras High Court under **Sections 9–10 of the Pondicherry (Administration) Act, 1962**.

The statement “only Delhi has its own High Court” means an exclusive UT High Court. It does not deny that J&K and Ladakh share a High Court bearing both names.

⚠️ **Get the name right — and notice that there is more than one correct name.** This is a point where a confident answer can go wrong by over-correcting.

Section 75(1)(a) of the 2019 Act originally spoke of a “Common High Court for the Union territory of Jammu and Kashmir and Union territory of Ladakh.” That cumbersome phrase was substituted by **S.O. 2860(E), dated 16 July 2021**, with the words **“High Court of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh”** — no comma.

Four forms are in lawful use, and each is correct in its own place:

| Where it appears | Exact form | Punctuation |
|---|---|---|
| Words substituted into Section 75(1)(a) by S.O. 2860(E), 2021 | High Court of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh | **No comma** |
| Section 1(1) short title of Regulation No. 10 of 2026 (Lesson 7) | Union territory of Ladakh (Sitting of Bench of the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir**,** and Ladakh in Ladakh) Regulation, 2026 | **Comma**; lower-case “Union territory” |
| Sections 2(1)–2(3) of that Regulation, referring to the Court itself | the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir**,** and Ladakh | **Comma** |
| The Court’s own ordinary usage | High Court of Jammu **&** Kashmir and Ladakh | Ampersand, no comma |

❌ **Do not adopt a universal rule in either direction.** There is no single house style here. It is as wrong to say “the title never has a comma” as to say “the title always has one”: the 2021 statutory substitution has none, the 2026 Regulation uses one throughout its own text, and the Court’s masthead uses an ampersand instead. The discipline is to reproduce the form used by whichever instrument you are citing, not to impose one of them on the others.

❌ Equally, do not call it a “Ladakh High Court.” There is one common High Court bearing both names; Lesson 7’s Regulation enables a **bench** of that Court to sit in Ladakh, and the principal seat is unchanged.

### 6. Ladakh bench framework

Regulation No. 10 of 2026 provides the legal mechanism for a bench of the common High Court to sit in Ladakh. Under Section 2(2), the “Judges and **division courts**” of the Court may also sit at such place in the Union territory of Ladakh as the **Chief Justice may, with the approval of the Lieutenant Governor of Ladakh, appoint**; Section 2(1) leaves the principal seat unchanged; and Section 2(3) permits the Chief Justice, in his discretion, to order that any case or class of cases arising in Ladakh be heard at Srinagar or Jammu. Ladakh notification S.O. 262 brought the Regulation into force on **5 September 2026**.

⚠️ **Borrow the Regulation’s noun.** It writes **“division courts,”** not “division benches.” The expressions are interchangeable in practice, but in a question about an instrument’s exact terms, the instrument’s own word is the safer one.

The official announcement stated that commencement would facilitate further consultation and judicial, administrative, infrastructural, financial and logistical action. Legal commencement therefore did not itself certify that regular sittings had begun. It improved the legal possibility of territorial access without creating a separate Ladakh High Court.

### 7. Access-to-justice debate

**Defence of shared jurisdiction:** It avoids duplicating a complete High Court establishment for small territories and uses existing judicial capacity.

**Critique:** Distance, island geography and travel costs may weaken practical access.

**Reply:** Circuit sittings, e-filing, video hearings and benches can reduce distance.

**Residual:** Digital access cannot wholly replace physical access for evidence, counsel, language support and vulnerable litigants. Administrative efficiency and territorial accessibility must be balanced.

- Distinguish exclusive, common and extended High Court jurisdiction.
- Quote the Court’s name in the form used by the instrument being cited; do not apply one punctuation rule everywhere.

- Link Article 241 to access to justice, not only factual matching.

### 1. Legislature-UT funds

Parliamentary statutes establish territorial financial architecture for Delhi, Puducherry and J&K, including:

- a Consolidated Fund;
- a Public Account;
- a Contingency Fund;
- annual financial statements;
- demands for grants;
- appropriation laws; and
- supplementary, additional or excess grants.

Money received as territorial revenues, Union grants or loans enters the legally specified fund. No expenditure may be drawn from the Consolidated Fund except under lawful appropriation, subject to charged expenditure rules.

### 2. Delhi

The GNCTD Act structures Delhi’s Consolidated Fund, Contingency Fund and Public Account, together with Assembly financial procedure. The LG causes the annual financial statement to be laid before the Assembly, and appropriation authorises withdrawal.

⚠️ **Name the source of the recommendation requirement correctly.** A demand for a grant cannot be made except on the recommendation of the Lieutenant Governor — but that requirement comes from the **Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi Act, 1991**, not from the Constitution. Article 113 imposes the parallel rule for the Union and Article 203 for a State; neither applies to Delhi of its own force. Writing that Delhi's grants “require the constitutionally prescribed recommendation” imports a provision that is not there.

⚠️ **Inference — the same lesson in a new place.** The financial-procedure architecture of Delhi *looks* constitutional because it mirrors Articles 112 to 117 almost clause for clause. It is in fact statutory, which means Parliament may reshape it by ordinary amendment. That is the fourth distinct context in which this topic's central distinction has appeared: institutions, seat reservation, breakdown provisions, and now money. The resemblance is deliberate; the entrenchment is not.

Because Parliament can legislate for Delhi and Union grants remain important, Assembly budget control is real but situated within the NCT’s wider Union relationship.

### 3. Puducherry

The 1963 Act provides equivalent core institutions for Puducherry. The elected ministry prepares and defends the budget; the Assembly debates grants and passes appropriation. Presidential/Administrator roles and parliamentary support remain stronger than in an ordinary State framework.

### 4. J&K

The 2019 Act provides a Consolidated Fund, Public Account and Contingency Fund for J&K and establishes budget and appropriation procedure. The functioning Assembly can therefore scrutinise the ministry’s fiscal priorities. Parliament may still legislate and provide grants, and reserved-domain expenditure remains connected with the UT’s special executive division.

### 5. Non-legislature UTs

Their expenditures are authorised through Parliament and the Consolidated Fund of India within the Union budget framework, usually under the responsible Union ministry’s Demands for Grants. Administrators and UT departments execute approved programmes.

Local authorities may possess assigned revenues, grants and statutory funds, but these do not create a territorial Consolidated Fund controlled by an Assembly.

| Demands for grants | Permits policy-wise legislative approval |
| Appropriation | Gives legal authority to withdraw funds |
| Supplementary/excess grants | Controls departures from original approval |
| CAG audit | Tests legality, regularity and performance |
| Legislative committees | Examine accounts and audit findings |

### 7. Finance Commission connection

Article 280’s State-devolution doctrine is wider than this lesson. The necessary connection here is narrower: UT finances rely on statutory funds, parliamentary grants and Union support, and a UT Assembly does not automatically become a State for every tax-devolution rule.

**Counterpoint:** Parliament represents UT residents and may authorise expenditure for territories without Assemblies.

**Residual:** Parliamentary scrutiny is national and episodic; territorial Assembly scrutiny is closer and continuous. Strong audit, transparent grants and empowered local bodies are especially important in non-legislature UTs.

**Functional case for asymmetry**

- national-capital security requires special reservation;
- strategic islands and frontiers may need close Union coordination;
- small territories may not require full State machinery;
- culturally distinct territories can receive tailored representation;
- transitional arrangements permit gradual institutional change.

**Democratic criticism**

- power and electoral accountability may be separated;
- Administrators can become sites of partisan conflict;
- centrally nominated members can affect local mandates;
- parliamentary scrutiny may be too distant;
- prolonged transitional status can weaken federal trust.

**Balanced principle:** Different treatment is defensible when it is connected to a legitimate territorial need, precisely defined, democratically moderated and judicially reviewable.

### 4. Dispute-resolution improvements

1. Define reserved and transferred fields precisely.
2. Require reasoned, time-bound use of referral and discretionary powers.
3. Publish transaction-of-business rules and decisions.
4. Strengthen intergovernmental consultation before litigation.
5. Use constitutional benches for recurring structural disputes.
6. Improve parliamentary committee scrutiny of non-legislature UTs.
7. Empower local and regional bodies without misdescribing them as legislatures.
8. Honour formally stated transitional commitments through transparent timelines.

### Concept check

**Question:** What makes a differentiated UT arrangement constitutionally defensible rather than merely unequal?

**Model answer:** It should respond to a legitimate territorial need, remain grounded in constitutional or statutory authority, preserve proportionate democratic accountability, define exceptional Union powers precisely and remain subject to legislative and judicial review.

**Misconception to avoid:** Asymmetry is neither automatically unjust nor automatically justified; its design and use require evaluation.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Prompt:** “India’s Union Territory framework is a laboratory of asymmetric governance, but also a test of democratic accountability.” Critically analyse.

#### Model answer

Part VIII establishes a common Union-dominant base: presidential administration under Article 239 and Parliament’s plenary competence under Article 246(4). Upon that base, India creates differentiated institutions. Delhi has a constitutional Assembly but reserves public order, police and land. Puducherry’s statutory Assembly has a broader applicable field and nominated voting members. J&K’s statutory Assembly excludes public order and police, while Ladakh and four other UTs lack territorial legislatures. Judicial, representation and regulation arrangements also vary.

This flexibility accommodates a national capital, strategic islands, former colonial enclaves, frontier regions and small territories without forcing uniform Statehood. Yet asymmetry can separate power from electoral responsibility. Delhi’s services conflict, Puducherry’s nominees, J&K’s enhanced LG role and non-legislature UTs’ limited territorial scrutiny illustrate the democratic cost.

The model is defensible when reserved powers are precise, Parliament provides transparent oversight, Administrators use discretion sparingly, courts protect the elected sphere and local institutions are meaningful. Transitional commitments, especially J&K Statehood, require credible implementation. Thus UT asymmetry is an adaptive constitutional instrument, but its legitimacy depends on matching Union control with accountable, reviewable and proportionate governance.

**Rubric (20):** common framework 3; differentiated models 5; functional case 3; democratic critique 4; reforms 3; balanced judgment 2.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Cumulative check 1 — category and source

**Question:** Arrange Delhi, Puducherry and J&K by the immediate source of their legislatures.

**Model answer:** Delhi—Article 239AA; Puducherry—Article 239A implemented by the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963; J&K—J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019, with Section 13 applying Article 239A.

## Cumulative check 2 — legislative fields

**Question:** Why is it wrong to transfer Delhi’s “public order, police and land” formula unchanged to J&K?

**Model answer:** J&K’s Section 32 excludes public order and police but not land generally. Delhi’s three exclusions arise from the distinct text of Article 239AA.

## Cumulative check 3 — exceptional powers

**Question:** Identify the correct route for a Parliament-equivalent presidential regulation in Ladakh.

**Model answer:** The power exercised is **Article 240** itself. Ladakh is not named in Article 240(1), but **Section 58(2)** of the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 provides that the President may make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of the Union territory of Ladakh **“under article 240.”** That is a statutory bridge into the constitutional power, not a separate power. **Section 58(3)** then gives such a regulation the force of an Act of Parliament and the ability to repeal or amend existing law for Ladakh. Regulation No. 10 of 2026 recites exactly this: “in exercise of the powers conferred by article 240 of the Constitution read with sub-section (2) of section 58.”

## Cumulative check 4 — democracy and administration

**Question:** What connects the 2018 Delhi judgment and the 2023 services judgment?

**Model answer:** Both protect responsible government within Delhi’s allotted field. The first makes aid and advice the ordinary rule; the second links control over implementing officers to ministerial, Assembly and electoral accountability.

## Cumulative check 5 — status discipline

**Question:** Name the three separate women’s-reservation instruments for Delhi, J&K and Puducherry, identify which provision holds each one back, and explain why none had placed a reserved seat in operation by 1 October 2026.

**Model answer:** Delhi’s reservation comes from the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023, inserting Article 239AA(2)(ba)–(bc) and commenced by S.O. 1922(E) on 16 April 2026; its condition is **Article 334A**, a constitutional provision. J&K’s comes from the J&K Reorganisation (Second Amendment) Act, 2023, inserting Sections 14A–14B and commenced by S.O. 1929(E) on 17 April 2026; its condition is **Section 14B**, a statutory provision. Puducherry’s comes from the Government of Union Territories (Amendment) Act, 2023 (Act 39 of 2023), inserting Sections 3A–3B and commenced by S.O. 1930(E) on 17 April 2026; its condition is **Section 3B**, also statutory. Each instrument matches the source of that Assembly, and so does the brake. All three require a delimitation based on the first census taken after their own commencement, run for fifteen years and rotate reserved seats after each delimitation — but **only Delhi’s condition is constitutionally prescribed**; the other two are conditions written into ordinary Acts. Commencement of an instrument is not operation of the reservation it creates. The April 2026 attempt to unlock this failed: the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 was defeated in the Lok Sabha on 17 April, 298–230, and the Delimitation Bill and the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 were withdrawn, so nothing was enacted and no position changed. The same commencement-versus-operation discipline applies elsewhere — Regulation No. 10 of 2026 commenced on 5 September under S.O. 262, while actual bench sittings required further operational arrangements.

## Cumulative check 6 — representation

**Question:** Explain the figures 19 and 8 without implying that every seat is presently occupied.

**Model answer:** They are the statutory allocations of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha seats to UTs respectively. Vacancies or election timing may make current occupancy different.

## Cumulative check 7 — finance

**Question:** What is the accountability difference between a legislature UT’s Consolidated Fund and expenditure on a non-legislature UT?

**Model answer:** The former is appropriated through a territorial Assembly that holds a ministry accountable; the latter is principally authorised through Parliament and the Consolidated Fund of India, then administered by Union/UT departments.

## Cumulative check 8 — constitutional evaluation

**Question:** State a one-sentence test for legitimate UT asymmetry.

**Model answer:** Differentiation is legitimate when tied to a genuine territorial need, grounded in law, proportionate, democratically moderated and reviewable.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question — 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the offices of a State Governor and a Union Territory Administrator.

**Model answer:** A State Governor is the constitutional head of a Part VI State, whereas a UT Administrator is the channel through whom the President administers a territory under Article 239. The Administrator is therefore described as the President’s agent, not as a head of State. Titles such as Lieutenant Governor or Administrator do not determine powers; the applicable Constitution, parliamentary statute and rules do. Article 239(2) highlights the distinction: when a State Governor is appointed Administrator of an adjoining UT, that person acts independently of the State Council of Ministers in the UT capacity. In legislature UTs, the Administrator’s relationship with ministers is territory-specific—Article 239AA for Delhi, the 1963 Act for Puducherry and the 2019 Act for J&K. Unlike a State’s normal federal field, Parliament may legislate on every subject for a UT under Article 246(4).

**Rubric (10):** constitutional location 2; agency/head distinction 2; source-specific powers 2; Article 239(2) 2; parliamentary consequence 2.

## Final 15-mark question — 250 words

**Question:** Compare the constitutional position of Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir as Union Territories with legislatures.

**Model answer:** All three combine elected government with Parliament’s plenary Article 246(4) power, but their sources and fields differ.

Delhi’s legislature is constitutionally entrenched in Article 239AA. Its 70-member Assembly may legislate on applicable State and Concurrent List matters except public order, police, land and related portions of Entries 64–66. Its seven-member maximum ministry is appointed through the President and collectively responsible to the Assembly. The 2018 judgment makes aid and advice the ordinary rule, while services are presently governed by Act 19 of 2023 under pending challenge.

Puducherry’s legislature is statutory under Article 239A and the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963. It has 30 elected and up to three centrally nominated voting members. Its applicable State List field lacks Delhi’s express three exclusions, though parliamentary law remains overriding.

J&K’s Assembly is statutory under the 2019 Reorganisation Act, with Section 13 applying Article 239A. Of 114 territorial seats, 24 are kept vacant statutorily by Section 14(4) and 90 are directly elected. Public order and police are excluded by Section 32(1), but land is not generally excluded. The LG retains enhanced responsibilities under Section 53(2) and under the 2019 Transaction of Business Rules as amended in 2024.

Thus all three provide real local democracy, but differ in entrenchment, composition, reserved fields and Union supervision.

**Rubric (15):** common base 2; Delhi 4; Puducherry 3; J&K 4; comparative conclusion 2.

## Final 20-mark question — 250 words

**Question:** A proposed parliamentary Bill would (i) exclude “land” from the J&K Assembly’s field, (ii) give the Puducherry Administrator final authority over every ministerial decision, and (iii) declare Ladakh to be textually included in Article 240. As constitutional adviser, assess each clause and suggest a legally accurate drafting route.

**Model answer:** Parliament has plenary competence for Union Territories under Article 246(4), but each clause must use the correct instrument.

First, J&K's exclusion of public order and police arises from Section 32 of the 2019 Act; land is not generally excluded. Parliament may narrow that statutory field by ordinary legislation, subject to review, but the Bill must expressly amend Section 32 rather than assume the exclusion exists.

Second, Puducherry's institutions arise under Article 239A and the 1963 Act. Parliament has wide design authority, yet blanket Administrator finality over every ministerial decision would sit uneasily with Section 44's Council collectively responsible to the Assembly. Enlarged discretion must identify defined subjects, procedure and safeguards instead of extinguishing responsible government by a general clause.

Third, the premise that ordinary legislation can never touch Article 240 is mistaken. Where Parliament legislates under Articles 2 or 3, Article 4 permits consequential provisions, and Article 4(2) declares such a law is not an Article 368 amendment. Section 4 of the Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu (Merger of Union Territories) Act, 2019 used that route to substitute entry (c) of Article 240(1). The line is consequential versus standalone: a reorganisation law may adjust the entry; a freestanding declaration needs Article 368. The clause is anyway unnecessary, since Section 58(2) already authorises regulations for Ladakh “under article 240.”

The redraft should amend Section 32, confine Puducherry discretion to stated matters with reasoned referral, and place any Article 240 change inside a genuine reorganisation law.

**Rubric (20):** identify three distinct defects 3; J&K amendment route 3; Puducherry responsible-government analysis 4; Article 4 consequential route with the DNHDD 2019 precedent 5; standalone Article 368 distinction 3; redrafting advice and conclusion 2.

# REMEDIATION

## Error family 1: wrong constitutional source

**Faulty recall:** Every legislature UT derives its Assembly from Article 239A.

**Repair:** Delhi derives its essential model from Article 239AA. Article 239A directly enables Puducherry’s statutory structure and applies to J&K through Section 13 of the 2019 Act.

## Error family 2: incorrect Article 240 list

**Faulty recall:** Article 240 covers every UT without a legislature, including Chandigarh and Ladakh; and because it is a constitutional provision, its list can only ever be altered by an Article 368 amendment.

**Repair:** Article 240 textually covers Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, and Puducherry only while its legislature is dissolved or its functioning is suspended. Chandigarh is outside it entirely. Ladakh is absent from the list but is not outside the power: Section 58(2) of the 2019 Act says the President may make regulations for Ladakh **“under article 240,”** and Section 58(3) supplies the Act-of-Parliament force. On the second limb: where Parliament legislates under Article 2 or 3, Article 4 permits supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions in the same ordinary law, and Article 4(2) provides that such a law is not an Article 368 amendment. Section 4 of the Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu (Merger of Union Territories) Act, 2019 used that route to substitute entry (c) and omit entry (d) of Article 240(1). A freestanding insertion unconnected to any reorganisation would still require Article 368 — which is why the reported November 2025 Chandigarh proposal did not become a Bill.

**Faulty recall:** Ladakh's regulation power is a free-standing statutory power in Section 58(2), separate from and parallel to Article 240.

**Repair:** Section 58(2) does not create a parallel power. Its own words direct that the regulations be made “under article 240 of the Constitution of India.” The correct formulation is Article 240 **read with** Section 58(2); the Act-of-Parliament effect comes from Section 58(3), not 58(2).

**Faulty recall:** Article 240 names Ladakh and every non-legislature UT.

**Repair:** Its current text names A&N, Lakshadweep, DNHDD and conditionally Puducherry. Ladakh reaches the power through Section 58(2); Chandigarh has no Article 240 route.

## Error family 3: Delhi field compressed

**Faulty recall:** Delhi excludes only police.

**Repair:** Entries 1, 2 and 18—public order, police and land—plus Entries 64–66 insofar as related.

## Error family 4: services chronology collapsed

**Faulty recall:** The 2023 Court judgment alone determines current Delhi services control.

**Repair:** Add Ordinance, Act 19 of 2023, NCCSA, LG finality after reconsideration and pending W.P.(C) 678/2023.

## Error family 5: J&K copied from Delhi

**Faulty recall:** J&K cannot legislate on land.

**Repair:** Section 32 excludes public order and police; land is not generally excluded.

## Error family 6: seat figures merged

**Faulty recall:** J&K has 114 elected members.

**Repair:** 114 territorial seats minus 24 kept vacant produces 90 directly elected seats. Section 15 permits two women nominees if inadequately represented; Section 15A permits not more than two Kashmiri migrant nominees, one of whom shall be a woman; Section 15B separately permits one displaced person from PoJK.

## Error family 7: LAHDC status inflated

**Faulty recall:** Ladakh’s hill councils are Sixth Schedule legislatures.

**Repair:** They are statutory regional local bodies, not Sixth Schedule councils and not a territorial Assembly.

## Error family 8: women’s reservation prematurely applied

**Faulty recall:** Delhi, J&K and Puducherry reserved one-third of seats immediately in April 2026; all three did so under the 106th Amendment; and all three await a constitutionally prescribed delimitation.

**Repair:** Every clause of that sentence is wrong. Three distinct instruments are involved, each matching the source of that Assembly. The Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 inserted Article 239AA(2)(ba)–(bc) for Delhi and commenced on 16 April 2026 under S.O. 1922(E); its condition is Article 334A and is constitutional. The J&K Reorganisation (Second Amendment) Act, 2023 inserted Sections 14A–14B and commenced on 17 April 2026 under S.O. 1929(E); its condition is Section 14B and is statutory. The Government of Union Territories (Amendment) Act, 2023 inserted Sections 3A–3B for Puducherry, including one-third of its SC-reserved seats, and commenced on 17 April 2026 under S.O. 1930(E); its condition is Section 3B and is also statutory. In every case operation awaits delimitation on the first post-commencement census; each runs fifteen years with rotation after each delimitation. Only Delhi’s delay is constitutionally prescribed. Nor did April 2026 change this: the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill was defeated 298–230 and the accompanying Delimitation and Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bills were withdrawn.

## Error family 9: representation and occupancy confused

**Faulty recall:** UTs currently have nineteen sitting Lok Sabha and eight sitting Rajya Sabha members in every circumstance.

**Repair:** Nineteen and eight are allocations; occupancy may vary with vacancies and election timing.

## Error family 10: current office-holder frozen in time

**Faulty recall:** The February 2026 MHA list remains sufficient for October, and a ministry’s composition can be read off whichever gazette was seen first.

**Repair:** Reconcile the baseline list with the March and September Presidential communiqués and assumption-of-office records. Apply the same discipline to ministries. Puducherry’s 2026 cycle had four appointment instruments, not one: S.O. 2348(E) of 8 May for the resignation and caretaker continuation; S.O. 2381(E) of 9 May appointing the Chief Minister from the date of his oath; S.O. 2459(E) of 13 May appointing two ministers on the same terms; and S.O. 2890(E) of 8 June appointing three more, who took office only on 17 June. The complete six-member Council with allocated portfolios appears only from the 5 August 2026 allocation under G.O. Ms. No. 69. Reporting the June appointees alone describes one stage as if it were the whole Council.

## Error family 11: Assembly resolution mistaken for legal change

**Faulty recall:** The J&K Assembly’s September 2026 resolution restored, or began restoring, statehood.

**Repair:** The resolution of 28 September 2026, moved by CM Omar Abdullah and carried by voice vote amid a BJP walkout, demanded immediate restoration of full statehood. It is politically significant but non-binding. A statutory UT legislature cannot confer Part VI State status on itself; that requires parliamentary legal reorganisation and the consequent notification, none of which was located by 1 October 2026. Statehood petitions also remained pending before the Supreme Court without any final direction compelling restoration.

## Error family 12: Bill treated as enacted law

**Faulty recall:** The April 2026 reservation and delimitation package changed the law; delimitation on the 2011 census was authorised, and UT representation rules were revised.

**Repair:** Three Bills were introduced on 16 April 2026 — the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026, the Delimitation Bill, 2026 and the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 covering Delhi, Puducherry and J&K. The constitutional amendment was **defeated** in the Lok Sabha on 17 April 2026 by 298 votes to 230, short of the special majority, and the other two Bills were then **withdrawn**. Nothing from that package became law. No seat allocation, delimitation basis or reservation condition was altered, and the position taught for all three Assemblies is unchanged. Treat the episode as evidence of entrenchment, never as a change in the statute book.

## Error family 13: Ladakh district count out of date

**Faulty recall:** Ladakh consists of Leh and Kargil, and the 2026 district reorganisation gave it a measure of self-government.

**Repair:** On 27 April 2026 five new districts — Sham, Nubra, Changthang, Zanskar and Drass — were notified, taking Ladakh from two districts to seven. Nubra, Sham and Changthang came from Leh; Zanskar and Drass from Kargil. The reform is administrative deconcentration: it brings revenue and service machinery closer across very long distances, but creates no legislature, no responsible ministry and no new elected body, and it did not multiply the two hill councils. Ladakh’s democratic position is unchanged.

## Error family 14: ministry-size and discretion rules over-generalised

**Faulty recall:** Every UT with a legislature caps its ministry at 10%, and the LG’s discretionary powers are broadly the same across the three.

**Repair:** Delhi’s 10% cap is in Article 239AA(4) — constitutional. J&K’s 10% cap is in Section 53(1) of the 2019 Act — statutory. Puducherry has **no numerical ceiling** in Sections 44–45 of the 1963 Act. Discretion also differs: Delhi’s LG must refer a difference of opinion to the President under the proviso to Article 239AA(4); Puducherry’s Administrator does so under the proviso to Section 44; J&K has **no such mandatory referral in Section 53** — the difference-of-opinion procedure is left to the rules the LG must make on ministerial advice under Section 55(1)(b). J&K’s LG instead holds a named statutory discretion under Section 53(2) in a matter that falls outside the purview of the powers conferred on the Assembly, one in which law requires discretion or a judicial function, and one related to All India Services and the Anti Corruption Bureau, with the **proviso to Section 53(2)** making his own view on that question final and **Section 53(3)** barring any inquiry into ministerial advice. There is no Section 53(4). Tenure also differs: Delhi’s Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the **President** under Article 239AA(5), while J&K’s hold office during the pleasure of the **Lieutenant Governor** under Section 54(2).

## Error family 15: Section 58(2) treated as a power outside Article 240

**Faulty recall:** Because Ladakh is not listed in Article 240, its regulations rest on a separate, free-standing statutory power created by Section 58(2), which also gives them the force of an Act of Parliament.

**Repair:** Two errors. First, Section 58(2) itself says the President may make regulations for Ladakh **“under article 240 of the Constitution of India”** — it is a statutory bridge into the constitutional power, not an alternative to it, and Regulation No. 10 of 2026 recites “article 240 of the Constitution read with sub-section (2) of section 58.” Second, the Act-of-Parliament force and the power to repeal or amend existing law come from **Section 58(3)**, not 58(2). What remains true is only that Ladakh is absent from the *list* in Article 240(1), so adding it there would need a constitutional route.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Foundation

- Part VIII principally covers Articles 239–241.
- Article 1(3) names three limbs: State territories, UTs in the First Schedule, and such other territories as may be acquired.
- Acquisition is a mode of entry into Indian territory, not a separate mode of government.
- Sikkim was **admitted as a State under Article 2** by the Thirty-sixth Amendment, 1975; it is not an Article 1(3)(c) acquired-territory example.
- Article 366(30) defines “Union territory” as one specified in the First Schedule **and** any other territory comprised within India but not so specified — so acquired territory is a UT from the moment of acquisition.
- India had eight UTs on 1 October 2026.
- Delhi, Puducherry and J&K had legislatures and ministries.
- A&N, Chandigarh, DNHDD, Ladakh and Lakshadweep lacked territorial legislatures.
- UT status may respond to national-capital, strategic, small-territory, historical or transitional needs.
- A legislature does not make a UT a State.
- Article 246(4) preserves Parliament’s competence on every subject for territory outside a State.
- The Ministry of Home Affairs is the Union nodal ministry for UT administration — an administrative fact, not a source of power.
- A proposal to bring Chandigarh under Article 240 with its own Administrator surfaced in November 2025; the MHA stated no Bill would be introduced that session and that no final decision had been taken. It was not enacted, and Chandigarh’s position is unchanged.

## President and Administrator

- Article 239(1): “Save as otherwise provided by Parliament by law,” the President administers every UT through an appointed Administrator.
- Only a parliamentary law may displace that default; executive instruction cannot.
- Displacing the manner of administration does not abolish the office: Article 239AA(1) says Delhi’s LG is the administrator “appointed under article 239.”
- Administrator is the President’s agent, not a Part VI State Governor.
- Designation—LG, Administrator or Chief Commissioner—does not itself determine power.
- Article 239(2): a State Governor appointed Administrator of an adjoining UT acts independently of the State Council of Ministers in the UT capacity.
- Every act remains subject to constitutional, statutory and judicial limits.

## Legislature routes

- Article 239A enables Parliament to create a legislature, ministry or both.
- A 239A law may provide elected or mixed composition.
- Such a law is not treated as an Article 368 amendment.
- Puducherry: Article 239A + Government of Union Territories Act, 1963.
- Delhi: Article 239AA constitutional scheme.
- J&K: J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019; Section 13 applies Article 239A.
- Entrenchment, not daily power, is the difference: a statutory structure changes by ordinary majority, a constitutional one by special majority.
- April 2026 proved the point. On 16 April three Bills were introduced — the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, the Delimitation Bill and the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill covering Delhi, Puducherry and J&K. On 17 April the constitutional amendment was defeated in the Lok Sabha 298–230; the other two were withdrawn. Nothing was enacted.

## Delhi

- Sixty-ninth Amendment inserted Articles 239AA and 239AB; it came into force on **1 February 1992**, and the Assembly and ministry began functioning after the **1993** election. The earlier Metropolitan Council under the Delhi Administration Act, 1966 was advisory, not a legislature.
- Delhi is the NCT; its Administrator is designated LG but is appointed under Article 239.
- Assembly: 70 elected.
- Article 239AA(2)(b): total seats, SC-reserved seats and constituencies are regulated by parliamentary law. Article 332 does not apply to Delhi.
- Article 239AA(2)(c): Articles 324–327 and 329 apply, so the Election Commission of India conducts Assembly elections; “appropriate Legislature” in Articles 326 and 329 means Parliament.
- Field: applicable State and Concurrent Lists.
- Exclusions: Entries 1, 2, 18 and Entries 64–66 insofar as related.
- Parliament may legislate on all subjects and ordinarily overrides inconsistency.
- Article 239AA(4): ministry not more than 10% of Assembly strength — presently seven including the CM. The cap is in the Article itself.
- Article 239AA(4) proviso: on a difference of opinion the LG **shall refer** the matter to the President and act on that decision, with an urgency power meanwhile.
- Article 239AA(5): the President appoints the CM, and other ministers on CM advice; ministers hold office during the pleasure of the **President**, not the LG.
- Article 239AA(6): the ministry is collectively responsible to the Assembly.
- 2018: aid and advice is ordinary rule; LG has no general independent power; referral is exceptional.
- 14 February 2019: Sikri J. adopted differentiated service control by officer level; Bhushan J. held Entry 41 unavailable to Delhi and denied general GNCTD executive control except by parliamentary law or delegation.
- 11 May 2023: elected government received services control outside reserved fields.
- 19 May 2023: Ordinance.
- Act 19 of 2023: NCCSA—CM, Chief Secretary, Principal Home Secretary.
- NCCSA decides by majority; bureaucratic members can outvote CM.
- LG may return a recommendation and has final statutory decision after reconsideration.
- 20 July 2023: W.P.(C) 678/2023 referred to a Constitution Bench on Article 239AA(7).
- 25 August 2023: amendment allowed so the enacted Act could replace the Ordinance as the challenged instrument.
- No final merits judgment was located through 1 October 2026.
- Act remains operative pending a contrary legal development.
- Women’s reservation: Article 239AA(2)(ba)–(bc) inserted by the 106th Amendment, commenced 16 April 2026 by S.O. 1922(E). The condition is **Article 334A** — constitutional — requiring delimitation on the first post-commencement census, with a fifteen-year duration and rotation after each delimitation. No seat was reserved on 1 October 2026.
- The attempt to lift that condition failed: the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 would have amended Articles 81, 82 and 334A — raising the House to not more than 815 members from the States and not more than 35 from the Union territories, 850 in all, and removing the post-2026 census requirement — and was defeated in the Lok Sabha on 17 April 2026, 298–230.
- Delhi's financial procedure, including the requirement that a demand for a grant be made only on the LG's recommendation, comes from the **GNCTD Act, 1991** — statutory, not from Articles 112–117 or 203.

## Exceptional provisions

- Article 239AB: Delhi breakdown route; distinct from Article 356.
- Article 239B is a constitutional provision; the 1963 Act does not confer ordinance power.
- Article 239B: ordinance during recess, with prior Presidential instructions.
- It directly applies to Puducherry and to Delhi through Article 239AA(8).
- An ordinance has the force of an Act of the territorial legislature and is void so far as that legislature could not have enacted it.
- It lapses on the expiry of six weeks from reassembly; a disapproving resolution ends it earlier; the Administrator may withdraw it earlier still, after obtaining Presidential instructions.
- Approval does not extend an ordinance; only a replacing Act preserves its content.
- Section 51 of the 1963 Act: the President may suspend the Act’s provisions and administer Puducherry under Article 239.
- **Section 52, 2019 Act:** the J&K LG may promulgate ordinances when the Assembly is not in session, limited to matters within the Assembly’s competence; **no prior Presidential instructions are required**; the ordinance has the force of an Act of the Assembly assented to by the LG, lapses six weeks from reassembly or earlier on a disapproving resolution, and **may be withdrawn at any time by the LG alone**.
- **Section 73, 2019 Act:** on a report from the LG or otherwise, the President may suspend the operation of all or any provisions of the Act and make incidental and consequential provisions, where the administration cannot be carried on in accordance with the Act or where it is necessary or expedient for proper administration.
- The **Proclamation of 31 October 2019** was one exercise of Section 73; it is the vehicle read with Section 55 in G.S.R. 386(E) of 2024, and should not be described as a response to the collapse of an elected UT government.
- Breakdown clauses exist only where a legislature exists; their absence for A&N or Lakshadweep is a consequence, not a gap.
- Article 240 regulations are Parliament-equivalent.
- Article 240 names A&N, Lakshadweep, DNHDD and conditionally Puducherry.
- Puducherry condition: legislature dissolved or functioning suspended.
- Ladakh is not named in Article 240, but **Section 58(2)** of the 2019 Act provides that the President may make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of the Union territory of Ladakh **“under article 240 of the Constitution of India”** — a statutory bridge to the constitutional power, not a parallel power.
- **Section 58(3)**, not 58(2), provides that such a regulation may repeal or amend any Act of Parliament or other law applicable to Ladakh and has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament applying there.
- Article 240’s own list can be altered by ordinary law where that law is an Article 2/3 reorganisation using Article 4 — Section 4 of the DNHDD (Merger of Union Territories) Act, 2019 substituted entry (c) and omitted entry (d). A freestanding insertion needs Article 368.
- Regulation No. 10 of 2026 was made “in exercise of the powers conferred by article 240 of the Constitution read with sub-section (2) of section 58 of the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019,” and promulgated 27 August 2026. Its Section 1(1) short title is “the **Union territory** of Ladakh (Sitting of Bench of the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir**,** and Ladakh in Ladakh) Regulation, 2026”; the all-capital Gazette heading is a printing convention.
- Section 1(3) of that Regulation left commencement to the Administrator; **S.O. 262** appointed 5 September 2026. Section 2(2) permits the Judges and **division courts** to sit at a place in Ladakh appointed by the Chief Justice with the Lieutenant Governor’s approval; Section 2(1) keeps the principal seat unchanged; Section 2(3) allows transfer of a Ladakh case to Srinagar or Jammu.
- Chandigarh has no Article 240 route at all, which is why a Chandigarh entry would be the standalone Article 368 case.

## Puducherry

- Statutory legislature under the 1963 Act.
- Thirty elected members; up to three Central nominees.
- Nominees possess voting rights.
- *K. Lakshminarayanan* upheld the nomination framework.
- Field: applicable State and Concurrent List subjects.
- No express Delhi-style public order/police/land trio.
- Parliament remains plenary and overriding.
- President appoints CM and ministers; appointment operates from the oath.
- Ministry collectively responsible to the Assembly under Section 44.
- Sections 44–45 contain **no numerical ceiling** on ministry size — unlike Delhi’s Article 239AA(4) or J&K’s Section 53(1).
- Proviso to Section 44: a difference of opinion between Administrator and Ministers goes to the President, with an interim urgency power — the statutory analogue of Delhi’s constitutional proviso.
- Section 51: the President may suspend the Act’s provisions and administer the territory under Article 239.
- Section 3(4)–(5) reserves seats for Scheduled Castes in census-based proportion — statutory, not Article 332.
- The Explanation to Section 3(5) freezes “population” to the **2001 census** until figures for the first census taken after 2026 are published.
- **Section 3(6)** ties the cessation of that SC reservation to the date it ceases in the House of the People under **Article 334**, with a proviso protecting existing representation until dissolution — the same device as Section 14(8) of the J&K Act.
- Act 39 of 2023 inserted **Sections 3A–3B**: one-third of directly elected seats reserved for women, including one-third of SC-reserved seats; operative after delimitation on the first post-commencement census; fifteen-year duration; rotation after each delimitation.
- S.O. 1930(E), 17 April 2026, commenced that amendment. The condition is **Section 3B — statutory**, not Article 334A.
- 2026 cycle: poll 9 April; results 4 May; resignation effective 7 May.
- S.O. 2348(E), 8 May 2026, notified acceptance of the resignation and caretaker continuation until a new Council was sworn; republished as G.O. Ms. No. 37 in Puducherry Extraordinary Gazette No. 92 of 9 May.
- S.O. 2381(E), 9 May 2026, appointed N. Rangasamy Chief Minister “with effect from the date he is sworn in”; republished as G.O. Ms. No. 38 in Puducherry Extraordinary Gazette No. 93.
- S.O. 2459(E), 13 May 2026, appointed A. Namassivayam and Malladi Krishna Rao Ministers “on the advice of the Chief Minister,” again with effect from the date of their oath; republished as G.O. Ms. No. 41 in Puducherry Extraordinary Gazette No. 94.
- A central notification and its local republication are one instrument seen twice, not two decisions.
- 13 May 2026: all three took oath. Same-day execution does not merge the appointment order and the assumption of office.
- S.O. 2890(E), 8 June 2026, appointed P. Rajavelu, V.P. Sivakolundhu and G.N.S. Rajasekaran, effective on their swearing-in; republished 10 June.
- 17 June 2026: those three took oath, completing a Council of six.
- 5 August 2026: G.O. Ms. No. 69 allocated business among all six office-holders.
- Allocation made “in exercise of the powers conferred under rule 4 of the Business of the Government of Puducherry (Allocation) Rules, 1963,” by the Lieutenant Governor in consultation with the Chief Minister, in so far as the Ministers aid and advise him under Section 44 of the 1963 Act.
- Allocation on 1 October 2026, in the notification’s own order and wording — **1. Thiru N. Rangasamy, Chief Minister:** Confidential & Cabinet Department; Planning & Finance; Personnel & Administrative Reforms; Cooperation; Revenue and Excise; General Administration; Health & Family Welfare; Hindu Religious Institutions; Wakf Board; Local Administration; Urban Basic Services; Transport; Science, Technology and Environment; Town & Country Planning; Information & Publicity; Fire Services; and Other subjects which are not allocated to any other Ministers.

- **4. Thiru P. Rajavelu, Adi Dravidar Welfare Minister:** Adi Dravidar Welfare; Art & Culture; Housing; Information Technology; Economics & Statistics; and Stationery & Printing.

- Where a portfolio name is placed inside quotation marks it must be the notification’s own wording — “Confidential & Cabinet Department,” “Fire Services,” “Port,” “Forest & Wild Life,” “DRDA,” and “Other subjects which are not allocated to any other Ministers.” Expansions such as District Rural Development Agency are editorial and should be flagged as such.
- The ministerial designations are allocation labels drawn from lead portfolios; they create no hierarchy within the Council.
- Keep appointment, assumption of office and allocation of business as three separate legal acts.

## J&K

- Reorganisation effective 31 October 2019.
- J&K became UT with legislature; Ladakh UT without legislature.
- J&K Assembly is statutory.
- 114 territorial seats after delimitation.
- Twenty-four seats kept vacant **statutorily**, by Section 14(4), for the area under Pakistan’s occupation; that area and those seats are excluded in delimiting constituencies.
- Ninety directly elected seats.
- Field excludes public order and police, by Section 32(1).
- Land is not generally excluded.
- Parliament retains plenary power under Section 32(2) and Article 246(4).
- Assembly legislates, authorises expenditure and holds ministry accountable.
- Section 14(6)–(7) reserves SC and ST seats in population proportion, with the Explanation’s proviso reading “population” as the **2011** census until the first post-2026 census figures are published; Section 14(8) ties cessation to Article 334.
- Ministry cap: 10%, in Section 53(1), which also excepts matters of discretion and judicial or quasi-judicial functions.
- Omar Abdullah headed a six-member ministry on 1 October 2026.
- Nomination routes: Section 15 permits nomination of two women if inadequately represented; Section 15A permits not more than two Kashmiri migrants, one of whom shall be a woman; Section 15B separately permits one displaced person from PoJK, defined to include successors-in-interest of those displaced during 1947–48, 1965 and 1971.
- Nominations and the 24 kept-vacant seats are separate.
- Section 53(2): the LG acts in his discretion in a matter “which falls outside the purview of the powers conferred on the Legislative Assembly”; one in which law requires him to act in his discretion or to exercise any **judicial** functions; or one “related to All India Services and Anti Corruption Bureau.” There is no entrustment condition in limb (i), and “quasi-judicial” belongs to sub-section (1), not to limb (ii).
- The **proviso to Section 53(2)** makes the LG’s own decision on whether a matter is discretionary final; **Section 53(3)** bars any court from inquiring into ministerial advice. There is **no Section 53(4)**.
- Section 54(1): the LG appoints the CM, and other Ministers on the CM’s advice. **Section 54(2):** Ministers hold office during the **pleasure of the Lieutenant Governor**. Section 54(3): collective responsibility to the Assembly.
- **Section 55(1):** the LG **shall make rules on the advice of the Council of Ministers** (a) for allocation of business and (b) for its more convenient transaction, including the procedure in case of a difference of opinion between the LG and the Council or a Minister. Section 55 contains no discretionary-business carve-out, and Section 53 contains no mandatory referral of a disagreement to the President.
- G.S.R. 386(E), 12 July 2024, amended the 2019 Rules and was made by the **President** under Section 55 read with the Proclamation of 31 October 2019 issued under Section 73 — a route that bypassed the ordinary advice requirement while the proclamation operated.
- Its exact five categories: specified Finance-concurrence proposals for police/public order/AIS/ACB; appointment of the Advocate-General and assisting law officers; prosecution sanction or appeals; prisons, Directorate of Prosecution and FSL; and Administrative Secretaries or AIS cadre posts.
- Two of those groups track discretion heads named in the Act. The others do not: the Advocate-General is appointed under **Section 79(1)**, and **Prisons is State List Entry 4**, inside the Assembly’s field under Section 32(1). The “route only, within an existing carve-out” description is therefore defensible but contested, and should be offered as analytical inference with both readings stated.
- Call the operative instrument the 2019 Rules **as amended in 2024**.
- **Section 52:** the LG may promulgate ordinances when the Assembly is not in session, without Presidential instructions, limited to the Assembly’s field; six-week lapse from reassembly; earlier death by disapproving resolution; withdrawal by the LG alone at any time.
- **Section 73:** presidential power to suspend all or any provisions of the Act; the Proclamation of 31 October 2019 was one exercise of it.
- Women’s reservation: Sections 14A–14B inserted by the Second Amendment Act, 2023, commenced 17 April 2026 by S.O. 1929(E). The condition is **Section 14B — statutory**; Delhi’s equivalent condition is in Article 334A and is constitutional.
- Elections were held in 2024.
- 28 September 2026: the Assembly passed a resolution moved by CM Omar Abdullah demanding immediate restoration of full statehood, carried by voice vote amid a BJP walkout.
- That resolution is non-binding; it does not confer Part VI State status, which requires parliamentary legal reorganisation and notification.
- No statehood-restoration notification was located by 1 October 2026.
- Petitions seeking enforcement of the Union’s statehood assurance remained pending before the Supreme Court; on **10 October 2025** the Court granted the Union **four weeks** to reply in the miscellaneous application in *Zahoor Ahmad Bhat*, and no final direction compelling restoration followed.
- A revised set of business rules was reported in August 2026 to be awaiting LG approval; none had been notified by the cut-off date.

## Ladakh and non-legislature UTs

- Ladakh has no Assembly or territorial ministry.
- Ladakh had **seven districts** from 27 April 2026: Leh, Kargil, Nubra, Sham, Changthang, Zanskar and Drass.
- Nubra (Diskit), Sham (Khaltse) and Changthang (Nyoma) were carved from Leh; Zanskar (Padum) and Drass (Drass-Ranbirpura) from Kargil; the MHA had approved the proposal in August 2024.
- District creation is administrative deconcentration, not devolution: no legislature, ministry or new elected body resulted, and the two hill councils were not multiplied.
- LAHDC Leh and Kargil are statutory regional councils, not Sixth Schedule councils.
- Five non-legislature UTs use Parliament, Administrators, local bodies and advisory arrangements.
- Article 240 directly covers only three of these five: A&N, DNHDD and Lakshadweep.
- Regulation No. 10 of 2026 was gazetted on 27 August and commenced on 5 September under Ladakh notification S.O. 262, having been made under Article 240 read with Section 58(2).
- Legal commencement enabled further institutional work; actual bench sittings require separate operational evidence.

## Parliament and courts

- Article 81(1)(b) caps UT representation in the Lok Sabha at **twenty** members, chosen in such manner as Parliament may by law provide.
- UT Lok Sabha allocation totals 19 — one seat below the ceiling.
- Delhi 7; J&K 5; DNHDD 2; each other UT 1.
- UT Rajya Sabha allocation totals 8, from the Fourth Schedule read with Article 80.
- Delhi 3; J&K 4; Puducherry 1.
- Allocation does not guarantee continuous occupancy.
- The First Schedule to the RPA 1950 supplies Lok Sabha allocations; Sections 10–11 of the 2019 Act carried through the J&K–Ladakh changes.
- Article 84 supplies citizenship/oath and age requirements; RPA 1951 Sections 3–4 supply elector and reserved-seat qualifications, including Section 4(cc) for the Lakshadweep ST seat.
- Article 241 permits separate, common or assigned High Court arrangements.
- Delhi has its own High Court under the Delhi High Court Act, 1966.
- J&K and Ladakh share a common High Court under Section 75 of the 2019 Act.
- A&N—Calcutta; Chandigarh—Punjab and Haryana; DNHDD—Bombay; Lakshadweep—Kerala; Puducherry—Madras.
- Naming is instrument-specific: S.O. 2860(E) of 16 July 2021 substituted “High Court of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh” into Section 75(1)(a), and those substituted words carry **no comma**; Regulation No. 10 of 2026 uses a **comma** throughout its own text, in its Section 1(1) short title and in Section 2 when naming the Court; the Court’s own ordinary usage is “High Court of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh.” Quote whichever instrument is being cited, assert no universal rule in either direction, and never write “Ladakh High Court.”
- Article 81(1)(b)’s cap of twenty is one seat above the present UT allocation of nineteen; a single further UT seat would exhaust it. The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 proposed not more than 815 State members and not more than 35 UT members, and was defeated.

## Finance

- Delhi, Puducherry and J&K possess statutory Consolidated Funds, Public Accounts and Contingency Funds.
- Their Assemblies consider annual financial statements and demands for grants.
- Delhi’s financial procedure, including the LG’s recommendation for a demand for a grant, is supplied by the **GNCTD Act, 1991**, not by Articles 112–117 or Article 203.
- Appropriation legally authorises withdrawals.
- Supplementary and excess procedures control departures.
- Ministries remain politically accountable for budgets.
- CAG audit supports financial legality and performance scrutiny.
- Non-legislature UT expenditure is principally authorised by Parliament through the Union budget and Consolidated Fund of India.
- Separate funds do not create State-equivalent fiscal autonomy.

## One-line comparative verdicts

- **Delhi:** constitutionally entrenched self-government under national-capital reservations and contested services control.
- **Puducherry:** broad statutory self-government qualified by nominees and parliamentary predominance.
- **J&K:** elected statutory UT government with stronger LG safeguards and unresolved Statehood transition.
- **Non-legislature UTs:** direct Union responsibility moderated by Parliament, courts, local bodies and advisory institutions.
- **Overall:** UT asymmetry is legitimate when territorial need, democratic accountability, precision and review remain aligned.
