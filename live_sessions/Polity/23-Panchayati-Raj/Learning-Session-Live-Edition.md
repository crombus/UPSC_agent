### Visual first: the distance problem

```text
Village need
    │
    ├── distant authority decides alone
    │      → weak local information
    │      → standardised scheme
    │      → low ownership and accountability
    │
    └── constitutionally enabled local body decides
           → local knowledge enters choice
           → citizens can monitor nearby
           → priorities can fit place
           → democracy becomes continuous, not merely electoral
```

The case for Panchayati Raj is not that every village decision is automatically wise. It is that decisions should be taken at the lowest level capable of taking them, while higher levels supply law, finance, expertise and rights protection.

### 1. Article 40: a constitutional direction, not an enforceable individual claim

Article 40, in Part IV, directs the State to organise village panchayats and endow them with powers and authority necessary to function as units of self-government. Three distinctions matter:

1. It is a **Directive Principle of State Policy**, not a Fundamental Right.
2. It asks for more than village committees: the phrase **units of self-government** implies meaningful authority.
3. Before 1993, it was a direction whose institutional fulfilment depended heavily on State political will. The 73rd Amendment supplied a constitutional framework, but the amount of operational power still depends substantially on State law.

Article 40 therefore supplies the **normative destination**; Part IX later supplies a **constitutional vehicle**. Neither by itself guarantees capable local government.

### 2. Democratic decentralisation

Democratic decentralisation is the transfer of public authority to elected bodies below the State level so that people participate in planning, implementation and accountability. It has four dimensions:

| Dimension | Meaning | Failure when absent |
|---|---|---|
| Political | Regular elections, representation and local deliberation | Administration without popular mandate |
| Functional | Clear responsibility for local services and development | A body exists but cannot decide |
| Administrative | Staff answerable to the local institution | Elected representatives depend on line departments |
| Fiscal | Predictable transfers plus power to raise and spend revenue | Mandates without means |

The expression “democratic decentralisation” is associated especially with the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee. It differs from mere **deconcentration**, in which the State posts its own officers closer to villages but retains authority upward in the same hierarchy.

> **Plain intuition:** Moving a State officer’s desk to the block is not the same as moving decision-making power to elected villagers.

### 3. Why the constitutional setting is State-centred

Local government appears in **Entry 5 of the State List** in the Seventh Schedule. State legislatures therefore make the core laws governing the constitution and powers of local authorities, subject after 1993 to Part IX’s constitutional floor. This creates a deliberately shared design:

```text
Constitution
  sets non-negotiable floor
  (institutions, elections, tenure, reservation, SEC, SFC)
             ↓
State legislature
  designs composition, functions, taxes, Gram Sabha powers,
  disqualification rules and operational machinery
             ↓
Panchayats
  exercise only the authority actually vested by law,
  within constitutional and statutory limits
```

This arrangement respects federalism and variation in settlement patterns. Its danger is that a State may comply with institutional form while withholding functions, staff or buoyant revenue.

### 4. The democratic promise and its limits

Panchayats may improve:

- **information:** villagers know local water, road, welfare and land-use priorities;
- **participation:** the Gram Sabha offers direct deliberation;
- **inclusion:** constitutionally protected representation can bring excluded groups into office;
- **accountability:** citizens can observe projects and question nearby representatives;
- **coordination:** local plans can converge schemes around place-specific needs;
- **leadership formation:** local office can become a training ground for wider political participation.

But proximity can also intensify local elite capture, caste hierarchy, patriarchy, coercion or exclusion. Hence decentralisation needs reservation, transparency, audit, grievance systems, independent elections and constitutional rights. “Local” is not a substitute for “constitutional”.

### Prelims discrimination

| Close pair | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Article 40 / Part IX | Article 40 is the DPSP root; Part IX is the later constitutional architecture |
| State List / State discretion without limit | Entry 5 gives States the legislative field, but Part IX imposes a constitutional floor |
| Deconcentration / devolution | Administrative presence below is not elected local authority |
| Local participation / local sovereignty | Panchayats are constitutionally recognised governments within State law, not sovereign entities |

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can Article 40 and Entry 5 together produce both a strong case for Panchayats and uneven Panchayat power across India?

**Model answer:** Article 40 directs the State to organise village panchayats as units of self-government, establishing the constitutional purpose of grassroots democracy. Entry 5 places local government in the State List, so States legislate operational powers, structures and revenue. Part IX later creates a common minimum framework, but much devolution remains State-law dependent. The combination therefore supports Panchayats while also permitting wide inter-State variation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Article 40 does not itself transfer twenty-nine subjects or create an enforceable Fundamental Right to a particular model of village government.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (10 marks, 150 words):** “Democratic decentralisation is more than administrative deconcentration.” Explain with reference to the constitutional setting of Panchayati Raj.

**Illustrative response:** Democratic decentralisation transfers authority to elected local institutions, whereas deconcentration merely locates State officials closer to citizens while retaining upward bureaucratic control. Article 40 directs organisation of village panchayats as units of self-government, making authority—not administrative presence—the goal. Entry 5 of the State List permits State-specific local-government legislation, while Part IX creates a national constitutional floor for elections, tenure, representation and fiscal review. Genuine decentralisation consequently requires political mandate, assigned functions, accountable staff and usable funds. It may improve information, participation and accountability, but proximity can also reproduce caste, class and gender domination. Reservation, Gram Sabha deliberation, independent elections, audit and rights protection must therefore accompany devolution. Panchayati Raj is best understood as constitutionally bounded local self-government, not either a field office of the State or an autonomous sovereign tier.

**Quantified rubric (10):** constitutional location 2; devolution–deconcentration distinction 2; four dimensions/mechanism 3; limitation and safeguards 2; reasoned conclusion 1.

Because Article 40 supplied a goal while Entry 5 left institution-building largely to States, the next problem is historical: why did programmes and ordinary State laws repeatedly fail to create durable self-government?

### 1. Why the Community Development approach was insufficient

The Community Development Programme of 1952 sought area development through official machinery; the National Extension Service followed in 1953. Their weakness was not absence of development activity but inadequate durable popular participation. Programmes administered **for** villagers were not the same as institutions governed **by** elected villagers.

This generated a design question: what political structure could connect village participation, block-level technical coordination and district planning?

### 2. Balwant Rai Mehta Committee, 1957

Asked to examine the working of the Community Development Programme and National Extension Service, the Committee recommended **democratic decentralisation** through a three-tier structure:

| Level | Institution | Core logic |
|---|---|---|
| Village | Gram Panchayat | Directly elected local base |
| Block | Panchayat Samiti | Executive hub for development administration |
| District | Zila Parishad | Advisory/coordinating apex in the original design |

It favoured genuine transfer of power and adequate resources. Rajasthan inaugurated Panchayati Raj at Nagaur on **2 October 1959**; Andhra Pradesh followed. The experiment demonstrated feasibility but not permanence. Elections became irregular in many places, bodies were superseded, finance remained weak and bureaucracy often dominated.

### 3. Ashok Mehta Committee, 1977–78

The Committee diagnosed decline and proposed a **two-tier** model:

- Zila Parishad at district level as the principal decentralised institution;
- Mandal Panchayat below it, covering a group of villages;
- district as the first point of decentralisation below the State;
- participation of political parties in Panchayat elections;
- stronger finances, social justice committee and regular elections;
- protection against arbitrary supersession.

Its recommendations shaped later debate, although the Janata Government fell before comprehensive implementation. The contrast is exam-relevant: **Balwant Rai Mehta = three tiers and block-centred executive emphasis; Ashok Mehta = two tiers and district-centred emphasis.**

### 4. G.V.K. Rao and L.M. Singhvi

The **G.V.K. Rao Committee (1985)** criticised the bureaucratisation of development administration. It placed the district at the centre of planning and argued that PRIs should play the principal role in rural development rather than stand at the margins of line departments.

The **L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986)** made the decisive constitutional move:

- constitutional recognition and protection for Panchayati Raj;
- Gram Sabha as the foundation of decentralised democracy;
- regular, free and fair elections;
- Nyaya Panchayats for clusters of villages.

Singhvi’s constitutional-status recommendation addressed the structural defect exposed by decades of supersession: an institution created solely by ordinary State law could be weakened by the same political executive.

### 5. Other reform signals

The **Thungon Committee (1988)** supported constitutional recognition, a three-tier structure, fixed tenure, planning and finance arrangements. The institutional consensus had matured: Panchayats needed a constitutional minimum, not another administrative instruction.

The **V.N. Gadgil Committee on Policy and Programmes (1988)** converted that consensus into a near-blueprint. It recommended constitutional status; village, block and district Panchayats; five-year tenure; direct election of members at all three levels; SC, ST and women’s reservation; responsibility for preparing and implementing socio-economic plans against a constitutionally specified subject list; local taxing authority; and State Finance and State Election Commissions. Its recommendations became the basis for drafting the constitutional amendment bill.

### 6. State experiments: institutional variety and revival

The early spread was not uniform. Rajasthan used three tiers, Tamil Nadu two and West Bengal initially four. In the Rajasthan–Andhra Pradesh pattern, the **Panchayat Samiti and block** were the stronger planning-development centre; in the Maharashtra–Gujarat pattern, the **Zila Parishad and district** were stronger. Some States also created Nyaya Panchayats for petty civil and criminal matters.

After the first-generation decline, three experiments showed that revival remained possible:

| State | Institutional significance |
|---|---|
| West Bengal | Panchayat elections held on a party basis on 4 June 1978, marking an important second-generation revival |
| Karnataka | A new Panchayati Raj Act was enacted in 1983, received presidential assent in July 1985, commenced on 14 August 1985 and was followed by elections in January 1987 |
| Andhra Pradesh | Followed the renewed West Bengal and Karnataka Panchayati Raj models in 1986 |

These experiments did not establish one universally superior structure. They demonstrated that State law and political commitment could revive elected institutions, while later dissolution or administrative takeover—such as Karnataka’s 1990–92 interruption—showed why constitutional continuity was still needed.

### 7. Failed bills before success

The **64th Constitutional Amendment Bill, 1989** passed the Lok Sabha but failed in the Rajya Sabha. A revised attempt in 1990 did not complete the legislative journey. A fresh bill was introduced in 1991, considered by a Joint Parliamentary Committee, and emerged as the Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act, 1992.

The failed bills matter because constitutionalisation involved a federal tension. A uniform national floor could secure local democracy, but detailed central prescription could be perceived as intruding into the State field. The final architecture responds by making elections, tenure and representation mandatory while leaving substantial functional and fiscal endowment to State legislation.

### Why early systems declined

```text
Irregular elections
      + prolonged supersession
      + weak own revenue and transfers
      + bureaucracy-led schemes
      + unclear functions and staff control
      + low political priority
      = institutions without continuity or authority
```

The 73rd Amendment directly tackles the first two and creates institutions for electoral and fiscal review. It only partly tackles the remaining problems because functional and fiscal devolution remain largely enabling.

### Concept check

**Question:** What common institutional lesson connects the Community Development Programme, the major committees and the failed constitutional bills?

**Model answer:** Development administration without elected authority could not sustain participation or accountability. Successive committees moved from a three-tier democratic structure to stronger district planning and finally constitutional protection. West Bengal, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh demonstrated State-led revival, while later interruptions exposed its fragility. Gadgil translated committee learning into the amendment bill’s institutional components. The eventual settlement reconciled a national democratic floor with substantial State control over operational devolution.

**Misconception to avoid:** The 73rd Amendment did not simply copy one committee; it synthesised several decades of institutional learning.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** Trace the evolution of Panchayati Raj from development administration to constitutional self-government.

**Illustrative response:** India’s early rural-development strategy began with the Community Development Programme (1952) and National Extension Service (1953). Their administration-led character exposed the absence of sustained popular participation. The Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) answered this through “democratic decentralisation” and a three-tier design, launched first in Rajasthan in 1959. Yet irregular elections, supersession, weak finance and bureaucratic dominance produced decline.

The Ashok Mehta Committee (1977–78) proposed a district-centred, two-tier system, stronger finances and party participation. West Bengal’s 1978 party-based elections and the later Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh reforms demonstrated State-led revival. G.V.K. Rao (1985) restored PRIs to the centre of development; L.M. Singhvi (1986) sought constitutional recognition and a foundational Gram Sabha. Thungon reinforced constitutional and fiscal planning, while Gadgil (1988) supplied the amendment-bill blueprint, including fixed tenure, direct elections, reservation, subjects, taxation, SEC and SFC.

The 64th Amendment Bill failed in the Rajya Sabha in 1989 and the 1990 effort lapsed, reflecting federal concern over central prescription in a State subject. The 1991 bill eventually became the 73rd Amendment, enacted in 1992 and effective from 24 April 1993.

Thus, the evolution was from schemes administered for rural people to elected bodies constitutionally protected from extinction. However, because functions, staff and revenue remain largely State-dependent, constitutionalisation secured existence more fully than self-government.

### 1. Birth and commencement

The Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act was enacted in **1992**. It came into force on **24 April 1993**, inserted **Part IX**, covering Articles **243 to 243O**, and added the **Eleventh Schedule** connected to Article 243G.

> **Date discipline:** enactment is 1992; commencement is 24 April 1993.

### 2. What constitutional status changed

Before Part IX, a State could postpone elections or supersede institutions for long periods under ordinary law. Part IX made specified institutional features constitutionally non-negotiable. It did not make every operational choice uniform.

```text
Constitutionalisation
  ├── guarantees institutional continuity
  ├── requires electoral renewal
  ├── secures representational floors
  ├── creates SEC and SFC
  └── leaves major operational fields to State law
          ├── Gram Sabha powers
          ├── village chairperson method
          ├── actual subject transfer
          ├── tax authority
          ├── staff control
          └── accounts/audit detail
```

### 3. Mandatory, conditional and enabling provisions

| Mandatory constitutional floor | Conditional or enabling field |
|---|---|
| Panchayats at village and district levels | Intermediate tier may be omitted in a State having a population not exceeding twenty lakh |
| Direct election to territorial seats | Method of electing village chairperson |
| SC/ST and women’s reservation floors | Backward-class reservation |
| Five-year tenure and election schedule | Powers of Gram Sabha |
| SEC and five-yearly SFC | Article 243G transfer of functions |
| State-law conforming structure | Article 243H tax authorisation |

This split is the master key to the entire topic. The amendment strongly constitutionalises **political decentralisation**, but uses “may, by law, endow” formulations for much of functional and fiscal decentralisation.

### 4. Article 243 definitions that prevent confusion

Part IX defines, among other terms:

- **district** as a district in a State;
- **Gram Sabha** as persons registered in the electoral rolls relating to a village within the Panchayat area;
- **intermediate level** as a level between village and district specified by the Governor;
- **Panchayat** as an institution of self-government constituted under Article 243B for rural areas;
- **Panchayat area**, **population** and **village** for the Part’s operation.

Definitions do legal work. A Gram Sabha is the electorate assembled; a Gram Panchayat is an elected institution. They are not synonyms.

### 5. Articles 243L, 243M and 243N: often-skipped edges

**Article 243L:** Part IX applies to Union Territories through exact substitutions. References to a State’s Governor are read as references to the **Administrator appointed under Article 239**. References to the Legislature or Legislative Assembly of a State are read, for a Union Territory having a Legislative Assembly, as references to that Assembly. The President may by public notification direct that Part IX applies to a Union Territory or part of it subject to specified exceptions and modifications.

**Article 243M:** Part IX does not automatically apply to:

- Scheduled Areas under the Fifth Schedule;
- tribal areas under the Sixth Schedule;
- Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram;
- specified hill areas of Manipur for which district councils exist;
- district-level Panchayats in the hill areas of Darjeeling district where the constitutionally named Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council existed under State law; Part IX also does not affect that Council’s functions and powers.

The Constitution then provides exact exception and extension machinery:

- Article 243M(3) is the Darjeeling provision; the extension route for **Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram** is Article 243M(4)(a). Under clause (4)(a), the Legislature of such a State may by law extend Part IX to that State, except the areas, if any, referred to in Article 243M(1), only if its Legislative Assembly first passes a resolution supported by **a majority of the total membership of that House** and **a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**. This route contains **no exclusion of Article 243D**. The distinct Article 243M(3A) carve-out concerns only Article 243D’s Scheduled Caste seat-reservation requirement in Arunachal Pradesh.
- Parliament may by law extend Part IX to the **Scheduled Areas and tribal areas** covered by Article 243M(1), subject to specified exceptions and modifications.
- A parliamentary extension law under Article 243M(4)(b) is **not deemed an amendment of the Constitution for Article 368**, even if it contains provisions incidental or consequential to the extension.

PESA uses the parliamentary route to extend a modified Panchayat framework to **Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas**. It does not extend Part IX to Sixth Schedule tribal areas.

**Article 243N:** inconsistent pre-existing Panchayat laws could continue for no more than one year after commencement unless sooner amended or repealed. Existing Panchayats could continue until expiry unless dissolved by the prescribed legislative resolution.

### 6. Why Article 243G is not a self-executing transfer

Article 243G permits a State legislature, by law, to endow Panchayats with powers and authority necessary to function as institutions of self-government, including preparation of plans and implementation of schemes relating to economic development and social justice, with reference to Eleventh Schedule matters. The words create a constitutional route and expectation; they do not automatically move all twenty-nine matters, staff and money on commencement day.

### Concept check

**Question:** Which part of the 73rd Amendment is strongest and which is structurally weakest?

**Model answer:** Its strongest part is the mandatory political-institutional floor: regular elected Panchayats, five-year tenure, reservation, SEC and SFC. Its structurally weaker part is operational self-government because Articles 243G and 243H rely on State legislation to devolve functions and authorise revenue. Thus constitutional status is uniform, while functional depth varies.

**Misconception to avoid:** Inclusion of a matter in the Eleventh Schedule is not proof that every State has transferred that matter with staff and finance to every Panchayat tier.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the mandatory–enabling design of Part IX. Does it explain the uneven performance of Panchayati Raj?

**Illustrative response:** Part IX combines a hard constitutional floor with a flexible State-law superstructure. Mandatory features include Panchayats at village and district levels, direct election to territorial seats, reservation for SCs, STs and women, five-year tenure, time-bound elections, an independent State Election Commission and a five-yearly State Finance Commission. These provisions prevent the earlier disappearance of elected local institutions.

Flexibility enters through the intermediate-tier exception for a State having a population not exceeding twenty lakh and through State-law control over Gram Sabha powers, village chairperson selection, backward-class reservation, accounts and audit details. More importantly, Article 243G enables rather than automatically effects the transfer of functions, while Article 243H allows State legislatures to authorise taxes, assignments, grants and funds. Staff control is not comprehensively constitutionalised.

The design has a federal rationale: local government is a State field and settlement patterns differ. Yet it also permits formal compliance without substantive devolution. A State may hold elections while retaining schemes, personnel and buoyant taxes in departments or parallel agencies.

Therefore, uneven performance is not accidental; it is partly built into the constitutional compromise. Part IX successfully secures representative continuity, but functionality depends on State political incentives, clear activity mapping, fiscal empowerment and local capacity.

**Quantified rubric (15):** accurate Article architecture 5; mandatory–enabling classification 4; federal rationale 2; causal link to variation 3; balanced verdict 1.

### Why Lesson 4 follows

An Article map remains abstract until its institutions are separated. The first operational distinction is between the people assembled in the Gram Sabha and the elected bodies organised across village, intermediate and district scales.

```text
        GRAM SABHA (243A)
   direct deliberative public forum

        ZILA PARISHAD (district)
```

### 1. Gram Sabha is not Gram Panchayat

Under Article 243, the Gram Sabha comprises persons registered in the electoral rolls relating to a village within the Panchayat area. Article 243A allows it to exercise powers and perform functions at village level as State law provides.

| Gram Sabha | Gram Panchayat |
|---|---|
| Body of all registered voters | Elected institution |
| Direct participation | Representative decision and administration |
| No separate election to membership | Members/chairperson selected under law |
| Functions depend on State law; PESA strengthens it in Scheduled Areas | Has statutory executive and developmental responsibilities |

Calling the Gram Sabha a “village parliament” is a useful analogy only if its legal limit is remembered: its actual ordinary-area powers are State-law dependent.

### 2. Why both direct and representative democracy are needed

The Gram Sabha can reveal local information, question spending, discuss beneficiary selection and legitimise priorities. The Gram Panchayat can act continuously, maintain records, contract works and coordinate officials. A mass meeting cannot administer every service daily; an elected council without a public forum may become insulated or captured.

> **Design principle:** Gram Sabha supplies voice and scrutiny; Gram Panchayat supplies continuous agency.

### 3. Article 243B and the three levels

Every State must constitute Panchayats at village, intermediate and district levels. A State whose population does not exceed **twenty lakh** may omit the intermediate level. The exception does not authorise omission of village or district Panchayats.

Names differ:

- village: Gram Panchayat;
- intermediate: Panchayat Samiti, Block Panchayat, Taluka Panchayat or Mandal;
- district: Zila Parishad.

The Constitution standardises levels, not nomenclature.

### 4. Why different tiers exist

| Scale | Suitable tasks | Coordination logic |
|---|---|---|
| Village | local assets, sanitation, beneficiary interface, community monitoring | high proximity and information |
| Intermediate | technical support, multi-village services, scheme coordination | economies across villages |
| District | integrated planning, inter-block priorities, departmental convergence | territorial overview |

This is a subsidiarity ladder, not a strict claim that every listed task belongs exclusively to one tier. State activity mapping should assign each component to the level with adequate scale and accountability.

### 5. Participation is a mechanism, not a ritual

A Gram Sabha meeting improves government only when citizens receive timely notice, intelligible records, a safe speaking environment and a response trail. Attendance alone does not prove influence. Women, Dalits, Adivasis, poorer households and migrants can face unequal voice even in formally open meetings.

```text
Notice + accessible agenda + disclosed records
        + inclusive facilitation + reasoned follow-up
        = meaningful participation

Meeting without these conditions
        = possible ritual compliance
```

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the Gram Sabha constitutionally important even though Article 243A leaves its powers to State law?

**Model answer:** It establishes the electorate itself as a recognised village-level institution and creates a constitutional space for direct participation, scrutiny and priority setting. The State-law dependence limits uniform power, but the body can connect citizens to the elected Gram Panchayat and make local administration publicly answerable. Its importance is therefore institutional, while its effectiveness is contingent.

**Misconception to avoid:** The Gram Sabha is neither the elected Gram Panchayat nor automatically vested everywhere with all powers that PESA gives in Fifth Schedule areas.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish Gram Sabha from Gram Panchayat and explain why their relationship determines the quality of grassroots democracy.

**Illustrative response:** The Gram Sabha comprises all registered voters of a village, whereas the Gram Panchayat is its elected representative institution. Article 243A constitutionally recognises the Sabha but leaves its powers to State law; Article 243B requires the Panchayat structure. The Sabha can deliberate on needs, scrutinise records and question beneficiary or expenditure choices. The Panchayat provides continuity, implements projects and coordinates officials. Their relationship therefore joins direct voice with representative agency. If the Sabha is reduced to ritual meetings, the Panchayat may become elite-controlled; if the Panchayat lacks authority, popular deliberation cannot become public action. Timely disclosure, inclusive meetings and response to Sabha decisions are consequently as important as formal convening. In PESA areas the Sabha has stronger statutory powers, but those should not be projected universally onto ordinary areas.

**Quantified rubric (10):** legal distinction 3; functional complementarity 3; failure modes 2; ordinary/PESA qualification 1; conclusion 1.

## Lesson 5 — Composition and Representation

Progress: 5 / 13 | Stage: Core | Dependency: who sits in each institution and through which mandate

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first: seat and chairperson routes

| Position | Constitutional route |
|---|---|
| Territorial seats at village level | Direct election |
| Territorial seats at intermediate level | Direct election |
| Territorial seats at district level | Direct election |
| Village Panchayat chairperson | Method determined by State law |
| Intermediate Panchayat chairperson | Elected by and from among elected members |
| District Panchayat chairperson | Elected by and from among elected members |
| MPs/MLAs and chairpersons represented at other levels | State law may provide representation under Article 243C |

### 1. Territorial representation

Article 243C leaves composition to State law but requires the ratio between the population of a territorial area and seats allotted by election to be, as far as practicable, uniform throughout the State. A Panchayat area is divided into territorial constituencies so that elected members represent local populations.

The constitutional phrase “all the seats in a Panchayat shall be filled by persons chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies” protects a direct electoral base at **all levels**. Yet this does not mean every person sitting in a Panchayat or every chairperson is directly elected.

### 2. Ex officio and cross-tier representation

State law may provide representation in Panchayats for:

- chairpersons of village Panchayats at the intermediate level;
- chairpersons of intermediate Panchayats at the district level;
- MPs and State legislators representing constituencies wholly or partly within the Panchayat area;
- Rajya Sabha and Legislative Council members registered as electors within the area.

This can improve vertical coordination. It can also allow higher-level politicians to overshadow the local mandate. The Constitution balances this by protecting directly elected territorial seats and specifying chairperson-election routes.

Article 243C(4) removes a further ambiguity: the chairperson and every other member of a Panchayat, **whether or not chosen by direct election from a territorial constituency**, has the right to vote in Panchayat meetings. The source of membership may differ, but meeting participation is not reduced to a merely ceremonial presence.

### 3. Direct mandate versus indirect leadership

```text
Citizen directly chooses territorial member
                  ↓
members at intermediate/district level choose chairperson
                  ↓
chairperson needs council confidence and coordination
```

Indirect election at upper tiers can facilitate coalition-building across constituencies. Its risk is bargaining detached from direct citizen choice. State-law design, transparency and anti-corruption safeguards determine the result.

### 4. Uniform ratio and representational equality

The “as far as practicable” population-seat ratio aims at roughly equal representation, but geography, remoteness and administrative feasibility matter. Delimitation is thus a technical and political act. Article 243O later limits judicial interruption of elections based on delimitation challenges, without making authorities free of legal duty.

### 5. Representation is not the same as effective voice

A seat can create access to agenda-setting, budgets, officials and public legitimacy. Yet effective representation requires:

- information in accessible form;
- training in law, finance and procedures;
- freedom from intimidation and proxy control;
- support staff and time;
- institutional respect from officials;
- channels to turn constituency needs into plans.

Thus composition answers **who enters**; capability and power answer **what entry can achieve**.

### Prelims traps

- “All members of Panchayats are directly elected” is overbroad because State law may include specified represented members.
- “All chairpersons are indirectly elected” is overbroad because the village chairperson’s method is left to State law.
- “Territorial seats are directly elected only at village level” is incorrect; they are directly elected at every tier.
- An MP’s possible representation in a Panchayat does not convert that MP into a territorially elected Panchayat member.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can it be simultaneously true that territorial seats at all three Panchayat levels are directly elected and that not every Panchayat position is directly elected?

**Model answer:** Article 243C mandates direct election for territorial seats at all levels. It separately permits State law to include specified legislators and lower-tier chairpersons and provides indirect election of intermediate and district chairpersons. The village chairperson’s route is State-law determined. Every chairperson and member, whether or not directly elected from a territorial constituency, may vote in Panchayat meetings. The direct-election rule therefore applies to territorial seats, not every office or represented member.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Directly elected Panchayats” does not mean every chairperson has a separate direct popular mandate.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how Article 243C combines direct representation with inter-tier coordination.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243C protects direct popular representation by requiring territorial seats at village, intermediate and district levels to be filled through direct election. It also seeks representational equality through a broadly uniform population-seat ratio. Coordination enters through State-law power to represent lower-tier chairpersons and MPs or State legislators at higher Panchayat levels. Intermediate and district chairpersons are elected by and from elected members, while the village-chair route is left to State law. Article 243C(4) gives every chairperson and member, whether or not territorially elected, voting rights in Panchayat meetings. This mixed design connects citizens directly to each tier while permitting vertical communication and collegial leadership. Its risk is that higher-level politicians or indirect bargaining may overshadow territorial members. Transparent chairperson elections and respect for the territorial mandate are therefore necessary.

**Quantified rubric (10):** direct-seat rule 3; chairperson rules 2; coordination provisions 2; critical assessment 2; precision/conclusion 1.

## Lesson 6 — Inclusion and Continuity: Reservation, Tenure, Dissolution and Disqualification

Progress: 6 / 13 | Stage: Core | Dependency: composition plus safeguards against exclusion and institutional extinction

### Visual first: inclusion and continuity together

```text
WHO GETS ENTRY?                         DOES THE BODY SURVIVE?
Article 243D                            Article 243E
SC/ST population-linked seats          fixed five-year duration
women: not less than one-third          election before expiry

backward class: State may provide       remainder-of-term rule
             \                          /
```

### 1. Article 243D: reservation architecture

**SC/ST seats:** Reserved in every Panchayat in proportion, as nearly as possible, to their population in the Panchayat area. Reserved seats may rotate among constituencies.

**Women’s seats:** Not less than one-third of the total seats filled by direct election in every Panchayat are reserved for women, including the number of seats reserved for SC/ST women. These seats may rotate.

**Chairperson offices:** Reserved for SCs and STs in a manner prescribed by State law, with the total across each level in the State broadly population-proportionate. Not less than one-third of all chairperson offices at each level are reserved for women. Article 243D(4) further requires the reserved chairperson offices to be **allotted by rotation to different Panchayats at each level**.

**Backward classes:** Article 243D(6) permits State legislation for reservation in favour of backward classes; it is enabling, unlike the mandatory SC/ST and women’s floors.

The constitutional expression is **not less than one-third**. State legislation may provide a higher share. The floor must not be misread as a ceiling or as a statement that every State uses only one-third.

### 2. Clause (5), Article 334 and the women’s-reservation exclusion

Article 243D(5) links the continuation of two categories to the period specified in Article 334:

- SC/ST seat reservations under clauses (1) and (2); and
- reservation of chairperson offices under clause (4), **other than reservation for women**.

Women’s seat reservation under clause (3) is outside this cessation rule, and clause (5) expressly excludes women’s chairperson reservation under clause (4). In the Constitution as in force in 2026, Article 334(a), as amended by the 104th Amendment, specifies **eighty years from the commencement of the Constitution** for SC/ST legislative-seat reservation. Accordingly, the Article 243D(5) categories remain operative in the present constitutional period and are scheduled to cease at the expiry of that Article 334 period—commonly stated as **25 January 2030**—unless the Constitution is amended again. This is a future cessation rule, not a claim that Panchayat SC/ST reservation has already ended.

### 3. The 83rd Amendment

The Constitution (Eighty-third Amendment) Act, 2000 inserted **Article 243M(3A)** so that Article 243D’s Scheduled Caste seat-reservation requirement does not apply to Arunachal Pradesh, where the Scheduled Caste population context differs. This is a narrow constitutional adjustment, not a general removal of reservation rules.

### 4. Representation: necessary but not sufficient

Reservation changes who can lawfully occupy public office. It can produce leadership experience, agenda change and symbolic transformation. Yet substantive empowerment may be constrained by:

- proxy exercise of office by male relatives;
- caste or local elite intimidation;
- low access to records and officials;
- rotation that weakens long-term constituency building;
- inadequate training, time and remuneration;
- social norms restricting mobility and speech.

The correct evaluation is neither “reservation solves patriarchy” nor “proxy cases prove reservation failed.” It is a structural opening whose outcomes depend on authority, capacity and social conditions.

### 5. Article 243E: five-year duration

A Panchayat continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting unless sooner dissolved under law. An amendment cannot dissolve an existing Panchayat before its term merely by changing the law.

Election must be completed:

- before expiry of the existing term; or
- before six months from dissolution.

If the remainder of the original term is less than six months, an election need not be held for that short period. A body elected after early dissolution serves only the **unexpired remainder**, not a fresh five-year term.

This closes the old route of indefinite supersession.

### 6. Article 243F: disqualification

A person is disqualified for membership if disqualified under the law applicable to elections to the State legislature, or under a State law made for Panchayats. However, a person who has attained **twenty-one years** cannot be disqualified merely for being below twenty-five.

Questions of disqualification are decided by the authority and procedure specified by State law. Eligibility age should not be confused with the State-legislature age rule.

### 7. Continuity can be undermined without formal dissolution

Even a regularly elected body may be hollowed out through delayed funds, vacant staff, parallel agencies or withheld functions. Article 243E secures **temporal continuity**; it does not by itself secure functional continuity.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should reservation and tenure be read together rather than as separate features?

**Model answer:** Reservation opens Panchayat office to groups historically excluded from power, while fixed tenure and time-bound elections ensure that the representative institution exists long enough to exercise that mandate. Article 243D(5) links specified SC/ST reservations to Article 334 but excludes women’s reservation from that cessation rule. Inclusion without continuity is symbolic; continuity without inclusion can preserve local hierarchy. Together the provisions constitutionalise access and institutional survival.

**Misconception to avoid:** A Panchayat elected after premature dissolution ordinarily serves the unexpired remainder of the old term, not an automatic new five-year term.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** Has reservation in Panchayati Raj converted descriptive representation into substantive empowerment? Discuss with constitutional safeguards and social limitations.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243D creates a major democratic opening. SC/ST seats are linked to local population; not less than one-third of directly elected seats, including SC/ST women’s seats, are reserved for women; chairperson offices also carry reservation; and States may provide backward-class reservation. Clause (5) links specified SC/ST reservations to Article 334 while expressly keeping women’s reservation outside that cessation rule. Article 243E complements inclusion through fixed tenure and time-bound elections.

This framework has descriptive value: excluded citizens enter formal authority, acquire political experience and can alter the visibility of water, health, welfare or violence-related concerns. Repeated participation may also weaken the belief that leadership is naturally male or upper-caste.

However, office does not ensure control over decisions. Proxy leadership, caste intimidation, dependence on officials, low fiscal discretion, weak training and rotation can dilute agency. These constraints do not show that reservation is futile; they show that representation operates within unequal social and institutional structures.

Substantive empowerment needs accessible records, capacity support, protection against coercion, predictable resources, stronger Gram Sabhas and party or community pathways beyond a single term. The 2019 GS-II demand on women and patriarchy should therefore receive a qualified verdict: reservation is an indispensable structural shock, but the transformation from presence to power depends on devolution and social change.

**Quantified rubric (15):** Article 243D architecture 4; positive mechanisms 3; limitations 4; remedies 2; qualified verdict 2.

### Visual first: election integrity chain

```text
State Election Commission (243K)

  of electoral rolls and Panchayat elections
```

### 1. Article 243K: State Election Commission

The superintendence, direction and control of preparation of electoral rolls and conduct of all Panchayat elections vest in the SEC. The Governor appoints the State Election Commissioner.

Independence safeguards include:

- removal only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a High Court judge;
- service conditions not varied to the Commissioner’s disadvantage after appointment;
- the Governor must make staff available when requested for electoral functions.

This is not the Election Commission of India. The ECI conducts elections to Parliament, State legislatures and specified constitutional offices; Panchayat elections belong to the SEC.

Article 243K(4) completes the framework: **subject to the Constitution**, the State Legislature may by law provide for all matters relating to, or in connection with, Panchayat elections. State election law supplies operational detail, but it remains subordinate to Part IX, constitutional rights and the SEC’s assigned field.

### 2. Article 243O: limited judicial interruption

Article 243O bars challenge to a law relating to delimitation of constituencies or allotment of seats and provides that a Panchayat election can be questioned only by an election petition presented to the authority and in the manner State law prescribes.

Its logic is sequencing:

```text
Do not stall the democratic calendar mid-process
                      ↓
complete election
                      ↓
use specialised statutory petition
                      ↓
judicial scrutiny at the legally proper stage
```

The provision is not a declaration that all electoral conduct is beyond law. It channels challenges so litigation does not casually derail constitutionally required elections.

### 3. Four cases as a doctrine map

| Case | Core principle for this topic | Exam-safe use |
|---|---|---|
| *Kishansing Tomar v. Municipal Corporation of Ahmedabad* (2006) | Timely local elections are a constitutional mandate; the SEC must act to preserve the schedule | Use for Article 243E/243K continuity |
| *K. Krishna Murthy v. Union of India* (2010) | Political reservation for backward classes in local bodies requires a distinct constitutional approach and empirical basis; aggregate reservation limits matter | Use to distinguish local political reservation from automatic extension of employment rules |
| *Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v. State of Maharashtra* (2021) | OBC local-body reservation requires the “triple test”: dedicated empirical inquiry, specification based on that inquiry, and compliance with the overall ceiling | Use for evidence-based backward-class reservation |
| *State of Goa v. Fouziya Imtiaz Shaikh* (2021) | SEC independence cannot be subordinated to executive control; an official holding a government post should not compromise institutional autonomy | Use for Article 243K independence |

The triple-test formulation should be expressed carefully: a dedicated commission conducts contemporaneous rigorous empirical inquiry into backwardness for local-body political reservation; the proportion is specified from its recommendations; and total SC/ST/OBC reservation must remain within the judicially stated aggregate limit.

### 4. Reservation, delimitation and election timing

States must complete preparatory tasks—delimitation, ward reservation, rolls—without using them as excuses to defeat Article 243E. At the same time, reservation must satisfy constitutional requirements. The solution is advance institutional planning, not election postponement.

### 5. Judicial control without judicial management

Courts protect constitutional boundaries, SEC independence and lawful reservation, but electoral administration remains institutionally assigned to the SEC. A strong answer avoids both extremes:

- “courts can never examine local elections”; and
- “ordinary litigation can interrupt every stage of the election”.

### Concept check

**Question:** How do Articles 243K and 243O jointly protect local democracy?

**Model answer:** Article 243K gives an independent SEC control over rolls and Panchayat elections, while Article 243O prevents routine judicial interruption through delimitation challenges and channels result disputes into statutory election petitions. Together they protect both autonomous administration and an orderly electoral calendar, without eliminating legal scrutiny.

**Misconception to avoid:** The bar in Article 243O is not an unlimited licence for unlawful elections or a complete extinction of judicial review.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate the constitutional independence of State Election Commissions in the light of judicial doctrine.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243K vests the superintendence, direction and control of Panchayat electoral rolls and elections in the State Election Commission. Appointment is by the Governor, but removal protection follows the High Court judge standard, service conditions cannot be worsened after appointment, and requested staff must be supplied. These safeguards recognise that local elections cannot depend on the convenience of the State executive.

Judicial doctrine strengthens this design. *Kishansing Tomar* treats timely local elections as a constitutional mandate, requiring preparatory action rather than indefinite postponement. *Fouziya Imtiaz Shaikh* protects the SEC from executive subordination and conflict-producing additional charge. *K. Krishna Murthy* and *Vikas Gawali* show that electoral autonomy coexists with constitutional discipline in backward-class reservation, including empirical inquiry and the triple test.

Article 243O complements autonomy by preventing delimitation litigation from interrupting elections and requiring election challenges to proceed through election petitions. It controls timing and forum rather than placing elections above law.

Operational weaknesses remain: dependence on State staff, delayed delimitation or reservation decisions and appointment practices can reduce autonomy. Advance election calendars, a permanent professional secretariat, transparent appointments and timely data for lawful reservation would make constitutional independence effective. The SEC is thus constitutionally protected, but its practical autonomy requires institutional insulation.

## Lesson 8 — From Subjects to Self-Government: Article 243G, Eleventh Schedule and Devolution

### Visual first: a subject list is not devolution

### 1. Article 243G’s two connected purposes

A State legislature may, by law, endow Panchayats with powers and authority necessary to function as institutions of self-government. Such law may cover:

1. preparation of plans for economic development and social justice; and
2. implementation of schemes for economic development and social justice, including matters in the Eleventh Schedule.

The provision is constitutionally significant because it states the destination—**institutions of self-government**—and identifies planning plus implementation. Its enabling wording means that the transfer must be operationalised through State law and subordinate arrangements.

### 2. All twenty-nine Eleventh Schedule matters

| Cluster | Matters |
|---|---|
| Natural resources and primary economy | 1 Agriculture including extension; 2 land improvement, land reforms, consolidation and soil conservation; 3 minor irrigation, water management and watershed development; 4 animal husbandry, dairying and poultry; 5 fisheries; 6 social and farm forestry; 7 minor forest produce |
| Rural production and habitat | 8 small-scale industries including food processing; 9 khadi, village and cottage industries; 10 rural housing; 11 drinking water; 12 fuel and fodder |
| Connectivity and energy | 13 roads, culverts, bridges, ferries, waterways and other communication; 14 rural electrification including distribution; 15 non-conventional energy |
| Human development | 16 poverty-alleviation programmes; 17 education including primary and secondary schools; 18 technical training and vocational education; 19 adult and non-formal education; 20 libraries; 21 cultural activities |
| Local economy and health | 22 markets and fairs; 23 health and sanitation including hospitals, primary health centres and dispensaries; 24 family welfare |
| Social justice and public systems | 25 women and child development; 26 social welfare including welfare of persons with disabilities; 27 welfare of weaker sections, especially SCs and STs; 28 public distribution system; 29 maintenance of community assets |

The Schedule groups domains suitable for local involvement. It does not say that every component of a domain must be performed at the same tier.

### 3. Activity mapping

“Health and sanitation” can contain village sanitation monitoring, block-level technical teams and district hospital coordination. “Roads” can range from a village lane to an inter-district road. Devolution therefore requires an **activity map**:

| Question | Example |
|---|---|
| What exact activity? | maintain village drinking-water asset |
| Which tier decides? | Gram Panchayat |
| Which tier supplies scale/technical support? | Block or district |
| Which staff answer for execution? | named engineer/operator |
| Which fund pays? | assigned untied/tied/own-source head |
| Who audits outcome? | accounts audit plus citizen/social scrutiny |

Without this specificity, a State may claim that a subject is “devolved” while the department retains decisions, personnel and budget.

### 4. The indivisibility of the 3Fs

```text
Function without fund       = unfunded mandate
Function without functionary = paper responsibility
Fund without function       = scheme-driven spending
All three without local discretion = agency, not self-government
```

Governance analysis often moves from the 3Fs to a fourth term: **functionality**. This asks whether the institution can actually produce services and accountable choices, not merely whether transfer orders exist.

### 5. Devolution Index Report 2024

The Ministry of Panchayati Raj’s named **Devolution Index Report 2024**, prepared through the Indian Institute of Public Administration, evaluates inter-State variation through six dimensions: framework, functions, finances, functionaries, capacity enhancement and accountability. Its safest use is diagnostic:

- devolution is multidimensional;
- State variation is constitutionally expected but can become substantive inequality;
- legal assignment must be tested against staff, finance, capacity and accountability.

Rankings can change with the reference period and methodology. The report’s multidimensional framework is therefore more useful for analysis than an undated league table.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Uniform devolution of all twenty-nine matters would ignore State diversity and economies of scale.

**Reply:** The constitutional case is not for identical micro-allocation. It is for transparent activity mapping, subsidiarity, minimum standards and alignment of authority with resources. Diversity justifies calibrated assignment, not institutional emptiness.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is saying “the Eleventh Schedule devolves twenty-nine subjects” constitutionally imprecise?

**Model answer:** The Schedule lists matters with reference to which State law may endow Panchayats under Article 243G. Actual devolution requires legal assignment of specific activities, appropriate tier allocation, staff, funds and accountability. The list creates a constitutional field and expectation, not automatic transfer.

**Misconception to avoid:** A State notification naming a subject does not by itself prove that decision-making authority, personnel and money have moved.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** “The Eleventh Schedule is a map of possible self-government, not a completed transfer deed.” Analyse.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243G permits State legislatures to endow Panchayats with authority necessary for self-government, including planning and scheme implementation relating to twenty-nine Eleventh Schedule matters. The Schedule covers natural resources, infrastructure, livelihoods, education, health and social justice. It therefore provides a broad constitutional map for local governance.

However, inclusion does not automatically transfer a department’s entire field. “Health” or “roads” contains activities at different scales. A functional settlement requires activity mapping among village, intermediate and district tiers, followed by staff control, budget assignment, technical support and accountability. A paper transfer without personnel produces responsibility without agency; without finance it produces an unfunded mandate.

The enabling wording preserves federal diversity and lets States match functions to geography and capacity. Yet it also allows formal compliance while departments or parallel bodies retain power. The Devolution Index Report 2024 appropriately examines not merely functions but finances, functionaries, capacity, accountability and the institutional framework.

The remedy is not mechanically identical devolution of all matters. States should publish justiciable or reviewable activity maps, align schemes and cadres, provide predictable untied resources and report outcomes tier-wise. The Schedule becomes self-government only when the 3Fs combine into functionality.

**Quantified rubric (15):** Article 243G meaning 3; all-domain understanding 2; activity mapping mechanism 4; enabling-design critique 3; reforms and verdict 3.

## Lesson 9 — Panchayat Finance: Article 243H, SFC and Own-Source Revenue

Progress: 9 / 13 | Stage: Advanced | Dependency: functions require revenue authority, transfers and fiscal review

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Articles 243H–243I, canonical finance notes and the Sixteenth Finance Commission’s local-body chapter.
🔍 CA Search: `"site:fincomindia.nic.in 16th Finance Commission report local body grants rural local bodies 2026 2031"`
📰 CA Found: **None in the last six months**
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first: four fiscal channels under Article 243H

```text
STATE LEGISLATURE MAY BY LAW
   ├── authorise Panchayat taxes, duties, tolls and fees
   ├── assign State-collected taxes, duties, tolls and fees
   ├── provide grants-in-aid from State Consolidated Fund
   └── constitute Panchayat Funds for receipts and withdrawals

Plus:
State Finance Commission (243I) reviews and recommends
Union Finance Commission route supports State resources for Panchayats
```

### 1. Article 243H is enabling but broad

The State legislature may authorise Panchayats to levy, collect and appropriate taxes, duties, tolls and fees; assign to them specified State revenues; provide grants-in-aid; and create Panchayat Funds. A Panchayat cannot invent a tax merely because local revenue is desirable: State law must provide authority and the tax must satisfy general constitutional limits.

### 2. Distinguish fiscal sources

| Source | Examples | Is it own-source revenue? |
|---|---|---|
| Own tax | house/property-related tax where authorised, market/fair levy, profession-related or local cess where lawful | Yes |
| Own non-tax | user charges, licence fees, rents, market/haat income, water or sanitation charges, income from Panchayat property | Yes |
| Assigned/shared revenue | State levy assigned or shared with Panchayat | Not locally imposed own tax; still non-grant revenue in a broader financing answer if accurately labelled |
| Grants | State grants, Union Finance Commission-linked grants, scheme transfers | No |
| Borrowing | limited and law-controlled | Finance, not recurrent own revenue |

The 2018 GS-II question specifically asks for sources apart from government grants. A good answer therefore foregrounds authorised taxes, fees, tolls, user charges, rents and community-asset income, while distinguishing assignments and borrowings.

### 3. Why own-source revenue matters

Own revenue can:

- give discretion for local priorities not covered by tied schemes;
- connect payment, service quality and citizen scrutiny;
- improve maintenance of assets;
- reduce exclusive dependence on higher governments;
- create better local registers of properties, businesses and services.

But local taxes can be regressive, administratively costly or politically unpopular. Poorer areas have weaker tax bases. Equalisation transfers remain essential; fiscal self-government is not fiscal abandonment.

### 4. Article 243I: State Finance Commission

The Governor constitutes an SFC within one year of the 73rd Amendment’s commencement and thereafter every fifth year. It reviews Panchayat finances and recommends:

- distribution between State and Panchayats of net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls and fees;
- allocation among Panchayat levels;
- taxes or revenues that may be assigned;
- grants-in-aid from the State Consolidated Fund;
- measures needed to improve Panchayat finances;
- other referred fiscal matters.

Under Article 243I(2), the State Legislature may by law specify the Commission’s **composition**, the qualifications required for appointment and the manner of selection. Article 243I(3) allows the Commission to determine its own procedure and gives it the powers that State law confers for performing its functions. The Governor lays the recommendations and an explanatory memorandum on action taken before the State legislature.

The SFC is the constitutional bridge between State fiscal capacity and local assignment. Delayed constitution, weak data, delayed reports and non-transparent action taken undermine that bridge.

### 5. Union Finance Commission interface

The Union Finance Commission’s constitutional terms include recommending measures needed to augment a State’s Consolidated Fund to supplement Panchayat resources on the basis of SFC recommendations. This is distinct from the State Finance Commission’s direct five-yearly review under Article 243I.

For the award period **2026–27 to 2030–31**, the Sixteenth Finance Commission action memorandum records accepted local-body recommendations including:

- national local-body grant division between rural and urban bodies in a 60:40 ratio;
- basic and performance components in an 80:20 ratio;
- entry conditions involving duly constituted bodies, timely provisional/audited accounts and regular SFC constitution with action taken reporting;
- within the basic component, half tied to sanitation/solid-waste and/or water management and half untied; performance components untied;
- transfer by States to local bodies within ten working days of receipt, with consequences for delay.

These are dated 2026–31 policy facts, not permanent constitutional ratios.

### 6. Fiscal failure mechanism

```text
weak tax mapping + politically difficult collection
          + State withholding buoyant bases
          + delayed SFC
          + tied scheme dominance
          + poor accounts
          = grant dependence and low local discretion
```

Reform requires better property and asset records, reasonable rates, transparent billing, digital and offline payment, hardship protection, collection accountability, predictable assignments and serious legislative consideration of SFC reports.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can greater own-source revenue and equalisation grants be complementary rather than contradictory?

**Model answer:** Own-source revenue gives Panchayats local discretion and strengthens the service–accountability link, but local tax capacity is unequal. Equalisation grants allow poorer jurisdictions to provide minimum services. A sound system combines genuine local effort with predictable transfers so autonomy does not become geographic inequality.

**Misconception to avoid:** Finance Commission grants are not own-source revenue, and Article 243H does not itself authorise every Panchayat to impose any tax it chooses.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess the working of Panchayati Raj with special reference to non-grant financing of Panchayat projects.

**Illustrative response:** The 73rd Amendment secured regular institutions but did not guarantee fiscal autonomy. Article 243H enables State law to authorise Panchayat taxes, duties, tolls and fees, assign State revenues, provide grants and create Panchayat Funds. Article 243I requires a five-yearly State Finance Commission to recommend tax sharing, assignments, grants and fiscal improvement.

For projects apart from government grants, Panchayats can use authorised house or property-related taxes, market and fair levies, user charges for water or sanitation, licence and registration fees, tolls, rents from buildings, income from haats, ponds, fisheries and other community assets, and carefully regulated borrowing. Assigned State taxes should be separately labelled because they are not locally imposed own taxes.

These sources increase local discretion, maintenance incentives and accountability. Yet narrow rural tax bases, outdated registers, political reluctance, low collection capacity, delayed SFCs and State retention of buoyant taxes sustain grant dependence. User charges may also exclude the poor unless designed with safeguards.

Reform should combine GIS/record-based assessment, transparent rates, easy payment, exemptions or lifeline use, professional collection, asset monetisation without dispossession and predictable State assignments. Equalisation grants must continue for poorer areas. Panchayat finance fails less for want of constitutional channels than for weak State authorisation, administration and fiscal accountability.

**Quantified rubric (15):** constitutional architecture 4; correctly classified non-grant sources 4; importance 2; constraints 3; balanced reforms/verdict 2.

### Why Lesson 10 follows

Revenue capacity is not an outcome by itself. Money must be converted into participatory plans, lawful transactions, reliable accounts and correctable public action, which brings planning and accountability into the chain.

## Lesson 10 — Planning, Accounts and Accountability: GPDP, Audit, Social Audit and Digital Workflows

Progress: 10 / 13 | Stage: Advanced | Dependency: authority and money must become plans, records, execution and scrutiny

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Article 243J, GPDP, MGNREGA Section 17 and prescribed accountability material.
🔍 CA Search: `"official Panchayat reform digital governance RGSA SVAMITVA September 2026 India"`
📰 CA Found: **“Building Viksit Bharat from Ground Up: 12 Years of Panchayat Reforms and Innovation” — published 15 June 2026**
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first: the local public-finance loop

```text
Gram Sabha needs and evidence
            ↓
Gram Panchayat Development Plan
            ↓
resource envelope and convergence
            ↓
technical approval / work execution
            ↓
eGramSwaraj records and payments workflow
            ↓
accounts + asset/work records
            ↓
AuditOnline / statutory audit
            ↓
social audit and Gram Sabha questioning
            ↓
recovery, correction, grievance and next plan
```

### 1. GPDP as a process, not a shopping list

The Gram Panchayat Development Plan should integrate local priorities with available resources and scheme norms. A credible cycle includes:

1. situation analysis and household/community evidence;
2. Gram Sabha prioritisation, including excluded groups;
3. mapping of own revenue, grants and scheme resources;
4. technical and environmental feasibility;
5. convergence across departments;
6. approval, implementation and public disclosure;
7. monitoring and feedback into the next plan.

The Ministry’s FY **2026–27** planning campaign continues digital planning linkages. The current relevance is the workflow—participatory planning connected to budget, progress and evidence—not a changing count of uploaded plans.

Kerala’s **People’s Plan Campaign**, launched in **1996**, is a bounded illustration of participatory decentralised planning: Gram Sabhas, elected local governments, working groups and technical scrutiny were connected in a planning sequence. It shows that participation becomes administratively consequential when citizen priorities are linked to devolved planning authority and resources. It is a Kerala experience, not a claim that every State adopted the same fiscal or institutional model.

### 2. Article 243J

State legislatures may by law make provisions regarding maintenance of Panchayat accounts and audit. The constitutional framework deliberately leaves procedure to State law, but audit is indispensable because Panchayats handle public money from several sources.

Audit asks whether transactions are authorised, recorded and compliant. Performance and social accountability ask whether expenditure produced intended and fairly distributed results. These are related but not identical.

### 3. Statutory audit and AuditOnline

AuditOnline is an official digital application intended to support audit processes for Panchayati Raj institutions, including scheduling, observations and follow-up. Its governance value lies in traceability:

```text
audit observation
   → responsible authority
   → response / compliance
   → recovery or correction
   → closure trail
```

A portal entry, however, is not self-proving. Data quality, auditor independence, timely response and public accessibility determine usefulness.

### 4. Social audit

Social audit is public verification of records, works, beneficiaries and outcomes by or with the affected community. Under **Section 17 of MGNREGA**, the Gram Sabha monitors works and conducts regular social audits, while the Gram Panchayat makes relevant records available.

| Financial/statutory audit | Social audit |
|---|---|
| Professional/legal review of accounts and compliance | Community-facing verification of records and lived outcomes |
| Tests vouchers, authorisation and accounting | Tests whether listed work, worker, asset or benefit exists and is fair |
| Periodic institutional process | Public hearing and participatory process |
| May identify irregular expenditure | May reveal ghost entries, wage delay, poor quality or exclusion |

Social audit is not merely a public meeting. It needs independent facilitation, complete records, field verification, safe testimony, reasoned findings and enforceable follow-up.

The **Meghalaya Community Participation and Public Services Social Audit Act, 2017** provides a second bounded example. It institutionalises community participation and social audit for covered public services and programmes within Meghalaya. Its value here is the statutory connection between public verification and corrective action; it is neither a nationwide Panchayat law nor a substitute for Article 243J accounts and audit.

### 5. eGramSwaraj and integrated workflow

eGramSwaraj supports local planning, progress reporting, accounting and related governance workflows. Its value is integration: a plan item can be linked to expenditure and progress rather than existing in isolated paper registers. It can improve standardisation and higher-level visibility.

Risks include:

- connectivity and device constraints;
- data entry by intermediaries without local understanding;
- “portal completion” replacing substantive deliberation;
- centralised templates narrowing local priorities;
- exclusion of citizens without digital access;
- inaccurate records becoming durable at scale.

Digital systems should produce citizen-readable disclosures and retain offline routes.

### 6. SabhaSaar and meeting intelligence

The Ministry’s 2026 digital linkage around **SabhaSaar** illustrates the use of technology to summarise Gram Sabha proceedings. Such assistance can reduce documentation burden and make decisions searchable, but any automated summary must be checked against the actual meeting, local language and signed record. Technology cannot decide whose voice was ignored.

### 7. SVAMITVA’s limited but useful relevance

SVAMITVA uses drone and other survey processes to support rural inhabited-area property records and property cards. For Panchayats, better records may support planning and an authorised property-related revenue base. It does not itself create a tax, settle every title conflict or guarantee collection. The legal authority still comes from State law.

### Accountability stack

```text
Election accountability
 + Gram Sabha deliberation
 + transparent plan and budget
 + transaction accounts
 + independent audit
 + social audit
 + grievance and enforceable follow-up
 = answerable local government
```

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does digitising Panchayat plans and accounts not automatically create accountability?

**Model answer:** Digitisation can improve traceability, integration and disclosure, but accountability also requires accurate data, understandable public access, independent scrutiny, citizen voice, reasoned responses and enforceable correction. A completed portal field may document a process without proving that deliberation, work or outcome was genuine.

**Misconception to avoid:** Social audit is not a substitute for statutory financial audit, and a digital record is not conclusive evidence of field performance.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain how participatory planning, audit and digital workflows can convert Panchayats from spending agencies into accountable governments.

**Illustrative response:** A Panchayat becomes governmental when it can identify priorities, allocate resources, execute decisions and answer publicly for results. The GPDP cycle should begin with evidence and inclusive Gram Sabha deliberation, map available grants and own revenue, test technical feasibility and converge departmental resources. This makes planning more than a list of desired works.

Article 243J permits State law on accounts and audit. Statutory audit tests authorisation, records and compliance; AuditOnline can add scheduling and follow-up traceability. Social audit performs a different task: community verification of works, workers, beneficiaries and outcomes. MGNREGA Section 17 anchors Gram Sabha monitoring and record availability.

eGramSwaraj can connect plan, physical progress and accounts. SVAMITVA-linked records may improve planning and an authorised revenue base, while meeting-support tools can reduce documentation burden. Yet portal completion can become ritual, inaccurate data can scale, and digital exclusion can silence citizens.

The safeguards are citizen-readable disclosure, offline access, independent facilitation, local-language records, data correction, time-bound audit compliance and grievance remedies. Technology should shorten the chain from decision to evidence to correction. It cannot replace devolution, deliberation or institutional responsibility.

**Quantified rubric (15):** GPDP cycle 3; audit distinctions 4; digital mechanisms 3; risks 3; safeguards/verdict 2.

### Why Lesson 11 follows

The ordinary Part IX workflow assumes that standard administrative villages and State-designed institutions can carry local choice. Scheduled Areas raise a deeper question of custom, habitation and community control, requiring PESA’s modified framework.

## Lesson 11 — PESA and Scheduled-Area Panchayats

Progress: 11 / 13 | Stage: Advanced | Dependency: ordinary Part IX design must be adapted to tribal self-governance

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — PESA Act 40 of 1996, Article 243M, scheduled-area notes and prescribed Laxmikanth pages.
🔍 CA Search: `"official PESA Gram Sabha Scheduled Areas rules 2026 India Panchayat"`
📰 CA Found: **“पंचायत उपबंध (अनुसूचित क्षेत्रों पर विस्तार) झारखंड नियमावली, 2025” — official MoPR document page dated 27 July 2026**
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first: scope before power

```text
PART IX ordinarily applies
          │
          ├── Article 243M excludes Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas
          │
          ├── PESA Act, 1996 extends a modified framework
          │        ONLY to Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas
          │
          └── Sixth Schedule tribal areas remain outside this extension

PESA village/Gram Sabha
  customary community + habitation logic
  local resources + culture + plans + accountability
```

### 1. Why PESA was necessary

Ordinary administrative villages and Panchayat structures may not reflect tribal habitation, customary institutions or community control over land and forests. PESA—the Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996—extends Part IX to Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas with modifications designed to protect self-governance.

PESA legislation must be consistent with customary law, social and religious practices and traditional management of community resources. A village may consist of a habitation or group of habitations managing affairs according to traditions and customs.

### 2. Gram Sabha foundation

Every village has a Gram Sabha comprising persons whose names are included in village electoral rolls. PESA makes it competent to safeguard:

- traditions and customs;
- cultural identity;
- community resources;
- customary mode of dispute resolution.

It approves plans, programmes and projects for social and economic development before Panchayat implementation and is responsible for identification or selection of beneficiaries under poverty-alleviation and other programmes. It also certifies utilisation of funds by the village Panchayat for such plans and programmes.

### 3. Section 4 power-and-verb map

| Field | Legal relationship |
|---|---|
| Development plans/programmes/projects | Gram Sabha approval before village Panchayat implementation |
| Beneficiary identification | Gram Sabha responsibility |
| Utilisation certification | Gram Sabha certification |
| Land acquisition and resettlement/rehabilitation | Consultation with Gram Sabha or appropriate Panchayat before acquisition; planning/implementation coordinated at State level |
| Minor-water-body planning/management | Entrusted to appropriate Panchayat |
| Minor-mineral prospecting licence or mining lease | Prior recommendation of Gram Sabha or appropriate Panchayat mandatory |
| Minor-mineral concession by auction | Prior recommendation mandatory |
| Intoxicants | power to enforce prohibition or regulate/restrict sale and consumption |
| Minor forest produce | ownership |
| Land alienation | power to prevent alienation and take appropriate action to restore unlawfully alienated land |
| Village markets | power to manage |
| Money-lending to Scheduled Tribes | power to exercise control |
| Social sectors | control over institutions and functionaries |
| Local plans and resources | control including tribal sub-plans, as law provides |

### 4. Consultation is not universal consent

PESA uses different verbs for different matters. Land acquisition and rehabilitation invoke **consultation**. Minor-mineral licences, leases and concessions require **prior recommendation**. Other provisions speak of ownership, control, approval or safeguarding.

```text
consultation ≠ consent ≠ prior recommendation ≠ ownership
```

The statute should not be rewritten as one universal Gram Sabha veto. Equally, consultation must be genuine, informed and timely rather than a post-decision ritual.

### 5. Reservation and leadership in Scheduled Areas

PESA requires reservation to be consistent with population, with Scheduled Tribe reservation not less than one-half of total seats, and all chairperson seats at all Panchayat levels reserved for Scheduled Tribes. Where Scheduled Tribes have no representation at the intermediate or district level, the State Government may nominate members of those communities, but such nomination must not exceed **one-tenth of the total members to be elected** at that level.

Higher-level Panchayats must not assume powers of a lower Panchayat or Gram Sabha. Section 4(o) requires State legislation, while designing the administrative arrangements in Panchayats at the district level in Scheduled Areas, to **endeavour to follow the pattern of the Sixth Schedule**. This is a legislative design direction within PESA’s Fifth Schedule setting; it neither imports the Sixth Schedule wholesale nor converts PESA areas into autonomous districts.

### 6. PESA’s own transition rule

PESA contains a statutory transition that must be kept separate from Article 243N. A provision of a Panchayat law already in force in Scheduled Areas immediately before PESA received presidential assent could continue despite inconsistency only until it was amended or repealed, or until **one year from assent**, whichever occurred earlier.

Existing Panchayats continued until the expiry of their duration unless sooner dissolved by a resolution of the State Legislative Assembly; in a bicameral State, dissolution required a resolution of **each House**. PESA received presidential assent on **24 December 1996**, so this one-year window is PESA-specific. Article 243N is the distinct constitutional transition tied to Part IX’s commencement.

### 7. PESA and the Forest Rights Act, 2006

PESA supplies a self-governance and resource-control framework; the FRA recognises specified individual and community forest rights and gives the Gram Sabha a central claims role. They can reinforce each other in minor forest produce, community forest resources and protection against alienation.

They are not interchangeable:

| PESA | FRA |
|---|---|
| Local self-governance statute for Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas | Forest-rights recognition statute with broader territorial application conditions |
| Panchayat/Gram Sabha powers and safeguards | Recognition and vesting of eligible forest rights |
| Custom, markets, money-lending, plans and specified resources | Individual/community tenure and use rights plus conservation responsibilities |

### 8. Implementation gap

State conformity laws or notified rules do not prove compliance. Problems include inconsistent definitions of village, departmental retention of control, weak recognition of customary institutions, incomplete transfer over markets or minor forest produce, formal consultation and poor awareness.

The Ministry’s live State-wise PESA-rules directory is useful for legal status. Within the six-month search window, it records **“पंचायत उपबंध (अनुसूचित क्षेत्रों पर विस्तार) झारखंड नियमावली, 2025”**, dated **27 July 2026**. That official listing supports only the existence and date of the named Jharkhand rules document; field implementation or substantive outcomes require separate evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it legally unsafe to summarise PESA as “the Gram Sabha has veto over all projects in Scheduled Areas”?

**Model answer:** PESA assigns different legal relationships to different fields: approval of local development plans, beneficiary identification, consultation before land acquisition and rehabilitation, mandatory prior recommendation for minor-mineral licences and concessions, and ownership or control over specified resources and institutions. These verbs carry different consequences. PESA strengthens self-governance but does not create one universal veto formula.

**Misconception to avoid:** PESA extends Part IX to Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas, not to Sixth Schedule tribal areas.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** PESA constitutionalises neither a symbolic consultation nor an unlimited village veto. Discuss its differentiated self-governance design and implementation limits.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243M excludes Scheduled Areas from ordinary Part IX application. PESA 1996 extends a modified Panchayat framework to Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas, requiring consistency with customary law, cultural identity and traditional community-resource management.

Its design is differentiated. The Gram Sabha safeguards traditions and community resources, approves local development plans, identifies beneficiaries and certifies utilisation. It owns minor forest produce and receives powers relating to land alienation, village markets, money-lending, intoxicants, social-sector institutions and local plans. Before land acquisition and rehabilitation it must be consulted. For minor-mineral prospecting licences, leases and auction concessions, prior recommendation is mandatory. These distinct verbs should neither be diluted into ceremonial hearing nor inflated into a universal veto.

PESA also strengthens ST representation, prevents higher Panchayat tiers from taking over lower-tier powers and requires State legislation to endeavour to follow the Sixth Schedule pattern when designing district-level arrangements. Its separate transition preserved inconsistent Scheduled-Area Panchayat laws for at most one year from assent and existing Panchayats for their duration unless dissolved by the prescribed legislative resolution. The Forest Rights Act can complement it through recognition of individual and community forest rights, but the statutes are not interchangeable.

Implementation is weakened by State-law dilution, non-habitation-based village definitions, departmental control, poor awareness, formal consultation and weak follow-up. Reform requires conformity-law audits, recognition of customary Gram Sabhas, prior disclosure in local languages, reasoned responses, restoration mechanisms and alignment with FRA processes. PESA’s promise is legally substantial but institutionally contingent.

**Quantified rubric (15):** scope/customary basis 3; complete differentiated powers 6; FRA distinction 2; implementation limits 2; reforms/verdict 2.

PESA makes the gap between legal recognition and field authority especially visible, but that gap is not confined to Scheduled Areas. The next lesson generalises the diagnosis: why constitutional status across India has not uniformly produced functional self-government.

## Lesson 12 — Why Constitutional Status Has Not Produced Functionality

Progress: 12 / 13 | Stage: Advanced | Dependency: compare constitutional form with operational power

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical functionality analysis, Devolution Index methodology and prescribed implementation discussion.
🔍 CA Search: `"official Panchayat Advancement Index 2.0 released 24 April 2026"`
📰 CA Found: **“Panchayat Advancement Index (PAI) 2.0 Report Out: 3635 Panchayats Emerge as Front Runners” — published 29 April 2026; report released at the 24 April 2026 event**
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first: the missing middle

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL STATUS
regular bodies + reservation + SEC + SFC
                 ↓
        [MISSING MIDDLE]
clear activity maps
accountable staff
predictable discretionary finance
technical capacity
data and audit follow-up
inclusive Gram Sabhas
                 ↓
FUNCTIONALITY
responsive services + local choice + answerability
```

### 1. Political decentralisation moved farther than administrative and fiscal devolution

Part IX made elected Panchayats difficult to abolish and normalised representation. But States continue to control:

- which activities actually move under Article 243G;
- which taxes are authorised under Article 243H;
- cadres, transfers and disciplinary control;
- design of many schemes and parallel bodies;
- response to SFC recommendations.

The system can consequently produce **constitutional presence without governing command**.

### 2. The functionality failure chain

| Constraint | Mechanism | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Vague functional assignment | Multiple departments and tiers claim the same task | blame shifting |
| Staff controlled upward | local representatives cannot direct implementation | weak responsiveness |
| Tied/grant-heavy finance | spending follows scheme categories | little local choice |
| Weak own revenue | low discretionary pool | poor maintenance and dependence |
| Delayed SFC | outdated or ignored fiscal settlement | unpredictable transfers |
| Parallel agencies | authority bypasses elected body | democratic hollowing |
| Capacity deficit | complex procurement/accounting overwhelms small bodies | error or official domination |
| Unequal Gram Sabha voice | elites shape priorities | exclusion and capture |
| Audit without follow-up | findings do not trigger correction | impunity |

### 3. Capacity is not a neutral excuse

States sometimes withhold power because Panchayats lack capacity. Yet capacity often grows through exercising real responsibility. The opposite sequence—demanding full capacity before any devolution—can become permanent paternalism.

```text
power withheld because capacity is low
        ↓
no opportunity to learn or recruit
        ↓
capacity remains low
        ↓
power continues to be withheld
```

The solution is sequenced devolution with training, shared technical pools, standard processes and mentoring—not indefinite non-devolution.

### 4. Unequal local society

Local democracy operates amid caste, class, gender and land inequalities. Elite capture may influence beneficiary lists, contracts, commons and meeting speech. Reservation and disclosure reduce but do not erase these structures. Social-justice safeguards, independent grievance channels and facilitation for marginalised groups are integral to decentralisation, not external additions.

### 5. Incentives of State-level actors

Devolution redistributes patronage, staff authority, contracting influence and political credit. Departments and State-level representatives may rationally resist it. This explains why a technically sound committee report may remain unimplemented.

> **Analytical move:** diagnose non-devolution as a political-institutional incentive problem, not merely an awareness gap.

### 6. Dated diagnostic evidence

The **Devolution Index Report 2024** uses six dimensions—framework, functions, finances, functionaries, capacity enhancement and accountability—confirming that no single transfer proves devolution.

The official **Panchayat Advancement Index 2.0 report for FY 2023–24** organises local performance around the localisation of Sustainable Development Goals and thematic measurement. Such indices can identify gaps and support comparison, but they do not by themselves establish causal impact. Data quality, indicator choice and unequal starting conditions must be examined.

### 7. The 2020 and 2022 PYQ logic

The 2020 GS-II demand asks why local institutions must move from their formative “functions, functionaries and funds” phase to **functionality**. The answer needs the missing-middle mechanisms above.

The 2022 GS-II demand asks the extent to which decentralisation has changed grassroots governance. A defensible answer is graded:

- substantially in electoral continuity and representation;
- unevenly in participatory planning and accountability;
- incompletely in administrative command and fiscal autonomy.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Panchayats are too small and vulnerable to capture for major responsibilities.

**Reply:** Scale should determine tier assignment and technical pooling, while capture calls for transparency, rights and oversight. Neither problem justifies bypassing elected government wholesale.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “lack of capacity” an incomplete explanation for weak Panchayat performance?

**Model answer:** Capacity matters, but it is partly produced by authority, resources and repeated practice. Panchayats cannot build governing capability if staff, decisions and funds remain elsewhere. Weak functionality also reflects political incentives, vague assignment, tied finance, parallel bodies, social inequality and weak accountability. Capacity is one link in a system, not a self-contained cause.

**Misconception to avoid:** Regular elections and high plan-upload rates do not by themselves prove functional self-government.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (15 marks, 250 words):** “Constitutional status has produced Panchayats, but not uniformly produced Panchayati Raj.” Critically examine.

**Illustrative response:** The 73rd Amendment transformed local democracy by securing elected institutions, reservation, five-year tenure, the State Election Commission and the State Finance Commission. It made Panchayats durable and widened descriptive representation. In this political-institutional sense, constitutionalisation succeeded.

Yet Panchayati Raj implies governing authority, not institutional presence alone. Article 243G leaves functional assignment to State law; Article 243H similarly depends on State authorisation. Incomplete activity mapping, upward-controlled staff, tied grants, weak own revenue, delayed SFCs and parallel agencies separate responsibility from authority. Capacity deficits and unequal Gram Sabha voice further reduce functionality.

The problem is self-reinforcing. States cite low capacity to withhold power, but institutions denied responsibility cannot learn. Departments and higher-level politicians also lose patronage and control when devolution deepens, creating resistance. The Devolution Index Report 2024’s multidimensional framework appropriately tests functions, finance, functionaries, capacity and accountability together.

Nevertheless, unevenness is not inevitable. State experiences with deeper participatory planning show that clear functions, predictable finance and political commitment matter. Reform needs published activity maps, local cadres or accountable staff pools, SFC discipline, untied resources, capacity tied to real responsibilities and enforceable audit. The amendment constitutionalised the arena; State-level devolution determines whether genuine Panchayati Raj occurs within it.

**Quantified rubric (15):** achievements 3; 3F/functionality diagnosis 5; political/social mechanisms 3; evidence 2; reforms and critical verdict 2.

### Why Lesson 13 follows

Once dysfunction is traced to misaligned authority, staff, finance, capacity and accountability, the final task is constructive: sequence reforms and convert the complete diagnosis into an exam-ready, qualified synthesis.

### 1. Reform sequence: from transfer order to enforceable settlement

**Functions**

- publish tier-specific activity maps;
- attach service standards and responsible authorities;
- prevent parallel agencies from exercising transferred functions without Panchayat accountability;
- require periodic devolution review through State legislatures.

**Functionaries**

- establish Panchayat cadres where suitable or clearly place shared staff under local functional control;
- create block/district technical pools for engineering, planning, accounts and data;
- protect professional standards while making work priorities locally answerable.

**Finances**

- constitute SFCs on time and table reports with reasoned action memoranda;
- authorise realistic own taxes and non-tax revenues;
- modernise registers and collection with equity safeguards;
- ensure predictable, timely and adequately untied transfers;
- link responsibilities to expenditure authority.

**Participation and accountability**

- schedule Gram Sabhas with advance records and inclusive facilitation;
- connect decisions to GPDP budgets and publish reasons for non-acceptance;
- integrate financial audit, social audit and grievance closure;
- protect whistle-blowers and marginalised speakers.

### 2. Capacity: Revamped RGSA

The Revamped Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan provides a current setting for discussing institutional training. For this lesson, the relevant analytical questions are whether elected representatives and staff practise planning, financial management, inclusive deliberation and coordination on real local work, and whether support continues after classroom instruction. Its duration, formal objective and funding pattern are not used as teaching claims here.

Capacity building must be continuous because elected cohorts change and responsibilities evolve. Training should be role-based, practised on real plans and accounts, and followed by on-the-job support. Completion certificates do not prove capability.

### 8. Final synthesis

Panchayati Raj is best described through three propositions:

1. **Constitutional achievement:** regular rural elected institutions, inclusion and independent electoral/fiscal-review bodies are irreversible democratic gains.
2. **Federal incompleteness:** the Constitution deliberately leaves functional and fiscal depth to State law, generating variation and space for hollow compliance.
3. **Reform direction:** move from institution-counting to aligned authority, capability, discretionary resources and enforceable public accountability.

### Concept check

**Question:** What single test should be applied to every Panchayat reform, whether fiscal, administrative or digital?

**Model answer:** Ask whether the reform aligns responsibility with actual decision authority, accountable personnel, usable resources and citizen-verifiable consequences at the appropriate tier. If it only adds reporting, training or a portal while decisions remain elsewhere, it improves administration at most, not self-government.

**Misconception to avoid:** Digitalisation, capacity building and grants can support devolution, but none is a substitute for legally assigned authority.

### Original lesson Mains practice

**Mains prompt (20 marks, 250 words):** Suggest a sequenced reform strategy to move Indian Panchayats from constitutional status to accountable self-government in the digital era.

**Illustrative response:** Part IX secured political continuity but left much functional and fiscal devolution to States. Reform must therefore align law, capability, money and accountability rather than add isolated schemes.

First, States should publish tier-wise activity maps under Article 243G, identify service standards and prevent parallel bodies from bypassing elected institutions. Second, functionaries should follow functions through Panchayat cadres or shared technical pools under clear local functional control. Capacity programmes such as Revamped RGSA should use real plans, accounts and works, with mentoring rather than one-off certification.

Third, fiscal reform should combine realistic Article 243H own revenues with equalisation transfers, timely SFCs and reasoned legislative action. Sixteenth Finance Commission conditions on constituted bodies, accounts and SFC regularity can strengthen institutions, provided compliant Panchayats are not penalised for State failure.

Fourth, GPDP decisions should connect to eGramSwaraj records, AuditOnline findings, social audit and time-bound grievance closure. SVAMITVA-derived records may aid planning and authorised revenue, but do not themselves create taxation or settle every title.

Fifth, PESA reform needs verb-accurate conformity laws, informed consultation, prior recommendation where required and FRA alignment.

Digital tools should reduce information asymmetry while preserving offline access and local choice. The sequence is devolution, supported capability, finance, transparent execution and enforceable correction. Technology must serve self-government, not convert Panchayats into data-entry units.

**Quantified rubric (20):** constitutional diagnosis 3; functions/functionaries reform 4; fiscal reform 4; participation/accountability/digital safeguards 4; PESA/inclusion 2; sequencing and balanced conclusion 3.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Block A — Lessons 1–3: origin, evolution and architecture

**Cumulative question:** Why did India need constitutionalisation after four decades of rural-development programmes and committee reform?

**Concise model answer:** Administrative programmes and statutory Panchayats could be bureaucratised, underfunded, superseded or denied elections. Committees progressively identified elected tiers, district planning, Gram Sabha participation and constitutional protection. Part IX then secured a mandatory political floor while retaining State flexibility over operational devolution.

**Cumulative question:** Construct the mandatory–enabling distinction in one sentence.

**Concise model answer:** Part IX mandates institutional existence, representation, tenure, SEC and SFC, but largely enables State law to specify Gram Sabha powers, functions, taxes, staff arrangements and several composition details.

## Block B — Lessons 4–7: institutions, inclusion and elections

**Cumulative question:** How do Gram Sabha, reservation, tenure and the SEC form one democratic chain?

**Concise model answer:** The Gram Sabha creates direct citizen voice; reservation makes representation socially inclusive; fixed tenure prevents institutional disappearance; and the SEC protects timely electoral renewal. Weakness in any link reduces the legitimacy of the whole system.

**Cumulative question:** State the proper relationship between electoral autonomy and courts.

**Concise model answer:** The SEC autonomously administers elections, Article 243O prevents disruptive mid-process litigation and routes election disputes to petitions, while constitutional courts continue to protect timing, lawful reservation and SEC independence at the proper stage.

## Block C — Lessons 8–10: functions, finance and accountability

**Cumulative question:** What converts an Eleventh Schedule matter into a functional local service?

**Concise model answer:** State-law assignment must be followed by tier-specific activity mapping, accountable staff, usable funds, standards, records, audit and citizen scrutiny.

**Cumulative question:** Why should own-source revenue, grants and audit be designed together?

**Concise model answer:** Own revenue supplies discretion and a local accountability link; grants equalise unequal tax bases and support national priorities; audit and social scrutiny ensure that both are lawfully and effectively used.

## Block D — Lessons 11–13: PESA, dysfunction and reform

**Cumulative question:** What is the safest way to explain PESA powers?

**Concise model answer:** Identify the precise statutory verb for each field—approval, consultation, prior recommendation, ownership, control, safeguarding or certification—rather than claiming either a universal veto or merely symbolic participation.

**Cumulative question:** Give the minimum viable reform sequence.

**Concise model answer:** Clarify functions, place staff under accountable arrangements, match funds and fiscal authority, build capability through real work, publish integrated records, enable inclusive participation and enforce audit/grievance correction.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Solved 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why regular Panchayat elections are necessary but insufficient for democratic decentralisation.

**Model solution:** Regular elections are indispensable because Articles 243E and 243K protect continuity, public mandate and periodic accountability. Reservation under Article 243D also ensures that the elected arena includes historically excluded groups. Without timely elections, no local self-government can exist.

Yet elections alone create representatives, not governing capacity. Article 243G functions and Article 243H revenue depend substantially on State law. If departments retain staff, grants are excessively tied, SFCs are delayed and parallel agencies execute schemes, elected bodies cannot convert preferences into action. Weak Gram Sabhas, inaccessible records and audit without follow-up further reduce accountability.

Democratic decentralisation therefore requires an aligned chain: elected authority, clear activity maps, accountable functionaries, predictable and discretionary finance, inclusive planning and enforceable scrutiny. Elections are the constitutional gateway; functionality determines whether citizens receive self-government rather than periodic selection of powerless intermediaries.

**Rubric (10):** electoral necessity 3; insufficiency through 3Fs 3; accountability dimension 2; integrated conclusion 2.

## Solved 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine whether the State Finance Commission is the missing constitutional bridge in Panchayat finance.

**Model solution:** Article 243I requires the Governor to constitute a State Finance Commission every fifth year to review Panchayat finances. It recommends State–Panchayat distribution of net tax proceeds, tier-wise allocation, assignment of revenues, grants-in-aid and measures to improve fiscal position. Its report and action memorandum must be laid before the State legislature. The SFC is therefore the constitutional bridge between Article 243H’s enabling channels and an evidence-based State-local fiscal settlement.

In practice, delayed constitution, poor local accounts, weak technical support, late reports and selective implementation break that bridge. Panchayats remain dependent on schemes and higher Finance Commission grants, while States may retain buoyant tax fields. A report without legislative debate or timely action cannot create predictability.

However, the SFC is not the sole missing element. Even a sound recommendation needs authorised local taxes, updated registers, collection capacity, accountable staff and transparent expenditure. Poor jurisdictions also require equalisation rather than unrealistic self-financing.

Reform should create a permanent SFC support cell, common local-finance data standards, constitution before the previous award expires, public reports, time-bound action memoranda and disclosure of accepted and rejected recommendations. The Sixteenth Finance Commission’s dated entry conditions linking grants with regular SFCs and accounts add external incentive. The SFC is thus a central bridge, but it carries traffic only when State law, administration and political commitment connect both ends.

**Rubric (15):** Article 243I mandate 4; implementation diagnosis 4; qualification 2; reforms/current link 3; verdict 2.

## Solved 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Panchayati Raj is a constitutional project of democracy, federalism and social justice whose results depend on State-level political economy. Discuss.

**Model solution:** Article 40’s village-self-government ideal became enforceable institutional architecture through the 73rd Amendment, Part IX and the Eleventh Schedule. Democracy appears in regular elected tiers, Gram Sabha participation and SEC autonomy. Social justice appears in population-linked SC/ST reservation and the women’s floor under Article 243D. Federalism appears in the compromise between this national floor and State control under Entry 5.

That compromise also shapes uneven results. Articles 243G and 243H enable State legislation rather than automatically transferring twenty-nine matters or revenue. Departments may retain staff, buoyant taxes and scheme control; parallel agencies can bypass elected bodies; delayed SFCs weaken fiscal settlement. Devolution redistributes patronage and administrative authority, so resistance is political, not merely technical.

Local social relations add another layer. Reservation opens office but proxy control, caste power and unequal Gram Sabha voice can limit agency. PESA’s stronger Fifth Schedule design similarly depends on verb-accurate State laws and real control over resources, not notification alone.

Reform must align functions, functionaries, finances and accountability. Published activity maps, locally answerable staff, own revenue with equalisation, timely SFCs, GPDP, social audit and grievance closure are essential. Digital platforms should make plans and accounts verifiable without replacing local choice.

Thus Panchayati Raj has decisively democratised institutional presence, but the federal allocation of operational power makes substantive self-government contingent on State political incentives and capacity-building choices.

**Rubric (20):** democracy/federalism/social-justice frame 5; exact constitutional design 4; political-economy mechanism 4; social/PESA dimension 2; reforms 3; synthesis 2.

## Article register

- **243:** definitions.
- **243A:** Gram Sabha powers as State law provides.
- **243B:** three tiers; the intermediate tier may be omitted in a State having a population not exceeding twenty lakh.
- **243C:** territorial seats directly elected; intermediate/district chairpersons elected by and from elected members; village-chair route by State law; specified cross-representation possible. The chairperson and every other member, whether or not directly elected from a territorial constituency, may vote in Panchayat meetings.
- **243D:** SC/ST population-linked reservation; not less than one-third women’s seats and chairperson offices; backward-class reservation enabling. Reserved chairperson offices are allotted by rotation to different Panchayats at each level. Clause (5) links specified SC/ST seat and non-women chairperson reservations to Article 334, while women’s reservation is excluded from that cessation rule. Under the Constitution in force in 2026, Article 334(a)’s eighty-year period runs to 25 January 2030 unless amended again.
- **243E:** five years; election before expiry or within six months of dissolution; short-remainder exception; reconstituted body serves remainder.
- **243F:** disqualification; twenty-one-year protection against the twenty-five-year rule.
- **243G:** State-law powers for self-government, planning and implementation; no automatic transfer.
- **243H:** authorised taxes, duties, tolls and fees; assigned revenues; grants; Panchayat Funds.
- **243I:** Governor constitutes SFC every fifth year; State law specifies composition, qualifications and selection; the Commission determines its procedure and exercises powers conferred by State law; report and action memorandum go before the legislature.
- **243J:** State-law accounts and audit.
- **243K:** SEC controls rolls and elections; removal/service/staff safeguards; subject to the Constitution, State law may provide for all matters relating to Panchayat elections.
- **243L:** Part IX applies to Union Territories by reading Governor as the Administrator appointed under Article 239 and, where the UT has a Legislative Assembly, State-legislature references as that Assembly; the President may notify exceptions or modifications.
- **243M:** exclusions for Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas, Sixth Schedule tribal areas, Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram, the specified Manipur hill areas and the constitutionally named Darjeeling hill arrangement. Clause (3) concerns Darjeeling; clause (4)(a) permits Nagaland, Meghalaya or Mizoram to extend Part IX by State law, except to clause (1) areas, after an Assembly resolution supported by a majority of total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. Article 243D is **not** excluded from that extension. Clause (3A) separately creates the narrow Arunachal Pradesh SC-seat-reservation exception. Parliament may extend Part IX to clause (1) Scheduled/tribal areas with exceptions or modifications, and that law is not deemed an Article 368 amendment.
- **243N:** transitional old-law continuation up to one year.
- **243O:** delimitation challenge bar and election-petition route.

## Finance register

- Article 243H supplies four channels: own levies; assignments; grants; Panchayat Funds.
- Own-tax and own non-tax receipts are distinct from grants.
- Typical lawful instruments: house/property-related levy, markets/fairs, fees, licences, user charges, tolls, rents and community-asset income, subject to State law.
- Article 243I SFC: State-law composition, qualifications and selection; own procedure and State-law-conferred powers; distribution, assignments, grants and fiscal-improvement recommendations.
- Weaknesses: delayed SFC, poor accounts, narrow bases, weak collection, State retention of buoyant taxes, tied transfers.
- Correct reform: own effort plus equalisation, not one instead of the other.
- Sixteenth Finance Commission facts are dated to 2026–31 and must not be constitutionalised.

## Accountability register

```text
Gram Sabha priority
→ GPDP
→ budget/resource convergence
→ execution
→ eGramSwaraj record
→ accounts
→ statutory audit/AuditOnline
→ MGNREGA Section 17 social audit where applicable
→ grievance, recovery and correction
```

- Digital trail helps only when accurate, public, intelligible and actionable.
- SVAMITVA may strengthen records; it does not itself create tax authority.
- SabhaSaar-type summaries require verification against actual proceedings.
- Kerala’s People’s Plan Campaign began in 1996 as a State-specific participatory decentralised-planning experiment.
- Meghalaya’s 2017 community-participation and public-services social-audit law is a State-specific statutory example, not a nationwide Panchayat rule.

## PESA register

- Applies to **Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas**; not Sixth Schedule areas.
- Must respect custom, culture and traditional community-resource management.
- Village can follow habitation/community logic.
- Gram Sabha safeguards tradition, identity, resources and customary dispute resolution.
- Approves plans; identifies beneficiaries; certifies utilisation.
- Consultation before land acquisition and rehabilitation.
- Prior recommendation for minor-mineral prospecting licences, leases and concessions.
- Ownership of minor forest produce.
- Powers concerning alienation/restoration, village markets, money-lending, intoxicants, social-sector institutions and local plans/resources.
- ST seats are not less than half; every chairperson office is reserved for STs; permitted intermediate/district nomination cannot exceed one-tenth of the elected membership.
- Section 4(o): State legislation shall endeavour to follow the Sixth Schedule pattern when designing district-level administrative arrangements in Scheduled Areas.
- PESA’s separate transition continued inconsistent Scheduled-Area Panchayat laws for at most one year from presidential assent on 24 December 1996, and existing Panchayats until their duration expired unless dissolved by Assembly resolution or, in a bicameral State, by each House.
- Do not convert differentiated verbs into a universal veto.
- FRA is complementary, not identical.

## Ten final traps

1. Article 40 is not a Fundamental Right.
2. Enacted 1992; commenced 24 April 1993.
3. Gram Sabha is not Gram Panchayat.
4. Intermediate tier can be omitted only under the population condition.
5. Territorial seats at every tier are directly elected; not every chairperson is.
6. Women’s one-third is a floor.
7. Candidate-age discriminator is twenty-one.
8. SEC, not ECI, conducts Panchayat elections.
9. Eleventh Schedule and Articles 243G/H do not automatically transfer functions or taxes.
10. PESA means Fifth Schedule and differentiated legal verbs, not Sixth Schedule or universal veto.

> **Cessation trap:** Article 243D(5) does not place women’s reservation under the Article 334 cessation rule; in 2026 the linked SC/ST categories remain operative during the current eighty-year Article 334(a) period.
