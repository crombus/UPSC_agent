### Institutional ladder map

```text
CONSTITUTIONAL AIM                    STATUTORY DELIVERY
Article 39A                           Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987
equal-opportunity justice             │
+ free legal aid                      ├─ NALSA
        │                             ├─ Supreme Court Committee
Articles 14 and 21 reinforce          ├─ SLSA / High Court Committee
equality and fair procedure           ├─ DLSA
                                      └─ Taluk Committee
```

The Constitution states the access objective; the 1987 Act supplies institutions, eligibility and services.

### 1. Start with the barrier, not the forum

A legal right is practically weak if a person cannot identify it, obtain advice, pay court costs, secure counsel or reach a forum. Article 39A therefore directs the State to make the legal system promote justice on a basis of equal opportunity and to provide free legal aid so that economic or other disability does not deny justice.

✅ **Fact:** Article 39A is a Directive Principle inserted by the Forty-second Amendment. It is not itself a court or a self-executing entitlement to every requested service.

✅ **Fact:** *M.H. Hoskot* and *Hussainara Khatoon* connect legal assistance and speedy, fair procedure to Article 21. The enforceable route is the right to fair procedure together with applicable statute; Article 39A remains non-justiciable as a Directive Principle.

### 2. The statutory ladder

| Institution | Main statutory role | Key office |
|---|---|---|
| NALSA | policy, principles, schemes, coordination and evaluation | CJI as Patron-in-Chief; Executive Chairman appointed through the statutory route |
| Supreme Court Legal Services Committee | legal services for Supreme Court matters | court-level committee |
| State Legal Services Authority | implements policy and coordinates State programmes and Lok Adalats | Chief Justice of the High Court as Patron-in-Chief |
| High Court Legal Services Committee | legal services for High Court matters | court-level committee |
| District Legal Services Authority | district implementation and coordination | District Judge as chairman |
| Taluk Legal Services Committee | local coordination, services and Lok Adalats | taluk/mandal-level body |

✅ **Fact:** These bodies are statutory, not constitutional. NALSA is the apex policy body, but delivery is distributed across the network.

### 3. Eligibility is not one income test

Section 12 includes status- and vulnerability-based categories such as:

- members of Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes;
- victims of trafficking or begar;
- women and children;
- persons with disability;
- victims of mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster;
- industrial workmen;
- persons in custody; and
- persons below the income ceiling prescribed by the competent authority.

Section 13 additionally requires the authority to be satisfied that the applicant has a prima facie case to prosecute or defend.

> **UPSC trap:** Do not impose one permanent national income ceiling. Several categories qualify by status, and notified income limits are authority-sensitive.

### 4. What “legal services” can include

Legal aid is wider than appointing a defence lawyer. It may include counsel, court and process fees, preparation of pleadings or appeal papers, certified copies, translation and related assistance. Legal literacy, clinics and settlement forums address informational and institutional barriers as well.

### Access challenge: objection and response

**Objection:** A nationwide hierarchy can become bureaucratic and measure success by case counts rather than quality.

**Reply:** A coordinated ladder permits national standards with local delivery and creates identifiable responsibility at every level.

**Residual:** Effective access still depends on awareness, competent counsel, timely communication, language access and monitoring of outcomes.

### Answer-building chain

For a legal-aid answer, use: **Article 39A → Article 21 reinforcement → Section 12 categories → Section 13 gate → NALSA network → delivery limits**.

### Entry-route flow

```text
                    SUITABLE DISPUTE
                          │
             ┌────────────┴────────────┐
             │                         │
       pending in court          pre-litigation
             │                         │
 court referral / party route   organising authority receives application
             └────────────┬────────────┘
                          ↓
               conciliation and persuasion
                   ┌──────┴──────┐
                   ↓             ↓
             genuine settlement  no settlement
                   ↓             ↓
                  award      ordinary legal route
```

An ordinary Lok Adalat helps parties settle; it does not replace their consent with its own view of the merits.

### 1. What kind of institution is it?

An ordinary Lok Adalat is a statutory settlement forum organised by a legal-services authority or committee. It commonly includes serving or retired judicial officers and other prescribed persons.

✅ **Fact:** It may deal with a case already pending before a court or a pre-litigation matter falling within a court's jurisdiction.

✅ **Fact:** It has no jurisdiction over an offence that is non-compoundable under law.

### 2. How a pending case enters

A court may refer a matter where:

- the parties agree;
- one party applies and the court gives the other side a reasonable opportunity before referral; or
- the court considers the matter appropriate for settlement under the statutory route.

The Lok Adalat then attempts compromise. The legal power to refer is not a power to force acceptance of settlement terms.

### 3. Pre-litigation entry

For a pre-litigation dispute, an organising authority may act on an application from a party and attempt amicable settlement before a suit begins. This widens access by reducing filing cost and adversarial escalation.

> **Trap:** “Lok Adalat” does not mean every matter is pre-litigation. Ordinary Lok Adalats can receive both pending and pre-litigation matters.

### 4. Procedure and powers

The Act gives specified civil-court powers for matters such as summoning witnesses, receiving affidavit evidence, requiring documents and requisitioning records. Those procedural powers help settlement administration; they do not turn the ordinary Lok Adalat into a merits court.

It is guided by justice, equity, fair play and legal principles. Flexibility means reduced technicality, not freedom from law or voluntariness.

### 5. Subject-matter boundary

| Matter | Ordinary Lok Adalat position |
|---|---|
| civil dispute capable of settlement | may be taken |
| motor-accident, bank, labour or family-related settlement matter | may be suitable within jurisdiction |
| compoundable criminal matter | may be settled according to law |
| non-compoundable offence | excluded |
| criminal guilt requiring trial | cannot be privately compromised merely by changing forum |

### Example and limit

Two parties dispute payment under a small contract. A pending suit may be referred and settled for a negotiated amount. The Lok Adalat may help clarify interests and draft terms. If one party rejects settlement, it cannot decide which witness is truthful and impose liability.

The example shows the settlement function. It does not imply that every civil right is waivable or every criminal allegation compoundable.

### Settlement-room stress test

**Objection:** Judicial presence may pressure a weaker party to settle.

**Reply:** The statutory legitimacy of an ordinary award rests on genuine compromise, not mere administrative disposal.

**Residual:** Authorities must protect informed consent, access to advice and freedom from coercion, especially where bargaining power is unequal.

### Examiner-facing rule

Always separate four questions: **entry stage, subject matter, need for consent, power after settlement fails**.

### Revision notes

1. Ordinary Lok Adalats are statutory settlement forums.
2. They may handle pending and pre-litigation matters.
3. The dispute must fall within a court's jurisdiction.
4. Non-compoundable offences are excluded.
5. Compoundable criminal matters may be settled according to law.
6. Referral is not compulsory settlement.
7. Civil-court procedural powers do not confer merits adjudication.
8. The forum uses conciliation, persuasion and compromise.
9. Justice, equity and fair play guide the process.
10. Procedural flexibility does not displace voluntariness.
11. A failed pending-case settlement returns to the ordinary route.
12. Consent quality is central to legitimacy.

### Concept check

**Question:** A court refers a pending dispute to an ordinary Lok Adalat, but one party rejects every proposed settlement. What follows?

**Model answer:** The Lok Adalat cannot impose a merits decision. If no compromise is reached, the pending case returns to the referring court for adjudication through the ordinary process.

**Misconception to avoid:** Court referral transfers a settlement opportunity, not the court's adjudicatory power.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the jurisdiction and working method of an ordinary Lok Adalat.

**Model answer (139 words):** An ordinary Lok Adalat is a statutory compromise forum under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. It may receive a case pending before a court or a pre-litigation matter that falls within a court's jurisdiction. Pending cases enter through party agreement, a permitted party application or a court's assessment that settlement should be attempted. The forum may use specified civil-court powers to obtain attendance and material, but these support conciliation rather than adjudication. It may handle civil disputes and legally compoundable criminal matters; non-compoundable offences are excluded. Its members guide parties through negotiation, equity and fair play. A binding award follows only a genuine settlement. If compromise fails, an ordinary Lok Adalat cannot determine liability on merits; the pending case returns to court or the parties pursue the normal remedy. Thus speed depends on consent, not substituted judicial command.

### Award-validity staircase

```text
GENUINE COMPROMISE
        ↓
Lok Adalat records award
        ↓
deemed civil-court decree + final and binding
        ↓
no statutory appeal
        │
        └── but constitutional supervision survives for exceptional illegality:
            no real consent / fraud / jurisdictional defect / natural-justice failure
```

Finality protects an agreed settlement; it does not immunise a non-consensual or jurisdictionally void act.

### 1. Legal effect of the award

✅ **Fact:** An ordinary Lok Adalat award is deemed to be a decree of a civil court or an order of the relevant court. It is final and binding on the parties, and no statutory appeal lies against it.

✅ **Fact:** Where a referred case settles, the court fee is refundable through the applicable statutory route.

The logic is straightforward:

```text
party-made compromise
        +
statutory recording
        =
immediate enforceability without a fresh suit
```

### 2. Why “no appeal” is not “no review”

An appeal reopens a decision through a statutory hierarchy. Constitutional review asks whether the forum acted within law. High Courts retain Articles 226/227 control over public and judicial bodies, subject to the nature of the impugned act and established limits.

⚖️ **State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh (2008):** an ordinary Lok Adalat performs no adjudicatory role; an award must embody a compromise or settlement.

⚖️ **Bhargavi Constructions v. Kothakapu Muthyam Reddy (2018):** a separate civil suit challenging a Lok Adalat award is not the ordinary route; limited constitutional challenge remains available where legally sustainable grounds such as fraud are alleged.

### 3. Review is not a disguised merits appeal

| Allegation | Proper legal character |
|---|---|
| “I later dislike the bargain” | ordinarily insufficient |
| “The forum should have valued evidence differently” | impermissible merits recasting where settlement was genuine |
| “I never consented / consent was fabricated” | potential challenge to the foundation of the award |
| “Fraud or coercion produced the recorded settlement” | exceptional legality challenge |
| “The forum acted outside subject-matter jurisdiction” | jurisdictional review |
| “A fair hearing necessary to establish consent was denied” | natural-justice review |

### 4. Failed settlement cannot become an award

If parties do not agree, the Lok Adalat must not write a reasoned merits order and label it an “award.” *Jalour Singh* is the decisive firewall. The case returns to court; a pre-litigation claimant may use the ordinary legal remedy.

### 5. The fairness bargain

Finality serves speed and certainty. Consent supplies legitimacy. Remove genuine consent, and the justification for unusually strong finality weakens.

### Finality paradox

**Objection:** Any possibility of writ review undermines final settlement.

**Reply:** Narrow legality review preserves, rather than destroys, finality by distinguishing genuine bargains from fraud, coercion and jurisdictional nullity.

**Residual:** Courts must prevent writ petitions from becoming routine appeals while still protecting the voluntary foundation of the award.

### Review-answer protocol

Use the sentence: **“No statutory appeal” describes the ordinary appellate route; it does not extinguish the High Court's constitutionally protected power to review exceptional illegality.**

### Revision notes

1. An ordinary award rests on compromise.
2. The award is deemed a civil-court decree.
3. It is final and binding.
4. No statutory appeal lies.
5. Court-fee refund supports settlement.
6. *Jalour Singh* denies ordinary Lok Adalats merits power.
7. Finality is justified by consent.
8. Fraud can attack the foundation of consent.
9. Absence of consent is not mere dissatisfaction.
10. Jurisdictional defect remains reviewable in principle.
11. Natural-justice control survives statutory finality.
12. Writ review is not a merits appeal.
13. A separate original suit is not the normal challenge route.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can a Lok Adalat award be “final” while a High Court may still examine it?

**Model answer:** Finality bars the ordinary statutory appeal and merits reopening. Constitutional review remains exceptional to test whether a genuine settlement existed and whether the forum stayed within jurisdiction and basic legality.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Final” does not validate fraud, fabricated consent or a decision the forum had no power to make.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse the relationship between consent, finality and writ review in ordinary Lok Adalat awards.

**Model answer (185 words):** An ordinary Lok Adalat derives decisional legitimacy from the parties' compromise. The Legal Services Authorities Act gives the resulting award the status of a civil-court decree, makes it final and binding and excludes a statutory appeal. This design saves time and prevents a party from accepting settlement benefits and then routinely reopening the merits.

Finality, however, cannot create a power the forum lacks. In *State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh*, the Supreme Court held that an ordinary Lok Adalat is conciliatory, not adjudicatory; failure of compromise must return the dispute to the ordinary route. Likewise, an award whose consent is fabricated or vitiated by fraud attacks the statutory foundation of the decree. A separate civil suit is not the normal corrective route, as clarified in *Bhargavi Constructions*. The High Court's constitutional jurisdiction may examine exceptional defects such as absence of genuine consent, fraud, jurisdictional error or denial of natural justice.

The balance is therefore narrow: review must not become a disguised appeal on valuation or bargain quality, but statutory finality cannot shelter a non-consensual or ultra vires act. Consent explains finality; constitutional legality limits it.

### Jurisdiction-screen decision tree

```text
PERMANENT LOK ADALAT JURISDICTION TEST

1. Is the dispute pre-litigation? ── no ──> PLA route unavailable
              │ yes
2. Does it concern a public utility service? ── no ──> unavailable
              │ yes
3. Does it relate to a non-compoundable offence? ── yes ──> excluded
              │ no (a compoundable-offence dispute may enter only for settlement)
4. Is it within the currently notified pecuniary ceiling? ── no ──> excluded
              │ yes
              ↓
        PLA may take cognizance
```

“Permanent” describes a standing institution, not an ordinary Lok Adalat sitting continuously.

### 1. Why Chapter VI-A created a different forum

The 2002 amendment inserted Permanent Lok Adalats for public utility services. Recurring essential-service disputes may be too small or urgent for prolonged litigation, yet failed conciliation can leave the user without practical relief. The PLA therefore combines a mandatory settlement attempt with a limited adjudicatory fallback.

### 2. Composition

A PLA consists of a Chairman who is or has been a District Judge, Additional District Judge or holder of a higher judicial office, and two persons with adequate experience in public utility services.

### 3. Public utility services

The statutory field includes:

- transport of passengers or goods by air, road or water;
- postal, telegraph or telephone service;
- supply of power, light or water to the public;
- public conservancy or sanitation;
- service in a hospital or dispensary;
- insurance service; and
- additional services notified through the statutory power.

✅ **Current official control:** S.O. 495(E), dated **16 February 2016**, issued under Section 22A(b), declares **education or educational institutions** and **housing and real estate service** to be public utility services from Gazette publication.

> **Trap:** A useful service is not automatically a statutory “public utility service.” Use the Act and a valid notification; attach notified additions to their notification and date.

### 4. Entry must be before court litigation

Any party may apply before the dispute is brought before a court. Once the application is made, the parties cannot invoke another court's jurisdiction over the same dispute while the statutory bar operates.

This pre-litigation design distinguishes PLA from an ordinary Lok Adalat, which may receive pending cases.

### 5. Pecuniary jurisdiction: figure plus control

The Act permits the Central Government, in consultation with the Central Authority, to increase the monetary ceiling by notification. **S.O. 803(E), dated 20 March 2015**, issued under the third proviso to Section 22C(1), superseded S.O. 2083(E) of 15 September 2011 and increased the limit of the value of the property in dispute for PLA jurisdiction to **₹1 crore**, effective from Gazette publication.

✅ **Current verified position through 3 October 2026:** ₹1 crore under S.O. 803(E), 20 March 2015.

⚠️ **Current-use rule:** The amount is notification-controlled, not an immutable number written permanently into the institutional design. State **₹1 crore under S.O. 803(E), 20 March 2015**, and re-check the operative notification for any later control date.

### Example and limit

A consumer alleges wrongful interruption of a notified public utility service and seeks relief within the current monetary jurisdiction before filing suit. The PLA may take the application. It cannot use this route for an unrelated private contract merely because one party performs a socially useful activity.

### Jurisdiction-screen stress test

**Objection:** Sector-specific jurisdiction fragments justice and creates classification disputes.

**Reply:** Narrow jurisdiction permits expertise and accessible pre-litigation relief for recurring essential services.

**Residual:** The benefit depends on clear notifications, public awareness and disciplined refusal of disputes outside the statutory field.

### Four-gate answer opening

The best PLA opening is: **standing + pre-litigation + public utility + conciliation first + limited merits power**.

### Revision notes

1. PLA comes from Chapter VI-A of the 1987 Act.
2. Chapter VI-A was inserted by the 2002 amendment.
3. PLA is a standing body.
4. Its jurisdiction is pre-litigation.
5. It is limited to public utility services.
6. Statutory and notified services must be distinguished.
7. Transport, communications, utilities, sanitation, health and insurance are statutory illustrations.
8. S.O. 495(E), 16 February 2016, notified education/educational institutions and housing/real-estate service.
9. Non-compoundable-offence disputes are excluded; a compoundable-offence dispute may enter for lawful settlement.
10. A same-dispute court bar follows a valid application.
11. S.O. 803(E), 20 March 2015, fixes the verified ceiling at ₹1 crore.
12. The ceiling remains notification-controlled; cite the amount with its notification/date.
13. “Permanent” does not mean an ordinary continuous sitting.

### Concept check

**Question:** A pre-litigation ₹80 lakh education-service dispute involves no offence. Which current controls make PLA entry potentially available?

**Model answer:** The dispute is pre-litigation; education or educational institutions were notified as public utility services by S.O. 495(E), 16 February 2016; and ₹80 lakh falls within the ₹1 crore ceiling fixed by S.O. 803(E), 20 March 2015. Other statutory conditions must still be met.

**Misconception to avoid:** Neither the sector nor ₹1 crore is a timeless assumption: each current control must be linked to its notification and date.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Identify the jurisdictional conditions for a Permanent Lok Adalat.

**Model answer (140 words):** A Permanent Lok Adalat is a standing Chapter VI-A statutory body for public utility services. A party must apply before the dispute is brought before a court. The subject must fall within the statutory or validly notified public-utility field, such as transport, communications, public supply, sanitation, hospital or insurance service. A matter involving a non-compoundable offence is excluded. The claim must also remain within the notification-controlled pecuniary limit, verified through 3 October 2026 as ₹1 crore under S.O. 803(E), 20 March 2015. S.O. 495(E), 16 February 2016, additionally notified education/educational institutions and housing/real-estate service. After a valid application, the same dispute cannot simultaneously be invoked before another court as permitted by the Act. Thus PLA jurisdiction is defined by stage, sector, criminal exclusion and notified value—not merely by convenience or the word “public.”

### Two-stage statutory walkthrough

```text
eligible PLA application
          ↓
exchange of statements and material
          ↓
independent and impartial conciliation
     ┌────┴─────────────┐
     ↓                  ↓
agreement          conciliation fails
     ↓                  ↓
settlement award   does dispute relate to any offence?
                        ├─ yes → no Section 22C(8) merits decision
                        └─ no  → eligible dispute may be decided on merits
     └──────────┬───────┘
                ↓
 final, binding, deemed civil-court decree
```

Merits power is a second stage, not a substitute for the mandatory conciliation stage.

### 1. The statutory sequence

The PLA directs parties to file statements, documents and replies, then conducts conciliation. It must assist them independently and impartially. If it sees possible settlement terms, it may formulate them for observations.

If the parties agree, the award records settlement. If they do not agree, Section 22C(8) permits merits adjudication only where the dispute **does not relate to any offence**. This is narrower than the entry bar: a dispute relating to a compoundable offence may be handled for lawful settlement, but failed settlement does not authorise the PLA to adjudicate that offence-related dispute on merits. A non-compoundable-offence dispute is excluded at entry itself.

### 2. The decisive ordinary/PLA difference

| Question | Ordinary Lok Adalat | Permanent Lok Adalat |
|---|---|---|
| stage | pending or pre-litigation | pre-litigation only |
| field | court-jurisdiction dispute, subject to bar | public utility only |
| first method | compromise | conciliation |
| if settlement fails | no merits decision | may decide only an otherwise eligible dispute that relates to no offence |
| institutional form | organised sitting | standing body |

⚖️ **InterGlobe Aviation v. N. Satchidanand (2011):** ordinary and Permanent Lok Adalats are distinct statutory institutions; PLA adjudicatory power cannot be imported into an ordinary Lok Adalat.

⚖️ **Bar Council of India v. Union of India (2012):** the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the public-utility conciliation-cum-adjudication design.

⚖️ **Canara Bank v. G.S. Jayarama (2022):** conciliation is mandatory in sequence; adjudication follows only after the statutory settlement effort fails and remains subject to Section 22C(8), including its no-offence condition.

### 3. Procedure after failure

The PLA is guided by natural justice, objectivity, fair play, equity and other principles of justice. It is not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure or the law of evidence in the same rigid manner.

Flexibility does not mean:

- skipping notice;
- prejudging merits during conciliation;
- using confidential settlement positions as automatic proof;
- adjudicating an offence-related dispute after failed settlement, even if the offence is compoundable;
- acting beyond public-utility or monetary jurisdiction; or
- issuing an unreasoned command without a fair opportunity.

### 4. Award and control

A PLA award is final and binding, deemed a civil-court decree and made by majority. The statute protects it from being called in question in an original suit, application or execution proceeding.

Constitutional review nevertheless survives for jurisdictional error, mandatory-procedure failure or natural-justice violation. The High Court does not sit as a routine first appeal on facts.

### Hybrid-role objection and safeguard

**Objection:** The same body that conciliates may acquire information or impressions that compromise neutral adjudication.

**Reply:** The statutory design requires independence, impartiality and a genuine staged process; the Supreme Court has upheld the model.

**Residual:** Fairness depends on procedural separation in practice, reasoned adjudication and constitutional correction of material illegality.

### Section 22C(8) drafting rule

Write **“after failed conciliation, may adjudicate an otherwise eligible dispute only if it relates to no offence”**. Sequence and the Section 22C(8) no-offence condition are both part of the doctrine.

### Revision notes

1. PLA begins with written statements and exchange of material.
2. Conciliation is mandatory.
3. The PLA must act independently and impartially.
4. It may formulate possible settlement terms.
5. Agreement produces a settlement award.
6. A compoundable-offence-related dispute may be settled where law permits; a non-compoundable one is excluded at entry.
7. If settlement fails, Section 22C(8) allows merits adjudication only where the dispute relates to no offence.
8. Merits power also remains confined to valid public-utility, pre-litigation and pecuniary jurisdiction.
9. *InterGlobe* separates ordinary LA and PLA.
10. *Bar Council of India* upheld the hybrid design.
11. *Canara Bank* protects the conciliation-first sequence.
12. PLA is guided by natural justice and fair play.
13. It is not rigidly bound by CPC or evidence law.
14. Its award is final, binding and a deemed decree.
15. Constitutional review is legality control, not routine appeal.

### Concept check

**Question:** After conciliation fails, may a PLA decide every dispute that could validly enter its process?

**Model answer:** No. Section 22C(8) permits merits adjudication only if the otherwise eligible dispute relates to no offence. A compoundable-offence dispute may be processed for lawful settlement, but settlement failure does not create merits power; a non-compoundable-offence dispute is excluded at entry.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Compoundable” permits lawful settlement; it does not satisfy Section 22C(8)'s separate no-offence condition for adjudication.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “The Permanent Lok Adalat is a hybrid forum, but not an unstructured one.” Examine.

**Model answer (210 words):** A Permanent Lok Adalat combines consensual settlement with limited statutory adjudication. Its jurisdiction is tightly structured: the dispute must be pre-litigation, concern a public utility service, avoid the non-compoundable-offence bar and remain within the notified pecuniary framework.

The procedure is equally staged. Parties exchange statements and documents; the PLA then conducts independent and impartial conciliation and may formulate possible settlement terms. Agreement results in an award. Only if conciliation fails **and the dispute relates to no offence** may it decide an otherwise eligible dispute on merits. A compoundable-offence dispute may be settled, but cannot pass into Section 22C(8) merits adjudication. *Canara Bank v. G.S. Jayarama* confirms that this sequence is mandatory. *InterGlobe Aviation* prevents transfer of this exceptional merits power to an ordinary Lok Adalat, while *Bar Council of India* upheld the conciliation-cum-adjudication design.

Its award is final, binding and deemed a civil-court decree. The PLA is not rigidly bound by the CPC or evidence law, but it remains governed by natural justice, objectivity, fair play and jurisdictional limits. High Court review therefore survives for material illegality, though it cannot become a routine factual appeal.

The PLA is hybrid because its mode changes after failed conciliation; it is structured because stage, subject, sequence and review remain legally bounded.

A Family Court prefers conciliation where appropriate but remains a court capable of binding adjudication.

### 1. Purpose and establishment

The Family Courts Act, 1984 seeks conciliation and speedy settlement of disputes relating to marriage and family affairs. A State Government establishes Family Courts after consultation with the High Court. The Act requires establishment for cities or towns above the statutory population threshold of one million and permits establishment elsewhere.

### 2. Jurisdiction

Family Courts deal with specified matters including:

- nullity, judicial separation, divorce and restitution of conjugal rights;
- declaration of marital validity or matrimonial status;
- property disputes between spouses;
- legitimacy;
- maintenance;
- guardianship, custody and access; and
- connected statutory family matters.

The exact cause of action matters. “Family-related” in ordinary speech is not enough to confer statutory jurisdiction.

### 3. Settlement orientation

The court must endeavour, where consistent with the nature and circumstances of the case, to assist and persuade parties toward settlement. It may associate counsellors, welfare agencies and experts.

⚠️ **Inference:** Conciliation can preserve relationships and tailor arrangements, but it is inappropriate to pressure settlement where violence, coercive control or severe power imbalance makes bargaining unsafe.

### 4. Procedure, privacy and representation

| Feature | Legal significance |

| legal representation | no right as of course; court may seek assistance of a legal expert |
| judicial decision | available when settlement fails or is unsuitable |
| appeal/control | statutory High Court route, subject to exclusions |

### 5. Boundary with Lok Adalat

A Family Court is a specialised statutory court. It can determine matrimonial status, custody or maintenance after hearing the parties. An ordinary Lok Adalat cannot impose such a merits decision; it can only record a lawful compromise.

### Example and limit

Parents may settle a custody schedule with counselling. If settlement fails, the Family Court can decide welfare and legal rights. The continuing relationship supports conciliation, but child welfare cannot be traded away solely for docket disposal.

### Family-court design trade-off

**Objection:** Informality and reduced lawyer participation may disadvantage a vulnerable spouse.

**Reply:** Specialisation, privacy, counsellors and active judicial management can reduce adversarial harm.

**Residual:** The court must preserve informed participation, legal assistance where justice requires, reasoned orders and safety-sensitive procedure.

**Model answer:** It is a statutory court with defined family jurisdiction and power to adjudicate when settlement fails or is unsuitable. An ordinary Lok Adalat depends on compromise and cannot impose a merits outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** A settlement orientation does not make every specialised court an ADR forum.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess the Family Court model as a combination of conciliation and adjudication.

**Model answer (193 words):** The Family Courts Act, 1984 recognises that family disputes require both legal authority and a less adversarial process. State Governments establish Family Courts in consultation with High Courts, mandatorily in cities or towns crossing the statutory population threshold and optionally elsewhere. Their jurisdiction includes matrimonial relief, spousal property, legitimacy, maintenance, guardianship, custody and access.

The model first encourages settlement. Counsellors, welfare agencies and experts may help parties identify durable arrangements. Flexible procedure and in-camera hearings protect privacy and reduce technical burden. Yet the institution remains a court: if settlement fails or is unsafe, it can determine rights and issue binding judgments, subject to the statutory High Court route. This distinguishes it from an ordinary Lok Adalat, whose outcome requires compromise.

The principal risk is that informality or the absence of legal representation as of right may deepen power asymmetry, particularly in cases involving violence or economic dependence. Judges must therefore test voluntariness, provide legal assistance where justice requires, prioritise child welfare and give reasoned orders. Properly administered, the Family Court is neither a compromise camp nor an ordinary civil court with a new label, but a specialised judicial forum with a settlement preference.

The institution brings a court closer to rural litigants; it is not a Panchayat dispensing customary justice.

### 1. Statutory character and establishment

The Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 aims to provide access to justice at citizens' doorsteps and prevent social, economic or other disability from defeating justice.

✅ **Fact:** The State Government may establish a Gram Nyayalaya after consultation with the High Court. The Act's establishment language is enabling, so implementation varies by State.

### 2. Location and mobility

A Gram Nyayalaya is designed for an intermediate Panchayat or a group of contiguous Panchayats. Its headquarters is linked to that area, while the Nyayadhikari may hold mobile sittings close to where parties reside or the cause arose.

Mobility reduces travel cost. It does not change territorial or subject-matter jurisdiction.

### 3. Nyayadhikari

The presiding officer is appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court and must satisfy the statutory qualification for appointment as a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class.

> **Trap:** A Nyayadhikari is a judicial officer, not an elected Panchayat member, village elder or informal conciliator.

### 4. Civil and criminal jurisdiction

The Gram Nyayalaya hears specified criminal offences and civil suits, claims or disputes listed in the Act's Schedules. The Schedules may be amended through the statutory route within legislative competence.

It follows a summary orientation in criminal cases and modified civil procedure. It may try to settle civil disputes through conciliators, but if settlement fails it can adjudicate.

### 5. Evidence, decree and appeal

The forum is guided by natural justice and statutory rules rather than rigid evidentiary technicality. Its judgment or order is enforceable through the Act's summary design. Criminal appeals go to the Court of Session and civil appeals to the District Court, subject to statutory exceptions.

### 6. Why implementation is difficult

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** A new forum does not create demand automatically. Overlap with existing taluka courts, weak police and Bar participation, inadequate staff, uncertain case transfer, low awareness and limited mobile infrastructure can reduce use.

### Implementation diagnosis

**Objection:** Where regular courts already exist, another local court may duplicate jurisdiction and cost.

**Reply:** Mobility and simplified local access can serve areas where formal courts remain physically or procedurally distant.

**Residual:** Establishment should follow a local justice-gap assessment, with clear case allocation, trained staff and appellate integration.

The source law, not the adjective, determines appointment, jurisdiction, procedure and appeal.

### 1. Fast Track Courts

Fast Track Courts add adjudicatory capacity for selected categories or pending cases. They usually function within the ordinary judicial system under State and High Court administration, sometimes supported by centrally recommended finance.

⚠️ **Trap:** “Fast track” describes priority and case-management design. It does not automatically alter evidentiary standards, appellate rights or constitutional hierarchy.

### 2. Special Courts

A statute may create or designate a Special Court for defined offences, persons or subject matter. Examples across the legal system differ sharply in judge qualification, territorial reach, procedure and appeal.

The correct exam method is:

```text
identify parent law
      ↓
identify appointment/designation authority
      ↓
identify exclusive or concurrent jurisdiction
      ↓
identify procedure and appeal
```

### 3. Commercial Courts

The Commercial Courts Act, 2015 creates Commercial Courts and appellate structures for commercial disputes of specified value, along with Commercial Divisions and Commercial Appellate Divisions in relevant High Courts.

They are statutory civil courts using case management and specialised commercial procedure. Pre-institution mediation may be mandatory where the governing law applies and urgent interim relief is not contemplated, as explained in *Patil Automation v. Rakheja Engineers* (2022).

Commercial Courts are not arbitral tribunals: one derives public jurisdiction from statute, the other derives adjudicatory authority from an arbitration agreement and the 1996 Act.

### 4. Evening and Morning Courts

These are administrative arrangements that extend hearing hours for suitable minor or high-volume matters. The judge remains part of the ordinary judicial establishment, and the source court's jurisdiction and appellate structure continue.

### 5. Capacity versus due process

Speed may come from additional benches, case triage, tighter schedules or specialised procedure. It must not come from:

- denying hearing;
- presuming guilt;
- reducing reasoned adjudication;
- eliminating lawful appeal; or
- selecting cases through arbitrary criteria.

### Capacity-versus-fairness exchange

**Objection:** Specialisation and fast tracking can create fragmented, unequal justice.

**Reply:** Defined jurisdiction and concentrated expertise can improve timeliness and consistency.

**Residual:** Parent-law clarity, judicial independence, transparent allocation and ordinary constitutional review must control the design.

### Design-classification rule

Never answer from the label alone. Use **source → jurisdiction → procedure → merits power → appeal/review**.

A Special Court is constituted or designated under a parent statute or notification for defined offences, persons or subjects, so its powers and appeal depend on that law. The decisive variables are consent, legal source, subject matter, stage, merits power and review.

### 1. Mediation

A mediator controls the process, not the outcome. Parties make the settlement. Under **S.O. 4384(E), 9 October 2023**, only Sections **1, 3, 26, 31-38, 45-47, 50-54 and 56-57** of the Mediation Act, 2023 were brought into force from that date; it is inaccurate to describe the entire Act as commenced.

✅ **Current official status through 3 October 2026:** **S.O. 4781(E), 27 August 2026** established the **Mediation Council of India** under Section 31(1), with its head office at Delhi. Establishing the Council implements a commenced institutional provision; it does **not** retrospectively commence the Act's remaining provisions.

An ordinary Lok Adalat also depends on agreement, but its statutory award is immediately treated as a decree. The two processes therefore share consent but differ in institutional source and legal form.

### 2. Arbitration

An arbitral tribunal derives jurisdiction from an arbitration agreement and the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. It hears evidence and determines arbitrable civil or commercial rights. Its award does not require post-hearing agreement and is controlled through the Act's challenge and appeal structure.

Criminal prosecution, guilt and punishment cannot be assigned to private arbitration. By contrast, an ordinary Lok Adalat may facilitate lawful settlement of a compoundable criminal matter because compounding is permitted by criminal law.

### 3. Tribunals

Tribunals are specialised statutory adjudicators. They may decide merits, apply natural justice and issue binding orders within the parent law. Statutory appeal may lie, and High Court judicial review survives under *L. Chandra Kumar*.

A tribunal is not ADR merely because it sits outside the ordinary court hierarchy. It substitutes specialised adjudication, not party-made settlement.

### 4. Regular courts

Regular courts provide coercive interim relief, full adjudication, precedent-governed procedure and hierarchical appeal. ADR and specialised courts complement them; they do not erase the constitutional role of High Courts.

**Model answer (150 words):** An ordinary Lok Adalat is a statutory settlement forum under the 1987 Act. It may receive pending or pre-litigation disputes and facilitates compromise; without agreement it cannot decide merits. A lawful settlement becomes a final deemed civil-court decree, with no statutory appeal and only limited constitutional review.

An arbitral tribunal derives authority from an arbitration agreement and the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. It adjudicates arbitrable civil or commercial rights and may issue an award despite one party losing; statutory setting-aside and appeal provisions control that award.

Lok Adalats may settle civil disputes and criminal matters that are legally compoundable, but non-compoundable offences are excluded. Arbitration cannot determine criminal guilt or punishment. Permanent Lok Adalats must be separately qualified: after failed conciliation they may adjudicate an eligible pre-litigation public-utility dispute only if it relates to no offence. Thus one forum ordinarily perfects compromise, while the other privately adjudicates civil rights.

### 1. Consent quality

A signature is evidence of agreement, but not always conclusive evidence of fairness. A robust settlement process tests:

- identity and authority to settle;
- comprehension of rights and consequences;
- access to independent advice;
- absence of threat, deception or improper pressure;
- language and disability access; and
- time for considered choice.

⚠️ **Inference:** Women, workers, accident victims, detained persons, consumers and persons with low digital access may face different forms of bargaining inequality. Safeguards should respond to the actual vulnerability rather than assume every settlement is equally negotiated.

### 2. Conciliation and adjudication roles

The ordinary Lok Adalat avoids the role-conflict problem by lacking merits power. A PLA's hybrid design is more difficult: the institution first promotes agreement and may later decide. Legitimacy therefore requires a visible transition, fair exchange of material, impartiality and reasons.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically examine voluntariness as the foundation of settlement-based justice.

**Model answer (195 words):** Settlement-based justice gains legitimacy because parties, rather than the forum, choose the outcome. This explains the strong finality of an ordinary Lok Adalat award: the State enforces a genuine compromise rather than imposing a merits judgment. Voluntariness can also preserve relationships, reduce cost and permit tailored remedies.

Formal assent, however, may conceal unequal bargaining power, poor legal advice, language barriers, custody, economic distress or pressure to improve disposal statistics. A signature should therefore be supported by comprehension of rights and alternatives, authority to settle, freedom from coercion and access to advice. Digital settlement adds identity, privacy, accessibility and connectivity risks.

The legal design responds in part. An ordinary Lok Adalat cannot adjudicate after failure, and exceptional writ review can test fraud or absent consent. A Permanent Lok Adalat is different because it may adjudicate after conciliation; its staged procedure, impartiality and natural-justice duties are therefore especially important.

The answer is not to convert settlement into a full trial. Proportionate safeguards, trained neutrals, interpreters, private consultation, offline access and outcome audits preserve speed while making consent meaningful. Voluntariness is not a formality at the end of settlement; it is the quality of agency throughout the process.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish ordinary Lok Adalats from Permanent Lok Adalats. (150 words)

**Model answer (150 words):** Both arise under the Legal Services Authorities Act, but their powers differ. An ordinary Lok Adalat is an organised sitting that may receive pending or pre-litigation disputes within court jurisdiction, excluding non-compoundable offences. It facilitates compromise only. If settlement fails, it cannot adjudicate and a pending case returns to court. A settlement award is a deemed civil-court decree, final and binding, with no statutory appeal.

A Permanent Lok Adalat is a standing Chapter VI-A body for pre-litigation public-utility disputes within the notified pecuniary framework. It first conducts mandatory conciliation. If agreement fails, it may decide an otherwise eligible dispute on merits only where the dispute relates to no offence, guided by natural justice and fair play. Its award is also final, binding and a deemed decree. Thus both promote accessible resolution, but only the PLA has limited post-conciliation adjudicatory power; that exception cannot be imported into an ordinary Lok Adalat.

## 15 marks

**Question:** Examine statutory finality in Lok Adalat institutions without overstating exclusion of judicial review. (250 words)

**Model answer (196 words):** The Legal Services Authorities Act gives both ordinary and Permanent Lok Adalat awards the status of civil-court decrees and makes them final and binding. Ordinary awards are non-appealable because they embody party compromise; PLA awards receive strong finality because the statute creates a pre-litigation public-utility process, including possible merits adjudication after failed conciliation only where the dispute relates to no offence.

Finality is not identical to constitutional immunity. In *State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh*, the Supreme Court held that an ordinary Lok Adalat cannot decide merits; an imposed “award” after failed compromise exceeds jurisdiction. *Bhargavi Constructions* rejects an ordinary collateral civil suit while leaving the narrow constitutional route for legally recognised defects. For PLAs, *Canara Bank v. G.S. Jayarama* makes conciliation a mandatory condition before adjudication.

High Court review may therefore examine absence of genuine consent, fraud, subject-matter excess, skipped mandatory procedure or denial of natural justice. It should not reassess the bargain, evidence or factual merits as if hearing a statutory appeal. The distinction protects both purposes: parties obtain rapid closure, while public bodies remain within law. Statutory finality is strongest when the forum follows the exact jurisdiction and procedure that justify it.

**Rubric (15):** award effects 3; ordinary consent basis 2; case law 4; review grounds 3; anti-appeal limit 2; conclusion 1.

## 20 marks

**Question:** “Access to justice requires differentiated institutions, not a single alternative to courts.” Analyse with reference to Lok Adalats and other statutory courts. (250 words)

**Model answer (240 words):** Article 39A addresses economic and other barriers to justice, but different disputes require different powers. The Legal Services Authorities Act therefore combines a legal-aid ladder with two distinct Lok Adalat models. Ordinary Lok Adalats settle pending or pre-litigation disputes through compromise and cannot adjudicate after failure. Permanent Lok Adalats address pre-litigation public-utility disputes through conciliation followed, if necessary, by merits adjudication only for disputes relating to no offence.

Other statutes answer different barriers. Family Courts specialise in matrimonial, maintenance and custody matters, using counselling, privacy and settlement efforts while retaining judicial power. Gram Nyayalayas bring scheduled civil and criminal jurisdiction closer to rural citizens through judicial officers and mobile sittings. Commercial Courts concentrate expertise and case management; special and fast-track courts respond to subject matter or capacity.

The differentiation is legally significant. Mediation facilitates party choice; arbitration privately adjudicates civil rights; tribunals exercise specialised statutory power; regular courts provide coercive relief, precedent, criminal trial and constitutional remedies. *Jalour Singh* protects consent in ordinary Lok Adalats, *Canara Bank* enforces the PLA sequence and *L. Chandra Kumar* preserves High Court review over tribunals.

Pluralism can nevertheless fragment jurisdiction, reproduce bargaining inequality and create underused institutions. Reform should improve legal advice, screening, staffing, reasoned decisions, digital and offline access, appellate coordination and outcome audits. Alternative mechanisms reduce cost and distance, but independent regular courts remain the constitutional backstop. Equal justice is achieved by matching forum to dispute while preserving legality, agency and review.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional and statutory base

- Article 39A: equal-opportunity justice plus free legal aid; DPSP inserted by the Forty-second Amendment.
- Articles 14 and 21 reinforce equality and fair procedure; *M.H. Hoskot* and *Hussainara Khatoon* are the access cases.
- Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987: NALSA → Supreme Court Committee → SLSA/High Court Committee → DLSA → Taluk Committee.
- Section 12: specified vulnerable/status categories plus notified income route; Section 13: prima facie case.
- Legal services include counsel, fees, documents, translation, appeal preparation, literacy and settlement access.

## Ordinary Lok Adalat

- pending or pre-litigation matter within court jurisdiction;
- non-compoundable offence excluded; lawful compoundable criminal settlement possible;
- conciliation/compromise only; civil-court procedural powers do not confer merits power;
- *Jalour Singh*: no adjudicatory role;
- genuine settlement award = deemed decree, final and binding, no statutory appeal;
- fee refund for settled referred matter;
- limited Articles 226/227 control for absent consent, fraud, jurisdictional or natural-justice defect;
- *Bhargavi Constructions*: no ordinary collateral suit; narrow constitutional route.

## Permanent Lok Adalat

- Chapter VI-A, inserted in 2002; standing institution;
- pre-litigation only; public utility service only; a non-compoundable-offence dispute is excluded at entry;
- a compoundable-offence-related dispute may be handled for lawful settlement, but Section 22C(8) allows failed-conciliation merits adjudication only where the dispute relates to no offence;
- public utilities: transport, communications, public power/light/water, sanitation, hospital/dispensary and insurance; S.O. 495(E), 16 February 2016, adds education/educational institutions and housing/real-estate service;
- verified pecuniary ceiling: ₹1 crore under S.O. 803(E), 20 March 2015; always identify its notification-controlled nature;
- valid application triggers the same-dispute court bar;
- written statements/material → mandatory impartial conciliation → settlement award, or (only if dispute relates to no offence) eligible merits decision;
- *InterGlobe*: ordinary LA and PLA are distinct;
- *Bar Council of India*: hybrid design upheld;
- *Canara Bank*: conciliation must precede merits adjudication;
- award final, binding, deemed decree; natural justice and constitutional review remain.

## Family Courts

- Family Courts Act, 1984; State establishes after High Court consultation.
- Mandatory in statutory cities/towns above one million; discretionary elsewhere.
- matrimonial status/relief, spousal property, legitimacy, maintenance, guardianship, custody and access.
- endeavour settlement where suitable; counsellors and experts may assist.
- in camera protection; flexible receipt of useful material.
- no legal practitioner as of right, but legal-expert assistance may be sought.
- adjudicatory court with statutory High Court control—not an ordinary Lok Adalat.

## Gram Nyayalayas

- Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008; enabling State establishment after High Court consultation.
- intermediate Panchayat or contiguous group; mobile sittings.
- Nyayadhikari is a judicial officer qualified as JMFC.
- scheduled civil and criminal jurisdiction; summary orientation and civil conciliation.
- natural justice; criminal appeal to Sessions Court, civil appeal to District Court, subject to exceptions.
- not Panchayati Raj, khap, mediation camp or Lok Adalat.
- main limits: uneven establishment, overlap, staffing, awareness and institutional participation.

## Other courts and forum boundaries

- Fast Track Court: added capacity/priority, not a new constitutional species.
- Special Court: powers come from parent law.
- Commercial Court: statutory civil court under 2015 Act; pre-institution mediation boundary under *Patil Automation*.
- Evening/Morning Court: extended-hours administrative arrangement.
- Mediation: neutral facilitates party-made settlement; S.O. 4384(E), 9 October 2023 commenced only Sections 1, 3, 26, 31-38, 45-47, 50-54 and 56-57 of the Mediation Act, 2023.
- S.O. 4781(E), 27 August 2026 established the Mediation Council of India under Section 31(1), head office Delhi; remaining provisions were not thereby commenced.
- Arbitration: agreement-based private civil/commercial adjudication; criminal guilt non-arbitrable.
- Tribunal: specialised statutory adjudication; High Court review preserved by *L. Chandra Kumar*.
- Regular court: coercive relief, full merits, criminal trial, precedent and appellate/constitutional hierarchy.

## Rapid traps and answer spine

- “People's Court” does not mean law-free justice.
- Pending/pre-litigation applies to ordinary LA; pre-litigation only to PLA.
- Consent finality belongs to ordinary LA; PLA merits fallback follows failed conciliation only where the dispute relates to no offence.
- No statutory appeal is not absolute constitutional immunity.
- Settlement statistics require dated sourcing and quality qualification.
- Universal answer spine: **source → entry stage → subject → consent → merits power → outcome → challenge → implementation**.
