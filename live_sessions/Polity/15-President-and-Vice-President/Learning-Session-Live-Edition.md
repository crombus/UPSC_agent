# President and Vice-President - Live Session Edition

The Constitution creates two Union offices that look similar but perform very different jobs. The President is the constitutional head of the Union and normally acts through the elected government. The Vice-President is principally the presiding officer of the Rajya Sabha and becomes a temporary constitutional substitute only when required.

The central puzzle is simple: if the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers govern, why retain a President? The answer lies in separating **constitutional continuity** from **political command**.

### 1. The office map

- **Article 52:** there shall be a President of India.
- **Article 53:** Union executive power is formally vested in the President and exercised directly or through subordinate officers in accordance with the Constitution.
- **Articles 54-62:** election, qualifications, conditions, oath, term, impeachment and vacancy.
- **Articles 63-71:** Vice-President, Chairmanship, acting presidency, election, term, oath and election disputes.
- **Article 72:** Union clemency power.
- **Article 73:** extent of Union executive power.
- **Articles 74-75:** the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister supplies the responsible-government mechanism through which presidential power is ordinarily exercised.

The President is therefore not an ornamental addition to the Prime Minister. The office gives legal form, continuity and constitutional identity to the Union executive. Government changes after elections or loss of confidence; the State continues.

For date-controlled office identification, **Droupadi Murmu** was sworn in as the 15th President of India on **25 July 2022**; the official presidential profile continued to identify her as President when checked on **30 September 2026**.

### 2. Nominal does not mean useless

In a parliamentary system, there is a **dual executive**:

| Constitutional head | Political executive |
|---|---|
| President | Prime Minister and Council of Ministers |
| Symbolises the State and authenticates constitutional action | Makes and defends policy |
| Normally acts on advice | Collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha |
| Has continuity across changes of ministry | Remains only while confidence survives |

Ambedkar's description of the President as the head of the State rather than the head of the executive captures this distinction. “Nominal” means that democratic responsibility belongs to ministers. It does not mean that presidential action is legally irrelevant.

**Example:** A Union appointment may be made formally by the President, but the political responsibility for that appointment ordinarily rests with the elected government. The analogy is a constitutional signature attached to a democratically accountable decision. Its limit is that not every presidential situation is routine: a hung Lok Sabha may require judgment about who should first be invited to prove a majority.

### 3. Why an elected President?

India rejected hereditary monarchy but retained parliamentary government. An indirectly elected President serves four purposes:

1. **Republican legitimacy:** the head of State is elected, not hereditary.
2. **Federal participation:** elected State legislators join elected Parliamentarians in the electoral college.
3. **No rival mass mandate:** indirect election avoids creating a directly elected executive competitor to the Prime Minister.
4. **Proportionate cost and time:** a nationwide direct election would impose the expense and mobilisation of an executive contest on an office designed to function ordinarily as a constitutional head.

The objection is that an indirectly elected head may appear democratically remote. The reply is institutional: the office is not designed to govern independently. Its legitimacy must be appropriate to a constitutional head within responsible government, not to a presidential executive.

### 4. Why a Vice-President?

The Vice-President has two constitutional functions:

1. **Ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** under Article 64.
2. **Acting President or temporary discharger of presidential functions** under Article 65.

The first is regular; the second is contingent. Calling the Vice-President merely a “President-in-waiting” reverses the office's actual workload.

### 5. The foundational distinction

```text
President: continuity + constitutional form + bounded reserve judgment
Prime Minister: policy + political command + parliamentary responsibility
Vice-President: Rajya Sabha presiding role + temporary presidential substitution
```

### UPSC application

A strong answer should avoid both extremes:

- **Wrong extreme 1:** “The President governs India.”
- **Wrong extreme 2:** “The President has no constitutional significance.”

The defensible position is that the office is **ministerial in normal politics and potentially consequential at constitutional margins**.

### Revision notes

1. Articles 52-62 principally govern the President.
2. Articles 63-71 principally govern the Vice-President.
3. Article 72 concerns presidential clemency; Article 73 concerns Union executive extent.
4. Article 53 formally vests Union executive power in the President.
5. Articles 74-75 make that executive politically responsible through ministers.
6. India has a dual executive: nominal President and real PM-led Council.
7. Indirect election prevents a competing popular mandate.
8. Federal participation enters through State MLAs in the presidential election.
9. The Vice-President's regular role is Chairmanship of the Rajya Sabha.
10. “Nominal” means advice-bound, not constitutionally irrelevant.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why would a directly elected, independently policy-making President be difficult to reconcile with India's parliamentary system?

**Model answer:** A directly elected President claiming an independent policy mandate could compete with the Lok Sabha-supported Council of Ministers. Parliamentary government instead locates political authority and accountability in ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, while the President supplies constitutional continuity and formal authority.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not argue that election itself gives the President an independent right to govern; the mode of election and Article 74 must be read together.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** “The Indian President is a constitutional head, not a constitutional nullity.” Explain.

**Model response (142 words):**

Article 53 formally vests Union executive power in the President, but Article 74 places its ordinary exercise within responsible government. The President therefore does not independently make policy; the Prime Minister-led Council, collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3), is the real executive.

Yet the office is not empty. Executive action is expressed in the President's name; the President appoints constitutional authorities, participates in Parliament, assents to legislation and exercises clemency and emergency powers through the prescribed constitutional process. The office also ensures continuity when ministries change. In exceptional situations, such as identifying a claimant capable of proving confidence in a hung Lok Sabha, bounded constitutional judgment becomes important.

Thus, ministerial advice prevents a rival executive, while formal powers and limited reserve judgment prevent the office from becoming a nullity. Its significance is greatest when normal party majorities fail.

**Why this earns marks:** It links the nominal-real distinction to Articles 53, 74 and 75 and gives a qualified rather than absolute verdict.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** constitutional location 2; responsible-government mechanism 3; functions and continuity 2; bounded-discretion example 2; qualified conclusion 1.

### Visual: from federal college to winning quota

```text
ELECTED MPs of both Houses
            +
ELECTED MLAs of States
            +
ELECTED MLAs of Delhi and Puducherry
            │
            ▼
   weighted electoral college
            │
   secret preferential ballot
            │
 proportional representation by STV
            │
            ▼
 candidate crosses the required quota
```

The election has to solve two different problems. States should participate because India is federal, but States differ enormously in population and Assembly size. Article 55 therefore creates a weighted rather than one-person-one-vote college.

### 1. Who votes?

The electoral college under Article 54 contains:

- elected members of the Lok Sabha;
- elected members of the Rajya Sabha;
- elected members of State Legislative Assemblies; and
- elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of Delhi and Puducherry, added through the Seventieth Amendment framework.

It excludes:

- nominated MPs;
- nominated MLAs;
- members of State Legislative Councils; and
- legislators from a Union Territory not constitutionally included in Article 54's explanation.

The inclusion rule is not “all legislators”. It is a carefully limited federal college of elected members from the specified institutions.

### 2. Why are votes weighted?

The Constitution seeks:

1. uniformity among States in the scale of representation; and
2. parity between the States collectively and Parliament collectively.

The value of an MLA's vote is based on the State's population divided by the number of elected Assembly members, with the constitutional thousand-unit calculation and prescribed rounding. For this purpose, the 1971 census population remains operative until the relevant figures of the first census taken after 2026 are published.

```text
Value of one MLA vote
   = State population ÷ elected MLAs ÷ 1000

Value of one elected MP vote
   = total value of all MLA votes
     ÷ total elected MPs of both Houses
```

Every elected MP has the same vote value, whether from the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha. The formula balances the Union and State components; it does not rank one House's MPs above the other's.

**Worked intuition:** A larger Assembly does not automatically give each MLA a larger vote. The decisive ratio is population divided by elected seats. More seats may reduce each individual MLA's value if population does not rise proportionately.

### 3. What does PR-STV mean here?

The ballot is:

- **secret**;
- **preferential**, because electors rank candidates;
- conducted through **proportional representation by the single transferable vote**; and
- counted through transfer of preferences until a candidate reaches the required quota.

Although only one office is being filled, preferential transfer ensures that the winner commands more than a simple plurality of first preferences.

The winning quota is calculated from valid votes polled: divide the total value of valid votes by two, disregard any fraction and add one. If no candidate reaches it on first preferences, the lowest candidate is excluded and those ballots transfer according to the next available preference.

### 4. Constitutional design and statutory administration

| Source | Role |
|---|---|
| Articles 54-55 | Electoral college, vote weighting and voting design |
| Article 71 | Supreme Court jurisdiction over election disputes |
| Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952 | Notification, nomination, scrutiny, withdrawal, poll and election petition |
| Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Rules, 1974 | Forms, ballot and counting mechanics |
| 1997 amendment | 50 proposers, 50 seconders and ₹15,000 deposit for a presidential candidate |

The Election Commission conducts the election, but disputes concerning the election are decided finally by the Supreme Court under Article 71. Article 71(4) adds a close-option safeguard: an election cannot be challenged merely because a vacancy existed, for any reason, among the members of the electoral college.

### 5. Vacancies in the college do not stop the election

An incomplete electoral college does not invalidate the election. A dissolved State Assembly simply contributes no elected MLAs at that time. This prevents institutional paralysis and protects the constitutional timetable.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** The weighted system is too complex and weakens democratic equality.

**Reply:** Complexity serves a constitutional purpose. A direct unweighted vote would erase the federal role of States or create a rival national mandate. The residual cost is that the method is less intuitive for citizens and requires careful technical explanation.

4. Only elected MLAs of States, Delhi and Puducherry participate.
5. MLCs do not participate.
6. MLA value depends on population divided by elected Assembly seats.
7. The 1971 census basis continues until relevant post-2026 census figures are published.
8. Every elected MP has the same presidential-election vote value.
9. The ballot is secret and preferential.
10. The system is PR-STV and uses preference transfer.
11. Article 71 sends disputes to the Supreme Court.
12. Vacancies in the college do not invalidate the election.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does adding more seats to a State Assembly not necessarily increase the value of each MLA's presidential-election vote?

**Model answer:** Each MLA's value depends on the ratio between the State's constitutionally relevant population and its number of elected Assembly seats. Increasing the denominator can reduce, rather than increase, each MLA's value unless population changes proportionately.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse the State's total contribution to the college with the value carried by each individual MLA.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine how the method of electing the President of India reconciles parliamentary government with federal representation.

**Model response (214 words):**

The presidential election is designed not merely to choose an individual but to fit the head of State into a parliamentary federation. Articles 54 and 55 therefore combine indirect election, State participation and weighted voting.

The electoral college includes elected members of both Houses of Parliament and elected MLAs of the States, Delhi and Puducherry. Nominated legislators and Legislative Council members are excluded. This gives the President a federal base while preserving democratic legitimacy through elected representatives.

Vote weighting performs two balances. The value of each MLA's vote reflects the State's population-to-elected-seat ratio, while the value of each elected MP's vote is calculated from the aggregate value of MLA votes. The design therefore seeks relative uniformity among States and parity between Parliament and the States collectively. PR-STV, secret ballot and preference transfer aim to produce a broadly acceptable winner rather than a mere plurality candidate.

The method also serves parliamentary logic. A direct popular election might create an independent political mandate capable of competing with the Lok Sabha-supported Council of Ministers. Indirect election instead legitimises a constitutional head who normally acts on advice.

Its weaknesses are complexity, use of an old census base and democratic remoteness. Nevertheless, the procedure is coherent: federal participation supplies breadth, weighting supplies balance, and indirect election prevents executive dualism.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains mechanisms and connects each to institutional design rather than listing electoral facts.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** constitutional college 3; vote-value mechanism 4; PR-STV explanation 2; parliamentary rationale 3; criticism and qualified verdict 3.

```text
                         │

                         │
```

The Constitution does not protect the President because the holder is above law. It protects the functioning of the highest constitutional office while preserving later legal accountability.

### 1. Qualifications - Article 58

A presidential candidate must:

- be a citizen of India;
- have completed 35 years;
- be qualified for election to the Lok Sabha; and
- not hold an office of profit under the Union, a State or a controlled public authority, subject to constitutional exceptions.

The test is qualification for the Lok Sabha, not actual Lok Sabha membership. If a sitting MP or MLA becomes President, the legislative seat is vacated on entering office.

For this qualification, holding the office of President, Vice-President, Governor or Union/State minister is not treated as holding an office of profit.

### 2. Serious-candidature safeguards

A nomination requires:

- 50 electors as proposers;
- 50 electors as seconders; and
- a ₹15,000 deposit.

The deposit is liable to forfeiture where the statutory vote threshold is not reached. These requirements discourage frivolous nominations without turning the election into a party-only contest.

The deposit is forfeited when an unelected candidate's valid votes do not exceed one-sixth of the votes necessary for return.

### 3. Oath

Article 60 requires the President to swear or affirm:

1. faithfully to execute the office;
2. to preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law; and
3. to devote himself or herself to the service and well-being of the people.

The Chief Justice of India administers the oath; in the CJI's absence, the senior-most available Supreme Court judge does so. A person acting as President or discharging presidential functions takes the corresponding oath.

The phrase “preserve, protect and defend” does not authorise personal policy-making. It describes the constitutional purpose within which advice-bound powers operate.

### 4. Conditions of office - Article 59

The President:

- cannot be a member of either House of Parliament or a State Legislature;
- cannot hold another office of profit;
- receives an official residence without rent;
- receives emoluments, allowances and privileges determined by Parliament; and
- enjoys protection against diminution of emoluments and allowances during the term.

These rules reduce financial and institutional dependence. They are safeguards for the office, not personal privileges without constitutional purpose.

### 5. Article 361 immunity

| Protection | Precise effect |
|---|---|
| Official acts | President is not answerable to a court for exercise and performance of official powers and duties |
| Criminal process | No criminal proceeding may be instituted or continued during the term |
| Arrest | No process for arrest or imprisonment may issue during the term |
| Personal civil acts | Civil proceedings may begin during the term only after two months' written notice |

Article 361 does not erase responsibility:

- governmental action taken in the President's name can still be judicially reviewed;
- ministers remain politically accountable;
- criminal proceedings are postponed, not permanently extinguished; and
- impeachment remains available for violation of the Constitution.

**Objection:** Such immunity creates inequality before law.

**Reply:** Temporary and functional protection prevents courts or litigants from disabling the constitutional head. The residual concern is real, but it is answered through review of governmental action, ministerial responsibility, impeachment and proceedings after the term.

### 6. Common distinctions

- Qualification for Lok Sabha is not the same as being a Lok Sabha member.
- Office immunity is not immunity for the validity of government action.
- Civil notice concerns personal acts; it does not make official decisions unreviewable.
- The oath does not create an independent reservoir of executive discretion.

### UPSC application

Questions commonly combine eligibility, office of profit, oath and immunity. Write each as a separate legal category. Do not merge personal immunity under Article 361 with the political responsibility of ministers.

4. Office of profit is disqualifying subject to constitutional exceptions.
5. Nomination needs 50 proposers and 50 seconders.
6. Deposit is ₹15,000.
7. The CJI normally administers the oath.
8. The oath includes preserving, protecting and defending the Constitution.
9. The President cannot remain a legislator.
10. Emoluments cannot be diminished during the term.

13. Article 361 does not immunise governmental action from review.

### Concept check

**Question:** A court cannot prosecute a sitting President criminally. Does that also mean an executive order issued in the President's name cannot be judicially reviewed?

**Model answer:** No. Article 361 protects the person of the President during the term, but it does not make governmental action immune. Courts may review the validity of executive action, while the responsible ministers and government remain answerable.

**Misconception to avoid:** Personal constitutional immunity and immunity of State action are not the same proposition.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains prompt:** Explain the constitutional purpose and limits of the immunities granted to the President of India.

**Model response (145 words):**

Article 361 protects the continuity and dignity of the presidential office, not an unaccountable individual. The President is not answerable to a court for the exercise of official powers; criminal proceedings cannot be instituted or continued, and arrest or imprisonment cannot issue during the term. Personal civil proceedings require two months' prior notice.

These protections prevent litigation from obstructing the functioning of the constitutional head. They are, however, bounded. Executive action taken in the President's name remains subject to judicial review, and ministers remain politically responsible for advice and policy. Criminal liability is postponed rather than extinguished, because proceedings may follow the term. Impeachment under Article 61 also remains available for violation of the Constitution.

Thus Article 361 creates temporary functional insulation while preserving constitutional, political and eventual legal accountability. It should be understood as an institutional safeguard, not as a licence for personal impunity.

**Why this earns marks:** It states each protection, explains its purpose and immediately supplies the accountability counterweights.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** protections 4; institutional rationale 2; review and ministerial responsibility 2; later accountability 1; balanced conclusion 1.

### Visual: removal and continuity operate together

```text
NORMAL TERM
 5 years ── successor delayed ── outgoing President continues

EARLY VACANCY
 death / resignation / removal / otherwise
                  │
                  ▼
 Vice-President acts as President
                  │

                  │
 new President receives full five-year term

VP unavailable
   └── CJI, then senior-most available Supreme Court judge, acts
```

Removal is deliberately difficult, but continuity is deliberately easy. The Constitution combines a high impeachment threshold with multiple fallback arrangements so that the office never becomes institutionally vacant.

### 1. Term and re-election - Articles 56-57

The President holds office for five years from entering office and may resign to the Vice-President. **Article 57** permits re-election without a constitutional term limit. Dr Rajendra Prasad remains the only President to have served two full terms.

If the five-year term expires before a successor assumes office, the incumbent continues. The Vice-President does not act merely because the calendar term has ended. This prevents an interregnum.

### 2. Impeachment under Article 61

The sole ground is **“violation of the Constitution”**, a phrase left undefined.

```text
Either House initiates
        │
notice signed by at least one-fourth of total members
        │
14 days' notice to the President
        │
first House passes charge by 2/3 of total membership
        │
other House investigates or causes investigation
        │
President may appear and be represented
        │
other House sustains charge by 2/3 of total membership
        │
removal from date of second resolution
```

Impeachment is quasi-judicial because the second House investigates the charge and the President has a defence right.

### 3. The election-impeachment reversal

| Group | Presidential election | Impeachment |
|---|---:|---:|
| Elected MPs | Yes | Yes |
| Nominated MPs | No | Yes |
| Elected MLAs | Yes | No |
| MLCs | No | No |

This is a favourite close-option area. Impeachment is a parliamentary process, so State legislators do not participate. Nominated MPs participate because Article 61 speaks of members of the Houses, not only elected members.

No President has so far been impeached.

### 4. Vacancy routes

A vacancy may arise through:

- expiry of term;
- resignation;
- impeachment removal;
- death;
- disqualification; or
- the election being declared void.

For an expected expiry, the election should be completed before the term ends. For a casual vacancy caused by death, resignation, removal or otherwise, an election must be held within six months. The person elected receives a fresh five-year term.

Article 71(2) protects acts already done before the Supreme Court declares a presidential election void. Otherwise, every official act could be destabilised retrospectively.

### 5. Acting and discharging functions

- On a vacancy, the Vice-President **acts as President** until the new President assumes office.
- During temporary inability through absence, illness or another cause, the Vice-President **discharges the functions** until the President resumes.
- Article 70 permits Parliament to provide for contingencies not otherwise covered. Under the **President (Discharge of Functions) Act, 1969**, if the Vice-Presidential office is vacant or the Vice-President cannot act, the CJI, or the senior-most available Supreme Court judge if necessary, performs the function.
- The acting person enjoys presidential powers, immunities and prescribed emoluments for that period.

The common shorthand “maximum six months” refers to the constitutional deadline for electing a successor after a casual presidential vacancy. It does not mean the Vice-President succeeds to the remainder of the term.

8. The second House investigates.
9. The President may appear and be represented.
10. Nominated MPs participate; State MLAs do not.
11. The incumbent continues after term expiry until the successor enters office.
12. A casual-vacancy election must occur within six months.
13. Acts before an election is declared void remain valid.
14. CJI and then senior-most available SC judge form the fallback.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the Vice-President not automatically begin acting as President when the incumbent's five-year term expires?

**Model answer:** Article 56 allows the outgoing President to continue until the successor assumes office. Acting arrangements are therefore unnecessary for an ordinary term-expiry delay and are reserved for a vacancy or inability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat every delay in presidential election as a casual vacancy requiring the Vice-President to act.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Analyse how the presidential impeachment and vacancy provisions balance accountability with continuity.

**Model response (206 words):**

The Constitution makes presidential removal difficult but presidential continuity automatic. This combination protects both accountability and institutional stability.

Article 61 permits impeachment only for “violation of the Constitution”. Either House may initiate a charge after fourteen days' notice signed by at least one-fourth of its total membership. The initiating House and the investigating House must each pass the resolution by two-thirds of total membership. The President may appear and be represented during the investigation, giving the process a quasi-judicial character. Nominated MPs participate, whereas State legislators do not, because impeachment is a parliamentary process. The high threshold protects the head of State from routine partisan removal, though the undefined ground leaves interpretive uncertainty.

Continuity rules address every vacancy. An outgoing President continues beyond five years until the successor assumes office. A casual vacancy triggers an election within six months, with the Vice-President acting meanwhile. Temporary inability is similarly covered. If the Vice-President cannot act, the CJI and then the senior-most available Supreme Court judge provide further fallback. Article 71(2) preserves acts done before an election is declared void.

The design therefore avoids both executive impunity and constitutional interruption. Its weakness is not procedural absence but the lack of practical precedent defining “violation of the Constitution”.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects the removal threshold to stability and includes defence rights, participation rules and the complete succession chain.

## Lesson 5 - The seven heads of presidential power

Progress: 5/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Executive, legislative, financial, judicial, diplomatic, military and emergency functions

### Visual: one office, seven constitutional gateways

```text
                           PRESIDENT
                               │
     ┌──────────┬──────────┬────┴────┬──────────┬─────────┬─────────┬─────────┐
 Executive  Legislative  Financial  Judicial  Diplomatic Military Emergency
 Art 53/77  Arts 79-123  Arts112-17 72/124/143 treaties  command 352/356/360
```

The President's powers are wide in subject matter but not personal in political ownership. The correct question is never merely “Does the President possess this power?” It is “Through what advice, procedure, parliamentary control or judicial limit is it exercised?”

### 1. Executive powers

All Union executive action is expressed in the President's name under Article 77. The President appoints, according to the applicable constitutional or statutory process:

- the Prime Minister and, on the PM's advice, other ministers;
- the Attorney-General;
- the Comptroller and Auditor-General;
- Governors;
- judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts;
- the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners;
- UPSC members;
- the Finance Commission; and
- other constitutional authorities.

Formal appointment does not mean unrestricted personal selection. Collegium recommendations, statutory selection committees, ministerial advice and constitutional conventions control different appointments.

The President makes rules for the convenient transaction of Union business and allocation among ministers under Article 77. Article 77(2) also requires orders and instruments made in the President's name to be authenticated in the prescribed manner; once so authenticated, their validity cannot be challenged merely on the ground that the President did not personally make or execute them. The President may seek information from the Prime Minister under Article 78 and may require a ministerial decision not considered by the Council to be placed before it.

Additional executive and special functions include administering Union Territories through appointed administrators under Article 239; making Article 240 regulations for the constitutionally specified Union Territories, subject to that Article's conditions; constituting an Inter-State Council under Article 263; appointing commissions under Articles 339(1), 340 and 344; and exercising functions assigned by the Fifth and Sixth Schedules concerning Scheduled and tribal areas. These powers remain advice-bound or procedure-bound rather than personal.

### 2. Legislative powers

The President is a constituent part of Parliament under Article 79, although not a member of either House. Functions include:

- summoning and proroguing Parliament;
- dissolving the Lok Sabha;
- addressing Parliament and sending messages;
- summoning a joint sitting where Article 108 permits;
- nominating 12 Rajya Sabha members under Article 80(3) from persons with special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service;
- appointing a Rajya Sabha or Lok Sabha member temporarily to perform presiding duties where Articles 91 or 95 apply;
- deciding an MP's Article 103 disqualification question after obtaining, and acting according to, the Election Commission's opinion;
- giving the prior recommendation required for Bills under Articles 3, 117 and 274;
- assenting to Bills;
- promulgating ordinances; and
- laying specified reports before Parliament through the constitutional process.

Articles 86 and 87 distinguish the President's legislative communications: Article 86 permits addresses and messages to either House or both Houses, while Article 87 requires the special address at the first session after each general election and the first session of every year. The former Anglo-Indian nomination to the Lok Sabha ceased under the 104th Amendment arrangement.

### 3. Financial powers

- The Annual Financial Statement is caused to be laid before Parliament.
- A Money Bill requires presidential recommendation before introduction.
- Demands for grants proceed on presidential recommendation.
- Advances may be made from the Contingency Fund according to law.
- The Finance Commission is constituted periodically.

The President does not personally design the Budget. These are constitutional gateways through which the elected government places financial business before Parliament.

### 4. Judicial powers

- Appointment of judges follows Articles 124 and 217 and the operative appointments process.
- Article 143 permits the President to seek the Supreme Court's advisory opinion; the opinion is not binding.
- Article 72 supplies clemency.

The appointment power is formally presidential but materially constrained. Advisory jurisdiction is consultative, not an appeal or binding direction.

The five-judge opinion in **Special Reference No. 1 of 2025**, delivered on **20 November 2025** and reported as **2025 INSC 1333**, supplies a concrete boundary. It is an Article 143 **advisory opinion and therefore non-binding**. On Articles 200-201 it held that courts cannot prescribe a uniform constitutional timeline for the Governor or President, cannot create “deemed assent” through Article 142, and cannot adjudicate a Bill's merits before it becomes law. It nevertheless recognised a limited mandamus directing a Governor to act, without dictating the outcome, where inaction under Article 200 is prolonged, unexplained and indefinite. The President need not make an Article 143 reference whenever a Bill is reserved under Article 201.

### 5. Diplomatic and military powers

Treaties and international agreements are formally concluded in the President's name, subject to constitutional practice and parliamentary legislation where domestic legal change is required. The President receives and accredits diplomatic representatives.

The President is Supreme Commander of the defence forces, formally appoints service chiefs and formally declares war or concludes peace. Operational and policy control, expenditure and any required legislation nevertheless remain with the elected government and Parliament within law.

### 6. Emergency powers

The President may issue proclamations under:

- Article 352: National Emergency;
- Article 356, read with Articles 355 and 365: failure of constitutional machinery in a State; and
- Article 360: Financial Emergency.

These powers are governed by Cabinet advice, parliamentary approval, duration rules and judicial review. The phrase “presidential satisfaction” does not create a free personal emergency jurisdiction.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** The long list proves the President is the real executive.

**Reply:** Constitutional form and political control must be distinguished. The powers are vested in or exercised by the President so that State action has a continuing legal personality. Article 74 and the specialised procedures governing appointments, finance, legislation and emergencies allocate real decision-making and accountability elsewhere.

### UPSC application

Use the mnemonic **E-L-F-J-D-M-E**, but do not stop at classification. Add one limitation to every head:

```text
power → procedure/advice → accountability → limitation
```

### Revision notes

1. Executive action is expressed in the President's name and authenticated under Article 77.
2. Formal appointment does not imply personal selection.
3. Articles 239-240 cover UT administration and specified UT regulation-making.
4. Articles 263, 339, 340 and 344 and the Fifth/Sixth Schedules assign council, commission and area-related functions.
5. The President is part of Parliament but not a member.
6. Articles 91 and 95 permit temporary presiding appointments; Article 103 MP-disqualification decisions follow the ECI's opinion.
7. Twelve members may be nominated to the Rajya Sabha; Anglo-Indian Lok Sabha nomination has ceased.
8. Articles 3, 117 and 274 contain specified prior-recommendation gates.
9. The Annual Financial Statement is laid through presidential authority.
10. Article 143 advice is non-binding; the 20 November 2025 opinion bars court-created assent timelines and deemed assent.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to infer personal presidential control merely from the Constitution stating that an appointment or executive action is made by the President?

**Model answer:** Constitutional attribution identifies the formal legal actor. The effective decision may be controlled by ministerial advice, a collegium, a statutory committee or another prescribed process. Formal vesting and political ownership are therefore different.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not replace the President with the Prime Minister in the constitutional text; explain instead how presidential form and ministerial responsibility operate together.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Classify the powers of the President of India and assess why their breadth does not create a presidential executive.

**Model response (223 words):**

The President's powers span the constitutional system but are embedded in parliamentary responsibility.

Executive powers include appointments, authenticated Union action, UT administration and specified councils, commissions and Scheduled/tribal-area functions. Legislative powers arise because the President forms part of Parliament: sessions, dissolution, addresses, nominations, temporary presiding appointments, Article 103 decisions on ECI opinion, prior-recommendation gates, assent and ordinances. Financial functions include Money-Bill and grant recommendations, the Annual Financial Statement and Finance Commission. Judicial functions include appointments, Article 143 references and clemency. Diplomatic and military powers include formal representation, supreme command and war/peace functions. Articles 352, 356 and 360 provide emergency powers.

This breadth does not create an American-style presidency. Article 74 binds the President to ministerial advice after one reconsideration. Appointments may also be governed by collegium recommendations, statutory selection processes or conventions. Financial action requires Parliament; emergencies require prescribed advice and parliamentary approval; ordinances are temporary and reviewable; clemency is advised and judicially reviewable on limited grounds. The non-binding Article 143 opinion of 20 November 2025 also rejected judicially created timelines and deemed assent under Articles 200-201.

The President therefore supplies a single constitutional personality through which diverse State action is expressed. The office is broad in legal form but bounded in political control. Its exceptional importance appears where the Constitution demands continuity, caution or temporary judgment, not ordinary policy leadership.

The President's constitutional position cannot be understood from a list of powers. It turns on who advises, whether that advice binds and what happens when no ministry clearly commands confidence.

### 1. Article 74 after the 42nd and 44th Amendments

- The **42nd Amendment, 1976** made ministerial advice expressly binding.
- The **44th Amendment, 1978** allowed the President to require reconsideration once.
- After reconsideration, the advice must be accepted.
- Article 74(2) bars judicial inquiry into whether advice was given and what its contents were, though the resulting governmental action may still be reviewed.

The return-once power is a warning mechanism, not a personal veto over government policy.

### 2. Article 75 and democratic responsibility

The President appoints the Prime Minister. In ordinary circumstances, convention identifies the choice: the leader who commands a Lok Sabha majority. Other ministers are appointed on the Prime Minister's advice. The Council is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.

This is why “pleasure of the President” does not normally permit personal dismissal of ministers. A minister is removed on the Prime Minister's advice; a ministry remains while it retains confidence.

### 3. Article 78 and the right to be informed

The Prime Minister must:

- communicate Council decisions;
- furnish information requested by the President; and
- place before the Council a ministerial decision that the President requires it to consider.

These rights allow warning, consultation and constitutional counselling. They do not transfer policy command to the President.

### 4. Situational discretion

The Constitution does not provide a general list of personal presidential discretion comparable to the Governor's Article 163 carve-out. Limited judgment arises from circumstances:

1. **Hung Lok Sabha:** identifying the claimant most likely to command confidence.
2. **Sudden vacancy in the premiership:** appointing an interim or successor leader pending political settlement.
3. **Ministry apparently losing majority:** requiring a prompt floor test rather than relying on private claims.
4. **Dissolution request after loss of confidence:** deciding whether an alternative government is viable before dissolving the House.
5. **Dismissal:** only where a ministry refuses to resign despite demonstrably losing confidence.

The controlling principle is restoration of Lok Sabha responsibility, not presidential preference.

**Examples:** Charan Singh in 1979 and Vajpayee in 1996 illustrate majority uncertainty; the legitimacy of the President's choice ultimately depended on the floor of the House. K.R. Narayanan's return of advice relating to proposed President's Rule in Uttar Pradesh in 1997 and Bihar in 1998 illustrates the one-reconsideration power.

### 5. “Rubber stamp” versus activist presidency

**Critique:** Binding advice makes the office a rubber stamp.

**Reply:** Democratic government requires ministers, not an unaccountable head, to decide policy. The President's contribution is consultation, warning, reconsideration and neutral handling of exceptional transitions.

**Opposite critique:** A President with an electoral mandate should actively correct government.

**Reply:** That would create a rival executive without parliamentary responsibility. The residual risk is that informal influence is difficult to measure and may become controversial during fragmented mandates.

### 6. Safe analytical formula

```text
normal majority politics → advice-bound formality
constitutional uncertainty → bounded judgment
all routes → floor test and ministerial responsibility
```

4. Reconsidered advice binds.
5. Article 74(2) protects the advice from judicial inquiry.
6. Government action itself may still be reviewed.

**Question:** In a hung Lok Sabha, what makes presidential judgment constitutional rather than personal?

**Model answer:** The President must use objective indications of support to identify a claimant who can promptly prove confidence on the Lok Sabha floor. The purpose is to establish responsible government, not to choose a preferred political programme.

**Misconception to avoid:** A hung House does not suspend parliamentary government or permit prolonged presidential rule.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** “The President's discretion is dormant in normal politics but decisive in abnormal politics.” Critically examine.

**Model response (211 words):**

The proposition captures the difference between clear-majority government and constitutional uncertainty. Under Article 74, strengthened by the 42nd Amendment, the President normally acts on ministerial advice. The 44th Amendment permits one reconsideration, but reconsidered advice binds. Articles 75 and 78 further locate political command and responsibility in the Prime Minister-led Council.

With a stable Lok Sabha majority, appointment of the Prime Minister, ministerial tenure, policy action and dissolution advice ordinarily leave little presidential choice. This is democratic design, not institutional weakness.

Situational judgment arises when ordinary party signals fail. In a hung House, the President must identify the claimant most likely to prove confidence. If a ministry appears to have lost its majority, a floor test is the constitutional answer. Before accepting dissolution from a defeated ministry, the President may examine whether an alternative government is viable. K.R. Narayanan's return of advice concerning President's Rule illustrates the limited reconsideration power.

However, such discretion is not a personal policy jurisdiction. It is bounded by objective material, conventions, judicially enforceable constitutional principles and the need for a prompt floor test. Excessive activism would create a rival executive without Lok Sabha accountability.

Therefore, the President matters most when party government is unclear, but even then the office must restore, not replace, responsible government.

“Presidential veto” is not one power. The available choice depends on the class and source of the Bill.

### 1. Legislative participation before assent

The President:

- summons Parliament, ensuring no more than six months between sessions;
- prorogues either House;
- dissolves the Lok Sabha;
- addresses the first session after each general election and the first session each year;
- may send messages;
- may summon a joint sitting for an ordinary-Bill deadlock; and
- nominates 12 Rajya Sabha members.

Adjournment belongs to the presiding officer. Prorogation and dissolution are presidential acts exercised through constitutional advice.

### 2. Article 111 choices

For an ordinary Bill, the President may:

1. assent;
2. withhold assent; or
3. return it once, with a message, for reconsideration.

If Parliament passes the returned Bill again, with or without amendment, the President must assent. Re-passage needs an ordinary majority, not a special veto-override majority.

A Money Bill cannot be returned. It may receive or be denied assent, but its introduction already required presidential recommendation.

### 3. Three veto labels

| Veto | Meaning | Constitutional effect |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute | Withhold assent | Bill ends |
| Suspensive | Return an ordinary Bill | Parliament may override by re-passing it |
| Pocket | Take no decision where no time limit is prescribed | Bill remains pending |

Examples traditionally associated with absolute veto include the PEPSU Appropriation Bill, 1954 and an MPs' salary measure in 1991. President Zail Singh's handling of the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986 is the classic pocket-veto illustration.

India has **no qualified veto**. That label belongs to systems where the legislature must use a higher majority to override.

### 4. Constitutional amendment Bills

After the 24th Amendment, Article 368 requires the President to assent to a duly passed Constitution Amendment Bill. The President cannot return or withhold it. No joint sitting resolves disagreement between the Houses.

### 5. Reserved State Bills

When a Governor reserves a State Bill, Article 201 permits the President to:

- assent;
- withhold assent; or
- direct the Governor to return a non-Money Bill to the State Legislature.

Even if the State Legislature passes the returned Bill again, the Constitution does not compel presidential assent. This differs sharply from Article 111 re-passage by Parliament.

Article 201's proviso nevertheless creates one express clock: when a non-Money Bill is returned at the President's direction, the State Legislature must reconsider it within six months from receiving the message. If passed again, it returns to the President for consideration, but assent still is not compulsory.

The difference reflects federal supervision, but it also creates a criticism: an open-ended Union-level assent route can delay State legislation. The defence is that reservation protects constitutional and national concerns. In **Special Reference No. 1 of 2025 (20 November 2025; 2025 INSC 1333)**, the Supreme Court advised that presidential action under Article 201 is not merits-justiciable before the Bill becomes law, courts cannot prescribe a uniform decision timeline, and Article 142 cannot produce deemed assent. The opinion is advisory and non-binding. Its no-fixed-deadline conclusion concerns presidential decision-making; it does not erase the express six-month reconsideration period imposed on the State Legislature.

### 6. Bills awaiting assent and dissolution

A Bill passed by both Houses and awaiting assent does not lapse merely because the Lok Sabha is dissolved. Nor does a returned Bill lapse for that reason. This separates the completed parliamentary stage from pending business within the dissolved House.

### UPSC traps

- The President is part of Parliament but not a member.
- A Money Bill cannot be returned.
- A Constitution Amendment Bill must receive assent.
- An ordinary Bill re-passed by Parliament must receive assent.
- A re-passed reserved State Bill does not compel assent.
- Pocket veto arises from absence of a constitutional time limit.
- Qualified veto is not an Indian presidential veto.

3. Adjournment is not a presidential act.
4. Article 108 permits specified joint sittings.
5. Article 111 governs parliamentary Bills.
6. Ordinary Bills may be returned once.
7. Money Bills may not be returned.
8. Re-passed ordinary Bills require assent.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can Parliament overcome a suspensive veto while a State Legislature cannot compel assent to a Bill re-passed after an Article 201 return?

**Model answer:** Article 111 expressly requires assent after Parliament re-passes a returned Bill. Article 201 contains no equivalent command for a reserved State Bill; the President therefore remains free to assent or withhold after the State re-passes it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not apply the parliamentary override rule automatically to reserved State Bills.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Examine the President's veto powers with reference to the different constitutional treatment of ordinary, Money, amendment and reserved State Bills.

**Model response (221 words):**

The expression “presidential veto” conceals distinct constitutional routes. Under Article 111, the President may assent to, withhold assent from or return an ordinary Bill. Return creates a suspensive veto: if Parliament re-passes the Bill, the President must assent. Withholding produces an absolute veto. Because no decision deadline is prescribed, prolonged inaction is described as a pocket veto.

A Money Bill cannot be returned, though assent may be granted or withheld. Its special treatment reflects Lok Sabha financial primacy and the fact that introduction already required presidential recommendation.

A Constitution Amendment Bill follows a still narrower route. Article 368, after the 24th Amendment, makes presidential assent obligatory. The President may neither return nor veto a duly passed amendment.

Article 201 governs State Bills reserved by a Governor. The President may assent, withhold assent or direct return of a non-Money Bill. The State Legislature must reconsider a returned Bill within six months, but re-passage does not compel assent. The non-binding Article 143 opinion of 20 November 2025 rejected court-created presidential timelines and deemed assent. This asymmetry gives the Union a stronger check but creates federal-delay concerns.

India therefore recognises absolute, suspensive and pocket vetoes, not the American qualified veto. The powers are best understood not as personal legislative supremacy but as differentiated constitutional checkpoints whose strength depends on the Bill's origin and category.

### Visual: the ordinance lifecycle

```text
Both Houses in session? ── YES ──> no Article 123 ordinance
          │
          NO: either House or both Houses not in session
          │
urgent circumstances requiring immediate action
          │

```

The correct trigger is precise: an ordinance may be promulgated when **both Houses are not simultaneously in session**. It is enough that either House is not in session, because Parliament cannot enact a law through one House alone.

### 1. Purpose and satisfaction

Article 123 addresses circumstances requiring immediate action during a parliamentary recess. It is not a parallel ordinary law-making channel.

The President acts on ministerial advice. The satisfaction requirement concerns urgency, but it is not immune from review. *R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* established that mala fide exercise may be examined; the 44th Amendment removed the attempted finality of presidential satisfaction inserted during the Emergency era.

### 2. Scope

An ordinance:

- has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament while operative;
- may address only a subject on which Parliament can legislate;
- may amend or repeal an existing Central Act;
- may operate retrospectively where legislation could do so;
- is “law” for Article 13 and cannot abridge Fundamental Rights; and
- cannot amend the Constitution.

The same force as an Act does not mean the same permanence. The ordinance is constitutionally temporary unless replaced by legislation.

### 3. Duration and reassembly

Every ordinance must be laid before both Houses. It ceases:

- six weeks after Parliament reassembles;
- earlier if both Houses pass disapproving resolutions; or
- upon withdrawal by the President.

Where the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six weeks run from the later date. The conventional maximum of approximately six months plus six weeks assumes the maximum permissible inter-session gap; it is not a guaranteed lifespan.

### 4. Re-promulgation

*D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* condemned serial re-promulgation as a “fraud on the Constitution”. The executive cannot keep temporary legislation alive merely by repeatedly renewing it without facing the legislature.

*Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2017), decided by seven judges with a 5:2 majority, strengthened the rule:

- laying before the legislature is mandatory;
- ordinance satisfaction is judicially reviewable;
- re-promulgation subverts legislative supremacy; and
- an expired or invalid ordinance does **not automatically create enduring rights or immunities**.

The final point requires precision. The Court rejected a blanket theory that every completed consequence survives forever. Whether a past transaction or effect can be recognised depends on constitutional principle, the nature of the action, public interest and justice; survival is not automatic simply because the ordinance once had Act-like force.

### 5. Institutional incentive and objection-reply

**Why executives use ordinances:** They permit immediate action and may bypass delay or an unfavourable parliamentary calendar.

**Objection:** The power undermines deliberation and bicameral scrutiny.

**Reply:** Genuine urgency can make temporary law necessary. The constitutional defence is therefore conditional: prompt laying, parliamentary decision, judicial review and no re-promulgation.

The unresolved practical problem is timing. Even a short-lived ordinance may alter conduct before Parliament or a court responds.

## Lesson 9 - Clemency under Article 72: mercy governed by law

Progress: 9/12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Forms, jurisdiction, advice, review and India-USA comparison

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried - Article 72 forms, Article 161 boundary and clemency cases
CA search: "Not run - no additional contemporary event is required for the clemency doctrine"
CA found: Not relevant
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: identify the form, then the jurisdiction

```text
PARDON      removes conviction, sentence and resulting disqualifications
REPRIEVE    temporary stay, especially before execution
RESPITE     lesser sentence due to special circumstance
REMISSION   reduces duration without changing sentence character
COMMUTATION substitutes a lighter form of punishment

Article 72 jurisdiction
 court-martial + Union-executive-law offences + every death sentence
```

Clemency is an executive safety valve. It can address compassion, public welfare, evident injustice or considerations that a court applying legal guilt and sentence may not fully accommodate. It is neither an appellate acquittal nor personal royal mercy.

### 1. The five forms

| Form | Effect |
|---|---|
| Pardon | Removes both conviction and sentence and absolves resulting punishment and disqualifications |
| Reprieve | Temporarily stays execution of a sentence |
| Respite | Awards a lesser sentence because of a special condition |
| Remission | Reduces the period without changing the nature of punishment |
| Commutation | Replaces the punishment with a lighter form |

The terms should not be used interchangeably. Reducing a prison term is remission; changing death to life imprisonment is commutation; complete legal forgiveness is pardon.

### 2. Article 72 jurisdiction

The President may act:

1. in court-martial cases;
2. for offences against laws relating to matters within Union executive power; and
3. in **all cases where the sentence is a sentence of death**.

The death-sentence limb is punishment-based and universal. The Union-law limb is subject-based.

### 3. Article 161 comparison - controlled formulation

Article 161 empowers the Governor to grant pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, suspension or commutation for an offence against a law relating to a matter within State executive power. It does not contain Article 72(1)(c)'s separate punishment-based power over **all** death sentences.

The Supreme Court in ***State of Haryana v. Raj Kumar @ Bittu* (2021)** reaffirmed that Article 161 clemency is an independent constitutional power, not cut down by statutory remission restrictions, and is exercised on State ministerial advice. A Governor may pardon, suspend, remit or commute a death sentence where the underlying offence falls within State executive competence. The President's distinctive reach is broader: Article 72 expressly covers every death sentence regardless of the Union-State subject division and also covers court-martial sentences.

This corrects a common textbook shorthand. “The Governor cannot pardon a death sentence” is legally overbroad. The accurate distinction is not pardon versus no pardon, but universal punishment-based jurisdiction under Article 72 versus offence-and-State-field jurisdiction under Article 161.

The controlled distinction is:

| Question | President, Article 72 | Governor, Article 161 |
|---|---|---|
| Court-martial | Expressly covered | Not covered |
| Death sentence | May pardon and otherwise grant clemency in **all** death-sentence cases | May pardon and otherwise grant clemency where the offence lies within State executive competence |
| Advice | Union Council's advice | State Council's advice |
| Judicial review | Limited | Limited |

Therefore, Article 72 gives the President a special all-death-sentences jurisdiction irrespective of the ordinary Union-State subject division, while Article 161 remains tied to State executive competence and lacks court-martial reach.

### 4. Advice and application of mind

*Maru Ram v. Union of India* establishes that constitutional clemency is exercised on ministerial advice. The President is not an independent personal dispenser of mercy.

*Kehar Singh v. Union of India* permits examination of the merits and materials afresh. Clemency may take a view different from the court on mercy-related considerations, but it does not overturn the judicial judgment as an appellate court would.

### 5. Judicial review

*Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh* confirms limited review where the decision is affected by:

- mala fides;
- arbitrariness;
- non-application of mind;
- wholly irrelevant considerations; or
- relevant material being kept out.

Courts review the legality of decision-making, not the desirability of mercy.

**Objection:** Review destroys the breadth of clemency.

**Reply:** Limited review preserves constitutional legality without converting the court into the clemency authority. The residual difficulty is delay, particularly where a death sentence makes time itself a matter of dignity and justice.

### 6. India and the United States

The US President's Article II power:

- is a personal constitutional executive power;
- covers federal offences;
- does not cover State offences;
- expressly excludes impeachment; and
- may be used before charge or conviction for completed conduct.

A **pre-emptive pardon** is not permission for a future crime. It protects against prosecution for conduct already completed even though formal proceedings have not begun.

India's Article 72 operates within parliamentary government, ministerial advice and limited judicial review. The US doctrine should not be mechanically imported.

### Visual: the Vice-President's narrower electoral base

```text

```

The electoral difference follows function. The President represents the Union within a federal system; the Vice-President's regular constitutional work is centred in Parliament.

### 1. Election

Article 66 creates an electoral college consisting of members of both Houses of Parliament:

- elected Lok Sabha members;
- elected Rajya Sabha members; and
- nominated members. In the present parliamentary composition this means nominated Rajya Sabha members, because the Lok Sabha now has no nominated-member category.

State legislators do not participate. The election uses PR-STV and secret ballot, but no State-MLA vote-value formula is required.

The Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952 and Rules, 1974 administer the process. A candidate requires 20 proposers, 20 seconders and a ₹15,000 deposit.

Disputes concerning the Vice-Presidential election, like presidential-election disputes, fall within the Supreme Court's final jurisdiction under Article 71.

### 2. Qualifications and oath

The candidate must:

- be an Indian citizen;
- be at least 35;
- be qualified for election to the Rajya Sabha; and
- hold no disqualifying office of profit.

Under Article 69, the oath is administered by the President or a person appointed by the President. This contrasts with the presidential oath administered by the CJI or senior-most available Supreme Court judge.

### 3. Term and re-election

The term is five years. The Vice-President:

- resigns to the President;
- may continue until a successor assumes office; and
- is eligible for re-election without a constitutional term limit.

The Constitution fixes no acting Vice-President. If the office is vacant, its Rajya Sabha functions are handled through the Deputy Chairman and House arrangements until a successor enters office.

The Constitution does not attach a separate salary to the Vice-Presidency as such. The office-holder draws the remuneration attached to the ex officio Chairmanship of the Rajya Sabha and, while acting as President, receives the prescribed presidential emoluments.

### 4. Removal

Article 67(b) does not use presidential impeachment.

```text
Rajya Sabha initiates
        │
14 days' notice
        │
resolution by effective majority
(majority of all the then members)
        │
Lok Sabha agrees by simple majority
        │
removal
```

No constitutional ground need be proved. The process begins only in the Rajya Sabha because the Vice-President is its ex officio Chairman.

**Distinction:** two-thirds of total membership belongs to presidential impeachment. Effective majority in the Rajya Sabha plus Lok Sabha agreement belongs to Vice-Presidential removal.

### 5. Vacancy and current constitutional illustration

After the 2025 casual vacancy, C. P. Radhakrishnan was declared elected on **9 September 2025** and assumed office on **12 September 2025**. The official constitutional-office profile continued to identify him as Vice-President when checked on **30 September 2026**.

This event illustrates Article 68:

- an election to fill an expected vacancy should be completed before term expiry;
- a casual vacancy must be filled **as soon as possible**;
- Article 68 sets no six-month outer deadline; and
- the successor receives a fresh five-year term.

Do not import Article 62's six-month presidential-vacancy rule into Article 68.

### 6. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** The Vice-President lacks an independent executive portfolio and has therefore been described by some commentators as “His Superfluous Highness”.

**Reply:** The criticism mistakes executive activity for constitutional value. The Chairmanship of the federal chamber is a continuing responsibility, while the acting-President function provides continuity during the highest-level vacancy.

The residual concern is political neutrality: a person elected through Parliament must preside impartially over a polarised House.

The Vice-President's constitutional significance is usually measured on the floor of the Rajya Sabha, not in the rare event of a presidential vacancy.

### 1. Ex officio Chairman

Articles 64 and 89(1) make the Vice-President ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. Article 89(2) requires the Rajya Sabha to choose a Deputy Chairman from among its members; Article 91 governs the Deputy Chairman's performance of the Chair's duties when the office is vacant or the Chairman is absent. The Chairman is not a member of the House. The office:

- presides over sittings;
- maintains order and decorum;
- interprets and applies rules;
- decides points of order and admissibility;
- regulates proceedings and recognises speakers;
- refers matters to committees where rules permit; and
- protects the institutional functioning of the Council of States.

Article 100(1), not the Chairman's non-membership, bars the presiding officer from voting in the first instance. The Chairman's non-membership is a separate institutional distinction. Article 100 permits a casting vote when votes are equally divided.

### 2. Article 92 - when the Chair is personally involved

While a resolution for removal of the Vice-President is under consideration:

- the Vice-President cannot preside;
- may speak and otherwise take part; but
- cannot vote on that resolution or on any other matter during those proceedings.

This differs from the Lok Sabha Speaker's Article 96(2) position: during the Speaker's own removal proceeding, the Speaker may vote in the first instance but has no casting vote in the event of equality.

### 3. Anti-defection function

Paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule makes the Chairman the deciding authority for Rajya Sabha members' disqualification.

*Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* treats the presiding officer as a tribunal subject to judicial review for mala fides, perversity, jurisdictional defects or violation of natural justice. Finality in the Schedule therefore does not mean immunity from constitutional review.

This role generates a structural objection: a political office-holder presides over and adjudicates disputes capable of altering party strength.

**Reply:** The Constitution places the decision within the House, and review supplies an external check. The residual problem is delay and perceived partisanship, which is why proposals for an independent tribunal recur.

### 4. Chairman versus Lok Sabha Speaker

| Dimension | Rajya Sabha Chairman | Lok Sabha Speaker |
|---|---|---|
| Office-holder | Vice-President | Elected LS member |
| Member of House | No | Yes |
| First-instance vote | No, under Article 100(1) | No ordinarily under Article 100(1); Article 96(2) permits it during the Speaker's own removal proceeding |
| Casting vote | Yes ordinarily | Yes ordinarily, but not during the Speaker's own removal proceeding |
| Money Bill certification | No | Yes, Article 110 |
| Joint sitting | Never presides | Speaker presides; if absent, Rule 6 succession is Deputy Speaker → Deputy Chairman → a member chosen by those present |
| Tenth Schedule | Decides for RS members | Decides for LS members |

The comparison is functional, not a ranking. The Chairman's neutrality matters especially because the Rajya Sabha represents States and revises legislation.

### 5. Acting as President

Under Article 65, the Vice-President:

- acts as President when the presidential office is vacant; and
- discharges presidential functions during temporary inability.

During this period:

- presidential powers, immunities and emoluments apply;
- the Vice-President does not perform Rajya Sabha Chairmanship duties; and
- the Deputy Chairman performs those duties.

Unlike the US Vice-President, the Indian Vice-President does not become President for the unexpired term. A new President is elected, and the acting arrangement ends.

### 4. Indian and US Vice-Presidents

The US Vice-President succeeds to the presidency for the remainder of the term when the presidency becomes vacant. The Indian Vice-President only acts until a newly elected President assumes office. This difference follows the separately elected fixed executive in the United States and the fresh-election continuity mechanism in India.

The Indian Vice-President is ex officio Chair of the Rajya Sabha; the US Vice-President is President of the Senate. Similar titles should not conceal differences in party system, succession and constitutional structure.

### 5. Integrated objections and replies

**Objection 1: The President is a rubber stamp.**
**Reply:** Advice is binding because ministers must be accountable, but reconsideration, information rights and bounded transition judgment preserve a constitutional role.

**Objection 2: The Vice-President is superfluous.**
**Reply:** The Rajya Sabha Chairmanship is continuous, and the acting-President function protects continuity.

**Objection 3: Indirect election lacks legitimacy.**
**Reply:** It supplies representative and federal legitimacy appropriate to a nominal head without creating a rival mass mandate.

**Objection 4: Veto and ordinance powers permit executive domination.**
**Reply:** Bill-specific limits, mandatory re-passage consequences, temporary ordinance life, legislative laying and judicial review constrain them.

**Objection 5: Clemency is arbitrary mercy.**
**Reply:** Ministerial advice and limited review constitutionalise the discretion.

### Concept check

**Question:** What single principle explains both India's indirect presidential election and the binding-advice rule?

**Model answer:** Both prevent the constitutional head from acquiring an independent political mandate against the Lok Sabha-responsible ministry. The President receives representative and federal legitimacy while political command remains with the accountable Council of Ministers.

### Original Mains practice - 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains prompt:** Evaluate whether the constitutional design of the President and Vice-President successfully combines democratic responsibility, federal participation and institutional continuity.

**Model response (240 words):**

India's two highest constitutional offices are designed around parliamentary responsibility rather than independent executive mandates.

The President receives representative legitimacy through an indirect electoral college of elected MPs and specified elected MLAs. Weighted votes introduce federal participation and Union-State parity, while indirect election avoids a rival popular mandate against the Lok Sabha-supported Council. Article 74 then binds ordinary presidential action to ministerial advice, preserving democratic responsibility. Yet continuity and limited reserve judgment survive through information rights, reconsideration and neutral handling of hung-House transitions.

The Vice-President follows a different logic. Election by elected and nominated MPs, without State legislators, reflects the office's regular function as Rajya Sabha Chairman. Articles 64, 92 and 100 provide procedural authority, a casting vote and self-removal safeguards. The temporary acting-President role under Article 65 prevents interruption without granting succession to the unexpired term.

Accountability is also differentiated. Presidential impeachment requires constitutional violation, investigation and two-thirds of total membership in both Houses. Vice-Presidential removal begins only in the Rajya Sabha, uses an effective majority and needs Lok Sabha agreement without a specified ground. Separate vacancy rules maintain continuity.

Weaknesses remain: indirect election is complex; advice may make the presidency appear passive; presiding-officer neutrality is contested; and open-ended assent or clemency processes may generate delay. Nevertheless, judicial review, floor-test conventions and parliamentary responsibility supply correctives.

The design broadly succeeds because it assigns legitimacy without rival command, federal participation without presidential government, and continuity without hereditary or automatic succession.

**Why this earns marks:** It synthesises election, advice, removal, Chairmanship, continuity and criticisms around the three demands in the question.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** democratic-responsibility analysis 5; federal-election design 4; Vice-Presidential architecture 4; accountability and continuity 3; criticisms/correctives 2; graded conclusion 2.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 - Election, eligibility and removal

**Question:** Explain why nominated MPs are excluded from the presidential election but included in impeachment and the Vice-Presidential election.

**Model answer:** Article 54 constructs a federal electoral college specifically from elected representatives, so nominated MPs are excluded. Article 61 speaks of members of Parliament for impeachment, so nominated members participate. Article 66 creates the Vice-Presidential college from members of both Houses, expressly including elected and nominated MPs.

**Misconception to avoid:** One status rule does not apply uniformly across three constitutionally distinct processes.

## Check 2 - Advice, veto and ordinance

**Question:** Compare the President's one-time return of ministerial advice, return of an ordinary Bill and promulgation of an ordinance.

**Model answer:** Advice may be returned once under Article 74 but binds after reconsideration. An ordinary Bill may be returned under Article 111 and must receive assent if re-passed. An ordinance under Article 123 is temporary legislation possible when either House is not in session, subject to urgency, laying and six-week expiry.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reconsideration of advice, suspensive veto and ordinance power are not versions of one general discretion.

## Check 3 - Clemency and federal boundaries

**Question:** A death sentence concerns an offence within State executive competence. How should Articles 72 and 161 be compared?

**Model answer:** Article 72 expressly empowers the President to pardon and otherwise grant clemency in every death-sentence and court-martial case. Article 161 has no universal punishment-based limb, but the Governor may pardon or otherwise grant clemency where the offence lies within State executive competence. *Raj Kumar @ Bittu* confirms that Article 161 is constitutional and advice-bound.

**Misconception to avoid:** State-field limitation is not a prohibition on pardon; it is a jurisdictional boundary tied to the offence.

## Check 4 - Integrated institutional purpose

**Question:** A hung Lok Sabha, a vacant presidency and a tied Rajya Sabha vote occur close together. Identify the relevant constitutional response to each.

**Model answer:** The President uses bounded judgment to appoint a Prime Minister who must promptly prove confidence. During a presidential vacancy, the Vice-President acts until a new President is elected within six months. In a tied Rajya Sabha vote, the Chairman exercises the Article 100 casting vote, unless acting as President, when the Deputy Chairman performs Chairmanship duties.

**Misconception to avoid:** Institutional emergencies do not merge the offices or remove the requirement of a floor test and fresh presidential election.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question - 150 words

**Question:** Why is the Vice-Presidency better understood as a presiding office than as a succession office?

**Directive decoding:** “Why” requires reasons and institutional significance, not a list of provisions.

**Model answer (147 words):**

The Vice-President's regular constitutional work arises from Article 64, which makes the office-holder ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The Chairman presides, maintains order, interprets rules, decides admissibility and points of order and exercises a casting vote under Article 100. Paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule also makes the Chairman the reviewable tribunal for Rajya Sabha defection disputes. These functions shape federal deliberation every time the House meets.

The succession role is exceptional and temporary. Under Article 65, the Vice-President acts as President during a vacancy or discharges functions during temporary inability. Unlike the US Vice-President, the Indian Vice-President does not inherit the unexpired presidential term; a new President must be elected. During the acting presidency, the Vice-President ceases to perform Chairmanship duties.

Thus constitutional frequency and institutional impact make the Vice-Presidency principally a presiding office, with acting succession as an essential but contingent continuity mechanism.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses frequency, powers and comparative succession to answer the “why” demand.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Article 64 basis 2; Chair powers 3; Tenth Schedule/casting vote 2; acting-role distinction 2; conclusion 1.

## Final 15-mark question - 250 words

**Question:** Critically examine the proposition that the President of India is a “rubber stamp”.

**Directive decoding:** “Critically examine” requires the apparent truth, counter-evidence, limits of the counter and a graded verdict.

**Model answer (222 words):**

The “rubber stamp” description reflects an important constitutional truth but overstates it. Article 74, made expressly binding by the 42nd Amendment, requires the President to act on ministerial advice. The 44th Amendment permits one reconsideration, after which the advice binds. Ministers, not the President, are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. In ordinary majority government, policy, appointments, legislation and dissolution therefore follow elected-government advice.

However, the office is not empty. The President may seek information under the Article 78 relationship, warn and counsel ministers and return advice once. In a hung Lok Sabha, the President must identify the claimant most likely to prove confidence. A dissolution request from a ministry that has lost confidence may require examination of alternatives. K.R. Narayanan's reconsideration of proposed President's Rule illustrates the practical value of the pause.

Further, presidential form ensures continuity: executive action, appointments, legislation, clemency and emergencies pass through constitutionally structured procedures. Courts may review the resulting action even though Article 74(2) protects the advice itself.

The counter-case must also be qualified. Situational discretion is not a licence for personal policy, and a prompt floor test must settle political majority. Excessive activism would create an unaccountable rival executive.

The President is therefore advice-bound by democratic design, not a mere stamp: restrained in normal politics, consultative in government and consequential at constitutional margins.

**Why this earns marks:** It concedes the binding-advice core, supplies bounded counterexamples and prevents the answer from turning presidential activism into a virtue.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** binding-advice case 4; constitutional functions 2; discretion examples 4; limits/floor test 3; qualified verdict 2.

## Final 20-mark question - 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate the constitutional checks on the President's legislative and judicial powers, with particular reference to veto, ordinance and clemency.

**Directive decoding:** “Evaluate” requires mechanisms, effectiveness, weaknesses and a reasoned overall judgment.

**Model answer (214 words):**

The President's legislative and clemency powers are substantial in form but distributed across ministerial, parliamentary and judicial controls.

Under Article 111, an ordinary Bill may be assented to, withheld or returned once; re-passage compels assent. Money Bills cannot be returned, while Article 368 makes assent to an amendment obligatory. Under Article 201, a State Legislature gets six months to reconsider a returned non-Money Bill, but re-passage does not compel assent. The non-binding Article 143 opinion of 20 November 2025 rejected court-created presidential timelines, pre-enactment merits review and deemed assent.

Article 123 permits an ordinance when either House is not in session and urgent action is necessary. Legislative competence and Fundamental Rights limit it; it cannot amend the Constitution. It must be laid before both Houses and ordinarily expires six weeks after the later reassembly. *D.C. Wadhwa* condemns re-promulgation; *Krishna Kumar Singh* makes laying mandatory and rejects automatic enduring effects.

Article 72 covers Union-field offences, court-martial cases and all death sentences. *Maru Ram* requires advice; *Kehar Singh* permits fresh consideration; *Epuru Sudhakar* allows limited legality review.

These checks generally preserve parliamentary responsibility and legality. Their weakness lies less in absent doctrine than in delay before Parliament or courts respond. The powers are constitutional safety valves only when temporary discretion returns promptly to accountable institutions.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates three powers through text, procedure, cases, strengths and implementation gaps.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** veto architecture 5; ordinance architecture and cases 6; clemency scope and cases 5; implementation critique 2; integrated verdict 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional location and central logic

- Articles 52-62: President; Articles 63-71: Vice-President; Article 72: clemency; Article 73: Union executive extent.
- Article 53 formally vests Union executive power in the President.
- Articles 74-75 make the PM-led Council the real, Lok Sabha-responsible executive.
- President = constitutional head and continuity; PM = political command; VP = Rajya Sabha Chair plus temporary presidential substitute.
- Droupadi Murmu was sworn in as the 15th President on 25 July 2022; official-profile status checked 30 September 2026.

## Presidential election and electoral law

- College: elected MPs of both Houses + elected State MLAs + elected Delhi and Puducherry MLAs.
- Excluded: nominated MPs, nominated MLAs, MLCs and non-included UT legislators.
- MLA value: population ÷ elected MLAs ÷ 1000, with prescribed rounding.
- MP value: aggregate MLA vote value ÷ elected MPs of both Houses.
- 1971 census population remains operative until relevant first post-2026 census figures are published.
- PR-STV, secret preferential ballot and quota.
- Articles 54-55 design; Article 71 dispute jurisdiction; Article 71(4) bars a challenge based merely on an electoral-college vacancy.
- Nomination: 50 proposers + 50 seconders + ₹15,000 deposit.
- Vacancies in the college do not invalidate or postpone the election.

## Entry, oath, office conditions and immunity

- Citizen, 35, qualified for Lok Sabha, no disqualifying office of profit.
- Oath by CJI or senior-most available SC judge.
- Oath: faithfully execute; preserve, protect and defend Constitution and law; serve people's well-being.
- Cannot remain legislator or hold another office of profit.
- Emoluments protected from diminution.
- Article 361: official-act answerability protection; no criminal proceeding or arrest during term; two months' notice for personal civil proceedings.
- Executive action remains reviewable; ministers remain responsible.

## Term, impeachment and continuity

- Article 56: five-year term and resignation to the Vice-President.
- Article 57: eligibility for re-election without a constitutional term limit.
- Outgoing President continues until successor enters office.
- Impeachment only for “violation of the Constitution”.
- Either House initiates; one-fourth signatures; 14 days' notice.
- Two-thirds of total membership in both Houses.
- Second House investigates; President may appear and be represented.
- Nominated MPs participate; State MLAs do not.
- No President has been impeached.
- Casual vacancy: VP acts; election within six months; successor gets fresh five-year term.
- Temporary inability: VP discharges functions.
- Article 70 and the President (Discharge of Functions) Act, 1969: if the VP cannot act, CJI, then senior-most available SC judge.
- Article 71(2) preserves acts done before election declared void.

## Seven heads of power

- **Executive:** appointments; action and authentication in the President's name; Articles 239-240 UT administration/regulations; Articles 263, 339, 340 and 344 councils/commissions; Fifth/Sixth Schedule functions; effective choice controlled by advice, convention, collegium, statute or prescribed procedure.
- **Legislative:** part of Parliament; Articles 86-87 addresses/messages, Article 80(3) nominations, sessions, dissolution, joint sitting, temporary presiding appointments under Articles 91/95, Article 103 MP-disqualification decisions on the ECI's opinion, prior recommendation under Articles 3/117/274, assent and ordinance.
- **Financial:** Annual Financial Statement, recommendation for Money Bills/demands, Contingency Fund, Finance Commission.
- **Judicial:** judge appointments, Article 143 reference and Article 72; the 20 November 2025 advisory opinion is non-binding.
- **Diplomatic:** formal international representation and treaty action.
- **Military:** Supreme Commander; formal appointments and formal war/peace action, subject to elected-government and parliamentary control.
- **Emergency:** Articles 352, 356/365 and 360, each with specialised controls.

## Aid, advice and situational judgment

- 42nd Amendment: advice expressly binding.
- 44th Amendment: return once; reconsidered advice binding.
- Article 74(2): advice contents not judicially inquired into; resulting action may be reviewed.
- Article 78: information, communication and Council consideration.
- Situational judgment: hung House, sudden PM vacancy, lost majority, dissolution request, refusal to resign.
- Controlling test: objective support and prompt floor test.
- K.R. Narayanan's 1997/1998 reconsideration illustrates the warning function.

## Veto and assent

- Ordinary Bill: assent / withhold / return once; re-passage compels assent.
- Money Bill: no return.
- Constitution Amendment Bill: mandatory assent under Article 368 after 24th Amendment.
- Absolute = withhold; suspensive = return; pocket = no decision where no deadline.
- No Indian qualified veto.
- Article 201: reserved State Bill may be assented to, withheld or returned through Governor; State re-passage does not compel assent.
- The Article 201 proviso requires State reconsideration within six months; the President still has no fixed decision deadline.
- Special Reference No. 1 of 2025 rejected court-created timelines, pre-enactment merits review and deemed assent; as an Article 143 opinion it is advisory and non-binding.
- Bill awaiting assent or already returned does not lapse merely on LS dissolution.

## Ordinance

- Article 123 available when either House or both Houses are not in session; unavailable when both Houses sit.
- Requires circumstances needing immediate action and ministerial advice.
- Same temporary force as Act; may amend Central Act and operate retrospectively.
- Cannot abridge FRs or amend Constitution; must remain within Parliament's competence.
- Mandatory laying before both Houses.
- Ceases independently no later than six weeks after later reassembly, or earlier on withdrawal or disapproval; a separately enacted replacement Act may carry the law forward, but approval never converts the ordinance itself into an Act.
- *R.C. Cooper*: limited review of satisfaction.
- *D.C. Wadhwa*: serial re-promulgation is fraud on Constitution.
- *Krishna Kumar Singh*: laying mandatory; satisfaction reviewable; re-promulgation invalid; enduring rights not automatic.

## Clemency

- Forms: pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, commutation.
- Pardon removes both conviction and sentence and absolves resulting punishment and disqualifications.
- Article 72: court-martial + Union-field offences + all death sentences.
- Article 161: State-field power; Governor may pardon or otherwise grant clemency where the offence lies within State competence; no court-martial.
- *State of Haryana v. Raj Kumar @ Bittu* (2021): Article 161 is independent of statutory remission restrictions and operates on State ministerial advice.
- *Maru Ram*: ministerial advice.
- *Kehar Singh*: fresh merits consideration, not judicial appeal.
- *Epuru Sudhakar*: review for mala fides, arbitrariness, non-application, irrelevant or excluded material.
- US President: personal federal-offence power; no State offences; impeachment exception.
- Pre-emptive pardon: completed conduct before charge/conviction, never future offences.

## Vice-President

- College: elected and nominated MPs of both Houses; no State legislators.
- PR-STV and secret ballot.
- Citizen, 35, qualified for Rajya Sabha, no office of profit.
- Nomination: 20 proposers + 20 seconders + ₹15,000 deposit.
- Oath by President or a person appointed by the President.
- Five years; resignation to President; unlimited re-election.
- Removal only initiated in RS; 14 days; effective majority; LS agreement; no stated ground.
- Casual vacancy filled as soon as possible; fresh five-year term.
- 2025 controlled illustration: elected 9 September; assumed office 12 September.

## Rajya Sabha Chair and acting presidency

- Articles 64 and 89(1): ex officio Chairman; not a member.
- Articles 89(2) and 91: Deputy Chairman and performance of the Chair's duties during vacancy/absence.
- Presides, maintains order, interprets rules, admissibility, points of order and proceedings.
- Article 100: no first vote; casting vote on equality.
- Article 92: cannot preside during own removal proceedings; may speak, but cannot vote on the resolution or any other matter during those proceedings.
- Article 96(2) contrast: during the Speaker's own removal proceeding, the Speaker may vote in the first instance but has no casting vote.
- Joint-sitting Rule 6 sequence: Speaker → Deputy Speaker → Deputy Chairman → member chosen by those present; the Chairman never presides.
- Tenth Schedule para 6: decides RS defection; *Kihoto Hollohan* review.
- Does not certify Money Bills.
- Article 65: acts during presidential vacancy; discharges functions during inability.
- While acting as President, does not chair RS; Deputy Chairman performs duties.
- Indian VP does not inherit the unexpired presidential term.

## Qualified conclusions

- The President is not a rival executive but a constitutional head whose limited judgment becomes important where ordinary political responsibility is uncertain.

## Reconciled source shorthand

- `upsc-ai-kit\knowledge\Polity\basic\President-and-Vice-President.md` and `upsc-ai-kit\knowledge\Polity\advanced\15_President-and-Vice-President.md` use the shorthand that the Governor cannot pardon a death sentence.
- The text of Article 161 and the official judgment in *State of Haryana v. Raj Kumar @ Bittu* require the more precise formulation used in Lesson 9: the Governor may pardon or otherwise grant clemency where the offence lies within State executive competence. Article 72 is wider because it expressly covers every death sentence regardless of subject and every court-martial sentence.
- `upsc-ai-kit\knowledge\Polity\basic\President-and-Vice-President.md` later uses the shorthand that Article 123 requires both Houses not to be in session. The constitutional text controls: an ordinance may issue whenever either House or both Houses are not in session, and is void only if promulgated while both Houses are sitting.
