# Amendment and Basic Structure - Live Learning Session

> **Learner-first lock:** This session begins with the practical problem of constitutional change. It first teaches why a Constitution must be amendable, then separates the three amendment routes, follows the exact Article 368 procedure, reconstructs the Court-Parliament chronology, and finally tests the procedural and substantive limits on constituent power. The doctrine is taught as a permission-and-limit system: Parliament may change the Constitution extensively, but it may not destroy the constitutional identity that makes the document the Constitution of India.

*India combines different degrees of entrenchment rather than choosing one amendment threshold for the entire Constitution.*

### 1. The learner's problem

A Constitution must perform two opposite tasks:

1. provide stable rules that governments cannot casually change; and
2. remain capable of responding to new institutions, social demands and practical defects.

If it is changed like an ordinary statute, the temporary majority may rewrite the rules that control it. If change is practically impossible, the Constitution can become unresponsive. India's solution is a **graded amendment system**.

### 2. Article 368 and the nature of the power

✅ **Fact:** Article 368 is in **Part XX**. Article 368(1) states that Parliament may, in exercise of its **constituent power**, amend any constitutional provision by **addition, variation or repeal**, subject to the procedure in that Article.

These three words cover the possible textual operations:

| Operation | Meaning | Simple illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | Insert new constitutional text | Add a new Article or clause |
| Variation | Alter existing text | Change an institutional rule |
| Repeal | Remove existing text | Omit a provision |

The word **constituent** matters. Parliament is ordinarily a legislature that makes statutes. When it acts under Article 368, the same institution exercises a higher text-changing power created by the Constitution.

```text
Same institution: Parliament
             |
       different legal capacities
             |
   +---------+------------------+
   |                            |
ordinary legislative power      constituent power
makes statutes                  changes constitutional text
under Arts 245-246              under Art 368
```

The distinction concerns the **source and legal effect of the power**, not the personal identity of the members exercising it.

### 3. Constituent power is wide, not original or unlimited

Parliament's power is **constituted power**: Article 368 grants and regulates it. Parliament did not create the Constitution and does not possess the unlimited original authority of the Constituent Assembly.

That produces the central logic of the topic:

```text
The Constitution grants the power
              |
              v
The power must follow constitutional procedure
              |
              v
The grantee cannot destroy the identity of the grant
```

⚠️ **Inference:** Constituent power is higher than ordinary legislative power because it can change the constitutional text, but it remains subordinate to the Constitution's controlling identity.

### 4. Why India is called a synthesis

India is often described as neither as flexible as the classic British model nor as rigid as the United States model.

| Model | Dominant logic | Indian qualification |
|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | Constitutional change may occur through ordinary legislation | India entrenches most provisions |
| United States | Formal amendment is exceptionally demanding and requires extensive State consent | India reserves State consent for a specified federal core |
| India | Different provisions receive different levels of entrenchment | Flexibility and rigidity coexist |

The comparison is useful only to explain the Indian mechanism. A full country-by-country comparison belongs elsewhere.

### 5. Named assessments of the Indian design

The standard constitutional assessments explain why diversity of procedure is not the same as incoherence:

- **K.C. Wheare** said the Indian method "strikes a good balance between flexibility and rigidity" and praised the variety of its amendment procedures as wise though rarely found.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru** defended flexibility because a Constitution governing a "living, vital, organic people" cannot be sealed against national growth.
- **B.R. Ambedkar** contrasted India's design with exceptionally rigid systems and described it as a **facile procedure** for amendment.
- **Granville Austin** called the amending process one of the Constitution's most ably conceived aspects: apparently complicated, but in substance diverse.

These assessments do not deny the procedure's defects. They identify its design objective: different constitutional subjects receive different degrees of entrenchment.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** If Parliament can amend any provision, the Constitution offers no permanent protection.

**Reply:** Article 368 supplies procedural entrenchment, and *Kesavananda Bharati* supplies a substantive ceiling: amendment may alter constitutional arrangements but may not damage the basic structure.

**Residual:** The phrase "basic structure" is not enumerated in the constitutional text. Protection therefore depends on judicial reasoning and case-by-case identification, which creates both adaptability and controversy.

### UPSC application

Use the formula:

> **Article 368 creates a graded, constituent power of change; the basic-structure doctrine prevents that power from becoming a power of constitutional destruction.**

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the same Parliament exercise legislative power in one case and constituent power in another without becoming a sovereign body above the Constitution?

**Model answer:** The legal character of Parliament's act depends on the constitutional source used. Under Articles 245-246, Parliament makes ordinary laws within distributed legislative competence. Under Article 368, it exercises constituent power to add, vary or repeal constitutional text. Both powers are conferred powers: ordinary legislation is limited by competence and Fundamental Rights, while constitutional amendment is limited by Article 368 procedure and the basic structure. Parliament therefore exercises a higher text-changing capacity without becoming legally sovereign above the Constitution that grants it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating constituent power as unlimited original power. Parliament exercises a power granted by the Constitution; it is not the Constituent Assembly recreated.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Explain why the Indian Constitution is described as a synthesis of flexibility and rigidity. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

The Indian Constitution avoids both unrestricted ordinary-law change and near-immutability. It adopts graded entrenchment according to the importance of the provision.

Certain constitution-affecting changes, including State reorganisation and citizenship regulation, use a simple parliamentary majority outside Article 368. Most provisions require the Article 368 special majority: a majority of the total membership of each House and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. Changes to the specified federal core additionally require ratification by not less than half of the State legislatures.

The design therefore combines adaptability with institutional consent. *Kesavananda Bharati* adds a substantive safeguard: even a procedurally valid amendment cannot damage the Constitution's basic structure.

Thus India is flexible in permitting different routes of change, rigid in entrenching important provisions, and constitutionally limited in protecting its essential identity.

*Every mandatory stage is cumulative; success at one stage cannot compensate for failure at another.*

### 1. Initiation

✅ **Fact:** An amendment under Article 368 may be initiated **only by introducing a Bill in either House of Parliament**.

- It may begin in the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha.
- It may be introduced by a minister or a private member.
- It does not require prior Presidential recommendation.
- A State legislature cannot introduce an Article 368 amendment Bill.

The State role begins only if the Bill changes a provision listed in the proviso to Article 368(2).

### 2. Passage in each House

Each House must pass the Bill separately by both of the following:

1. a majority of the **total membership** of that House; and
2. a majority of **not less than two-thirds of members present and voting**.

The two limbs answer different questions:

```text
Limb 1: Is support greater than half of the House's full constitutional strength?
Limb 2: Is support at least two-thirds of those who actually cast a vote?
```

Vacancies and absences do not lower the total-membership denominator. Abstentions are not counted among members present and voting, but they do not reduce the total membership.

### 3. No joint sitting

✅ **Fact:** Article 368 contains no mechanism comparable to Article 108 for a joint sitting. If one House rejects the Bill, fails to pass it with the required majority or insists on a different text, the other House cannot override that failure through a combined vote.

This protects bicameral consent: the Rajya Sabha is not reduced to a suspensive chamber for constitutional amendment.

### 4. The State-ratification stage

If the Bill changes a matter in the Article 368 proviso, it must be ratified by resolutions of the legislatures of **not less than one-half of the States** before presentation to the President.

The ratification stage:

- is not required for every constitutional amendment;
- follows parliamentary passage;
- uses a simple majority in the State legislature;
- has no constitutional deadline;
- cannot be forced upon a State;
- does not permit a State to initiate the amendment.

The text is silent on withdrawal of a State's ratification after it has been given. Silence must be preserved rather than converted into an invented rule.

### 5. Presidential assent

✅ **Fact:** After the 24th Amendment, the President **shall give assent** to a duly passed amendment Bill. The President cannot withhold assent or return the Bill for reconsideration.

This differs from the President's options regarding an ordinary non-Money Bill under Article 111.

### 6. Exactly one current linkage - the 131st Amendment Bill division

✅ **Fact:** The Constitution (One Hundred and Thirty-First Amendment) Bill, 2026 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on **16 April 2026**. On **17 April 2026**, the official division recorded **298 ayes and 230 noes**.

```text
Present and voting = 298 + 230 = 528
Two-thirds of 528 = 352
Support obtained = 298
Result: two-thirds limb not satisfied
```

The official record states that the motion did not pass according to Article 368 and was rejected.

What the example proves:

- an ordinary governing majority is not necessarily an amending majority;
- both majority limbs must be satisfied simultaneously;
- failure in the first House ends that Bill's progress;
- no joint sitting can repair the failure.

What it does **not** prove:

- the proposed constitutional text never became law;
- the defeat is not a judicial precedent;
- the State-ratification stage never arose;
- no inference should be made about whether that Bill would have required State ratification.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Mandatory assent turns the President into a mere formality.

**Reply:** The entrenchment checks occur before assent: two independently consenting Houses, two simultaneous majority limbs and, where required, State ratification. A discretionary Presidential veto would add an unelected political checkpoint not specified by Article 368.

**Residual:** The procedure does not eliminate political dominance where a coalition commands the necessary numbers. That is why the substantive basic-structure ceiling remains important.

2. Either House may be the originating House.
3. A minister or private member may introduce it.
4. Prior Presidential recommendation is unnecessary.
5. Each House must pass the same amendment separately.
6. The total-membership limb and present-and-voting limb are cumulative.
7. Vacancies do not reduce total membership.

### Concept check

**Question:** In a House with total membership 543, 528 members vote on an amendment and 298 support it. Which Article 368 limb is satisfied, which fails, and why can a joint sitting not save the Bill?

**Model answer:** Support of 298 exceeds half of the total membership of 543, so the total-membership limb is satisfied. However, two-thirds of 528 members present and voting is 352, while only 298 voted in favour; the second limb therefore fails. Article 368 requires both limbs in each House separately. It contains no joint-sitting mechanism, so votes from the other House cannot be combined with the first House to cure the deficiency.

**Misconception to avoid:** Comparing 298 only with half of 528. The first denominator is total membership; the second is members present and voting.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Examine the procedural safeguards governing the passage of a Constitution Amendment Bill under Article 368. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 368 entrusts amendment to ordinary legislative institutions but subjects them to constituent safeguards.

An amendment Bill may originate only in either House of Parliament. A minister or private member may introduce it, and prior Presidential recommendation is unnecessary. Each House must pass the Bill separately by a majority of its total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. Vacancies do not lower the first denominator, while abstentions are excluded from the second. There is no joint sitting if the Houses disagree.

Where the Bill affects the federal proviso, resolutions of not less than half the State legislatures are additionally required before presentation to the President. States cannot initiate the Bill; Article 368 prescribes no ratification deadline and is silent on withdrawal. After compliance, the President shall assent and cannot return or withhold the Bill.

These safeguards secure bicameral, super-majoritarian and, for the federal core, territorial consent. Their limitation is that sufficiently large parliamentary numbers can still satisfy them. Judicial review of the basic structure therefore supplies a separate substantive safeguard.

```text
Does the Constitution itself authorise Parliament to change the matter by law?
                         |
             +-----------+-----------+
             |                       |
            YES                      NO
             |                       |
simple majority through           Article 368
```

*Not every statute that changes a constitutional arrangement is an amendment made under Article 368.*

### 1. The key distinction

✅ **Fact:** Some constitutional provisions expressly permit Parliament to make changes by ordinary law. Those laws may alter schedules, boundaries, institutional details or constitutional arrangements, yet the Constitution states or implies that they are not amendments for Article 368 purposes.

The majority is the ordinary majority of members present and voting.

### 2. Territorial and citizenship changes

| Matter | Constitutional anchor | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Admission or establishment of new States | Articles 2 and 4 | Parliament may admit or establish new States; consequential First/Fourth Schedule changes are outside Art. 368 |
| Formation, area, boundary or name of States | Articles 3 and 4 | Parliament may reorganise territory by law after the prescribed reference process |
| Citizenship acquisition and termination | Article 11 | Parliament regulates citizenship by law |

The fact that State boundaries are constitutionally important does not itself determine the amendment route. The Constitution deliberately gives Parliament an ordinary-law power under Articles 2-4.

### 3. Legislative institutions and working rules

| Matter | Anchor | Examination caution |
|---|---|---|
| Creation or abolition of a State Legislative Council | Article 169 | State Assembly initiates a resolution request; Parliament decides by law |
| Quorum | Articles 100(3) and 189(3) | Parliament or the relevant legislature may otherwise provide |
| Salaries and allowances of legislators | Articles 106 and 195 | Changed by law, not Article 368 |
| Rules of procedure | Articles 118 and 208 | Legislative procedure is internally regulated |
| Privileges | Articles 105(3) and 194(3) | Parliament/State law may define privileges |
| Language in legislatures | Articles 120 and 210 | Statutory change operates within the constitutional authorisation |

Article 169 is the source of a common trap. A State Assembly may pass a special resolution requesting creation or abolition of its Legislative Council, but this is **not State initiation of an Article 368 amendment**. Parliament may accept, reject or take no action on the request.

### 4. Courts, language, elections and Union Territories

| Matter | Anchor | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Number of Supreme Court puisne judges | Article 124(1) | Parliament may prescribe a larger number |
| Additional Supreme Court jurisdiction | Article 138 | Parliament may confer further jurisdiction |
| Official language provision by law | Article 343(3) and connected provisions | Parliament may continue or regulate specified language use |
| Elections to Parliament and State legislatures | Articles 327-328 | Legislative regulation of elections |
| Delimitation/readjustment | Articles 82 and 170 | Parliament supplies the statutory machinery following the constitutional trigger |
| Union Territory institutional arrangements | Article 239A and other express Part VIII law-making clauses | Specified laws may alter arrangements without Art. 368 treatment |

### 5. Schedules and related matters

The standard simple-majority inventory also includes:

- changes authorised in relation to the **Second Schedule**, including specified emoluments and allowances;
- the **Fifth Schedule**, especially its express amendment mechanism;
- the **Sixth Schedule**, especially its express amendment mechanism;
- parliamentary salaries, procedure and privileges;
- use of English in Parliament;
- elections and delimitation.

The list must be learned as an **authorisation taxonomy**, not as a random list. In each case, a constitutional clause permits change through law.

### 6. Simple majority does not mean legally trivial

State reorganisation can reshape federal politics; citizenship law can affect membership of the polity; delimitation can alter representation. The route is simpler because the Constitution chose adaptability for these fields, not because the consequences are minor.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Calling these changes "outside Article 368" denies that they alter the Constitution.

**Reply:** They may alter constitutional arrangements or schedules, but their legal source is an ordinary-law power expressly recognised by the Constitution. "Constitution-affecting change" and "Article 368 amendment" are not identical categories.

**Residual:** Some entries are expressed through multiple Articles or Schedule paragraphs rather than one neat provision. Answers should name the matter and the best anchor instead of inventing a single exhaustive Article.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is an Article 169 resolution by a State Assembly not an exception to the rule that States cannot initiate an Article 368 amendment?

**Model answer:** Article 169 creates a separate ordinary-law mechanism. A State Assembly may request creation or abolition of its Legislative Council through the constitutionally specified resolution, but the resulting law is made by Parliament and is expressly outside the Article 368 amendment process. The State does not introduce an amendment Bill, cannot compel Parliament to legislate and does not exercise constituent power under Article 368.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every State resolution that precedes constitutional change as State initiation of an amendment.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish constitutional change by simple majority from amendment under Article 368. Illustrate with constitutional provisions. (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Constitutional change is broader than an Article 368 amendment. Where the Constitution expressly authorises Parliament to act by ordinary law, the resulting change is made by a simple majority and is not deemed an amendment for Article 368 purposes.

Articles 2-4 permit admission, creation and territorial reorganisation of States, including consequential First and Fourth Schedule changes. Article 11 authorises citizenship law, while Article 169 permits Parliament to create or abolish a State Legislative Council after the prescribed Assembly resolution. Other examples include quorum, legislative salaries and procedure, the number and additional jurisdiction of Supreme Court judges, election regulation, delimitation, specified Union Territory arrangements, and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.

By contrast, most constitutional provisions require the Article 368 special majority, with State ratification for the specified federal core.

The distinction rests on the Constitution's chosen legal route, not merely on the political importance of the change.

## Lesson 4 - The special majority and its arithmetic

### Visual: two gates in each House

```text
HOUSE SUPPORT
     |
     +--> Gate A: votes for > half of TOTAL MEMBERSHIP
     |
     +--> Gate B: votes for >= 2/3 of PRESENT AND VOTING
     |
     v
Both open in Lok Sabha AND both open in Rajya Sabha
     |
     v
Parliamentary passage complete
```

*The phrase "special majority" is shorthand for two independent numerical tests applied separately in both Houses.*

### 1. Total membership

**Total membership** means the full membership constituting the House, irrespective of vacancies or absentees. The first limb therefore prevents a small attendance from amending the Constitution.

Example:

```text
Total membership: 245
Members present: 180
Members voting: 174

First limb requires more than half of 245 -> at least 123 votes for
Second limb requires at least two-thirds of 174 -> at least 116 votes for
Effective minimum in this example -> 123 votes for
```

The higher requirement in the actual division controls.

### 2. Present and voting

The second denominator excludes members who are absent and members who are present but abstain. It tests the intensity of support among those casting a vote.

| Member status | Total-membership denominator | Present-and-voting denominator |
|---|---:|---:|
| Vacancy | Included in full House strength as constitutionally constituted | No |
| Absent member | Included | No |
| Present but abstaining | Included | No |
| Voting aye or no | Included | Yes |

### 3. Why both limbs exist

The first limb prevents low-attendance alteration. The second prevents a narrow majority among a heavily attended House from changing constitutional text.

```text
Gate A protects institutional breadth.
Gate B protects super-majoritarian depth.
```

### 4. Application at legislative stages

The constitutional text speaks of the Bill being passed by the required majority. Standard parliamentary treatment requires the special majority at the effective voting stages; the textbook formulation notes that the strict constitutional requirement attaches to final passage, while House rules apply the safeguard at effective stages out of caution.

For UPSC purposes, the safe rule is:

> An amendment Bill must secure the Article 368 special majority in each House; do not suggest that an ordinary majority can carry its operative clauses.

### 5. Special-majority coverage

This route covers:

- Fundamental Rights;
- Directive Principles of State Policy;
- provisions not assigned to an express simple-majority route; and
- provisions not included in the federal ratification proviso.

It is therefore the residual and numerically largest amendment category.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Two-thirds of those voting may still permit amendment when many members abstain.

**Reply:** The total-membership limb prevents abstention from reducing support below an absolute institutional majority. Both safeguards operate together.

**Residual:** A disciplined coalition with exceptional numbers can satisfy both. Arithmetic protects against ordinary majorities, not against every concentrated political majority.

### UPSC application

Never write "two-thirds majority of the House." Specify:

> **Majority of total membership plus not less than two-thirds of members present and voting, in each House separately.**

### Revision notes

1. Special majority contains two simultaneous limbs.
2. Total membership is the full House strength.
3. Vacancies and absences do not lower the first denominator.
4. Present-and-voting excludes abstentions.
5. A member voting "no" remains in the second denominator.
6. Calculate both thresholds separately.
7. The higher operative threshold determines whether the vote succeeds.
8. Both Houses repeat the test independently.
9. No combined parliamentary total is permitted.
10. FR and DPSP generally use this route.
11. The route is residual after removing simple-majority and federal-proviso matters.
12. A governing majority and an amending majority are different.

### Concept check

**Question:** A House has total membership 240. Of 180 members present, 15 abstain and 165 vote. What are the two thresholds, and what minimum number of affirmative votes is needed?

**Model answer:** The total-membership limb requires more than half of 240, so at least 121 affirmative votes. The present-and-voting limb uses 165, because the 15 abstainers are excluded; two-thirds of 165 is 110. Both limbs must be satisfied, so the effective minimum is 121 affirmative votes.

**Misconception to avoid:** Including abstainers in present and voting or using 180 as the second denominator.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Why does Article 368 prescribe both a total-membership majority and a two-thirds present-and-voting majority? (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

Article 368 uses two thresholds because each addresses a different danger in constitutional amendment.

The majority of total membership ensures broad institutional support. Vacancies, absences and abstentions cannot reduce the affirmative requirement below more than half of the House's full strength. A Constitution therefore cannot be amended by mobilising a small sitting.

The requirement of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting tests the depth of support among participating members. It prevents a narrow division in a well-attended House from changing constitutional text.

Both conditions apply cumulatively and separately in the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha. There is no joint sitting to aggregate support.

The design combines breadth, intensity and bicameral consent. It does not make amendment impossible, but ensures that an ordinary political majority cannot automatically become an amending majority.

*State consent protects only the constitutionally specified federal core; it is not a general third chamber for all amendments.*

### 1. Exact federal proviso

State ratification is required if an amendment changes:

1. **Articles 54 and 55** - election of the President and its manner;
2. **Articles 73 and 162** - extent of Union and State executive power;
3. **Article 241** - High Courts for Union Territories;
4. **Article 279A** - Goods and Services Tax Council;
5. **Chapter IV of Part V** - Union judiciary;
6. **Chapter V of Part VI** - High Courts in the States;
7. **Chapter I of Part XI** - legislative relations between Union and States;
8. **any List in the Seventh Schedule**;
9. **representation of States in Parliament**; and
10. **Article 368 itself**.

The shorthand "Supreme Court and High Courts" is incomplete because Article 241 is separately named. "Centre-State relations" is too broad because only the specified Chapter, executive-power Articles, Lists and representation limb are entrenched by the proviso.

### 2. What ratification requires

✅ **Fact:** Ratification must occur:

- by resolutions of State legislatures;
- in not less than one-half of the States;
- after Parliament has passed the Bill;
- before presentation to the President.

The accepted parliamentary formulation is a simple majority in the State legislature. The Constitution prescribes no special State majority.

### 3. What States cannot do

States cannot:

- introduce an Article 368 amendment Bill;
- demand that Parliament take up a proposed amendment;
- amend the parliamentary Bill during ratification;
- compel other States to ratify;
- replace the requirement of parliamentary passage.

The Constitution contains no deadline for State action. It is also silent on whether a State may withdraw a ratification already given. An answer should identify the silence, not manufacture a settlement.

### 4. Why only half the States?

The Indian design gives States a blocking role over the federal core but does not require the three-fourths threshold used in the United States.

⚠️ **Inference:** This is asymmetric amendment federalism. Parliament dominates amendment of most of the Constitution; States become indispensable only when the enumerated federal bargain is altered.

### 5. Procedural review in *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah*

✅ **Fact:** The 97th Amendment introduced Part IXB concerning co-operative societies and made connected changes. In *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (2021), the Supreme Court held that the Part IXB provisions dealing with co-operative societies operating within States affected an area of State legislative competence and had not received the constitutionally required State ratification.

The Court invalidated Part IXB to that extent. It remained operative for multi-State co-operative societies; the changes to Article 19(1)(c) and Article 43B were not struck down.

The case teaches a distinct point:

```text
Basic-structure review asks: what does the amendment damage?
Procedural review asks: was the constitutionally required route followed?
```

An amendment can therefore fail without a court first finding destruction of a basic feature.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Requiring only half the States insufficiently protects federalism.

**Reply:** The Constitution combines three safeguards: Parliament's special majority, consent of both Houses and ratification by at least half the States. The Supreme Court can additionally enforce the procedure and the basic structure.

**Residual:** States still cannot initiate amendments, and unequal representation in the Rajya Sabha means State influence is not equivalent to the United States model.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did *Rajendra N. Shah* present a procedural problem rather than merely a disagreement about the merits of co-operative-society reform?

**Model answer:** The defect concerned the route used to change the Constitution. Part IXB altered the constitutional position of co-operative societies within the State field, yet the 97th Amendment had not received ratification by not less than half of the State legislatures. The Court therefore enforced the federal consent requirement under Article 368. The issue was not whether co-operative reform was wise but whether Parliament had complied with the mandatory constituent procedure.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every invalid constitutional amendment as a basic-structure ruling. Procedural non-compliance is an independent ground.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Analyse the federal character of the State-ratification requirement under Article 368. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 368 protects a specified federal core by requiring consent beyond Parliament. After both Houses pass the amendment by special majority, changes to Articles 54-55, Articles 73 and 162, Article 241, Article 279A, the specified Union and State judiciary Chapters, Chapter I of Part XI, any Seventh Schedule List, State representation in Parliament or Article 368 itself must be ratified by not less than half the State legislatures.

The design is federal because neither the Union legislature nor the States can unilaterally alter these arrangements. Yet it is asymmetric: States cannot initiate Article 368 amendments, their consent is unnecessary for most provisions, and only half need ratify. There is no prescribed ratification deadline, while withdrawal is textually unresolved.

*Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* demonstrates that State consent is legally enforceable, not ceremonial. Part IXB was invalidated insofar as it affected State co-operative societies without the required ratification.

Thus Article 368 preserves a limited federal veto over the constitutional bargain while retaining Parliament as the principal amendment initiator.

*Kesavananda* upheld this restoration of power but denied that restored power was unlimited.

```text
Operative law:
```

- the Amendment Act has commenced;
- the reservation is not yet operational merely because commencement occurred;

*The early cases turned on the legal category of a constitutional amendment, not on whether Fundamental Rights were politically important.*

### 1. *Shankari Prasad v. Union of India* (1951)

The First Amendment inserted Articles 31A and 31B and created the Ninth Schedule, principally to protect land-reform legislation.

✅ **Holding:** Parliament's Article 368 power extended to Fundamental Rights. A constitutional amendment was an exercise of constituent power, not "law" within Article 13's ordinary-law prohibition.

The reasoning was:

1. Article 13 controlled ordinary legislative power.
2. Article 368 concerned amendment of the Constitution.
3. The two powers were legally distinct.
4. Therefore, the First Amendment was not void merely because it affected Fundamental Rights.

### 2. *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan*

The 17th Amendment expanded agrarian protection and inserted additional State laws into the Ninth Schedule.

✅ **Holding:** The Supreme Court reaffirmed *Shankari Prasad*: Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights through Article 368.

### 3. Date and reporting distinction

The judgment was delivered in **1964** and is commonly reported in the **1965** law reports. Both forms appear in standard material.

Use:

> *Sajjan Singh* - decision 1964, commonly cited/reported 1965.

The qualification avoids turning a reporting convention into a false doctrinal disagreement.

### 4. The seeds of later dissent

Although the majority reaffirmed amendment power, the case exposed anxiety about whether Parliament could remove the Constitution's most essential features. The later basic-structure doctrine did not appear fully formed, but the concern had become visible.

⚠️ **Inference:** The case marks the point at which the question shifted from "Can Fundamental Rights be amended?" toward "Are there features whose destruction would change the Constitution's identity?"

### 5. Comparison

| Case | Amendment challenged | Article 13 treatment | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Shankari Prasad* | First Amendment | Amendment not "law" | Power to amend FR upheld |
| *Sajjan Singh* | 17th Amendment | Reaffirmed distinction | Power to amend FR upheld |

The cases agree in result. *Sajjan Singh* matters because its separate opinions foreshadowed the structural concern that later transformed the doctrine.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Excluding constitutional amendments from Article 13 gives Parliament a simple method to evade Fundamental Rights.

**Reply:** Amendment requires a higher procedure than ordinary law, and the early Court treated constituent power as legally distinct.

**Residual:** Procedure alone does not answer whether a super-majority may abolish democracy, judicial review or equality. That unresolved issue drove the next reversal.

### UPSC application

Use these cases to begin the chronology, not to state the present law. Their present value is explanatory: they establish the original constituent-power reading that *Golak Nath* rejected and *Kesavananda* later restored with a new limitation.

**Model answer:** Both treated a constitutional amendment under Article 368 as an exercise of constituent power rather than "law" within Article 13. This categorical distinction allowed Parliament to amend Part III.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying that the cases held Fundamental Rights unimportant or unenforceable. They distinguished the legal route of amendment from ordinary legislation.

**Mains prompt:** Explain the constitutional reasoning in *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh*. Why did it remain incomplete? (10 marks, 150 words)

#### Model answer

*Shankari Prasad* (1951) upheld the First Amendment by distinguishing ordinary legislative power from constituent power. It held that "law" in Article 13 referred to ordinary law, while an Article 368 amendment could alter Fundamental Rights. *Sajjan Singh*, decided in 1964 and commonly reported in 1965, reaffirmed this reasoning while upholding the 17th Amendment.

The approach gave Parliament broad amendment capacity and prevented Article 13 from freezing Part III. Yet it remained incomplete because procedural distinction did not answer a deeper question: could Parliament use constituent power to abolish democracy, judicial review, federalism or the essence of rights?

Separate judicial concerns in *Sajjan Singh* anticipated this identity problem. *Golak Nath* responded by making Fundamental Rights unamendable, while *Kesavananda* later devised the narrower basic-structure ceiling.

*The 24th Amendment did not itself invent unlimited power; it restored amendability, which the Court then bounded in 1973.*

### 1. *I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab* (1967)

The challenge concerned the 17th Amendment and laws placed in the Ninth Schedule.

✅ **Holding:** The Supreme Court reversed the earlier approach. It treated a constitutional amendment as "law" within Article 13 and held that Parliament could not abridge or take away Fundamental Rights.

The majority described Fundamental Rights as occupying a transcendental and immutable position.

### 2. Prospective overruling

The Court did not invalidate all past amendments retrospectively. It used **prospective overruling**:

```text
Past completed constitutional position protected
                         |
new rule applies for future exercise of amendment power
```

This remedial technique avoided immediate constitutional disruption while changing the law for the future.

Prospective overruling controls the temporal effect of a new rule. It is not the same as saying the prior decisions were correct.

### 3. The constitutional problem created

*Golak Nath* protected Fundamental Rights strongly but generated a rigidity problem:

- Parliament could not respond through amendment even where Part III required adjustment;
- the entire category of Fundamental Rights appeared frozen;
- the distinction between changing a right and destroying constitutional identity disappeared.

### 4. The 24th Amendment response

The 24th Amendment:

1. expressly stated that Parliament exercises **constituent power** under Article 368;
2. authorised amendment by addition, variation or repeal;
3. added Article 13(4), excluding Article 368 amendments;
4. added Article 368(3), stating Article 13 does not apply;
5. made Presidential assent obligatory.

It therefore restored the *Shankari Prasad* distinction in explicit constitutional text.

### 5. What the 24th Amendment did not settle

The Amendment answered:

> Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights?

It did not conclusively answer:

> Can Parliament destroy every defining feature of the Constitution?

That second question reached the thirteen-judge Bench in *Kesavananda Bharati*.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The 24th Amendment allowed Parliament to place its amendments beyond rights review and therefore created absolute power.

**Reply:** It removed the Article 13 barrier, but Article 368 remained a conferred power. *Kesavananda* upheld the Amendment while deriving a structural limit from the Constitution as a whole.

**Residual:** The doctrine now needed a criterion more precise than "Fundamental Rights are wholly unamendable" and more protective than "every amendment is valid."

### UPSC application

Use a three-stage sentence:

> *Golak Nath* froze Fundamental Rights through Article 13; the 24th Amendment restored express constituent power; *Kesavananda* preserved that power but prohibited damage to the basic structure.

### Revision notes

1. *Golak Nath* was decided in 1967.
2. It arose from the 17th Amendment/Ninth Schedule context.
3. It treated constitutional amendment as Article 13 "law."
4. Parliament could not abridge Fundamental Rights.
5. The Court used prospective overruling.
6. Past amendments were not generally unravelled.
7. The rule created constitutional rigidity.
8. The 24th Amendment was enacted in 1971.
9. It made constituent power explicit.
10. It excluded Article 13 from Article 368 amendments.
11. It made Presidential assent obligatory.
12. It restored amendability but did not conclusively settle unlimited power.
13. *Kesavananda* supplied the next doctrinal compromise.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did prospective overruling matter in *Golak Nath*, and why did the 24th Amendment still require judicial review in *Kesavananda*?

**Model answer:** Prospective overruling allowed *Golak Nath* to announce that Parliament could not abridge Fundamental Rights without automatically invalidating past amendments and destabilising existing legal arrangements. The 24th Amendment then reversed the future disability by making constituent power and Article 13's exclusion explicit. Judicial review remained necessary because restoring the power to amend Fundamental Rights did not determine whether that power could destroy the Constitution's defining identity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying prospective overruling meant *Golak Nath* approved earlier amendments on their merits. It controlled the temporal consequences of the new rule.

**Mains prompt:** Assess the constitutional significance of *Golak Nath* and the 24th Amendment in the evolution of amending power. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

*Golak Nath* (1967) marked the sharpest judicial restriction on Parliament's amending power. Reversing *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh*, the Court treated a constitutional amendment as "law" under Article 13 and held that Fundamental Rights could not be abridged. Prospective overruling protected prior arrangements while applying the prohibition to future amendments.

The ruling strongly protected Part III but created excessive rigidity: every Fundamental Right appeared immune even from reform that did not destroy constitutional identity.

Parliament responded through the 24th Amendment, 1971. It made constituent power explicit in Article 368, authorised addition, variation and repeal, added Article 13(4) and Article 368(3), and made Presidential assent obligatory. The Amendment restored the legal distinction between ordinary law and constitutional amendment.

Its significance was not the creation of unlimited power. In *Kesavananda Bharati*, the Court upheld the 24th Amendment but held that constituent power could not damage the basic structure.

Thus *Golak Nath* posed the rigidity problem; the 24th restored flexibility; *Kesavananda* supplied the enduring limit.


*The decision rejected both complete rigidity and unlimited parliamentary power.*

### 1. The litigation setting

*Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* was decided on **24 April 1973** by a **thirteen-judge Bench**, the largest Bench constituted by the Supreme Court.

The petitions required the Court to examine the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments against the background of property and land-reform disputes.

### 2. The precise holding

✅ **Fact:** The Court upheld Parliament's power to amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights. It upheld the 24th Amendment and rejected *Golak Nath*'s total prohibition.

✅ **Fact:** By a narrow majority, it simultaneously held that Article 368 does not permit Parliament to alter, damage or destroy the Constitution's **basic structure or essential features**.

The safe formula is:

> **Parliament may amend every provision, but it may not use amendment to destroy the Constitution's basic structure.**

Avoid saying:

- no basic-structure-related provision can ever be altered;
- every Fundamental Right is individually unamendable;
- the Court supplied one exhaustive majority-approved list;
- the judgment made Parliament subordinate to every judicial preference.

### 3. Why the doctrine is structural

The Court did not locate a sentence saying "basic structure shall not be amended." It reasoned from the Constitution's nature:

```text
Amendment preserves continuity with the existing Constitution
                         |
destruction creates a constitution of a fundamentally different identity
                         |
Article 368 grants amendment, not legal revolution
```

An amendment may change institutional detail, redistribute power or qualify a right. It crosses the line when the Constitution ceases to retain its essential identity.

### 4. The 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments

**24th Amendment:** upheld. Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights, Article 13 did not apply to an Article 368 amendment, and assent was mandatory.

**25th Amendment:** the limited Article 31C protection for laws implementing Article 39(b)-(c) was sustained, but the attempt to exclude judicial examination completely could not survive. The case thus distinguished constitutional priority from an ouster of review.

**29th Amendment:** the Kerala land-reform enactments placed in the Ninth Schedule formed part of the controversy. The case established the date from which later Ninth-Schedule insertions would eventually face basic-structure control.

### 5. The Preamble's role

The Court held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution and may be amended, but its basic features cannot be destroyed.

The Preamble assists identification of constitutional commitments such as:

- sovereign, democratic and republican government;
- justice, liberty and equality;
- secular constitutional order;
- unity and integrity.

It guides structural reasoning but does not independently confer legislative power or create a free-standing cause of action.

### 6. No single frozen Kesavananda list

The judges expressed different formulations. Features identified across opinions included constitutional supremacy, republican and democratic government, secularism, federalism and separation of powers.

Later cases attributed further elements. Therefore:

> The doctrine was propounded in *Kesavananda*; its content developed case by case.

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The Court amended Article 368 by inventing a limitation absent from the text.

**Reply:** The majority treated "amend" as a power to change while preserving constitutional continuity. A power granted by a limited Constitution cannot logically authorise destruction of the conditions that make the grant legally meaningful.

**Residual:** The absence of a closed textual list leaves substantial interpretive discretion with judges. The legitimacy of that discretion depends on reasoned, feature-specific and precedent-sensitive application.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Discuss the constitutional compromise achieved in *Kesavananda Bharati*. (20 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

*Kesavananda Bharati* resolved the conflict between constitutional flexibility and constitutional identity. A thirteen-judge Bench considered the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments on 24 April 1973.

Second, the majority denied that this power was unlimited. Article 368 authorises amendment, not destruction. Parliament may add, vary or repeal provisions, but it cannot damage the Constitution's basic structure. The Preamble was recognised as part of the Constitution and as an aid to identifying its essential commitments.

Third, the Court applied differentiated review. It did not treat every alteration as invalid; the 25th Amendment was assessed clause by clause, and exclusion of meaningful judicial examination could not be sustained.

The compromise preserves democratic capacity to reform the constitutional order while preventing a temporary super-majority from legally abolishing democracy, judicial review or other defining features.

Its weakness is indeterminacy: no exhaustive list was produced. Its legitimacy therefore depends on careful case attribution and impact-based reasoning.

*The decisive contradiction was that Parliament used a limited power to declare that the power had no limits.*

### 1. The 39th Amendment and *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*

The 39th Amendment inserted an election-immunity arrangement in Article 329A. A specified clause attempted to place election disputes involving the Prime Minister and Speaker beyond ordinary judicial adjudication.

✅ **Holding:** The Supreme Court invalidated the offending clause. The judges relied on features including free and fair elections, equality, rule of law and judicial review.

The case demonstrated that the doctrine was operational, not merely theoretical: a constitutional amendment could be severed and invalidated for its impact on basic features.

### 2. The 42nd Amendment's attempt at unlimited power

Section 55 of the 42nd Amendment inserted:

- **Article 368(4):** no constitutional amendment, including one affecting Part III, could be questioned in any court on any ground;
- **Article 368(5):** there was no limitation whatever on Parliament's constituent power.

It also expanded Article 31C's priority beyond laws implementing Article 39(b)-(c) to laws implementing any Directive Principle.

### 3. *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980)

✅ **Holding:** The Supreme Court invalidated Article 368(4)-(5).

Its reasoning can be reconstructed:

1. The Constitution grants Parliament limited amending power.
2. If Parliament could remove every limit, the granted power would become absolute.
3. A donee of limited power cannot enlarge that power into an unlimited one.
4. Judicial review is necessary to enforce the limit.
5. Limited amending power and judicial review are themselves basic features.

### 4. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

*Minerva Mills* also invalidated the 42nd Amendment's enlargement of Article 31C.

The Court did not establish absolute supremacy of Fundamental Rights over Directive Principles. It protected their **harmony and balance**:

```text
Fundamental Rights -> discipline the means and protect liberty
Directive Principles -> guide social transformation and welfare
                         |
                         v
Neither part may be used to destroy the other
```

This balance is a basic feature because liberty without social justice is incomplete, while social transformation without enforceable rights permits authoritarian means.

### 5. Printed text and operative validity

Article 368(4)-(5) remains visible in the official constitutional text, accompanied by a footnote recording that the provision was declared invalid in *Minerva Mills*.

An examiner may use this to test the difference between:

- enacted text;
- commenced text; and
- operative constitutional law after judicial review.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** By striking constitutional text, the Court placed itself above the Constitution.

**Reply:** The Court enforced the Constitution's own limitation on a conferred power. Without judicial review, the declaration of limited power would be unenforceable against the body exercising it.

**Residual:** Judicial enforcement must remain tied to identifiable constitutional features. Review cannot become a general judicial veto over policy disagreement.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why did Article 368(5)'s declaration of unlimited constituent power contain a logical constitutional defect?

**Model answer:** Article 368 is the source of Parliament's constituent power. A power granted and limited by the Constitution cannot be used by its recipient to erase every limitation on itself, because that would convert a constituted power into original sovereign power. Article 368(5) attempted precisely that conversion. *Minerva Mills* therefore treated limited amending power and judicial review as basic features necessary to preserve the constitutional grant.

**Misconception to avoid:** Saying the Court opposed all strong amendments. The defect was the attempted destruction of the limit and review mechanism, not the political ambition of the Amendment.

**Mains prompt:** "A limited amending power cannot be converted into an unlimited one." Explain with reference to the 42nd Amendment and *Minerva Mills*. (15 marks, 250 words)

*Article 31B is powerful validation, but post-Kesavananda insertion is not absolute immunity.*

### 1. Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule

The First Amendment inserted Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule. Article 31B validated scheduled enactments against specified Fundamental Rights challenges.

The original policy context was agrarian reform, but later insertions broadened the Schedule's use. That raised the question whether Parliament could immunise a law by constitutional amendment even when the law's effect damaged a basic feature.

### 2. *Waman Rao v. Union of India*

✅ **Holding:** In *Waman Rao*, the Court adopted **24 April 1973** - the date on which *Kesavananda Bharati* was decided - as the prospective dividing line for Ninth-Schedule insertion amendments.

- Ninth-Schedule insertions before the cut-off were left protected.
- Constitutional amendments inserting laws after the cut-off were open to basic-structure review.

### 3. Date and reporting distinction

The judgment was delivered in **1980** and is commonly reported/cited as **1981**.

Write:

> *Waman Rao* - decision 1980, reported 1981.

The legal cut-off rule comes from *Waman Rao*; the date selected by that ruling is the *Kesavananda* decision date, not the date of the *Waman Rao* judgment.

### 4. *I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007)

The nine-judge Bench examined protection given by Article 31B to laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after the cut-off.

✅ **Holding:** Such insertions remain subject to basic-structure review. The Court examines the **impact** of the scheduled law on Fundamental Rights that form part of the basic structure.

The sequence is:

```text
Identify the right affected
          |
determine whether its essential content forms part of a basic feature
          |
assess the degree and effect of abrogation
          |
test the constitutional amendment that inserted the law
```

### 5. What Coelho does not say

It does not establish that:

- every Ninth-Schedule law is invalid;
- every ordinary Fundamental Rights violation automatically destroys basic structure;
- pre-1973 insertions can be reopened merely because the doctrine later developed;
- Article 31B has no legal effect.

The decisive issue is structural impact, not the mere presence of a rights argument.

### 6. Relationship between law and amendment

The scheduled enactment is an ordinary law, but its immunity is conferred by a constitutional amendment inserting it into the Schedule. The challenge targets the amendment's protective effect by examining the impact of the protected law.

This avoids a false binary:

```text
not ordinary-law review alone
not automatic immunity through amendment
but impact-based review of the insertion amendment
```

### 7. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The cut-off arbitrarily privileges identical laws inserted one day apart.

**Reply:** Prospective constitutional adjudication often protects settled pre-doctrine arrangements. The cut-off gives legal certainty while preventing future evasion after Parliament was on notice of the basic-structure limit.

**Residual:** Pre-cut-off immunity remains a significant exception. The doctrine values stability as well as principled review.

> **The basic structure is an illustrative, judicially evolving set of essential constitutional features identified through cases.**

The recognised inventory includes:

- supremacy of the Constitution;
- sovereign, democratic and republican character;
- secularism;
- federalism;

### 3. Bounded specialist links

**Preamble:** supplies values and constitutional identity; it is part of the Constitution, amendable but not destructible in its basic features.

**Fundamental Rights:** Article 13 controls ordinary laws; amendments are tested through basic structure, not ordinary Article 13 treatment. Rights are not all individually frozen.

**Federalism:** *S.R. Bommai* reinforces federalism and secularism. The detailed operation of Centre-State relations belongs to the federal topics.

**Judiciary:** judicial review and judicial independence are protected features. The complete jurisdiction and appointment systems remain separate topics.

**Interpretation:** basic structure limits amendment. It is not a licence to replace constitutional text with judicial policy.

### 4. Later applications and qualifications

**L. Chandra Kumar (1997):** review by the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and the Supreme Court under Article 32 forms part of the basic structure; tribunal decisions remain subject to constitutional-court scrutiny.

**NJAC judgment (2015):** the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act were struck down. Judicial independence was treated as a basic feature. The judgment did not establish that every existing appointment detail is itself eternally unchangeable.

**Janhit Abhiyan (2022):** the 103rd Amendment introducing EWS provisions was upheld by a 3:2 majority. The case demonstrates that invocation of equality/basic structure does not automatically invalidate an amendment; the Court assesses whether the amendment destroys the equality code's constitutional identity.

### 5. Feature versus institution

A basic feature should not be confused with every institutional arrangement serving it.

```text
Basic value: judicial independence
Possible institutional design: collegium, commission or another system
Review question: does the new design preserve or destroy independence?
```

This prevents the doctrine from freezing all present mechanisms.

### 6. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** An open list gives judges unlimited capacity to label preferred values as basic.

**Reply:** Legitimate application requires textual and structural grounding, case attribution, proof of essentiality and an impact analysis. A court must identify the feature and explain how the amendment damages it.

**Residual:** These disciplines reduce but do not remove judicial discretion. The doctrine trades numerical certainty for protection against novel forms of constitutional destruction.

**Misconception to avoid:** Converting a protected constitutional end into permanent immunity for one administrative design.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** The basic structure is an illustrative doctrine, not a closed constitutional list. Discuss with case attribution. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

The Constitution does not enumerate its basic structure, and *Kesavananda Bharati* did not produce one identical list accepted by every majority judge. The doctrine protects constitutional identity through case-specific attribution.

*Kesavananda* identified features including constitutional supremacy, democracy, republican government, secularism, federalism and separation of powers. *Indira Nehru Gandhi* applied the doctrine to free and fair elections, equality, rule of law and judicial review. *Minerva Mills* recognised limited amending power, judicial review and harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. *S.R. Bommai* reinforced federalism and secularism; *L. Chandra Kumar* protected High Court and Supreme Court review over tribunals; the NJAC judgment protected judicial independence.

The list remains open because a novel amendment may threaten constitutional identity through a method not previously litigated. Yet openness is not unlimited discretion. A court must identify the feature, ground it in text and structure, attribute precedent and demonstrate damaging impact.

Thus the doctrine evolves without freezing every institutional detail, but its legitimacy depends on disciplined, reasoned application.

## Lesson 13 - Constitutional guardianship or judicial overreach?

Progress: 13/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: logic, criticisms, replies and unresolved legitimacy

### Visual: the legitimacy balance

```text
        restraint for ordinary disagreement or policy choice
```

*The best evaluation does not deny judicial discretion; it asks whether doctrine and institutional safeguards justify that discretion.*

### 1. The defence: constitutional supremacy

India follows constitutional supremacy rather than British-style parliamentary sovereignty. Parliament:

- is created by the Constitution;
- receives enumerated legislative powers;
- receives amendment power from Article 368;
- remains subject to enforceable constitutional limits.

If the body exercising amendment power could abolish elections, federalism, review and rights safeguards, procedural super-majority alone would convert constitutional government into elected legal absolutism.

### 2. The identity argument

The doctrine distinguishes **change** from **replacement**:

```text
Amendment -> continuity despite change
Replacement -> destruction of the legal identity from which power comes
```

The Constitution may evolve, but an amendment cannot transform a democratic constitutional republic into its opposite while claiming continuity under Article 368.

### 3. The major criticisms

#### A. Textual criticism

The Constitution contains no express basic-structure clause. Critics argue that the Court supplied a limitation that the framers did not write.

#### B. Democratic criticism

Unelected judges can invalidate an amendment passed by super-majorities and, where required, ratified by States. This appears to place judicial judgment above exceptional democratic consent.

#### C. Indeterminacy criticism

The feature list is open and developed through cases. Uncertainty may allow later Benches to expand the doctrine.

#### D. Institutional criticism

Judges may protect arrangements that also preserve judicial power, as alleged in criticism of the NJAC decision.

#### E. Amendment-procedure criticism

Separately, the procedure itself is criticised because:

- no special constitutional convention exists;
- States cannot initiate Article 368 amendments;
- most provisions require no State consent;
- only half the States are needed where ratification applies;
- no ratification deadline exists;
- withdrawal after ratification is unresolved;
- no joint sitting breaks deadlock;
- apart from the special-majority and ratification requirements, the process resembles ordinary parliamentary legislation;
- the text is sketchy enough to generate litigation.

### 4. Named defence and the parliamentary-sovereignty debate

The procedural criticism must be balanced against the assessments introduced in Lesson 1. Wheare praised the balance and variety of the scheme; Nehru stressed adaptability for a living polity; Ambedkar described the procedure as facile rather than prohibitively rigid; and Granville Austin regarded its apparent complexity as purposeful diversity.

The doctrine itself re-entered public debate during the **fiftieth anniversary of *Kesavananda Bharati* in 2023**. Vice-President **Jagdeep Dhankhar** criticised the basic-structure doctrine in parliamentary-sovereignty terms, arguing that judicially imposed limits diminish the authority of elected constitutional amendment.

Its legal status must be kept exact:

- the remarks did not amend Article 368;
- they did not overrule *Kesavananda Bharati*;
- the doctrine remains binding unless reconsidered by the Supreme Court or displaced through a constitutionally valid development.

The debate is useful because it states the democratic criticism in its strongest public form while preserving the distinction between political disagreement and operative constitutional law.

### 5. The strongest replies

| Criticism | Strongest reply |
|---|---|
| No textual clause | "Amendment" and the structure of a limited Constitution imply preservation of constitutional identity |
| Anti-democratic | Democracy is more than majority rule; free elections, rights and review preserve the conditions of future democratic choice |
| Indeterminate | Case attribution, textual grounding and impact analysis constrain application |
| Judicial self-interest | A feature must be institutionally justified, and judicial reasoning remains open to criticism, larger Benches and constitutional redesign that preserves independence |
| State weakness | The proviso still creates a real federal veto for specified matters, enforceable through procedural review |

### 6. Comparative caution

The United Kingdom illustrates legislative constitutional flexibility; the United States illustrates formal rigidity and deep State participation. India combines graded amendment routes with a judicially enforced substantive ceiling.

The comparison should support, not replace, Indian constitutional analysis.

### 7. A qualified verdict

The doctrine is most defensible when:

1. the feature is clearly anchored in constitutional text and structure;
2. precedent attributes the feature or supplies a principled extension;
3. the court demonstrates actual structural damage;
4. ordinary policy disagreement is excluded;
5. the remedy is no broader than necessary.

⚠️ **Inference:** The doctrine's legitimacy does not come from judicial infallibility. It comes from transparent reasoning, institutional restraint and the need for an enforceable distinction between amendment and constitutional destruction.

### Concept check

**Question:** What is the strongest democratic defence of a judicially created limit on a super-majoritarian constitutional amendment?

**Model answer:** Constitutional democracy protects not only present majority choice but also the institutional conditions for future choice: free elections, opposition, rights, federal competition, rule of law and judicial remedies. A super-majority cannot claim democratic authority to abolish those conditions permanently. Judicial review of structural destruction can therefore preserve democracy's continuing framework, provided courts identify a constitutionally grounded feature, prove damaging impact and avoid converting policy disagreement into basic-structure review.

**Misconception to avoid:** Answering that courts are always wiser than legislatures. The defence rests on constitutional role and future democratic conditions, not superior judicial policy judgment.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Critically evaluate the basic-structure doctrine as a safeguard against majoritarian constitutionalism. (20 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

The basic-structure doctrine prevents Parliament from using Article 368 to destroy the constitutional conditions under which democratic power is exercised. It is therefore a safeguard against majoritarian constitutionalism, not merely against ordinary legislation.

Its strongest defence is constitutional supremacy. Parliament is a constituted body; amendment is a granted power and cannot logically become authority to abolish democracy, federalism, judicial review or the essence of rights. The doctrine also preserves future democratic choice against a temporary super-majority.

However, the objections are substantial. The limitation is not textually enumerated, its feature list is open, and unelected judges may invalidate amendments supported by exceptional parliamentary and State consent. The NJAC controversy sharpens concern about institutional self-protection.

The answer is not abandonment but discipline. Courts should require textual and structural grounding, case attribution, proof of damaging impact and a narrow remedy. Ordinary policy disagreement must remain outside review.

Thus the doctrine is justified as constitutional guardianship when it prevents destruction of democratic identity; it becomes overreach when structural language substitutes for reasoned constitutional injury.


*Procedure asks whether power was exercised correctly; substance asks whether the power reached a constitutionally forbidden result.*

### 1. Procedural review

A constitutional amendment may fail because:

- it used Article 368 where an express ordinary-law route governed, or vice versa;
- either House did not satisfy both special-majority limbs;
- the Houses did not pass the required text separately;
- required State ratification was absent;
- assent occurred before the required State stage;
- another mandatory constitutional step was omitted.

*Rajendra N. Shah* is the clearest ratification example. The Court need not first declare co-operative reform hostile to constitutional identity; failure to obtain mandatory federal consent was sufficient.

### 2. Substantive review

A procedurally perfect amendment may still fail if it damages the basic structure.

The substantive inquiry requires:

1. identify the alleged basic feature;
2. cite the case recognising or supporting it;
3. explain the amendment's actual legal effect;
4. distinguish alteration from destruction;
5. state the limiting qualification.

Examples:

- 39th Amendment election immunity -> free elections, equality, rule of law and review;
- 42nd Amendment Article 368(4)-(5) -> limited power and judicial review;
- post-1973 Ninth-Schedule insertion -> structural impact on essential rights;
- 99th Amendment/NJAC -> judicial independence.

### 3. Constituent versus legislative review

| Instrument | Power | Primary grounds of review |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary statute | Legislative power | Legislative competence, Fundamental Rights and other constitutional provisions |
| Constitutional amendment | Constituent power | Article 368 procedure and basic structure |
| Simple-majority constitutional change | Express ordinary-law constitutional power | Compliance with enabling Article plus ordinary constitutional review |

### 4. The ordinary-law boundary in *Anjum Kadari*

✅ **Fact:** In *Anjum Kadari v. Union of India* (2024 INSC 831), the Supreme Court clarified that an ordinary statute cannot be invalidated solely because it is said to violate an abstract basic feature.

For ordinary legislation, the challenger must connect invalidity to:

- lack of legislative competence;
- violation of Part III;
- or another constitutional provision.

Basic structure remains relevant to interpretation and institutional understanding, but it is not a free-standing substitute for the constitutional grounds governing ordinary law.

### 5. The two-axis answer demanded in 2025

The 2025 GS-II question requires two distinct columns:

| Procedural limitations | Substantive limitations |
|---|---|
| Parliament-only initiation | Basic-structure ceiling |
| Two majority limbs | Constitutional identity |
| Separate passage; no joint sitting | Limited amending power |
| State ratification for exact federal list | Judicial review of damaging effect |
| Mandatory assent | Ninth-Schedule impact test |

An answer that discusses only *Kesavananda* misses half the directive. An answer that lists procedure without constitutional identity also remains incomplete.

### 6. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Combining procedural review with an open-ended substantive doctrine gives courts a veto over every serious attempt to amend the Constitution.

**Reply:** The two inquiries are bounded differently. Procedural review enforces express constitutional commands such as the dual majority and State ratification. Substantive review requires identification of a recognised structural feature and proof of damaging effect; it does not authorise rejection merely because judges consider the amendment unwise.

**Residual:** The distinction is only as strong as the reasons supplied. A reviewing court should identify the feature, explain the impact, distinguish alteration from destruction and consider whether severing the defective portion preserves the valid remainder.

### 7. Final qualified position

Article 368 creates a power that is:

- **constituent** in legal character;
- **graded** in procedure;
- **federal** for specified provisions;
- **reviewable** for mandatory procedural compliance;
- **limited** by constitutional identity.

That is why Parliament may change the Constitution but cannot legally convert itself into an unlimited sovereign body.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Mains prompt:** Examine the procedural and substantive limitations on Parliament's power to amend the Constitution. (15 marks, 250 words)

#### Model answer

Article 368 grants Parliament constituent power to amend by addition, variation or repeal, but the power is limited on two axes.

Procedurally, an amendment may originate only in either House of Parliament. Each House must pass it separately by a majority of total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting; no joint sitting is available. Amendments affecting the exact federal proviso require ratification by not less than half the State legislatures before presentation to the President, who shall assent. *Rajendra N. Shah* confirms that failure to obtain mandatory ratification can invalidate an amendment.

Substantively, *Kesavananda Bharati* permits amendment of every part, including Fundamental Rights, but prohibits damage to the basic structure. *Indira Gandhi*, *Minerva Mills*, *I.R. Coelho* and the NJAC judgment protect free elections, judicial review, limited amending power, essential rights impact and judicial independence.

The doctrine does not freeze every provision or permit review of ordinary statutes on an abstract basic-feature claim.

Thus Parliament exercises wide constituent power, but only through prescribed procedure and within the continuing identity of the Constitution.

**Question:** Why can the two-thirds present-and-voting requirement never be used as a substitute for the total-membership majority?

**Model answer:** The thresholds serve different functions and are cumulative. Without the first limb, low attendance could permit amendment; without the second, a narrow participating majority could suffice.

## Cumulative check 6 - open list

**Question:** Why should an answer name a case after each claimed basic feature?

**Model answer:** The list is not textually enumerated or exhaustively settled in *Kesavananda*. # ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question

**Question:** Differentiate ordinary legislative power from constituent power under the Indian Constitution. (10 marks, 150 words)

### Model answer

Ordinary legislative power and constituent power differ in source, object and review.

Parliament exercises ordinary legislative power under provisions such as Articles 245-246 to enact statutes within its assigned fields. Such laws are reviewed for legislative competence, Fundamental Rights and other constitutional limitations. Some constitution-affecting laws, including those under Articles 2-4, 11 and 169, also use expressly authorised simple-majority legislative routes outside Article 368.

Under Article 368, Parliament exercises constituent power to add, vary or repeal constitutional provisions. The Bill must satisfy the special-majority procedure, with State ratification for the specified federal core. Constitutional amendments are not ordinary "law" under Article 13, but they remain reviewable for procedural defects and damage to the basic structure.

Thus constituent power is legally higher in effect but not sovereign: it changes constitutional text while remaining derived from and limited by the Constitution.

## Final 15-mark question

**Question:** Explain how the basic-structure doctrine evolved from the Fundamental Rights amendment controversy. (15 marks, 250 words)

### Model answer

The basic-structure doctrine emerged from disagreement over whether Article 368 could amend Fundamental Rights.

In *Shankari Prasad* (1951), the Supreme Court upheld the First Amendment, holding that a constitutional amendment was constituent action rather than "law" under Article 13. *Sajjan Singh*, decided in 1964 and reported in 1965, reaffirmed this while upholding the 17th Amendment.

*Golak Nath* (1967) reversed course. It treated an amendment as Article 13 law and made Fundamental Rights prospectively unamendable. Parliament responded through the 24th Amendment, 1971, which made constituent power explicit, excluded Article 13 and made Presidential assent obligatory.

In *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), the thirteen-judge Bench upheld the 24th Amendment and restored Parliament's power to amend every part, including Fundamental Rights. However, it held that Article 368 could not damage the Constitution's basic structure.

The doctrine was then applied in *Indira Gandhi* to election immunity and in *Minerva Mills* to Article 368(4)-(5), which attempted to make amending power unlimited and exclude judicial review. *Waman Rao* and *I.R. Coelho* extended control to post-1973 Ninth-Schedule insertions.

The evolution therefore replaced both total rights immutability and unlimited amendment with wide but structurally limited constituent power.

**Quantified rubric:** **15 marks = Shankari/Sajjan stage 3 + Golak Nath and prospective effect 2 + 24th response 2 + precise Kesavananda compromise 4 + later consolidation 3 + synthesis 1.**

## Final 20-mark question

**Question:** "The basic-structure doctrine constitutionalises distrust of both temporary majorities and unchecked judicial discretion." Critically examine. (20 marks, 250 words)

### Model answer

The basic-structure doctrine distrusts temporary majorities because Article 368 numbers, however exceptional, may still be used to eliminate the conditions of constitutional democracy. *Kesavananda Bharati* therefore permits amendment of every part but prohibits damage to constitutional identity. *Indira Gandhi*, *Minerva Mills*, *I.R. Coelho* and the NJAC judgment apply this control to elections, unlimited constituent power, Ninth-Schedule immunity and judicial independence.

The doctrine protects future democratic choice. A present super-majority cannot abolish free elections, federalism, rights remedies or review and still claim ordinary constitutional continuity. Parliament is constituted by the Constitution and cannot convert its limited power into original sovereignty.

Yet the doctrine must also distrust unchecked judicial discretion. It is not textually enumerated, its feature list is open, and judges may protect institutions associated with their own authority. A vague appeal to "constitutional identity" could become a policy veto.

Legitimate review therefore requires discipline: identify the feature, ground it in text and structure, attribute precedent, prove damaging impact, distinguish institution from value, and use a proportionate remedy. *Anjum Kadari* reinforces restraint by denying basic structure as a free-standing ground against ordinary statutes.

Thus the doctrine is defensible as reciprocal distrust: it checks elected constitutional destruction while demanding reasoned judicial self-limitation.

**Quantified rubric:** **20 marks = interpretation of the quotation 3 + majoritarian-risk argument with cases 5 + judicial-discretion criticism 4 + five review disciplines 5 + Anjum boundary 1 + reciprocal-distrust verdict 2.**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core constitutional design

- **Article 368, Part XX:** Parliament may exercise **constituent power** to amend by **addition, variation or repeal**.
- India uses graded entrenchment: simple majority, special majority, and special majority plus State ratification.
- The power is wide but not original or unlimited.
- Ordinary legislative power creates statutes; constituent power changes constitutional text.
- The same Parliament exercises different powers because the constitutional source and legal effect differ.
- The basic-structure doctrine distinguishes amendment from constitutional replacement.

### Named assessments

- **K.C. Wheare:** good balance between flexibility and rigidity; procedural variety is wise though uncommon.
- **Nehru:** a living, vital and organic people require constitutional adaptability.
- **B.R. Ambedkar:** the Constitution adopts a facile amendment procedure rather than extraordinary rigidity.
- **Granville Austin:** the process is ably conceived; its apparent complexity is purposeful diversity.

## 2. Three amendment routes

### A. Simple majority outside Article 368

- Articles **2-4:** admission, establishment, formation, area, boundary and name of States; consequential First/Fourth Schedule change.
- Article **11:** citizenship law.
- Article **169:** creation or abolition of State Legislative Councils after the prescribed Assembly resolution.
- Articles **100(3)/189(3):** quorum.
- Articles **106/195:** legislative salaries and allowances.
- Articles **118/208:** procedure.
- Articles **105(3)/194(3):** privileges.
- Articles **120/210:** language in legislatures.
- Article **124(1):** number of Supreme Court judges.
- Article **138:** additional Supreme Court jurisdiction.
- Article **343(3)** and connected clauses: statutory language arrangements.
- Articles **327-328:** election regulation.
- Articles **82/170:** readjustment and delimitation machinery.
- Article **239A** and other express Part VIII provisions: specified Union Territory arrangements.
- **Second Schedule**, **Fifth Schedule** and **Sixth Schedule** matters authorised for legislative change.

> **Trap:** constitution-affecting ordinary legislation is not automatically an Article 368 amendment.

### B. Special majority

- Majority of the **total membership** of each House.
- Not less than **two-thirds of members present and voting**.
- Both conditions operate simultaneously.
- Each House passes the Bill separately.
- No joint sitting.
- Covers Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and provisions outside the other two routes.

### C. Special majority plus State ratification

Additional ratification by not less than half the State legislatures for:

1. Articles 54 and 55;
2. Articles 73 and 162;
3. Article 241;
4. Article 279A;
5. Chapter IV of Part V;
6. Chapter V of Part VI;
7. Chapter I of Part XI;
8. any List in the Seventh Schedule;
9. representation of States in Parliament;
10. Article 368.

## 3. Article 368 procedure

```text
Either House -> minister/private member -> no prior Presidential recommendation
-> dual special majority in House 1 -> same in House 2
-> no joint sitting -> State ratification where required
-> presentation to President -> President shall assent
-> Constitutional Amendment Act
```

- States cannot introduce Article 368 Bills.
- Article 169 is a separate request-and-law mechanism.
- Ratification follows parliamentary passage and precedes Presidential assent.
- No constitutional ratification deadline exists.
- Withdrawal of ratification is textually unresolved.
- The President cannot withhold or return a duly passed amendment Bill.

## 4. Majority arithmetic

- **Total membership:** full House strength; vacancies and absences do not reduce it.
- **Present and voting:** only members casting aye or no; abstainers excluded.
- Calculate the two thresholds independently.
- Apply them independently in each House.
- A governing majority may satisfy total membership but fail two-thirds present and voting.

## 7. Kesavananda precision

- Thirteen-judge Bench.
- Upheld the 24th Amendment.
- Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights.
- Rejected *Golak Nath*'s total prohibition.
- Amendment cannot damage or destroy basic structure.
- The doctrine protects identity, not every existing provision.
- The Preamble is part of the Constitution and amendable, but basic features remain protected.
- No single exhaustive feature list was agreed.
- The 25th Amendment was reviewed clause by clause.

> **Answer line:** Parliament may change the Constitution, but it cannot use the Constitution's amending clause to abolish the Constitution's identity.

## 8. Indira Gandhi and Minerva Mills

- 39th-Amendment election immunity failed.
- Free and fair elections, equality, rule of law and review informed *Indira Gandhi*.
- Article 368(4) attempted to exclude every court challenge.
- Article 368(5) declared no limitation whatever.
- *Minerva Mills* invalidated both.
- Limited amending power and judicial review are basic features.
- FR-DPSP harmony and balance is a basic feature.
- Article 368(4)-(5) may remain printed but are legally invalid.

## 9. Ninth Schedule

```text

Insertion on/after 24 April 1973 -> open to review
                                  |
                                  v
Coelho impact test:
Does immunity permit abrogation of essential rights content
forming part of basic structure?
```

- Post-cut-off does not mean automatically invalid.
- An ordinary rights violation alone is insufficient.
- The amendment creating immunity is reviewed through the law's structural impact.

## 12. Constituent versus legislative power

- Ordinary laws: competence + Part III + other provisions.
- Article 368 amendments: mandatory procedure + basic structure.
- Express simple-majority changes: enabling clause + ordinary constitutional review.
- *Anjum Kadari*: ordinary law cannot be struck down merely for violating an abstract basic feature.
- Basic structure may inform interpretation but does not replace the operative ordinary-law ground.

## 13. Criticism and reply

- **No express clause:** reply through limited constitutional identity.
- **Judicial overreach:** reply through protection of future democratic conditions.
- **Open-ended list:** reply through text, structure, precedent and impact.
- **Institutional self-interest:** distinguish protected value from present mechanism.
- **Weak State role:** acknowledge asymmetry while recognising enforceable federal consent.
- **Procedure resembles ordinary legislation except for special majority/ratification:** reply through graded entrenchment and the named assessments of adaptability and purposeful diversity.
- **Dhankhar parliamentary-sovereignty critique at the 2023 fiftieth anniversary:** analytical context only; it changed neither Article 368 nor the binding force of *Kesavananda*.
- **Qualified conclusion:** doctrine is guardianship when tied to structural destruction; overreach when used as a policy veto.
