# Official Language — Live Session Edition

Every few years a politician says "Hindi is our national language" and a counter-politician replies "India has no national language" — and both sides act as if this settles a constitutional question. It does not, because neither side has named which provision they are relying on. This topic exists precisely because the Constitution drew a careful, technical boundary around language that ordinary political speech keeps blurring: an *official* language for the Union, a transitional role for English that never actually ended, independent choice for every State, a separate regime for courts and legislative text, and a scatter of safeguards for linguistic minorities — with no single "national language" anywhere in the text.

This session teaches that boundary from the ground up — why the framers built a hedged compromise instead of a clean switch, how a time-bound constitutional transition quietly became an open-ended statutory settlement, what exactly Parliament locked against unilateral withdrawal, how courts have repeatedly stopped language policy from overriding minority and parental choice in education, and how two live 2026 developments — a Lok Sabha reply on the Eighth Schedule and the government's own Hindi Diwas reporting — fit into this decades-old architecture without changing a single word of it. It closes with the debate that will not go away: how far can a plurilingual federation promote one language without ever letting that promotion harden into imposition.

### Where this topic begins

A government form says "Hindi — Official Language of India." A news anchor says "Hindi, our national language." A student revising the night before Prelims assumes these are two ways of saying the same thing. They are not, and the gap between them is this topic's single most valuable fact: the Constitution names an *official* language for specific governmental functions; it never names a *national* language at all.

### Visual: Six language categories, one Constitution

```text
OFFICIAL LANGUAGE     -> authorised for Union/State governmental functions
NATIONAL LANGUAGE     -> no such constitutional declaration exists for India
SCHEDULED LANGUAGE    -> listed in the Eighth Schedule (Lesson 14)
CLASSICAL LANGUAGE    -> separate Union executive recognition (Lesson 15)
COURT LANGUAGE        -> governed by Article 348 and statute (Lessons 9-10)
MEDIUM OF INSTRUCTION -> education-policy/institutional choice, bounded by courts (Lesson 12)
```

*Caption: these six categories overlap in everyday speech but never share one legal source or one legal consequence — confusing any two of them is this topic's commonest trap.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of "official" as a job description and "national" as a title of ownership. A job description tells you what a language is authorised to *do* — conduct Union business, appear on currency, head a form. A title of ownership would tell you which community the whole country's identity *belongs to*. The Constituent Assembly was willing to give Hindi the job; it deliberately refused to hand any single language — including Hindi — the title, because doing so in a country with dozens of major language communities would have converted an administrative choice into a permanent symbolic hierarchy.

### Technical doctrine: Article 343(1)

Article 343(1) does two separate things in one sentence. First, it makes **Hindi in Devanagari script** the **official language of the Union**. Second, and independently, it fixes the **international form of Indian numerals** — not the Devanagari numeral glyphs — as the form to be used for the official purposes of the Union. Script and numeral form are two separate constitutional choices sitting inside the same clause; an exam question can legitimately test either one without the other.

Nowhere in Article 343, or anywhere else in Part XVII, does the Constitution use the words "national language." This is not an oversight corrected later; it is the deliberate design this whole Part protects, as Lesson 2's history will show.

| Question | Constitutional answer |
|---|---|
| Is there a national language of India? | No — the Constitution declares none |
| Is Hindi the official language of the Union? | Yes — Article 343(1) |
| What numeral form applies to Union official purposes? | The international form of Indian numerals |
| Does "official language of the Union" bind every State? | No — States choose under Article 345 (Lesson 6) |
| Are all Eighth-Schedule languages therefore official languages of India? | No — scheduled status is a separate category (Lesson 14) |

### Example and its limit

A Union government circular can be validly issued in Hindi under Article 343 read with the Official Languages Act (Lesson 7). The same circular, if it concerned a date or serial number, would use "2026" in that same international numeral form even in an otherwise Hindi document — the script changes with the official language, the numeral form does not automatically follow it. The limit of this example: it shows the script/numeral split for *Union* documents only; it says nothing about what numerals a State must use in its own official business, which is a separate question the Constitution leaves to State law.

### Comparison: why the distinction survives into every later lesson

Every subsequent lesson in this session depends on keeping "official" distinct from the other five categories above. Article 345's State choice would be unintelligible if "official language" already meant "the one language of the whole nation." Article 344's Eighth-Schedule-linked Commission would be pointless if scheduled status already equalled official status. The classical-language scheme (Lesson 15) would look like a backdoor to the Eighth Schedule if the two categories were the same thing. Precision here is not pedantry; it is the hinge the entire topic turns on.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Hindi is spoken by the largest number of people, used for the most Union business, and taught as a compulsory subject in most States — isn't it the national language in substance, whatever the text says?"

**Reply:** Functional dominance is not the same legal fact as constitutional declaration. The Constitution could have named Hindi the national language and chose not to — a choice this session's next lesson shows was neither accidental nor a loophole, but the price of holding a multilingual federation together. Demographic or administrative weight describes *how much* a language is used; it does not create a textual category the framers refused to write.

**Qualified residual:** The distinction protects against one specific risk — a single community claiming exclusive symbolic ownership of national identity — without denying that Hindi genuinely occupies a privileged administrative position. Both things are true at once; the exam tests whether you can hold them separately rather than collapsing one into the other.

### UPSC application

This is the single most frequently tested idea in the entire topic, appearing in Prelims statement-elimination questions, CSAT-adjacent general-awareness items, and as the opening line of almost any Mains answer on language policy. A Mains answer that opens with "Hindi is India's official language, and India has no national language" earns more in its first sentence than three paragraphs of description that never names Article 343 correctly.

### Revision notes

1. Article 343(1) makes Hindi in Devanagari script the official language of the Union — not the national language.
2. India has no constitutionally declared national language; this is design, not omission.
3. Article 343(1) separately fixes the international form of Indian numerals for Union official purposes.
4. "Official" means authorised governmental function; "national" would mean a symbolic-identity claim the Constitution never makes.
5. Scheduled status (Eighth Schedule), classical status and court-language status are three further, independent categories.
6. A State's own official language is a separate question under Article 345, not controlled by Article 343.
7. Not every Eighth-Schedule language is an "official language of India" — the Schedule is linked to Articles 344(1) and 351, not to Article 343.
8. Devanagari numerals are not the Union default; the international form of Indian numerals is.
9. Demographic dominance of a language is not legal evidence of a "national language" declaration.
10. Every later lesson in this session assumes this six-category discipline.
11. This is the most repeated UPSC trap in the topic — treat it as a reflex check before answering any question here.

### Concept check

**Question:** A coaching-centre poster claims "Article 343 makes Hindi the national language of India, written in the international numeral form." Identify every error in this single sentence.

**Model answer:** Two errors. First, Article 343 makes Hindi the *official language of the Union*, not the national language — the Constitution declares no national language at all. Second, the clause does not put Hindi *itself* "in the international numeral form"; it fixes Hindi's script as Devanagari and separately fixes the *numerals* used for Union official purposes as the international form of Indian numerals. The poster conflates official with national, and conflates a script choice with a numeral choice.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "official language of the Union" as a shorthand for "national language," and treating the numeral clause as describing how the language itself is written rather than a wholly separate constitutional choice.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "India has an official language of the Union but no national language." Explain this constitutional distinction and state its practical significance.

**Model solution:** Article 343(1) makes Hindi in Devanagari script the official language of the Union, authorising it for specified governmental functions; the same clause separately fixes the international form of Indian numerals for Union official purposes. The Constitution does not anywhere declare a national language. The distinction matters practically because "official" is a bounded, functional grant — it governs Union business, not every State, court or citizen — while a "national language" would have claimed symbolic ownership of India's identity for one linguistic community among many. By withholding that title, the framers gave Hindi real administrative status without converting linguistic diversity into a hierarchy, preserving space for State choice (Article 345), minority safeguards (Articles 350-350B) and continued English use (Official Languages Act, 1963).

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Correct statement of Article 343(1)'s content — 3; correct statement that no national language is declared — 2; numeral-form point correctly separated from the script point — 1; practical/federal significance of withholding a national-language title explained — 3; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### The learner's problem

Lesson 1 established *what* Part XVII says. This lesson asks *why* it is written so cautiously — fifteen-year transitions, numeral carve-outs, no national language, State freedom. A text this hedged did not emerge from indifference; it emerged from an argument the Constituent Assembly could not resolve any other way.

### Visual: the compromise as a bargain, not a victory

```text
CLAIM A: rapid, complete replacement of English by Hindi
                       \
                        > NEGOTIATED SETTLEMENT (1949)
                       /   Hindi official + 15-year English transition
                      /    + Parliament's later Art 343(3) power
                      /    + no national-language declaration
CLAIM B: continuity of administration, access and non-Hindi confidence
```

*Caption: neither side fully won — the text is a bargain, which is exactly why it reads like one.*

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine two factions in a committee: one wants to switch the organisation's working language overnight to make a symbolic point; the other warns that overnight switches break ongoing business and alienate members who do not speak the new language fluently. Rather than letting either faction win outright, the committee agrees on a title for the new language, a long transition period for the old one, and a promise to revisit the question later. That is, in miniature, the compromise historically associated with **K.M. Munshi** and **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar** — a label the sources use descriptively, not a phrase that appears in the constitutional text itself.

### Technical doctrine: what the compromise actually fixed

The September 1949 settlement produced four linked outcomes, each of which became a separate constitutional provision: Hindi in Devanagari as the Union's official language (Article 343(1)); the international form of Indian numerals rather than Devanagari numerals (same clause); a fifteen-year continuation of English from commencement (Article 343(2)); and a standing power for Parliament to legislate for English's continued use after that period (Article 343(3)). No declaration of a national language was ever part of the bargain — its absence is as deliberate as every inclusion.

### Visual: the political-constitutional timeline

| Date/year | Development | Significance |
|---:|---|---|
| September 1949 | Constituent Assembly language compromise | Produces Articles 343-351 |
| 26 January 1950 | Constitution commences | Fifteen-year transition begins |
| 1955 | First Official Language Commission (B.G. Kher) | First constitutional review under Article 344 |
| 1957 | Kher Commission examined by the G.B. Pant parliamentary committee | Article 344(4) committee's first working cycle |
| 10 May 1963 | Official Languages Act enacted | Statutory framework precedes the transition's expiry |
| 26 January 1965 | Article 343(2) transition expires; Act's section 3 appointed day | Statute, not Constitution, now governs continuity |
| 1965 | Anti-imposition mobilisation, especially in Tamil Nadu | Demonstrates a hard switch was politically unworkable |
| 1967 Amendment / 8 January 1968 | Section 3 substituted, adding the discontinuance lock | English's continuance becomes conditionally durable |
| 1976 | Official Languages Rules notified | Administrative implementation begins |

*Caption: the settlement is not one 1949 moment; it is a chain running through statute and rule-making into the present.*

### Example and its limit

The 1965 agitation is best read as a bounded federal episode: resistance concentrated on the *compulsory, immediate* displacement of English, not on Hindi's existence as such. This example shows how political resistance shaped legal design — the 1967 Amendment's discontinuance lock is a direct response to exactly this episode. Its limit: regional reactions were internally diverse, and treating either Hindi-promotion or non-Hindi resistance as uniformly secessionist or anti-national misreads a genuinely mixed political landscape.

### Comparison: constitutional transition versus statutory accommodation

A constitutional transition (Article 343(2)) has a fixed expiry date written into the text itself; nothing short of a fresh constitutional amendment can extend it as such. A statutory accommodation (the 1963 Act, detailed in Lesson 7) has no fixed expiry and can in principle be amended by ordinary legislative majority — except where the statute itself builds in an extraordinary consent requirement, which is exactly what the 1967 Amendment did. Confusing the two produces the most common historical trap in this topic: believing English "ended" in 1965 because a transition ended, when in fact a different legal instrument had already taken over.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Wasn't the whole 1949 compromise simply an appeasement of non-Hindi elites who wanted to preserve their own linguistic privilege through English?"

**Reply:** This caricatures a genuinely two-sided negotiation. Hindi-protagonists in the Assembly had equally serious claims — decolonising the administrative language, reflecting the demographic weight of Hindi speakers, and symbolically completing independence. The compromise did not simply defer to one side's convenience; it built enforceable commitments on both sides — Hindi's official status is real and growing; English's continuance is conditional, not permanent.

**Qualified residual:** The settlement bought durability by refusing finality on either claim. That is precisely why, eight decades later, the same tension — promotion versus imposition — remains this topic's liveliest debate (Lesson 16), rather than a historical question fully closed by 1976.

### UPSC application

Mains questions that ask candidates to evaluate "the constitutional wisdom" of the official-but-not-national-language scheme are, in substance, asking for this lesson's compromise narrative, not a bare recitation of Article 343. Naming the 1965 agitation and the 1967 Amendment as evidence of a *living* compromise, not a static one, is what separates a strong answer from a descriptive one.

### Revision notes

1. The compromise is historically described as the Munshi-Ayyangar formula — a descriptive label, not constitutional wording.
2. Four linked 1949 outcomes: Hindi-Devanagari official status, international numerals, a 15-year English transition, and Parliament's later Article 343(3) power.
3. No national-language declaration was ever part of the bargain.
4. First Official Language Commission: B.G. Kher (1955); examined by the G.B. Pant parliamentary committee (1957).
5. Official Languages Act enacted 10 May 1963, ahead of the 1965 transition expiry.
6. 26 January 1965: Article 343(2)'s transition expired; the Act's section 3 took over as the operative instrument.
7. 1965 anti-imposition agitation, concentrated in Tamil Nadu, targeted compulsory displacement of English.
8. 1967 Amendment (effective 8 January 1968) added the discontinuance lock in response to that agitation.
9. 1976 Rules operationalised Union administrative communication.
10. A constitutional transition has a fixed expiry; a statutory accommodation can build in its own extraordinary consent lock.
11. Neither Hindi-promotion nor non-Hindi resistance should be treated as uniformly secessionist or anti-national.
12. The same promotion-versus-imposition tension recurs through this entire session, culminating in Lesson 16.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student writes: "English legally ended in India in 1965." Using this lesson's timeline, explain precisely what did and did not happen on 26 January 1965.

**Model answer:** What ended was Article 343(2)'s fifteen-year constitutional transition for English's Union use. What did not happen was the disappearance of English from Union administration: the Official Languages Act, 1963, already enacted, had its section 3 come into force on that same date, continuing English in addition to Hindi for specified Union purposes under the power Article 343(3) gives Parliament. The legal instrument governing English changed from a constitutional transition to a statute; English's actual use did not cease.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the expiry of a constitutional transition period as the end of the underlying practice it governed, rather than checking whether a different legal instrument had already taken over.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why the 1965 anti-imposition agitation is historically significant for understanding the Official Languages Act's later design.

**Model solution:** The 1965 agitation, concentrated in non-Hindi regions and especially Tamil Nadu, mobilised against the compulsory, immediate displacement of English once Article 343(2)'s fifteen-year transition expired. It demonstrated that a hard legal switch, even one fully authorised by the Constitution's text, could generate serious federal instability if treated as a political fait accompli. Parliament's response, the Official Languages (Amendment) Act, 1967 — substituting section 3 with effect from 8 January 1968 — directly answered this episode by converting continuity from a one-time statutory choice into a conditionally durable settlement, requiring resolutions from every non-Hindi-official-language State legislature and then both Houses of Parliament before specified English-use provisions could be discontinued. The agitation therefore did not merely provoke protest; it reshaped the statute's own architecture.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Correct description of the 1965 agitation's target — 3; correct link to the 1967 Amendment's timing and content — 4; identification of the discontinuance-lock mechanism as the direct response — 2; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### The learner's problem

Lesson 1 taught the headline fact of Article 343(1). This lesson opens the clause itself: what exactly clauses (2) and (3) do, why their difference matters, and why "English's constitutional protection expired in 1965" is one of the most persistent myths in this topic.

### Visual: the clause ladder

| Clause | Rule | Temporal character |
|---|---|---|
| 343(1) | Hindi-Devanagari official language; international-form numerals | Continuing baseline |
| 343(2) | English continued for 15 years from commencement; narrow presidential authorisation during that window | Historical transition, now expired |
| 343(3) | Parliament may by law provide for English/Devanagari-numeral use after the 15 years | Enabling power, exercised through the 1963 Act |

*Caption: clause (2) is a sunset; clause (3) is the door Parliament walked through before the sun actually set.*

### Plain-language intuition

Picture a building with a temporary exit ramp (clause 2) that the architects knew would be removed after fifteen years, and a permanent side door (clause 3) that only opens if someone builds a key for it in advance. Parliament built that key — the Official Languages Act — in 1963, two years before the ramp came down. So when the ramp disappeared in 1965, nobody was stuck outside; they simply started using the side door that was already installed and waiting.

### Technical doctrine: the narrow temporal box of presidential power

During the fifteen-year window only, Article 343(2) allowed the President to authorise the use of Hindi *in addition to* English, and the Devanagari form of numerals *in addition to* the international form, for specified purposes. This presidential power was never open-ended; it existed solely within the transition window. After 1965, any continued English or Devanagari-numeral use rests on Parliament's Article 343(3) legislation — not on a renewed or extended presidential order.

```text
1950 ---------------------------- 1965
      ARTICLE 343(2) TRANSITION

Within this window only, the President MAY authorise:
   Hindi in addition to English
   Devanagari numerals in addition to the international form

After 1965:
   continued use depends on an Article 343(3) LAW, not a
   renewed presidential order under the expired clause.
```

*Caption: confusing a time-boxed presidential power with a permanent executive power is the clause's standard trap.*

### Example and its limit

A 1964 presidential order authorising Hindi for additional Union purposes during the transition was a valid, clause-(2) exercise of power. An identical order issued in 1970 could not rest on clause (2) at all — it would need to trace its authority to Parliament's clause-(3) legislation instead. The example shows the boundary cleanly; its limit is that it says nothing about *what* Parliament actually legislated after 1965 — that substantive content is Lesson 7's Official Languages Act, not this lesson's clause anatomy.

### Comparison: the "1965 myth" corrected

| Common error | Precise correction |
|---|---|
| "English became unconstitutional in 1965." | Only the Article 343(2) transition expired; nothing in the Constitution made English unlawful. |
| "The President simply kept renewing the old order forever." | Post-1965 continuance runs through Parliament's Article 343(3) legislation, not a renewed clause-(2) order. |
| "Devanagari numerals are the constitutional default." | The international form of Indian numerals is the Article 343(1) baseline; Devanagari numerals were only an *additional*, transition-era option. |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If English's post-1965 survival depends entirely on ordinary legislation, isn't its position actually less secure than the Constitution's own fifteen-year guarantee?"

**Reply:** Textually, yes — an ordinary statute is, in principle, easier to amend than the Constitution. In practice, the statute Parliament actually enacted (Lesson 7) built its own extraordinary safeguard, the section 3(5) double lock, which is harder to trigger than an ordinary legislative majority. The *form* of protection changed from constitutional to statutory; its practical durability did not necessarily weaken.

**Qualified residual:** This is a genuine, not merely rhetorical, shift in the *source* of English's legal security — from the Constitution's own text to Parliament's discretion, however tightly Parliament later chose to bind that discretion. Keep clause and statute analytically separate even while recognising how tightly Lesson 7 links them.

### UPSC application

This clause ladder is the standard source of Prelims statement-matching questions that test whether a candidate can correctly attribute a rule to clause (1), (2) or (3) rather than treating Article 343 as one undifferentiated sentence. Mains answers gain precision by citing the exact subclause rather than "Article 343" generally.

### Revision notes

1. Article 343(1): Hindi-Devanagari official language; international-form numerals.
2. Article 343(2): English continued for 15 years from commencement (1950-1965); narrow presidential authorisation existed only within that window.
3. Article 343(3): Parliament's standing power to legislate for English/Devanagari-numeral use after the 15 years.
4. The presidential power under clause (2) was time-boxed, not a renewable permanent power.
5. Post-1965 continuance of English rests on Parliament's clause-(3) legislation (the 1963 Act), not on the Constitution's clause (2) alone.
6. "English became unconstitutional in 1965" is false; only the transition clause expired.
7. The international form of Indian numerals, not Devanagari numerals, is the Article 343(1) default.
8. Script (Devanagari for Hindi) and numeral form (international) are two independent choices inside the same clause.
9. The statutory source of English's post-1965 survival is, in principle, more amendable than a constitutional guarantee — a genuine shift in protection's legal form.
10. Lesson 7's section 3(5) double lock is the practical answer to that theoretical vulnerability.

### Concept check

**Question:** In 1972, could the President validly issue a fresh order under Article 343(2) authorising wider use of Devanagari numerals for Union purposes? Explain your answer.

**Model answer:** No. Article 343(2)'s presidential authorisation power existed only during the fifteen-year transition period (1950-1965). By 1972 that window had long closed, so any authority for continued or expanded Devanagari-numeral use would have to come from Parliament's Article 343(3) legislation, not from a fresh exercise of the now-expired clause-(2) power.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Article 343(2)'s presidential power as a standing, renewable authority rather than one strictly bounded to the fifteen-year transition window.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish the constitutional source of English's use in the Union's official business before 1965 from its source after 1965.

**Model solution:** Before 26 January 1965, English's use for Union official purposes rested directly on Article 343(2), which continued pre-commencement English use for a fixed fifteen-year transition, with the President additionally empowered during that window to authorise supplementary Hindi or Devanagari-numeral use. After that date, Article 343(2) had expired by its own terms; English's continued use instead rests on Parliament's Article 343(3) power, exercised through the Official Languages Act, 1963, whose section 3 permits English to continue in addition to Hindi for specified purposes. The legal source therefore shifted from a self-executing constitutional transition to ordinary parliamentary legislation — a change in form that Lesson 7's statutory safeguards were designed to offset.

### The learner's problem

Lesson 3 showed that Parliament, not the Constitution alone, now governs English's continuance. But how does the Constitution itself keep checking whether the transition from English to Hindi is proceeding fairly? Article 344 builds exactly that review machinery — and its two most-tested traps are about *when* it runs and *who* sits on it.

### Plain-language intuition

Think of Article 344 as a scheduled health check for the language transition, not a permanent monitoring subscription. The Constitution asked for exactly two check-ups — one at year five, one at year ten — each producing a report that Parliament would then examine. It did not promise a check-up every decade for the life of the Republic.

### Technical doctrine: Commission, recommendations and the balancing duty

Article 344(1) required the President to constitute a Commission at the expiration of five years from commencement, and again at the expiration of ten years from commencement. The Commission consists of a Chairperson and members representing India's different Eighth-Schedule languages, all appointed by the President. Its recommendations cover the progressive use of Hindi, restrictions on English, the language to be used for Article 348 purposes, the form of numerals, and any other matter referred to it.

Article 344(3) then builds in an explicit balancing duty: the President must have due regard to India's industrial, cultural and scientific advancement, and to the just claims and interests of persons belonging to the non-Hindi-speaking areas in regard to public services. This is not a side note — it is the textual guarantee that the Commission's brief is never a one-directional mandate to promote Hindi at any cost.

| Restrictions on English | Non-Hindi-speaking interests |
| Language for Article 348 purposes | Legal uniformity and access |
| Form of numerals | Practical and scientific context |
| Other referred matters | Union-State and inter-State communication |

Article 344(4) creates a thirty-member parliamentary committee — twenty from the Lok Sabha, ten from the Rajya Sabha — elected by each House through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote. Its job is to examine the Commission's recommendations and report its opinion to the President, who may then issue directions under Article 344(6).

Its limit: a single historical instance does not prove the Commission has recurred on a fixed decennial schedule ever since — Article 344(1)'s textual milestones remain historical points, not a standing calendar obligation independent of subsequent events.

### Comparison: Commission versus committee, within Article 344 itself

Do not merge the Commission and the committee even though both sit inside Article 344. The Commission recommends; the committee examines those recommendations and forms an opinion for the President. One produces substantive proposals about Hindi's progressive use; the other is a parliamentary check on those proposals before any presidential direction follows.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Doesn't a Commission appointed entirely by the President risk becoming a rubber stamp for whichever government is in power, simply recommending faster Hindi promotion regardless of non-Hindi concerns?"

**Reply:** Article 344(3)'s express balancing duty is a textual safeguard against exactly this risk — the President must have due regard to non-Hindi-speaking interests in public services, not merely to the pace of Hindi's spread. The Commission's membership, representing different Eighth-Schedule languages, is itself structured to resist a single-community capture.

**Qualified residual:** A textual safeguard constrains the Commission's mandate; it does not guarantee that every recommendation will be balanced in practice, nor that the committee's opinion and any resulting presidential direction will always honour that balance. The safeguard is real but advisory in ultimate effect — Lesson 5 shows precisely why a report, however balanced, is not itself law.

The sharpest Mains application is naming Article 344(3)'s balancing duty as textual evidence that the official-language scheme was never designed as one-directional promotion — directly reinforcing Lesson 1's foundational distinction.

**Question:** A candidate writes that "Article 344 requires a fresh Official Language Commission every ten years." Evaluate this claim precisely.

**Model answer:** The claim overstates the text. Article 344(1) required Commissions at two specific historical points — the expiration of five years, and again the expiration of ten years, from constitutional commencement — not a Commission convened every subsequent decade in perpetuity. The provision fixes two milestones measured from 1950, not a standing periodic obligation that automatically regenerates thereafter.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reading "at the expiration of ten years from commencement" as "every ten years from now on," rather than as a second, final historical milestone alongside the five-year one.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the significance of Article 344(3)'s balancing duty for understanding the Official Language Commission's constitutional design.

**Model solution:** Article 344(3) obliges the President, in making recommendations under Article 344, to have due regard to the industrial, cultural and scientific advancement of India and to the just claims and interests of persons belonging to non-Hindi-speaking areas in regard to public services. ### The learner's problem

Everything so far has been about the Union. This lesson turns to a question candidates routinely get wrong by assuming the Union's scheme simply repeats itself at the State level: can a State choose any language it likes, and does that choice have to come from the Eighth Schedule?

### Visual: the State's choice tree

### Plain-language intuition

Article 345 hands each State a genuinely open menu: pick any language actually in use within that State, or Hindi, for all or only some of its official business. There is no requirement that the chosen language first pass through the Eighth Schedule's constitutional gate — State administrative choice and constitutional scheduling are two separate doors, and walking through one does not require walking through the other first.

### Technical doctrine: Articles 345, 346 and 347 in sequence

**Article 345** lets a State Legislature adopt, by law, one or more languages in use in the State, or Hindi, for all or any of the State's official purposes — subject to Articles 346 and 347. Until it legislates otherwise, English continues for the State purposes for which it was used immediately before commencement.

**Article 346** fixes the language authorised for Union official purposes as the baseline for communication between States and between a State and the Union, with a proviso allowing two or more States to agree to use Hindi between themselves instead. This constitutional baseline is overlaid by the Official Languages Act's section 3, which requires English for Union communication with a State that has not adopted Hindi, and requires an English translation where a Hindi-adopting State communicates in Hindi with a non-Hindi State — the full statutory detail belongs to Lesson 7, but the overlay itself must be read together with Article 346 to understand how intergovernmental communication actually works.

**Article 347** operates on demand: if the President is satisfied that a substantial proportion of a State's population desires official recognition of a language spoken by them, the President may direct recognition throughout the State, or a part of it, for a specified purpose. No fixed percentage defines "substantial," and a demand alone does not produce recognition — presidential satisfaction and the exact terms of the direction both matter.

| Article | Principal actor | Core subject | Safeguard |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 345 | State Legislature | Choice of State official language(s) | Subject to Articles 346-347; English continues until changed |
| 346 | Union and States | Inter-State and State-Union communication | Section 3 overlay; voluntary Hindi agreements between States |
| 347 | President | Recognition of a language spoken by a section of a State's population | Demand plus presidential satisfaction; no fixed percentage |

### Example and its limit

A State may legislate to use a language that is not in the Eighth Schedule at all for its own administration — several States have in practice adopted non-Schedule tongues for specific State purposes. This example confirms that scheduled status is not a gate to State-level official recognition. Its limit: this administrative freedom says nothing about how that State communicates with the Union or with other States, which is governed separately by Article 346 and the Act's overlay, nor about Article 347's quite different, demand-triggered recognition mechanism for a population *within* a State.

### Comparison: three distinct federal questions, one chapter

Articles 345, 346 and 347 are often tested as though they were one undifferentiated "State language" rule. They answer three genuinely different questions: what language does *this* State use for its own business (345); what language do States and the Union use to talk to *each other* (346); and what happens when a *section* of a State's population, not the State government itself, wants its own language recognised (347). Confusing the actor — legislature, Union-State pair, or President responding to a population's demand — is the chapter's standard trap.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If a State's chosen language need not be scheduled, could a State simply invent an administrative classification for a dialect to manufacture recognition it would not otherwise receive?"

**Reply:** Article 345's power is legislative, not an unchecked executive whim — it requires the State Legislature to actually pass a law, subject to the ordinary political process that entails. Article 347's separate recognition mechanism, which is the one most relevant to a sub-State population's demand, requires presidential satisfaction of a substantial-proportion threshold, which is a discretionary check against exactly the kind of manufactured or trivial claim the objection worries about.

**Qualified residual:** Neither safeguard is airtight — ordinary legislative majorities can still make contestable choices, and "substantial proportion" is undefined in percentage terms, leaving real discretion in presidential satisfaction. The chapter's design trades a bright-line rule for case-by-case political and executive judgment.

2. Until such a law is made, prior English use continues for the relevant State purposes.
3. A State's chosen official language need not be in the Eighth Schedule.
4. Article 345 operates subject to Articles 346 and 347.
5. Article 346: the language authorised for Union official purposes is the constitutional baseline for inter-State and State-Union communication.
6. Article 346's proviso allows two or more States to agree to use Hindi between themselves.
7. The Official Languages Act's section 3 overlays Article 346 with specific English/Hindi communication safeguards (full detail in Lesson 7).
8. Article 347: on demand, if the President is satisfied a substantial proportion of a State's population desires recognition of a language they speak, the President may direct recognition for the whole State, part of it, or a specified purpose.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State adopts a language not listed in the Eighth Schedule as its sole official language for all State purposes. Is this constitutionally valid, and does it automatically determine how that State communicates with the Union?

**Model answer:** Yes, this is valid: Article 345 permits a State Legislature to adopt any language in use in the State, or Hindi, for its own official purposes, without any requirement that the chosen language be in the Eighth Schedule. However, this choice does not by itself determine Union-State communication, which is governed separately by Article 346 — the language authorised for Union official purposes remains the constitutional baseline for such communication, overlaid by the Official Languages Act's specific safeguards, regardless of what the State has chosen for its own internal administration.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming a State's internal official-language choice under Article 345 automatically becomes the language used for that State's communication with the Union or with other States, rather than treating Article 346 as a separate, specific rule.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** How do Articles 345 and 347 together protect both a State's administrative autonomy and the linguistic claims of a section within that State?

**Model solution:** Article 345 gives the State Legislature itself the power to adopt one or more languages in use in the State, or Hindi, for its official purposes — a genuine exercise of State administrative autonomy, since no Eighth-Schedule or Union pre-approval is required. Article 347 addresses a narrower, different concern: a section of the State's own population whose preferred language may not match the State's chosen official language. Here, on a demonstrated demand and the President's satisfaction that a substantial proportion desires recognition, a direction may secure official recognition for that language within the State or a part of it, for a specified purpose. Together, the two articles let a State govern in its chosen language while leaving a constitutional channel open for sub-State linguistic claims that the State's own legislative choice might otherwise leave unaddressed.

Reading it as "English continues, full stop" misses four distinct engineering choices that together explain why the Act has survived for over six decades without becoming either a dead letter or a flashpoint for sudden withdrawal.

### Technical doctrine: subsection by subsection

**Section 3(1)** provides that English *may continue to be used in addition to Hindi* for Union official purposes for which it was used immediately before the appointed day, and for business in Parliament. Its first proviso requires English for communication between the Union and a State that has not adopted Hindi as its official language; its second proviso requires an English translation to accompany Hindi where a Hindi-adopting State communicates in Hindi with a non-Hindi State; its third proviso preserves voluntary Hindi use and agreements between States.

**Section 3(2)** requires translation into the other language in specified intra-Union and government-controlled-corporation communication until the concerned staff acquire working knowledge of Hindi.

**Section 3(3)** requires both Hindi and English for a named list of high-value instruments: resolutions, general orders, rules, notifications, reports, press communiques, parliamentary papers, contracts, agreements, licences, permits, notices and tender forms.

**Section 3(4)** authorises rules for Union official work, but expressly requires that such rules secure quick and efficient disposal, serve the public interest, and protect against disadvantage employees proficient in either Hindi or English but not both.

**Section 3(5)** is the discontinuance lock: section 3(1)(a) and subsections (2), (3) and (4) remain in force until, first, the legislatures of all States that have not adopted Hindi as their official language pass resolutions for discontinuance of English for those purposes, and second, after considering those resolutions, each House of Parliament passes a discontinuance resolution.

3(3) -> mandatory bilingual high-value documents
3(4) -> efficiency + public interest + employee non-disadvantage
3(5) -> the double-lock discontinuance condition
```

ONLY THEN may the specified section 3 provisions discontinue
```

*Caption: one State resolution, one House, or an executive order is never enough — the lock requires both tiers in sequence.*

### Example and its limit

Imagine a single non-Hindi State passing a resolution calling for English's discontinuance, and a Union government publicly announcing support for the move. Neither act, alone or together, satisfies section 3(5) — every non-Hindi-official-language State would need to pass its own resolution, and both Houses of Parliament would separately need to act afterward. The example shows how deliberately difficult the lock is to trigger; its limit is that the lock covers only the exact provisions named in subsection (5) — section 3(1)(a) and subsections (2)-(4) — not every conceivable use of English across Indian administration.

### Comparison: statute, not convention

| Incorrect explanation | Correct explanation |
|---|---|
| "English survives purely by convention or habit." | Section 3 is operative, enforceable statutory law. |
| "English can be ended by a Union executive order." | Only the section 3(5) double-lock legislative process can discontinue the named provisions. |
| "All uses of English anywhere in India are protected identically." | Section 3(5) names specific subsections; its protection is not universal. |

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Why should non-Hindi States effectively hold a veto over Union language policy through section 3(5), when Parliament alone enacted the Official Languages Act in the first place?"

**Reply:** The lock is a self-imposed federal-consent mechanism — Parliament, responding to the 1965 agitation (Lesson 2), chose to bind its own future discretion precisely because a unilateral Union decision on this question had already proved politically destabilising. This is not an external veto imposed on Parliament; it is Parliament's own considered design for how federal consent should work on a uniquely sensitive question.

**Qualified residual:** Because section 3(5) is itself an ordinary statutory provision, it remains theoretically open to a future Parliament to amend section 3(5) itself by ordinary legislative process, which would in turn make the double lock easier to remove. The safeguard is deliberately difficult to trigger as currently worded, not constitutionally entrenched beyond Parliament's own continuing power to legislate.

### UPSC application

Expect detailed statement-matching Prelims questions on which exact subsections section 3(5) protects, and Mains questions asking candidates to "examine how the Official Languages Act converted a transition into a durable settlement" — precisely this lesson's double-lock architecture, read together with Lesson 2's historical trigger.

2. Section 3(1): English may continue in addition to Hindi for specified Union purposes and parliamentary business.

### Concept check

**Question:** One non-Hindi State passes a resolution in favour of discontinuing English for Union purposes, and the Union Cabinet publicly endorses the idea. Has section 3(5)'s discontinuance condition been satisfied?

**Model answer:** No. Section 3(5) requires, first, resolutions from *every* State legislature that has not adopted Hindi as its official language — not just one — and second, discontinuance resolutions passed by *each* House of Parliament after considering those State resolutions. A single State's resolution and a Cabinet endorsement satisfy neither tier of this double lock; the specified section 3 provisions remain fully in force until both conditions are completed in full.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating one supportive State resolution, or an executive/Cabinet statement of intent, as sufficient to trigger section 3(5), rather than recognising that the lock requires universal State-level action followed by both Houses of Parliament.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine how the Official Languages Act, 1963, and its 1967 Amendment converted Article 343's time-bound transition into a durable federal accommodation.

**Model solution:** Article 343(2) gave English only a fifteen-year constitutional transition, due to expire in 1965; Article 343(3) anticipated that Parliament might legislate further. The Official Languages Act, 1963, exercised that power even before the transition expired, and its substituted section 3 — effective 8 January 1968, following the 1967 Amendment — built the durable settlement actually in force today. First, section 3(1) allows English to continue *in addition to* Hindi for specified Union purposes and parliamentary business, avoiding an abrupt 1965 cut-off. Second, its provisos create a consent-sensitive communication bridge: English for Union-to-non-Hindi-State communication, English translations where a Hindi State writes to a non-Hindi State, and continued room for voluntary Hindi use. Third, subsections (2)-(4) build operational fairness — transition-period translation, mandatory bilingual high-value documents, and explicit protection against disadvantaging employees who know only one of the two languages. Fourth, and most decisively, section 3(5) locks the named provisions in force until every non-Hindi-official-language State legislature, and then both Houses of Parliament, separately pass discontinuance resolutions. Together these four elements converted a constitutional sunset clause into a statute that only an extraordinarily difficult, federally consensual process can unwind — durability achieved through consent and safeguards, not through cultural victory for either language.

### The learner's problem

A translation of a law is useless if nobody can say with certainty which version is the legally binding one. This lesson maps every route through which a non-English or non-original-language legal text becomes *authoritative* — and the separate administrative machinery, the 1976 Rules, that organises everyday Union communication beneath all of it.

### Visual: four authoritative-text routes

| Route | Language/output | Authority safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Article 348(1) | English authoritative baseline | Constitution itself |
| Act, section 5 | Authorised Hindi text of Central instruments | Presidential authority + Gazette publication |
| Act, section 6 | Authorised Hindi translation of specified State Acts/ordinances | Governor's authority + Gazette publication |
| Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973 | Authorised Central-law text in other Eighth-Schedule languages | Presidential authority + Gazette publication |

*Caption: translation becomes legally authoritative only through one of these four named routes — never through accuracy or fluency alone.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of "authoritative" as a stamp, not a quality rating. A translation can be linguistically flawless and still carry no legal weight unless the right official — President, Governor, or in the judgment context, the High Court — has stamped it through the specific channel the Constitution or the Act provides. Four different stamps exist for four different situations; using the wrong one, or assuming fluency substitutes for a stamp, is the recurring error this lesson corrects.

### Technical doctrine: Sections 4, 5, 6 and 8

**Section 4** establishes the statutory Committee on Official Language, already distinguished from its constitutional cousin in Lesson 5; its review culminates in a presidential power to issue directions that cannot be inconsistent with section 3.

**Section 5** deems specified, presidentially authorised Gazette translations to be authoritative Hindi texts of Central Acts, presidential ordinances and delegated instruments, and requires Hindi translations to accompany the authoritative English text of parliamentary Bills and amendments.

**Section 6** allows an authorised Hindi translation of a State Act or ordinance where the State Legislature prescribed a language other than Hindi, in addition to the Article 348(3) English translation that the Constitution already requires for such a text.

**Section 8** is simply the Act's rule-making power, the source of the 1976 Rules discussed below. The consolidated statutory text also omits a former Jammu and Kashmir-specific section 9 — a historical detail worth noting but not a live operative provision.

### The Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973

This is a distinct statute from the 1963 Act. It supplies authoritative translations of Central laws in Eighth-Schedule languages *other than* Hindi: a translation published in the Official Gazette under the President's authority is deemed the authoritative text in that language. It must not be confused with Article 348's English baseline or with sections 5-6 of the 1963 Act, which govern Hindi texts specifically.

```text
SECTION 5: Central law/instrument -> authorised Hindi text
SECTION 6: State law in a non-Hindi prescribed language
           -> Article 348(3) English translation (constitutional)
           -> optional authorised Hindi translation (statutory)
1973 ACT:  Central law -> authorised text in another Eighth-Schedule language
```

*Caption: each route answers a different "which text is authoritative, and in which language?" question.*

### The 1976 Rules: Regions and administrative use

The Official Languages (Use for Official Purposes of the Union) Rules, 1976 — as amended through 1987, 2007 and 2011 by the Department of Official Language's own consolidated record — extend to India **except Tamil Nadu**. They divide Union administrative communication into Region A (the listed Hindi-region States and Delhi/Andaman and Nicobar Islands), Region B (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Punjab and listed Union Territories) and Region C (everywhere else). Rule 3 governs communication from Central offices to non-Central recipients across these regions; Rule 4 governs Central-to-Central communication; Rule 5 requires a Hindi reply to a communication received in Hindi; Rule 6 requires bilingual documents matching section 3(3); Rules 7-8 protect an employee's choice of Hindi or English for applications and file notes, subject to detail; Rules 9-10 distinguish "proficiency" in Hindi from mere "working knowledge."

| Rules do | Rules do not |
|---|---|
| Organise Union administrative communication | Declare a national language |
| Implement sections 3 and 8 | Add languages to the Eighth Schedule |
| Require bilingual use in specified documents | Determine every State's own official language |
| Protect employee efficiency and fairness | Override Article 348 |

### Example and its limit

A Central Government office in Region C communicating with a non-Central recipient would ordinarily use English under Rule 3's regional matrix, while the same office replying to a Hindi-language communication would use Hindi under Rule 5 regardless of region. This shows how sender, recipient and document type must each be checked before applying a single regional rule mechanically. Its limit: the Rules' territorial exception for Tamil Nadu does not mean Tamil Nadu is outside section 3's communication safeguards altogether — it continues to receive English communication from the Union under section 3(1)'s own proviso, independent of whether the 1976 Rules extend there.

### Comparison: administrative category, not constitutional rank

Regions A, B and C classify *how Union offices communicate*, nothing more. They do not rank States, languages or cultures, and they do not touch Article 348's separate, constitutional treatment of courts and authoritative legislative texts — a boundary this lesson's next one develops in full.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If the Rules exclude Tamil Nadu entirely, doesn't that create an unequal administrative relationship compared to States inside Regions A, B or C?"

**Reply:** The exclusion concerns only the 1976 Rules' own territorial extent for organising Union-office communication patterns; it does not touch Tamil Nadu's own Article 345 choice of official language, nor the Official Languages Act's section 3 safeguards, both of which continue to apply to communication with Tamil Nadu exactly as with any other State.

**Qualified residual:** A genuine administrative asymmetry remains — Tamil Nadu's exclusion reflects its historically distinct position in the language debate (Lesson 2) — but asymmetry in one administrative instrument does not amount to exclusion from the underlying constitutional and statutory safeguards that protect every State alike.

### UPSC application

This lesson's four-route table is a reliable source of "which provision authorises this translation" Prelims questions, and the Regions A/B/C framework recurs as a Prelims-style elimination device distinguishing administrative categorisation from constitutional ranking.

### Revision notes

1. Four authoritative-text routes: Article 348(1) (English baseline), Act section 5 (Hindi, Central), Act section 6 (Hindi, specified State Acts), and the Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973 (other Eighth-Schedule languages, Central laws).
2. Section 5 requires presidential authority and Gazette publication for authoritative Hindi Central texts.
3. Section 6 supplements, rather than replaces, Article 348(3)'s English-translation requirement for specified State Acts.
4. The 1973 Act is a distinct statute governing non-Hindi, non-English Eighth-Schedule-language texts of Central laws.
5. Section 8 is the Act's rule-making power; a former Jammu and Kashmir-specific section 9 is now omitted from the consolidated text.
6. The 1976 Rules' official title: Official Languages (Use for Official Purposes of the Union) Rules, 1976, amended through 1987, 2007 and 2011.

### Concept check

**Question:** A privately produced Hindi translation of a Central Act, widely circulated online, is linguistically accurate. Is it an authoritative text for legal purposes?

**Model answer:** No. Authoritative status under section 5 of the Official Languages Act requires a Hindi translation specifically authorised under presidential authority and published in the Official Gazette. A privately produced translation, however accurate, has not passed through this stamp-of-authority process and therefore carries no authoritative legal status, whatever its linguistic quality.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating translation accuracy or fluency with legal authoritativeness, rather than checking whether the specific constitutional or statutory authorisation-and-publication route was actually used.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish the legal route for an authoritative Hindi translation of a Central law from the route for an authoritative translation of a Central law into an Eighth-Schedule language other than Hindi.

**Model solution:** An authoritative Hindi translation of a Central Act, presidential ordinance or delegated instrument proceeds under section 5 of the Official Languages Act, 1963: a translation authorised under presidential authority and published in the Official Gazette is deemed the authoritative Hindi text, and Hindi translations must also accompany the authoritative English text of parliamentary Bills and amendments. An authoritative translation of the same Central law into any other Eighth-Schedule language instead proceeds under the distinct Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973, which similarly requires presidential authority and Gazette publication but applies specifically to non-Hindi scheduled languages. Both routes share the authority-plus-Gazette structure, but they rest on two separate statutes and must not be cited interchangeably.

### The learner's problem

Can a High Court actually conduct a case, and write its judgment, in Hindi? The honest answer splits into two different questions with two different answers — and conflating them is this lesson's central trap.

### Visual: Article 348's scope

```text
ARTICLE 348(1)
   |
   +--> SC proceedings -> English
   +--> every HC proceeding -> English
   |
   +--> authoritative texts:
          Bills and amendments
          Acts
          Ordinances
          orders / rules / regulations / bye-laws
```

*Caption: Article 348 governs both adjudicative proceedings and the authority of legislative and delegated texts — it is wider than "courtroom language" alone.*

### Plain-language intuition

Split the High Court's work into two moments: the hearing itself — arguments, submissions, oral exchange — and the final written judgment that becomes precedent and gets appealed. The Constitution treats these two moments differently. It opens a door for the hearing to happen partly in Hindi or a State's official language; it keeps that door firmly shut for the judgment itself, because a judgment needs to travel — to the Supreme Court on appeal, to lawyers across India researching precedent — in a form the whole legal system can read.

### Technical doctrine: proceedings versus judgments

Article 348(1)(a) makes English the language of all Supreme Court and every High Court proceedings. Article 348(1)(b) makes English the authoritative text of parliamentary and State Bills/amendments, Acts, ordinances, and orders, rules, regulations and bye-laws made under the Constitution or any law — until Parliament by law otherwise provides.

Article 348(2) creates a narrow exception: a Governor, with the **previous consent of the President**, may authorise Hindi or another language used for State official purposes in High Court proceedings at that High Court's principal seat. Critically, the constitutional proviso **expressly excludes judgments, decrees and orders** from this authorisation — the hearing may shift language; the written judgment, under Article 348(2) alone, may not.

**Section 7** of the Official Languages Act fills exactly this gap. With previous presidential consent, a Governor may authorise Hindi or the State's official language, **in addition to English**, for a High Court judgment, decree or order — but any such non-English output must be accompanied by an **English translation issued under the High Court's own authority**.

| Question | Article 348(2) | Act, Section 7 |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | High Court proceedings | Judgments, decrees and orders |
| Who authorises | Governor | Governor |
| Consent required | Previous consent of the President | Previous consent of the President |
| Permitted language | Hindi/other State official language | Hindi/State official language, in addition to English |
| English safeguard | Constitutional judgment exception (English remains the judgment language unless section 7 is used) | Mandatory High-Court-authorised English translation |

```text
GOVERNOR PROPOSES LANGUAGE AUTHORISATION
                |
                v
PREVIOUS PRESIDENTIAL CONSENT
                |
                v
HINDI / OTHER STATE OFFICIAL LANGUAGE
     permitted in HC PROCEEDINGS (Art 348(2))
                |
                X
     Art 348(2) ALONE does not reach
     judgments, decrees or orders
                |
                v
SECTION 7 (separate statutory route):
     non-English judgment/decree/order permitted
     IF accompanied by an English translation
     issued under the High Court's authority
```

*Caption: two consent gates, two different outputs — a proceeding can switch language under the Constitution alone; a judgment needs the statute's extra safeguard.*

### Example and its limit

Picture a High Court where, following valid Article 348(2) authorisation, counsel argue a case in Hindi. The bench may then write its judgment in Hindi too — but only if section 7's separate authorisation has also been obtained, and only if an English translation issued under the High Court's own authority accompanies that judgment. This shows both routes working together. The limit: if only Article 348(2) authorisation exists and section 7 has not separately been invoked, the judgment itself must still be in English, regardless of what language the hearing used — proceedings and judgments are genuinely independent permissions, not one flowing automatically from the other.

### Comparison: authoritative-text safeguards beyond the courtroom

Article 348(3) separately addresses a State that prescribes a non-English language for its own Bills, Acts, ordinances or delegated texts: an English translation published under the Governor's authority in the State Gazette is deemed the authoritative English text. This operates alongside, not instead of, sections 5-6 of the Official Languages Act (Lesson 8) and the Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973 — together forming the complete authoritative-text architecture this topic tests.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Doesn't requiring an English translation for every non-English High Court judgment defeat the entire purpose of expanding language access in the first place?"

**Reply:** The translation requirement preserves two things that genuinely matter beyond the litigant directly before the court: nationwide precedential usability, since lawyers and lower courts across India must be able to read and cite the judgment, and the Supreme Court's own appellate review, which still operates in English. Access at the point of hearing and uniformity at the point of precedent are different goals, and section 7 is designed to deliver both rather than sacrificing one for the other.

**Qualified residual:** This is a genuine, unresolved tension, not a fully solved problem — litigants who do not read English still depend on the quality and availability of that accompanying translation for full comprehension of their own judgment, an access gap Lesson 16's reform discussion returns to directly.

### UPSC application

This is one of the most precisely tested provisions in the topic: Prelims questions frequently test whether a candidate knows that Article 348(2) excludes judgments, and Mains questions ask candidates to "analyse how multilingual access to justice can expand without fragmenting authoritative legal communication" — exactly this lesson's proceedings-versus-judgments architecture.

### Revision notes

1. Article 348(1)(a): English for all Supreme Court and every High Court proceedings.
2. Article 348(1)(b): English as the authoritative text of Bills, Acts, ordinances, orders, rules, regulations and bye-laws.
3. Article 348(2): Governor, with previous presidential consent, may authorise Hindi/State official language in High Court proceedings at the principal seat.
4. Article 348(2)'s proviso expressly excludes judgments, decrees and orders.
5. Section 7 of the Official Languages Act separately permits non-English HC judgments/decrees/orders, with previous presidential consent, if accompanied by a High-Court-authorised English translation.
6. Proceedings and judgments require genuinely independent permissions — one does not automatically follow from the other.
7. Article 348(3): a Governor-authorised Gazette English translation of a State's non-English legislative text is deemed authoritative.
8. Sections 5-6 of the Act and the 1973 Authoritative Texts Act operate alongside Article 348, not as substitutes for it.
9. English in higher courts supports nationwide precedent and Supreme Court appellate review; it can also be an access barrier for non-English-speaking litigants.
10. "Article 348(2) alone authorises non-English HC judgments" is false — it covers proceedings only; section 7 is the separate, judgment-specific route.

### Concept check

**Question:** A High Court has valid Article 348(2) authorisation for Hindi proceedings but has not separately invoked section 7 of the Official Languages Act. In what language must its judgment be delivered?

**Model answer:** English. Article 348(2)'s authorisation covers proceedings only and expressly excludes judgments, decrees and orders. Without a separate, validly obtained section 7 authorisation — Governor plus previous presidential consent, with an accompanying English translation issued under the High Court's authority — the judgment itself remains governed by Article 348(1)(a)'s English baseline, regardless of the language used during the hearing.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that Article 348(2) authorisation for proceedings automatically extends to the judgment that concludes those proceedings.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "Multilingual access to justice must be expanded without fragmenting authoritative legal communication." Analyse this statement with reference to Article 348 and the Official Languages Act.

**Model solution:** Article 348 creates an English baseline for Supreme Court and High Court proceedings and for the authoritative texts of specified legislative instruments, preserving a single, nationwide legal language for precedent and appellate review; the Official Languages Act then adds controlled, consent-based routes to expand access without displacing that baseline. Article 348(2) lets a Governor, with previous presidential consent, authorise Hindi or a State's official language for High Court proceedings — expanding access at the hearing stage — but its proviso expressly excludes judgments, decrees and orders, preserving uniformity for the written precedent. Section 7 then separately permits those excluded outputs in an authorised language, but only if accompanied by a High-Court-authorised English translation, so that wider courts and lawyers can still read the judgment. Sections 5, 6 and the Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973 extend the same authority-plus-Gazette logic to legislative and delegated texts in Hindi and other Eighth-Schedule languages. The design therefore separates two genuinely different goals — litigant-facing access and system-wide authoritative uniformity — and answers each with its own specific, consent-gated mechanism, rather than collapsing them into a single rule that would either deny access or fragment the legal system's shared precedent base.

### The learner's problem

Not every safeguard printed in the Constitution is still operating today. Article 349 is this topic's clearest example of a provision that remains in the text while its functional life has already ended — and UPSC specifically tests whether candidates notice the difference.

### Visual: the time box

```text
1950 ------------------------------ 1965
     ARTICLE 349 SPECIAL PROCEDURE OPERATIVE

During this window, a Bill or amendment on Article 348(1)
language purposes required:
   previous Presidential sanction
   consideration of Article 344 Commission/committee material

AFTER 1965:
   do not treat Article 349 as a routine current precondition
```

*Caption: a provision can remain printed in the Constitution long after its own stated period has run out.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of Article 349 as a temporary extra signature requirement attached to one specific kind of paperwork, valid only for the Republic's first fifteen years. Once that window closed, the requirement itself lapsed by its own terms — the paperwork clause is still there to read, but nobody needs the extra signature for it anymore.

### Technical doctrine

Article 349 applied during the first fifteen years from constitutional commencement. During that period, a Bill or amendment concerning the language used for Article 348(1) purposes required the President's previous sanction, and the President was required to consider the Article 344 Commission's recommendations and the parliamentary committee's report before giving that sanction. The fifteen-year period ended in 1965, exactly when Article 343(2)'s own transition ended — the two provisions share the same expiry clock because both protected the same transition-era settlement from unilateral alteration.

### Example and its limit

If Parliament in, say, 1960 had wanted to legislate on the language used for Supreme Court proceedings, Article 349 would have required the President's prior sanction, informed by Article 344 material, before that Bill could even be introduced in that form. The same hypothetical Bill introduced in 1970 would face no such Article 349 requirement — Parliament's ordinary legislative process would apply instead, informed by whatever the Official Languages Act and its own amendment procedures permit. The limit of this example: it shows Article 349's historical operation cleanly, but it should not be read as implying the underlying subject matter — courts' language — became unregulated after 1965; that subject remains fully governed by Article 348 and section 7 (Lesson 9), just without Article 349's extra procedural gate.

### Comparison: two expired safeguards, two different genera

Article 343(2) and Article 349 both expired in 1965, but they protected different things in different ways. Article 343(2) was a substantive transition rule about English's actual use. Article 349 was a procedural safeguard about *how* certain language-related Bills could be introduced during that same transition. Both are now historical; treating either as a live, current constraint is the same category of error, applied to two different provisions.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If Article 349 has been functionally dead since 1965, why does UPSC keep testing it at all?"

**Reply:** Precisely because candidates routinely and wrongly treat it as a current procedural gate for any language-related legislation — testing it rewards candidates who can correctly place a constitutional provision on a historical timeline rather than assuming everything printed in Part XVII is equally operative today.

**Qualified residual:** The provision's continued presence in the printed Constitution is not decorative error; it preserves the historical record of how the transition was safeguarded, which remains relevant for understanding why the 1967 Amendment and section 3(5) (Lesson 7) were designed the way they were.

### UPSC application

Expect Article 349 to appear as a deliberately placed false option in Prelims statement questions, testing whether a candidate assumes every Part XVII provision is a routine current rule. In Mains, correctly noting that a provision is historically bounded — rather than silently ignoring it or wrongly treating it as current — demonstrates exactly the kind of precision examiners reward.

1. Article 349 applied only during the first fifteen years from constitutional commencement (1950-1965).
2. During that window, Bills or amendments on Article 348(1) language purposes needed previous presidential sanction.
3. The President had to consider Article 344 Commission/committee material before giving that sanction.
4. The fifteen-year period ended in 1965, aligning with Article 343(2)'s own expiry.
5. Article 349 remains printed in the Constitution but is not a routine current precondition for language-related Bills today.
6. Article 343(2) and Article 349 are two distinct provisions that happened to share the same 1965 expiry — one substantive, one procedural.
7. Courts' language today is governed by Article 348 and section 7, independent of Article 349's now-expired extra gate.
8. UPSC tests this provision specifically to catch candidates who assume every printed provision is currently operative.

**Question:** In 2024, could a Bill altering the language used for Supreme Court proceedings be blocked for want of "previous presidential sanction under Article 349"?

A 2024 Bill on this subject would proceed through the ordinary legislative process and would be governed substantively by Article 348 and the Official Languages Act, not by Article 349, which has no current operative application.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Article 349 as a perpetual current precondition for any language-related legislation, rather than recognising it as a historically bounded safeguard that expired in 1965.

### The learner's problem

If a citizen does not speak the official language used around them, where does the Constitution actually protect them? This lesson maps three distinct answers — an access right, an education endeavour, and an oversight office — and warns against treating any one of them as doing more work than its exact text supports.

### Visual: three safeguards, three different jobs

```text
ARTICLE 350  -> grievance representation in any language
                used in the Union/State, as applicable
ARTICLE 350A -> primary-stage mother-tongue instruction
                facilities: an ENDEAVOUR, not an absolute right
ARTICLE 350B -> Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities:
                investigates and reports, does not adjudicate
```

*Caption: entry-point access, an education endeavour, and an accountability office — none of them is the others.*

### Plain-language intuition

Article 350 is a door: it lets you knock on government's door in your own language when you have a grievance. Article 350A is a promise to try: States and local authorities must *endeavour* to give linguistic-minority children adequate primary-stage instruction in their mother tongue, not guarantee it unconditionally. Article 350B is a watchman: an officer who checks whether the house's other safeguards are actually being honoured, and tells the President and Parliament what they find — without the power to fix a violation directly.

### Technical doctrine

**Article 350** entitles every person to submit a representation for redress of grievances to any Union or State officer or authority, in any language used in the Union or the State, as the case may be. This protects the language of *entry* into the grievance process; it does not necessarily require every later stage or the final order to use the representor's chosen language.

**Article 350A**, added by the Seventh Amendment, 1956, makes it "the endeavour of every State and of every local authority within the State" to provide adequate facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage to children belonging to linguistic-minority groups; the President may issue directions considered necessary or proper to secure such facilities.

**Article 350B**, also added by the Seventh Amendment, 1956, creates a Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities, appointed by the President, to investigate all matters relating to constitutional safeguards for linguistic minorities and to report to the President at such intervals as the President directs. The President causes these reports to be laid before each House of Parliament and sent to the concerned State Governments. The office's headquarters moved from Allahabad to New Delhi on 1 June 2015, and the Ministry of Minority Affairs today identifies the office as the Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities, confirming that linguistic-minority character is contextual — a language need not be in the Eighth Schedule to qualify a group as a linguistic minority for this purpose.

|---|---|
| Duty bearer | Every State and every local authority within it |
| Beneficiary | Children belonging to linguistic-minority groups |
| Stage | Primary education only |
| Object | Adequate mother-tongue instruction facilities |
| Operative verb | "Shall be the endeavour" |
| Union oversight | President may issue directions |

### Example and its limit

A State that has made genuine, demonstrable efforts — recruiting teachers, producing materials — to provide primary-stage mother-tongue instruction for a linguistic-minority community, but has not achieved full coverage in every school, is not automatically in constitutional default: Article 350A's text asks for an *endeavour*, not a guaranteed outcome. The limit of this example: "endeavour" is not a licence for indifference either — the President's power to issue directions exists precisely to press a State whose efforts are genuinely inadequate, and Lesson 12's case law shows courts have refused to let this qualified language become a pretext for either compelling or denying instruction arbitrarily.

### Comparison: adjacent but distinct cultural and educational protections

Articles 29(1) and 30, outside Part XVII, sit close to this safeguard cluster without being identical to it. Article 29(1) protects a section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture in their right to conserve it; Article 30 protects religious or linguistic minorities in establishing and administering their own educational institutions. Article 350A concerns a *State's own endeavour* in its own schools; Articles 29-30 concern a minority's own *right* to conserve its culture and to run its own institutions. Different beneficiaries, different duty-bearers, different consequences — do not merge them merely because all four provisions touch language and education.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "Since the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities can only investigate and report, isn't the office effectively powerless against a State that ignores linguistic-minority safeguards?"

**Reply:** The office's power is accountability through transparency, not adjudication: its reports are laid before both Houses of Parliament and sent to the concerned State Governments, creating sustained political visibility and pressure that a silent, unreported violation would never generate. This is a genuinely different, and genuinely weaker, form of enforcement than a court order — but it is not nothing.

**Qualified residual:** The limitation is real: without binding adjudicatory power, the office depends on political will translating its findings into action. This is precisely the kind of qualified, non-absolute safeguard this whole lesson's three provisions share — access, endeavour and oversight, none of them an unconditional guarantee.

### UPSC application

Prelims traps frequently inflate Article 350A into an unconditional Fundamental Right or inflate Article 350B's officer into an adjudicatory authority.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State has made partial but genuine efforts toward primary-stage mother-tongue instruction for a linguistic-minority community, without achieving full coverage. Has it violated Article 350A?

### The learner's problem

The provisions taught so far describe what governments and legislatures *may* do with language. This lesson asks what courts have stopped them from doing — because three Supreme Court judgments, spanning five decades, each drew a different limit on how far language policy can reach into education without crossing into compulsion.

### Plain-language intuition

Across fifty years, courts kept answering the same underlying question from three different angles: can an authority *force* a particular language of instruction onto an institution or a family that does not want it? Each time, for a different legal reason — Union legislative competence, minority educational rights, parental choice — the answer has been no, even while each case left the authority's power to *promote* its preferred language, in its own institutions, largely standing.

### Technical doctrine: three cases, three distinct holdings

**Gujarat University v. Krishna Ranganath Mudholkar (AIR 1963 SC 703)** concerned a university's attempt to make Gujarati or Hindi the exclusive medium of instruction and examination. The Supreme Court held that higher-education standards, including medium of instruction at the university level, fall substantially within the Union's legislative competence under Entry 66, List I (Seventh Schedule) once Parliament has legislated in that field; a State university's exclusive-medium rule could not override that Union competence. The case clarifies that Article 345's State-language power does not automatically extend to overriding Union authority over university-level education standards.

**D.A.V. College, Bhatinda v. State of Punjab (AIR 1971 SC 1731)** concerned the Guru Nanak University Act's compulsory affiliation of Arya Samaj-managed D.A.V. colleges and its promotion of Punjabi as the medium of instruction. The Supreme Court held that Arya Samajis constituted a religious minority in Punjab for Article 30(1) purposes, and that compelling such a minority institution to adopt one prescribed medium infringed its Article 29(1) right to conserve its own language and script and its Article 30(1) right to administer its own educational institution. State promotion of a regional language cannot be imposed as compulsion on a genuine minority institution's own choice.

**State of Karnataka v. Associated Management of (Government Recognised Unaided English Medium) Primary and Secondary Schools (2014)** concerned Karnataka's government orders compelling mother-tongue, or Kannada, medium of instruction in classes 1 to 4 across recognised schools. The Supreme Court held that the choice of medium of instruction at the primary stage belongs to the child and the child's parents, protected under Article 19(1)(a), and that the State cannot compel this choice in unaided schools; it further clarified that Article 350A obliges the State only to provide adequate mother-tongue facilities — it does not empower the State to compel mother-tongue instruction across the board.

### Example and its limit

Read together, these cases show the limit tightening in scope even as the underlying principle stays the same: Gujarat University addressed federal legislative competence over one sector (higher education); D.A.V. College addressed a specific minority's constitutional rights; Associated Management addressed the general parental/child choice for any unaided school, minority or not. The limit of treating them as one undifferentiated line: each case rests on its own distinct provision — Entry 66 competence, Articles 29/30, and Article 19(1)(a) respectively — and citing the wrong one for the wrong fact pattern is a precise, commonly tested error.

### Comparison: what remains within the State's power

None of these cases abolishes a State's power to promote its own official language through its own government and aided institutions, or through curriculum design generally; the Karnataka High Court itself preserved the compulsion for government and aided schools even while striking it down for unaided ones. What is barred is compulsion specifically against unaided institutions' and parents' choice (Associated Management), against a recognised minority's conservation and administration rights (D.A.V. College), and against Union competence over higher-education standards (Gujarat University) — a bounded, not an absolute, limitation on State language promotion.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If courts keep striking down medium-of-instruction mandates, does the State retain any meaningful power to promote its official language in education at all?"

**Reply:** Yes — government and aided institutions, and curriculum policy generally, remain within State reach; what each case specifically forecloses is *compulsion* against a particular protected choice-holder: Union competence in one case, a minority institution in another, and parents/the child in the third. The power to promote is intact; the power to compel against these specific holders is not.

**Qualified residual:** The three holdings leave open genuinely contested terrain — how far a State may go in incentivising (short of compelling) a preferred medium even in unaided schools, and how Article 350A's "endeavour" interacts with a State's own promotional preferences — a residual tension this session's Lesson 16 situates within the wider federal debate.

### Technical doctrine

Article 351 makes it the Union's duty to promote the spread of Hindi and to develop it as a medium of expression for all elements of India's composite culture. Enrichment is to proceed by assimilating, without interfering with Hindi's genius, the forms, style and expressions used in Hindustani and in the other languages of the Eighth Schedule; for vocabulary, the Union is directed to draw primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on other languages, wherever necessary or desirable.

Crucially, Article 351 is a **directive within Part XVII**, not a Fundamental Right and not an Article 37 Directive Principle in Part IV — it guides Union policy without being independently enforceable in court, and it sits alongside, not above, Article 345's State-choice power, Articles 350A-350B's minority safeguards, and the Official Languages Act's own statutory protections.

### The learner's problem

Two further categories keep getting mistaken for the Eighth Schedule: "classical language" status and the "three-language formula." Neither is a constitutional category at all — one is an executive honour, the other is education policy — and this lesson draws both boundaries precisely.

### Visual: two frameworks answering different questions

### Plain-language intuition

Classical-language status is closer to a heritage award than to a legal classification — it recognises a language's antiquity and independent literary tradition, carrying prestige, academic chairs and funding, but no constitutional standing whatsoever. The three-language formula, meanwhile, is a long-standing education-policy recommendation about how many languages a school curriculum should cover, not a rule derived from Article 343 or any other provision in Part XVII. Both sit entirely outside this topic's constitutional core, even though both are routinely dragged into "official language" conversations.

### Technical doctrine: the classical-language scheme

Classical-language recognition is a Union executive scheme, administered by the Ministry of Culture on the advice of a Linguistic Experts Committee, resting on criteria such as high antiquity of recorded texts spanning roughly 1500-2000 years, a body of ancient literature regarded as a valuable heritage, an original literary tradition not borrowed from another speech community, and a classical form distinct from the language's later, modern form. It confers prestige, academic chairs and funding — not Eighth-Schedule status, not Union-official status, and no automatic administrative or judicial standing. Following October 2024 additions (Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali), the officially reported total stood at eleven, against an earlier six (Tamil, 2004; Sanskrit, 2005; Kannada and Telugu, 2008; Malayalam, 2013; Odia, 2014).

| Test | Scheduled language | Classical language |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Constitution, Eighth Schedule | Executive recognition scheme |
| Change route | Constitutional amendment | Government decision under the scheme |
| Core exam link | Articles 344 and 351 | Antiquity and literary-heritage criteria |
| Administrative effect | No universal official-language status | No automatic administrative status |

### Technical doctrine: the three-language formula

The three-language formula belongs to education policy, historically traced to the Kothari Commission and the National Policy on Education, 1968, and reaffirmed by the National Education Policy, 2020. Because education sits in the Concurrent List, States retain real implementation discretion, which is precisely where the formula's recurring federal controversy arises: proponents frame it as supporting multilingual competence and national integration, while Tamil Nadu, following its own longstanding two-language policy, has read the formula as a vehicle for "Hindi imposition" and an intrusion on State educational autonomy.

### Example and its limit

A State implementing the three-language formula by offering its own regional language, English, and a third language of the student's choice (which need not be Hindi) shows one legitimate way of honouring the formula's multilingual spirit without mandating any particular third language. The limit of this example: it does not resolve the genuine federal disagreement over whether, in practice, the formula's third-language slot tends to default to Hindi in many States' implementation — that empirical and political question is a live policy debate, not a question this constitutional topic can settle from the text alone.

### Comparison: why these categories must stay separate from Part XVII

Neither category changes anything taught in Lessons 1-14. Classical-language status does not add a language to the Eighth Schedule, does not make it a Union or State official language, and does not alter Article 348's court-language regime. The three-language formula does not derive from, and cannot override, Article 345's State-choice power or any other Part XVII provision — it operates entirely within education policy's own, separate legal and political space.

### Objection, reply and qualified residual

**Objection:** "If classical-language status confers no constitutional or administrative power, why does the government keep expanding the list — isn't this just symbolic politics?"

**Reply:** Symbolic recognition is not necessarily empty: prestige, dedicated academic chairs and funding for a language's study and preservation are real, tangible outcomes even without constitutional status, and expanding the list can genuinely support language scholarship and cultural visibility for communities whose languages might otherwise receive less institutional attention.

**Qualified residual:** The objection is nonetheless right to flag a risk: because the scheme carries no constitutional weight and operates by executive discretion, its expansion pattern can track political considerations as easily as objective antiquity criteria, a risk this lesson's trap register below exists specifically to guard against conflating with genuine constitutional change.

The hardest reform question in this topic is not whether to expand multilingual access — nearly every legitimate voice agrees it should expand — but how to do so without quietly eroding the authoritative-text discipline that sections 5-7 of the Act, the 1973 Act, and Article 348(3) were built to protect. Fast, fluent, unauthorised translation and slow, authorised, Gazette-published translation serve two different needs; good reform expands the former without weakening the latter.

This session's single most transferable paragraph-level habit, introduced in Lesson 16 and used implicitly throughout, is: state a **claim**, support it with **named evidence** (an exact Article, section or case), **analyse** what that evidence actually establishes, and add a **qualification** that prevents overclaiming. A worked example: claim — "Article 348(2) expanded High Court language access"; named evidence — the Governor-plus-previous-presidential-consent authorisation for proceedings; analysis — this lets a hearing proceed in Hindi or a State's official language at the principal seat; qualification — the same clause expressly excludes judgments, decrees and orders, which require the separate section 7 route with an English translation.

```text
CLAIM: a specific, checkable proposition about this topic
   -> NAMED EVIDENCE: the exact Article/section/case
   -> ANALYSIS: what that evidence actually proves
   -> QUALIFICATION: its limit, so the claim is not overstated
```

*Caption: four moves, repeated once per paragraph, turn a list of facts into an examiner-grade answer.*

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks test connections across multiple lessons rather than a single lesson's content. Attempt each before reading the model answer.

### Cross-lesson check 1 (Lessons 1, 14)

**Question:** Does a language's inclusion in the Eighth Schedule make it an "official language of India"?

**Model answer:** No. Article 343(1) makes Hindi the official language of the Union specifically; no Eighth-Schedule provision confers equivalent Union-official or State-official status on any listed language. The Schedule is textually linked only to Article 344(1) (Commission representation) and Article 351 (Hindi's enrichment resource) — categories entirely distinct from "official language" as Lesson 1 defines it. A language can be scheduled without being official anywhere, and Hindi is official without every scheduled language sharing that status.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Eighth-Schedule membership as conferring a general "official language of India" status, rather than recognising the Schedule's narrow, textually specific function.

### Cross-lesson check 2 (Lessons 3, 7)

**Question:** Explain why Article 343(3)'s enabling power, taught as a bare clause in Lesson 3, cannot be fully understood without Lesson 7's section 3(5) double lock.

**Model answer:** Article 343(3) merely empowers Parliament to legislate for continued English use after the fifteen-year transition; read alone, it says nothing about how secure or revocable that continued use would be. Section 3(5) is the actual content Parliament chose when exercising that power — converting a bare enabling clause into a durable, federally consensual settlement requiring every non-Hindi-official-language State's legislature and then both Houses of Parliament to act before specified provisions can lapse. Without Lesson 7, Lesson 3's clause ladder would wrongly suggest English's post-1965 position is only as secure as an ordinary, easily amendable statute.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Article 343(3) as self-sufficient evidence of English's practical durability, without checking what Parliament actually legislated under that power.

### Cross-lesson check 3 (Lessons 9, 12)

**Question:** How does Lesson 12's judicial doctrine on compelled medium of instruction relate to, but remain distinct from, Lesson 9's Article 348 court-language regime?

**Model answer:** Both lessons concern limits on how far language policy can reach, but into different domains. Lesson 9's Article 348 regime governs the language of judicial proceedings and authoritative legal texts themselves — a constitutional and statutory question about courts' own operation. Lesson 12's cases (Gujarat University, D.A.V. College, Associated Management) instead concern education — whether a university, State, or government order can compel a particular medium of instruction on an institution, a minority, or a family. The two domains share a conceptual theme — language choice versus compulsion — but rest on entirely different provisions (Article 348/section 7 for courts; Entry 66/Articles 29-30/Article 19(1)(a) for education) and must not be cited interchangeably.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming a single "language access" principle governs both courts and education identically, rather than tracing each domain to its own specific constitutional provisions.

### Cross-lesson check 4 (Lessons 5, 16)

**Question:** The Committee of Parliament on Official Language (Lesson 5) and the reform pillars discussed in Lesson 16 both aim at improving official-language implementation. Do they operate through the same mechanism?

**Model answer:** No. The Committee of Parliament on Official Language is a specific statutory body under section 4 of the 1963 Act, reviewing progress of Hindi's use for Union official purposes and reporting to the President, with its influence bounded by section 4(4)'s proviso that any resulting direction cannot be inconsistent with section 3. Lesson 16's reform pillars are a broader, non-institution-specific evaluative framework — multilingual access, translation capacity, legal-text parity, court access, minority education, federal consultation, and fair civil-service implementation — each paired with its own guardrail. The committee is one existing institutional mechanism; the reform pillars are an analytical lens for evaluating and improving the entire system, including but not limited to that committee's work.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the Committee of Parliament on Official Language as the complete embodiment of "reform," rather than as one existing institution operating within the larger evaluative framework Lesson 16 develops.

### Synthesis Mains — 10 marks, strict ceiling 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the legal consequence of a language's Eighth-Schedule membership from the legal consequence of a State's own choice of official language under Article 345.

**Model solution:** Eighth-Schedule membership is a constitutional listing, changeable only through an Article 368 amendment, textually linked to Article 344(1)'s Commission-representation function and Article 351's Hindi-enrichment resource — it confers no automatic official-language status anywhere. A State's choice under Article 345 is an ordinary legislative act, requiring no Eighth-Schedule membership at all, and determines which language or languages that specific State uses for its own official purposes, subject to Articles 346-347's communication and recognition safeguards. The two operate on entirely different legal planes: one is a constitutional honour roll serving two specific textual functions; the other is a State's genuine administrative power to govern in a language of its own choosing, scheduled or not.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Eighth-Schedule consequence correctly explained — 4; Article 345 consequence correctly explained — 4; explicit statement that the two are independent — 1; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 15 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "Each Part XVII safeguard for linguistic minorities is qualified rather than absolute, yet together they form a meaningful protective architecture." Examine this statement.

**Model solution:** Individually, every minority-facing safeguard in Part XVII carries a real qualification. Article 350 protects only the language of *entry* into a grievance-redress process, not every later stage. Article 350A obliges States and local authorities only to make an "endeavour" toward adequate primary-stage mother-tongue facilities, not to guarantee universal coverage — a qualification the Supreme Court in State of Karnataka v. Associated Management (2014) confirmed cannot be converted into a compulsion power over unaided schools. Article 350B's Special Officer can only investigate and report, not adjudicate or enforce. Article 347's recognition mechanism depends on undefined presidential satisfaction of a "substantial proportion," with no automatic entitlement. Yet together, these qualified instruments form a genuine protective layer: Article 350 ensures no citizen is shut out of administration by language at first contact; Article 350A creates sustained pressure, backed by presidential direction power, toward educational access; Article 350B converts silent neglect into a matter of recorded parliamentary and State-government attention; and Article 347 offers a constitutional channel, however discretionary, for claims a State's own Article 345 choice might otherwise ignore. The architecture's strength lies precisely in this layering — no single provision guarantees an outcome, but a minority facing one qualified safeguard's limit typically finds a second, differently qualified safeguard addressing the gap from another angle.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Each of the four safeguards' qualification correctly stated — 6 (1.5 each); the Associated Management reinforcement correctly cited — 2; coherent explanation of how the safeguards complement each other's gaps — 5; 250-word structure with a reasoned conclusion — 2.

### Synthesis Mains — 20 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "India's official-language scheme survives not because any single provision is airtight, but because its component safeguards interlock." Critically evaluate this statement with reference to this session's complete coverage.

**Model solution:** The statement holds. Article 343's official-not-national distinction would be an empty symbolic gesture without Article 343(3)'s enabling power and the Official Languages Act's section 3(5) double lock, which together converted a time-bound transition into a durable, federally consensual settlement no single State or House can unilaterally unwind. Article 345's State-choice freedom would risk administrative fragmentation without Article 346's communication baseline and section 3's provisos ensuring the Union and other States can still communicate reliably. Article 348's court-language English baseline would become an inflexible access barrier without section 7's judgment-translation route, while both would risk uncontrolled compulsion without the judicial boundary Gujarat University, D.A.V. College and Associated Management draw around any authority's promotional power. Article 351's Hindi-development directive would risk tilting toward imposition without the minority safeguards of Articles 350, 350A and 350B and without Article 345's surviving State autonomy constraining it. Even the Eighth Schedule's 2026 inclusion debate illustrates the same interlocking logic in reverse: the absence of settled criteria, confirmed by the government's own 2026 reply, shows what happens when one component — a transparent inclusion process — remains underdeveloped, leaving genuine linguistic claims without a clear path despite the rest of the architecture functioning as designed. No single article is self-sufficient; the scheme's durability is a property of the whole interlocking system.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### 1. Official, not national — the foundational distinction (Lesson 1)

- Article 343(1): Hindi in Devanagari script is the official language of the Union; the international form of Indian numerals applies to Union official purposes.
- India has no constitutionally declared national language.
- Six distinct categories recur across this topic: official, national (undeclared), scheduled, classical, court language, medium of instruction.
- Demographic or administrative dominance is not legal evidence of a "national language" declaration.

### 2. The Constituent Assembly compromise and political evolution (Lesson 2)

- The Munshi-Ayyangar compromise (1949): Hindi-Devanagari official status, international numerals, 15-year English transition, Parliament's later Article 343(3) power — no national-language declaration.
- Timeline: 1950 commencement; 1955 Kher Commission; 1957 Pant committee; 1963 Act; 26 Jan 1965 transition expiry; 1965 agitation; 1967 Amendment/1968 effect; 1976 Rules.
- The 1965 agitation, concentrated in Tamil Nadu, directly produced the 1967 Amendment's discontinuance lock.
- Neither Hindi-promotion nor non-Hindi resistance should be treated as uniformly secessionist or anti-national.

### 3. Article 343 — Union language and the English transition (Lesson 3)

- Clause ladder: 343(1) baseline; 343(2) time-boxed transition and narrow presidential power; 343(3) Parliament's standing legislative power.
- The 1965 myth corrected: only the transition expired; English did not become unlawful.
- Post-1965 English rests on Parliament's legislation (the 1963 Act), a genuine shift in the source of its legal security.

### 4. Article 344 — the Commission and the constitutional committee (Lesson 4)

- Commission constituted at five and ten years from commencement — historical milestones, not a recurring decennial cycle.
- First Commission: B.G. Kher (1955); examined by the G.B. Pant committee (1957).
- Article 344(3)'s balancing duty: industrial/cultural/scientific advancement plus non-Hindi-speaking interests in public services.
- Article 344(4): 30-member committee (20 LS + 10 RS, PR-STV) examining Commission recommendations; Article 344(6) possible presidential directions.

### 5. Two thirty-member committees (Lesson 5)

- Article 344(4) committee: constitutional, tied to the Commission's recommendations.
- Section 4 committee: statutory, reviewing Hindi's progress for Union official purposes; today known as the Committee of Parliament on Official Language.
- Section 4(4)'s proviso: directions cannot be inconsistent with section 3.
- 2026 reinforcing note: the 6th All India Official Language Conference/Hindi Diwas (14-15 September 2026) reported over 20,000 central offices inspected, consistent with the committee's thirteenth report — administrative reinforcement, not legal change.

### 6. Articles 345-347 — State and regional languages (Lesson 6)

- Article 345: State Legislature may adopt any language(s) in use in the State, or Hindi, without Eighth-Schedule restriction.
- Article 346: the Union's official language is the baseline for inter-State/State-Union communication, overlaid by section 3.
- Article 347: recognition on demand, subject to presidential satisfaction of a "substantial proportion" — undefined numerically, not automatic.

### 7. The Official Languages Act, 1963 — section 3's architecture (Lesson 7)

- Section 3(1): English continues in addition to Hindi, with three communication provisos.
- Sections 3(2)-(4): transition translation, mandatory bilingual high-value documents, employee-fairness safeguards.
- Section 3(5): the double lock — every non-Hindi-official-language State's legislature, then both Houses of Parliament.
- English's continuance is operative statute, not convention; the lock protects only the exact named provisions.

### 8. Sections 4-8, the 1973 Act, and the 1976 Rules (Lesson 8)

- Section 5: authorised Hindi texts of Central instruments (presidential authority, Gazette).
- Section 6: authorised Hindi translation of specified State Acts, supplementing Article 348(3).
- Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973: authoritative Central-law texts in other Eighth-Schedule languages.
- 1976 Rules: Regions A/B/C, extending to India except Tamil Nadu; Rules 3-10 govern sender/recipient/region/reply/document/proficiency questions.

### 9. Article 348 and Section 7 — courts and authoritative legal texts (Lesson 9)

- Article 348(1): English for SC/HC proceedings and authoritative legislative/delegated texts.
- Article 348(2): Governor plus previous presidential consent may authorise Hindi/State language for HC proceedings; expressly excludes judgments, decrees, orders.
- Section 7: the separate judgment route, requiring a High-Court-authorised English translation.
- Article 348(3): Governor-authorised Gazette English translation of a State's non-English legislative text is deemed authoritative.

### 10. Article 349 — a historically bounded safeguard (Lesson 10)

- Applied only during the first fifteen years from commencement (1950-1965).
- Required previous presidential sanction, informed by Article 344 material, for specified language Bills.
- Expired in 1965; not a current procedural gate, though still printed in the text.

### 11. Articles 350, 350A and 350B — access and minority safeguards (Lesson 11)

- Article 350: grievance representation in any language used in the Union/State — entry-point access only.
- Article 350A: an "endeavour," not an absolute right, for primary-stage mother-tongue facilities; added by the Seventh Amendment, 1956.
- Article 350B: President-appointed Special Officer (today, Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities), investigating and reporting, not adjudicating; HQ moved to New Delhi, 1 June 2015.
- Linguistic-minority status is contextual, independent of Eighth-Schedule membership.

### 12. Judicial doctrine on language choice and medium of instruction (Lesson 12)

- Gujarat University v. Mudholkar (1963): Union Entry-66 competence over higher education prevails over a State university's exclusive-medium rule.
- D.A.V. College v. State of Punjab (1971): compulsory medium cannot override a minority institution's Articles 29(1)/30(1) rights.
- State of Karnataka v. Associated Management (2014): medium-of-instruction choice belongs to parents/child under Article 19(1)(a) in unaided schools; Article 350A is an endeavour, not a compulsion power.
- State power to promote its language through government/aided institutions and curriculum policy remains intact.

### 13. Article 351 — developing Hindi within composite culture (Lesson 13)

- Union's duty: promote Hindi's spread and develop it as a medium of expression for India's composite culture.
- Enrichment method: assimilate forms/expressions from Hindustani and other Eighth-Schedule languages; vocabulary primarily from Sanskrit.
- A Part XVII directive — non-justiciable, harmonised with State autonomy and minority safeguards, not superior to them.

### 14. The Eighth Schedule — purpose, list, amendment ladder, current linkage (Lesson 14)

- Linked to Articles 344(1) and 351 only — not a national-language or universal-official-language list.
