# PM and Council of Ministers — Live Session Edition

India’s parliamentary executive is built around a deliberate constitutional separation between **formal authority** and **political responsibility**. Executive action is expressed in the President’s name, but the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers determine policy, direct administration and remain answerable to the Lok Sabha.

This session follows that operating logic. It begins with the location of executive power, moves through the Prime Minister’s appointment and authority, explains the Council’s responsibility and internal structure, and then traces how Cabinet decisions become government action. It ends by testing whether India has moved from cabinet government toward prime-ministerial government.

The diagram shows a chain of democratic responsibility. The President supplies constitutional form and continuity; the ministry supplies political choice and answers to the elected House.

### 1. Begin with the apparent puzzle

Article 53 formally vests the Union’s executive power in the President. Article 77 requires executive action to be expressed in the President’s name. Yet public policy is made and defended by the Prime Minister and ministers.

This is not a contradiction. Parliamentary government separates:

- the **head of State**, who embodies constitutional continuity; and
- the **head of government**, who leads the politically responsible executive.

The President is therefore the **formal or nominal executive**, while the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers constitute the **real executive**.

### 2. The constitutional chain

| Provision | Constitutional function |
|---|---|
| **Article 53** | Formally vests Union executive power in the President |
| **Article 73** | Defines the broad extent of Union executive power |
| **Article 74(1)** | Creates a Council of Ministers headed by the PM to aid and advise the President |
| **Article 75(3)** | Makes the Council collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha |
| **Article 77** | Gives formal expression and authentication to Union executive action |
| **Article 78** | Makes the PM the constitutional information channel to the President |

Article 73 gives the Union executive two connected fields. First, it extends to matters on which Parliament may legislate. Second, it extends to rights, authority and jurisdiction exercisable by the Government of India under a treaty or agreement. Its federal proviso matters: unless the Constitution or a parliamentary law expressly provides otherwise, Union executive power does not ordinarily extend within a State to a matter on which the State legislature also has power. Legislative competence therefore supplies the outer field, while constitutional text, parliamentary law and the federal proviso determine who executes within it.

The democratic logic is:

```text
executive power
      ↓
exercised through ministerial advice
      ↓
ministry survives only with Lok Sabha confidence
      ↓
executive authority remains politically answerable
```

### 3. Binding advice, with one reconsideration

The original constitutional arrangement generated debate over whether the President could disregard ministerial advice. The **Forty-second Amendment Act, 1976** expressly required the President to act in accordance with the advice. The **Forty-fourth Amendment Act, 1978** added a limited safeguard: the President may require reconsideration once, but must accept the advice tendered after reconsideration.

This creates neither a personal presidential veto nor a ceremonial nullity. The President may warn, question and seek reconsideration, but cannot permanently substitute personal policy for responsible ministerial advice.

### 4. Article 74(2): the precise protection

Article 74(2) provides that courts shall not inquire into **whether any advice was tendered and, if so, what advice was tendered** by ministers to the President.

The protection belongs to the confidential advice itself. It does **not** mean:

- every executive action is immune from judicial review;
- illegality becomes valid because ministers advised it;
- the government may withhold all underlying material merely by calling it advice; or
- constitutional satisfaction can never be tested against relevant material.

> **Answer line:** Article 74(2) protects the confidentiality of advice, not the legality of every action taken after that advice.

### 5. Why the President’s name is used

Government requires a continuous legal personality even when ministers change. Orders are therefore issued in the President’s name and authenticated under rules. This gives official action an institutional form; it does not establish personal presidential administration.

Consider a ministry issuing environmental regulations. The notification may be expressed in the President’s name, but the responsible minister and department must possess legal authority, follow required procedure and answer for the decision. Authentication does not cure a lack of legislative competence, violation of fundamental rights or failure to comply with a parent statute.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the President must accept ministerial advice, the office is merely a rubber stamp.

**Reply:** The office preserves continuity, receives information under Article 78, may return advice once, appoints a Prime Minister in constitutionally uncertain situations and performs formal acts necessary for lawful government.

**Residual:** Presidential influence is greatest when party politics does not produce an obvious answer. It must still serve responsible government rather than become an alternative source of political power.

### 7. UPSC application

The 2020 objective demand on parliamentary government tests the central idea that the government is responsible to the legislature and removable through the confidence mechanism.

For a Mains answer:

1. state the dual-executive structure;
2. connect Articles 53, 74, 75 and 77;
3. explain why responsibility lies with ministers;
4. distinguish formal action from personal decision; and
5. qualify the “rubber stamp” description.

### Revision notes

1. Article 53 formally vests Union executive power in the President.
2. Article 73 links Union execution to Parliament's legislative field and treaty/agreement authority.
3. Its federal proviso preserves State execution in shared fields unless the Constitution or parliamentary law provides otherwise.
4. The President is head of State; the PM is head of government.
5. Article 74 creates the PM-headed Council of Ministers.
6. Ministerial advice is binding after one reconsideration.
7. The Forty-second Amendment made the binding character express.
8. The Forty-fourth Amendment permits one return for reconsideration.
9. Article 74(2) protects the existence and content of advice from judicial inquiry.
10. Article 74(2) does not immunise the resulting executive act from legality review.
11. Article 75(3) connects executive power to Lok Sabha confidence.
12. Article 77 provides formal expression and authentication, not personal presidential rule.
13. The dual executive joins constitutional continuity with democratic accountability.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the requirement that executive action be taken in the President’s name not make the President the real executive?

**Model answer:** The President’s name supplies the formal legal identity of Union action, while Articles 74 and 75 place policy choice and responsibility in the Prime Minister-headed Council of Ministers. Ministers advise, administer and remain collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Formal expression therefore does not displace political responsibility.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate formal authorship, authentication or constitutional dignity with personal policy-making power.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain how the Constitution reconciles a formally vested presidential executive with politically responsible ministerial government.

**Model answer — 140 words:**
Articles 53 and 77 vest and formally express Union executive power through the President. Article 73 defines its legislative and treaty field, subject to a federal proviso. This supplies continuity and legal form.

Real political authority, however, lies with the Prime Minister-headed Council of Ministers. Article 74 requires the President to act on ministerial advice, subject only to one reconsideration under the Forty-fourth Amendment. Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, ensuring that those who decide policy can be removed through the confidence mechanism. Article 78 further makes the Prime Minister the communication link between the Council and President.

The President is therefore not an independent policy executive, but neither is the office meaningless. It can counsel, seek information and act decisively where no clear political claimant exists. India thus combines constitutional form with democratic responsibility.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** dual-executive thesis 2; Articles 53/73/74/75/77/78 4; responsibility mechanism 2; presidential qualification 1; direct conclusion 1.

Once the location of executive power is clear, the next problem is how one person acquires authority to lead the responsible ministry.

The President appoints; the Lok Sabha ultimately validates. Appointment begins authority, but confidence sustains it.

### 1. What Article 75 says—and does not say

Article 75(1) says that the Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President. It does not prescribe:

- an election of the PM by the whole electorate;
- a mandatory nomination vote before appointment;
- a fixed rule that only the leader of the single largest party must be chosen; or
- membership of the Lok Sabha as a precondition.

Constitutional convention fills this textual space. Under a clear mandate, the President appoints the recognised leader capable of commanding a Lok Sabha majority. There is no personal choice.

### 2. Where situational judgment arises

Judgment becomes relevant when:

- no party or pre-poll alliance has a clear majority;
- competing coalitions claim support;
- a government falls and an alternative is proposed;
- the incumbent PM dies suddenly before the governing party identifies a successor; or
- support documents are disputed.

The President’s task is not to choose a preferred programme. It is to identify the person most likely to command confidence and to ensure that the claim is tested in the House.

**Charan Singh in 1979** and **A.B. Vajpayee in 1996** illustrate appointment before proved confidence. **Rajiv Gandhi’s appointment in 1984**, following Indira Gandhi’s assassination, illustrates the need for immediate continuity before the governing party formally confirmed its leader.

### 3. Why the floor test matters

The President may examine objective indications of support, but the decisive constitutional forum is the Lok Sabha.

```text
letters and assurances
       = preliminary evidence

vote on the House floor
       = constitutional proof
```

This protects two interests:

1. the State is not left without a government while claims are tested; and
2. presidential assessment does not replace legislative confidence.

The exact timetable depends on circumstances. The controlling principle is prompt, fair testing rather than indefinite government by assertion.

### 4. Membership of either House

The Indian PM may belong to the **Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha**. Indira Gandhi, H.D. Deve Gowda and Manmohan Singh served as Prime Minister while members of the Rajya Sabha.

This differs from the British convention under which the Prime Minister sits in the House of Commons. The Indian Constitution links government survival to Lok Sabha confidence, not necessarily the PM’s personal membership of that House.

### 5. The six-month non-member rule

Article 75(5) permits a person who is not a member of either House to serve as a minister, including Prime Minister, for **six consecutive months**. If the person does not become a member of either House within that period, the office must be vacated.

In ***S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda* (1996)**, the Supreme Court accepted the appointment of a non-member as Prime Minister subject to this six-month limit.

This rule is a temporary bridge, not an alternative route to indefinite executive office.

### 6. Oath, term and pleasure

The President administers the oaths of **office and secrecy** in the form prescribed by the Third Schedule.

The oath of office requires the PM to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, uphold India’s sovereignty and integrity, faithfully and conscientiously discharge the office, and do right to all people according to the Constitution and law without fear or favour, affection or ill will. The secrecy oath protects matters encountered as a Union minister except where disclosure is required for the proper discharge of duties.

The PM has no fixed constitutional term. Article 75(2) says ministers hold office during the President’s pleasure, but this expression operates within parliamentary government:

- while the PM commands Lok Sabha confidence, the President cannot dismiss the PM merely because of disagreement;
- if confidence is lost, the PM should resign or seek dissolution;
- if a defeated PM refuses to resign and no valid dissolution issue protects the position, dismissal may become constitutionally relevant.

Thus, “pleasure” is not ordinary personal pleasure. It is structured by confidence.

### 7. Appointment versus majority

An appointment can be constitutionally valid before a majority is formally demonstrated. That does not mean the appointee possesses a permanent right to office.

**Analogy:** Appointment is permission to enter the examination hall; the floor test is the examination. The analogy stops there because a PM begins exercising public authority immediately and must therefore face the test promptly.

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Presidential appointment power can be manipulated to favour one coalition.

**Reply:** Objective support material, equal treatment, a prompt floor test and judicially enforceable constitutional norms sharply limit partisan discretion.

**Residual:** Conventions work best when political actors accept defeat. Written priority rules could reduce uncertainty but might become too rigid for unexpected coalition configurations.

### 9. UPSC application

The 2020 objective question concerning a non-legislator State minister tests an analogous six-month rule at State level. The Union rule is Article 75(5); the State counterpart is Article 164(4). The exact eligibility proposition must not be reduced to mere voter status: eligibility to vote is not the same as qualification for legislative membership.

An appointment answer should distinguish:

- text from convention;
- preliminary presidential assessment from floor proof;
- either-House eligibility from Lok Sabha confidence; and
- temporary non-membership from an unlimited exception.

### Revision notes

1. Article 75(1) provides appointment by the President but no detailed selection procedure.
2. A clear majority leaves no personal presidential choice.
3. A hung House creates bounded situational judgment.
4. Objective support may guide appointment; the floor proves confidence.
5. Appointment and established majority are distinct stages.
6. The PM may be a member of either House.
7. Lok Sabha confidence does not require the PM personally to sit in Lok Sabha.
8. Article 75(5) permits non-membership for six consecutive months only.
9. *S.P. Anand* accepted a non-member PM subject to the six-month rule.
10. The President administers oaths of office and secrecy.
11. The PM’s term is not fixed.
12. Presidential pleasure is controlled by the confidence principle.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a Prime Minister be validly appointed before proving a majority without giving the President an unlimited power of choice?

**Model answer:** Appointment ensures immediate government and may rest on objective indications of support. The President’s assessment remains provisional because the Lok Sabha is the authoritative forum for confidence. A prompt floor test converts a political claim into constitutional proof and ends presidential judgment.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat appointment by the President as either a purely personal discretion or proof that majority support already exists.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** The President’s discretion in appointing the Prime Minister is “dormant under a decisive mandate and bounded under a fractured mandate.” Examine.

**Model answer — 170 words:**
Article 75(1) states that the President appoints the Prime Minister but prescribes no detailed selection procedure. Convention therefore controls the ordinary case. Where a party or recognised alliance has a clear Lok Sabha majority, the President must appoint its leader; discretion is dormant.

A fractured mandate creates situational judgment. The President may compare coalition claims, support letters and the capacity to provide stable government. Charan Singh in 1979 and A.B. Vajpayee in 1996 show that appointment may precede proof of confidence. A sudden death before the governing party selects a successor may also require an immediate continuity decision, as in 1984.

The judgment is nevertheless bounded. The President cannot select a preferred ideology, postpone proof indefinitely or replace the House’s verdict. Confidence must be tested promptly on the Lok Sabha floor. The PM may come from either House or initially be a non-member, but Article 75(5) imposes a six-month limit.

Thus, presidential judgment fills a temporary political vacuum. It is legitimate only when directed toward, and terminated by, legislative confidence.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** Article 75 and convention 3; decisive-mandate rule 2; hung-House contingencies 4; named examples 2; floor-test control 2; qualified verdict 2.

Appointment explains entry into office; the next lesson explains why the office becomes the centre of several institutional relationships after entry.

```text
               majority leadership · crisis direction
```

The PM’s strength does not come from a single article. It comes from occupying the junction of several constitutional and political relationships.

### 1. Powers in relation to the Council of Ministers

The President formally appoints other ministers **on the PM’s advice**. This gives the PM decisive influence over:

- selection of ministers;
- allocation and reshuffling of portfolios;
- convening and presiding over ministerial deliberation;
- coordination among ministries;
- asking a minister to resign; and
- advising removal where resignation is refused.

The PM’s resignation or death brings the existing ministry to an end politically because the Council is constitutionally organised around a Prime Minister at its head.

### 2. Article 78: the information bridge

Article 78 imposes three duties on the PM:

1. communicate to the President all Council decisions relating to Union administration and legislative proposals;
2. furnish information the President calls for; and
3. if required, place before the Council a matter decided by an individual minister but not considered collectively.

The third duty is especially important. It permits the President to insist that a potentially significant individual decision receive collective consideration, without personally deciding the policy.

> **Answer line:** Article 78 makes the Prime Minister both the voice of the Council to the President and the route through which individual ministerial action may be returned to collective scrutiny.

### 3. Advice on important appointments

The PM is central to ministerial advice concerning high constitutional and administrative appointments. The exact appointment process varies by office and may include a collegium, committee, statutory consultation or parliamentary framework. Therefore:

- standard examples include the Attorney-General, Comptroller and Auditor General, Union Public Service Commission members, Election Commissioners and Finance Commission members;
- do not write that the PM personally appoints every constitutional authority;
- identify the applicable procedure where the question names an office; and
- use the PM’s role here as evidence of coordinating influence, not unlimited personal patronage.

### 4. Relationship with Parliament

The PM normally leads the government in Parliament and:

- announces and defends major policy;
- coordinates the legislative programme;
- advises the President on summoning and prorogation;
- may advise dissolution of the Lok Sabha;
- responds to major confidence and national-policy debates; and
- uses party leadership to secure passage of government business.

An Indian PM may sit in the Rajya Sabha, but the ministry remains responsible to the Lok Sabha. Where the PM is not a Lok Sabha member, another senior minister may handle some day-to-day floor leadership, but ultimate political leadership remains with the PM.

### 5. Party leadership and political authority

The Constitution does not say that the PM shall control the governing party. Yet party leadership is often the practical source of authority over:

- candidature and career expectations;
- legislative whips;
- ministerial promotion;
- coalition negotiation; and
- public communication.

This distinction matters because constitutional powers remain constant while political strength varies. A PM leading a cohesive single-party majority is usually stronger than one dependent on several coalition partners.

### 6. Administrative and national roles

The PM provides political coordination during national crises, represents the government internationally and chairs or leads important coordinating bodies under their applicable arrangements. Such roles must be treated as institutional practice, not as an unlimited constitutional list.

Standard administrative texts have listed the PM as chair of bodies such as NITI Aayog, the Inter-State Council, National Integration Council and National Water Resources Council. These are executive or institutional arrangements, not a common set of constitutional powers; present composition must be checked if a question specifically asks for current officeholders.

The PM also works through staff and coordination institutions, especially the Prime Minister’s Office and Cabinet Secretariat. Those bodies assist; they do not independently acquire every ministry’s statutory power.

The office has also repeatedly drawn leaders with prior Chief Minister experience: Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, P.V. Narasimha Rao, H.D. Deve Gowda and Narendra Modi. The pattern illustrates the administrative and federal experience that State leadership may bring to the Union; it is not a constitutional qualification.

### 7. Role descriptions: use with attribution

Political writers have described the PM as:

- Lord Morley’s **“primus inter pares”** and “keystone of the cabinet arch”;
- Jennings’s **“sun around which planets revolve”**;
- Laski’s **“pivot”** of governmental machinery; and
- Munro’s **“captain of the ship of state.”**

B.R. Ambedkar argued that if any Indian constitutional functionary were comparable to the US President in practical governmental importance, it was the PM rather than the President.

These are analytical characterisations, not constitutional provisions. Use them to frame an argument and then prove the argument with institutions.

### 8. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The broad list of functions proves that the PM is constitutionally omnipotent.

**Reply:** Many powers are relational. Appointment advice depends on legal procedures; dissolution advice operates within constitutional conditions; Parliament, coalition allies, courts and elections remain constraints.

**Residual:** Even formally shared powers may become PM-centred when party discipline and information control are strong. The critical issue is institutional practice, not merely textual allocation.

### 9. UPSC application

The 2026 comparative demand asks for the **actual authority** of the Indian PM compared with the US President. This lesson supplies the Indian side:

- control of the ministry;
- party and legislative leadership;
- Article 78 communication;
- dissolution advice; and
- dependence on Lok Sabha confidence.

The comparison is completed in Lesson 11.

### Revision notes

1. The PM recommends other ministers.
2. Portfolio allocation and reshuffle create practical control over the ministry.
3. A minister’s removal is effectively enforced through PM advice.
4. Article 78 contains three distinct communication duties.
5. The President may require an individual minister’s matter to be placed before the Council.
6. The PM coordinates the government’s legislative programme.
7. Dissolution is advised, not personally decreed by the PM.
8. Party leadership is political practice, not an enumerated constitutional power.
9. Prime-ministerial strength varies with majority and coalition conditions.
10. Appointment influence must be stated with the procedure applicable to the named office.

12. PMO and Cabinet Secretariat assist authority; they do not replace lawful departmental competence.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the Prime Minister’s power best understood as relational rather than as a single constitutional grant?

**Model answer:** The PM’s authority arises from connecting several institutions: recommending ministers, allocating portfolios, communicating with the President under Article 78, leading government business in Parliament and controlling the governing party. Each relationship creates leverage, but each also carries its own legal or political constraints.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not convert practical centrality into a claim that the PM personally exercises every constitutional, statutory or departmental power.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** The Prime Minister’s office is powerful because it stands at the intersection of institutions rather than above them. Analyse.

**Model answer — 188 words:**
The Constitution does not confer a single comprehensive executive power on the Prime Minister. The office becomes central by connecting institutions.

Within the ministry, the PM recommends appointments, allocates portfolios, coordinates policy and can secure a minister’s exit. In relation to the President, Article 78 makes the PM the authoritative channel for Council decisions, information and collective reconsideration. In Parliament, the PM leads policy, shapes government business and may advise dissolution. As party leader, the PM can combine executive office with legislative discipline and electoral authority.

This intersection produces structural leverage. A ministry depends on the PM for its formation and survival; the President receives ministerial information through the PM; and a disciplined majority can make the executive the agenda-setter of Parliament.

Yet the PM is not institutionally superior to the Constitution. Appointment processes may involve statutory committees, the ministry remains collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, courts review unlawful action, and coalition allies or federal politics may constrain personnel and policy.

The PM is therefore the system’s hinge, not its legal owner. Power grows when the connected institutions are politically aligned and contracts when they are not.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** intersection thesis 2; ministry relationship 3; President/Article 78 3; Parliament and party 3; institutional limits 3; reasoned conclusion 1.

### Visual: Government does not switch off

```text
LOSS OF CONFIDENCE / RESIGNATION / DISSOLUTION / PM'S DEATH
                           ↓
Existing political mandate is exhausted or interrupted
                           ↓
But Article 74 still requires a Council to aid and advise
                           ↓
Outgoing arrangement continues for necessary administration
                           ↓
Convention narrows major policy choices
                           ↓

```

The Constitution avoids both extremes: an outgoing government does not retain an unlimited political mandate, but the Union cannot be left without lawful executive advice.

### 1. Why the PM’s exit affects the whole ministry

The Council exists with the Prime Minister at its head. Therefore:

- the resignation of an ordinary minister creates a vacancy;
- the resignation or death of the PM ends the existing ministry politically; and
- a successor must form or reconstitute a Council.

This is a consequence of collective organisation, not a claim that every administrative office becomes vacant instantly.

### 2. Continuity after dissolution

In ***U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi* (1971)**, the Supreme Court rejected the idea that the Council automatically ceases to exist merely because the Lok Sabha has been dissolved. Article 74 is mandatory: the President cannot exercise ordinary executive power without ministerial aid and advice.

The reason is practical and constitutional. Elections, public finance, security, appointments and routine administration continue between Houses.

### 3. Satisfaction is ordinarily ministerial satisfaction

In ***Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974)**, the Supreme Court emphasised that where the Constitution speaks of presidential or gubernatorial satisfaction, it ordinarily means satisfaction reached under the parliamentary system through ministerial advice, except where the Constitution lawfully creates discretion.

This prevents formal wording from becoming a route to personal rule.

### 4. What is a caretaker government?

The Constitution does not create a separately named “caretaker government”. The expression describes an outgoing Council that continues temporarily:

- after resignation;
- after loss of confidence;
- after dissolution for an election; or
- until a successor can assume office.

The source of legal continuity is the ordinary constitutional executive. The restraint is primarily a **convention**.

### 5. The convention of restraint

A caretaker arrangement should ordinarily avoid:

- major irreversible policy changes;
- large new financial commitments;
- appointments intended to bind the successor;
- partisan use of state resources; and
- decisions that can reasonably await the incoming government.

It may and must act where delay would harm:

- national security;
- public safety;
- financial stability;
- disaster response;
- legal deadlines; or
- essential administration.

The test is necessity, reversibility and fairness—not complete inactivity.

Historical guidance reinforces the convention. The **Tarkunde Committee (1974–75)** advised against new policies, projects and major benefits during the caretaker period. When the Lok Sabha was dissolved in 1979, President N. Sanjiva Reddy recorded assurances that the outgoing government would avoid significant new policy and spending while continuing urgent national-interest work. A Calcutta High Court judgment later that year observed that the Constitution does not expressly name a caretaker government, but that day-to-day administration must continue until an alternative arrangement is made.

### 6. Analogy and its limit

A caretaker government resembles a trustee holding an institution between two elected boards. It preserves assets and handles urgent matters but avoids redesigning the institution.

The analogy is limited because the outgoing Council remains the lawful government and may face emergencies requiring substantial action. Convention cannot compel paralysis.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Caretaker restraint is too uncertain because it is not codified.

**Reply:** Flexibility is necessary because emergencies cannot be predicted, and election law, judicial review, parliamentary accountability after reassembly and political convention provide controls.

**Residual:** Clear publicly available guidance can improve consistency without pretending that every decision can be mechanically classified in advance.

### 8. UPSC application

A strong answer distinguishes:

```text
constitutional continuity
        ≠
continuing full political mandate
```

Use *U.N.R. Rao* for the legal need for advice, *Shamsher Singh* for the ministerial character of formal satisfaction, and caretaker convention for self-restraint.

### Revision notes

1. The PM’s resignation or death ends the existing ministry politically.
2. An ordinary minister’s exit creates only a vacancy.
3. Article 74 requires continuing ministerial aid and advice.
4. *U.N.R. Rao* preserves the Council after Lok Sabha dissolution.
5. The President cannot ordinarily govern without ministerial advice.
6. *Shamsher Singh* treats formal satisfaction as ministerial satisfaction in ordinary fields.
7. “Caretaker government” is a convention, not a separate constitutional category.
8. Caretaker restraint concerns major avoidable commitments.
9. Urgent national-interest and routine decisions remain permissible.
10. Restraint prevents electoral advantage and protects the successor’s freedom.
11. Convention must not be confused with executive paralysis.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can an outgoing ministry lack a fresh political mandate yet remain constitutionally necessary?

**Model answer:** Loss of confidence or dissolution weakens the ministry’s authority to make avoidable long-term choices, but Article 74 still requires the President to receive ministerial advice. The outgoing Council therefore continues lawfully for necessary administration while convention limits politically irreversible decisions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say that dissolution leaves the President free to govern personally or that a caretaker government can make no decisions at all.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain the constitutional basis and conventional limits of caretaker government in India.

**Model answer — 138 words:**
The Constitution does not establish a distinct caretaker government. The term describes an outgoing Council continuing after resignation, loss of confidence or Lok Sabha dissolution until a successor assumes office.

Its constitutional basis is continuity. Article 74 requires a Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President. In *U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi* (1971), the Supreme Court held that dissolution does not leave the President without ministerial advice. *Shamsher Singh* (1974) similarly affirmed the ministerial character of ordinary executive satisfaction.

Its limits arise mainly from convention. A caretaker ministry should avoid major irreversible policies, partisan appointments and substantial new commitments that can await the elected successor. It may still perform routine administration and act urgently for security, safety, legal deadlines or financial stability.

Thus, caretaker government is lawful continuity under reduced political discretion: restrained, but never paralysed.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** constitutional continuity 3; cases 2; conventional restraints 3; urgent-action qualification 1; concise verdict 1.

Continuity explains why a Council must exist; the next lesson identifies the exact constitutional rules governing that Council.

### Visual: The constitutional article map

```text

                           ↓
```

These articles perform different jobs. A recurring UPSC trap is to move a rule from one article into another.

### 1. Article 74

Article 74 establishes:

- a Council of Ministers;
- the Prime Minister at its head;
- aid and advice to the President;
- one opportunity for reconsideration; and
- protection of the advice from judicial inquiry under clause (2).

It does not contain the ministry-size cap or collective-responsibility clause. Those belong to Article 75.

### 2. Article 75

| Clause | Main rule |
|---|---|
| **75(1)** | PM appointed by President; other ministers appointed on PM’s advice |
| **75(1A)** | Total Union ministers, including PM, cannot exceed 15% of total Lok Sabha membership |
| **75(1B)** | A member of either House belonging to a political party and disqualified under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule is barred from ministerial appointment for the constitutionally stated period |
| **75(2)** | Ministers hold office during the President’s pleasure |
| **75(3)** | Council collectively responsible to Lok Sabha |
| **75(4)** | President administers oaths of office and secrecy |
| **75(5)** | Non-member minister ceases after six consecutive months without membership |
| **75(6)** | Salaries and allowances determined by Parliament |

Clauses (1A) and (1B) were inserted by the **Ninety-first Amendment Act, 2003**. The cap is based on the **Lok Sabha alone**, not both Houses.

### 3. The defection office bar

For a member of either House belonging to a political party who is disqualified under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule, Article 75(1B) adds an office consequence: that person cannot be appointed a minister for the relevant barred period.

The bar runs from disqualification until the date on which the member’s term would have expired, or—if the person contests and is declared elected earlier—until that election date.

This prevents immediate ministerial reward for defection. The complete grounds, merger defence, adjudication and judicial-review doctrine belong to the anti-defection framework; for this topic, remember the link between disqualification and ministerial office.

### 4. Articles 77 and 78

Article 77 controls:

- action in the President’s name;
- authentication; and
- rules for convenient transaction and allocation of business.

Article 78 controls the PM’s duties to the President. Article 77 concerns the **machinery of action**; Article 78 concerns the **flow of constitutional information**.

### 5. Article 88—state it precisely

Article 88 permits every minister and the Attorney-General to:

- speak in and otherwise take part in proceedings of either House;
- participate in a joint sitting; and
- participate in a parliamentary committee of which the person may be named a member.

But Article 88 does **not**, by itself, confer a right to vote. A minister who is a member of one House votes in that House by virtue of membership. At a joint sitting, voting follows membership of Parliament and the applicable joint-sitting rules; committee voting similarly depends on committee membership and procedure.

> **Safe formulation:** Article 88 permits participation beyond one’s own House but creates no independent voting right; every vote must rest on the person’s actual membership and the procedure of the House, joint sitting or committee concerned.

A non-member minister may speak under Article 88 but cannot vote in either House, at a joint sitting or in a parliamentary committee.

### 6. The ministerial categories

The Constitution does **not** classify ministers into four or any other number of ranks. Governmental practice traditionally distinguishes:

1. **Cabinet Ministers**—senior ministers who form the Cabinet;
2. **Ministers of State**—with independent charge or attached to a Cabinet Minister; and
3. **Deputy Ministers**—assist senior ministers and hold no independent charge.

Actual appointments need not always fill every traditional category. The constitutional Council consists of the ministers validly appointed under Article 75, not of a permanently mandated rank structure.

### 7. Minister of State with independent charge

Such a minister heads a ministry or department without a Cabinet Minister above them. The minister exercises political charge over that portfolio but is not automatically a Cabinet member. Attendance at Cabinet may occur by invitation when relevant business is considered.

### 8. Deputy Prime Minister and parliamentary secretaries

The Constitution creates no separate office of Deputy Prime Minister. In the challenge concerning **Devi Lal’s 1990 oath**, the Supreme Court treated “Deputy Prime Minister” as a descriptive designation that does not confer the PM’s constitutional powers.

Union parliamentary secretaries have historically assisted ministers in parliamentary work but are not thereby a separate constitutional rank within the Council. Do not transfer contemporary State-level office-of-profit controversies into the Union structure without a specific question.

In the traditional Union description, parliamentary secretaries were appointed by the Prime Minister rather than by the President, held no department and assisted senior ministers in parliamentary duties. They were not members of the Council merely by virtue of that designation.

### 9. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Because ministerial ranks are conventional, they are legally meaningless.

**Reply:** The ranks organise access, responsibility and decision-making even though the Constitution does not enumerate them. Constitutional validity of appointment and political hierarchy are different questions.

**Residual:** Excessive reliance on informal hierarchy can obscure who is answerable. Portfolio notifications and business allocation should make responsibility visible.

### 10. UPSC application

The 2022 objective question directly tests:

- whether the Constitution classifies four ministerial ranks; and
- whether the Union ministry, including the PM, is capped at 15% of Lok Sabha membership.

The question must be approached by separating **constitutional text** from **administrative convention**.

### Revision notes

1. Article 74 creates the PM-headed aid-and-advice Council.
2. Article 74(2) protects advice, not every resulting action.
3. Article 75 contains appointment and responsibility rules.
4. Article 75(1A) caps Union ministers, including PM, at 15% of Lok Sabha membership.
5. Article 75(1B) creates the defection-related ministerial bar.
6. Article 75(3) makes responsibility collective and owed to Lok Sabha.
7. Article 75(5) contains the six-month non-member rule.
8. Article 77 controls formal action and business rules.
9. Article 78 controls PM-to-President information duties.
10. Article 88 grants participation but no independent voting right.
11. The Constitution does not classify ministers into four ranks.
12. Cabinet Minister, Minister of State and Deputy Minister are conventional categories.
13. Deputy Prime Minister is a descriptive designation without separate constitutional power.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the traditional ministerial hierarchy be constitutionally valid even though the Constitution does not classify ministers into ranks?

**Model answer:** Article 75 authorises appointment of ministers without prescribing their administrative ranks. The government may organise political work through Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State and other categories, provided appointments, oaths, responsibility and statutory authority comply with the Constitution and law.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat a textbook classification as express constitutional text or assume that every traditional category must exist in every ministry.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Distinguish the constitutional status of the Council of Ministers from the conventional organisation of ministerial ranks.

**Model answer — 144 words:**
The Council of Ministers is a constitutional institution. Article 74 creates it with the Prime Minister at its head, while Article 75 governs appointment, pleasure, collective responsibility, oaths, the six-month rule and the ministry-size ceiling.

Ministerial ranks are organisational conventions. Cabinet Ministers form the senior decision core; Ministers of State may hold independent charge or assist Cabinet Ministers; Deputy Ministers traditionally assist without independent charge. The Constitution does not classify ministers into four ranks or require every category to be filled.

The distinction has practical consequences. A descriptive title such as Deputy Prime Minister does not confer the Prime Minister’s powers, as the Supreme Court clarified regarding Devi Lal’s oath. Similarly, a Minister of State with independent charge may lead a portfolio without automatically becoming a Cabinet member.

Thus, the Council’s existence and responsibility are constitutional; its internal rank arrangement is flexible governmental practice.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** constitutional provisions 4; conventional hierarchy 3; Devi Lal or equivalent illustration 1; practical consequence 1; clear conclusion 1.

The Council’s structure is only half the story; its democratic purpose becomes visible through the responsibility doctrines.

## Lesson 6 — Collective, Individual and Legal Responsibility

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: Three responsibilities that must not be merged

| Responsibility | Constitutional or conventional basis | Enforced by | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collective** | Article 75(3) plus Cabinet solidarity | Lok Sabha confidence; PM and Cabinet discipline | Whole ministry stands or falls together |
| **Individual** | Article 75(2) pleasure, operating through PM advice | Prime Minister | Individual minister resigns or is removed |
| **Legal** | No British-style personal countersignature rule in the Constitution | Courts review the legality of action, not confidential advice | Unlawful action may fail; minister is not automatically personally liable merely for countersigning |

The categories overlap politically but answer different questions.

### 1. Collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha

Article 75(3) makes the Council **collectively responsible to the House of the People**. It is not collectively responsible to the Rajya Sabha.

Collective responsibility has two linked meanings:

1. **confidence responsibility**—if the Lok Sabha withdraws confidence, the entire ministry must resign or seek a constitutionally permissible dissolution; and
2. **policy solidarity**—ministers must publicly support Cabinet decisions or leave office.

Even ministers who sit in the Rajya Sabha fall with the ministry after a successful no-confidence motion in the Lok Sabha.

### 2. “Swim and sink together”

The ministry presents itself as one accountable government rather than a collection of independent departmental rulers.

```text
Cabinet decision
       ↓
all ministers defend the policy
       ↓
Lok Sabha judges the government as one unit
       ↓
confidence retained or whole ministry exits
```

Without solidarity, Parliament could never know whether a minister spoke for the government or merely for a department.

### 3. Dissent and resignation

Cabinet discussion permits internal disagreement. Collective responsibility begins at the point of authoritative decision. A minister who cannot publicly defend the decision should resign.

**B.R. Ambedkar tendered his resignation in 1951** amid disagreement over the stalled Hindu Code Bill programme. The relevant year is 1951, not 1953. The example shows that public dissent from an adopted governmental position is resolved by exit rather than by operating as an internal opposition.

The example has limits. Not every ministerial resignation proves a formal Cabinet vote or one single cause; use it to illustrate solidarity, not to oversimplify historical motives.

### 4. Individual responsibility

Article 75(2) says ministers hold office during the President’s pleasure. In practice, the Prime Minister enforces individual responsibility:

- asks a minister to resign;
- reshuffles the portfolio; or
- advises the President to remove the minister.

The President does not normally retain a minister whom the PM no longer accepts. This authority enables the PM to preserve collective discipline.

Ambedkar described collective responsibility as achievable through the instrumentality of a Prime Minister empowered to nominate and dismiss ministers. The observation explains why individual discipline is necessary to sustain collective answerability.

### 5. Departmental accountability

Individual responsibility also has a parliamentary dimension. A minister answers for the department’s policy, expenditure and administration through:

- questions;
- debates and motions;
- demands for grants;
- committee scrutiny;
- audit findings; and
- judicial proceedings concerning departmental legality.

Modern administration complicates this ideal because decisions are dispersed across civil servants, regulators, missions and agencies. Yet delegation does not erase ministerial responsibility for policy direction and lawful oversight.

### 6. No British-style legal responsibility

In Britain, the historical countersignature convention expressed the idea that the monarch could not be personally liable and a minister accepted responsibility for official acts.

India does not require every presidential order to carry a minister’s countersignature as a condition of legal responsibility. Nor does Article 74(2) make ministers personally immune or place all executive action beyond courts.

The correct distinction is:

```text
courts may not inquire into confidential ministerial advice
                         BUT
courts may test legal authority, procedure, rights and relevant material
```

### 7. Three different secrecy ideas

Cabinet confidentiality is a political-institutional condition for candid deliberation and collective solidarity: ministers may argue internally but must defend the authorised decision publicly. The Third Schedule adds a personal constitutional oath not to communicate or reveal matters brought under a minister's consideration or known as a minister, except as required for proper discharge of duties. Article 74(2) is narrower still: it bars a court from inquiring whether advice was tendered to the President and what that advice was.

These rules overlap in purpose but are not interchangeable. Cabinet confidentiality does not convert every government paper into constitutionally protected advice; the secrecy oath permits disclosure required by official duty; and Article 74(2) does not prevent review of the legality of the resulting act or examination of material that is not itself protected ministerial advice.

### 8. Coalition variation

Coalitions test solidarity because allies may bargain publicly, maintain separate manifestos and criticise particular proposals.

**Critique:** Collective responsibility becomes a legal fiction when ministers publicly disagree.

**Reply:** The decisive legal rule survives. A coalition ministry still stands or falls together on confidence, and the PM must ultimately produce a common governmental position.

**Residual:** The law remains binary—confidence or no confidence—while political solidarity exists on a spectrum.

### 9. Parliament’s control in practice

The 2021 Mains demand asks how far Parliament ensures executive accountability. Collective responsibility supplies the constitutional foundation, but its practical strength depends on:

- meaningful sitting time;
- questions and debates;
- committee scrutiny;
- control of finance;
- access to information; and
- whether party discipline permits genuine examination.

### 10. UPSC application

For an answer on responsibility:

1. separate collective, individual and legal responsibility;
2. use Articles 75(2), 75(3) and 74(2) accurately;
3. explain both confidence and solidarity;
4. add coalition or administrative complexity; and
5. conclude with a law-versus-practice distinction.

### Revision notes

1. Collective responsibility is Article 75(3).
2. It is owed only to the Lok Sabha.
3. A no-confidence defeat removes the entire ministry, including Rajya Sabha ministers.
4. Cabinet decisions bind all ministers publicly.
5. Internal disagreement is compatible with solidarity until the final decision.
6. A minister unable to defend policy should resign.
7. Ambedkar tendered resignation in 1951 in the Hindu Code Bill context.
8. Individual responsibility rests on Article 75(2).
9. The PM is the effective enforcer of individual responsibility.
10. Departmental accountability operates through questions, finance, committees and audit.
11. India lacks a British-style constitutional countersignature rule for legal responsibility.
12. Cabinet confidentiality protects candid internal deliberation and supports public solidarity.
13. The Third Schedule secrecy oath permits disclosure required for proper official duty.
14. Article 74(2) protects whether and what advice reached the President, not all records or unlawful action.
15. Coalition bargaining may weaken solidarity in practice without abolishing the legal rule.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can collective responsibility remain legally intact even when coalition ministers publicly disagree?

**Model answer:** Article 75(3) imposes a single confidence relationship between the whole Council and the Lok Sabha. Public disagreement may weaken political solidarity, but the ministry still falls together if it loses confidence and must ultimately present an authoritative common policy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer from coalition bargaining that each minister acquires an independent tenure or separate confidence mandate.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Collective responsibility is constitutionally binary but politically graduated. Discuss in the context of coalition government.

**Model answer — 178 words:**
Article 75(3) makes the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Constitutionally, the rule is binary: the ministry possesses confidence or it does not. A successful no-confidence motion requires the entire Council, including Rajya Sabha ministers, to leave office.

The doctrine also demands policy solidarity. Ministers may disagree internally, but must defend the final Cabinet position or resign. B.R. Ambedkar’s 1951 resignation in the Hindu Code Bill context illustrates the exit principle.

Coalition politics makes solidarity graduated in practice. Allies retain separate organisations, negotiate portfolios, issue public reservations and may support only a common minimum programme. This can make the government appear as several voices rather than one.

Yet coalition bargaining does not abolish the legal structure. The Prime Minister must still coordinate an authoritative position, and allies cannot retain ministerial office after bringing down the government. Parliamentary questions, finance and committees continue to address ministers as members of one executive.

Thus, collective responsibility remains legally indivisible while political cohesion varies with coalition arithmetic. The doctrine survives, but its daily operation becomes negotiated rather than automatic.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** Article 75(3) and confidence 3; solidarity doctrine 3; coalition variation 4; named evidence 2; counter-argument 2; graded conclusion 1.

Responsibility attaches to the whole Council, but actual high-level decisions are concentrated in the smaller Cabinet examined next.

The Council, Cabinet and kitchen cabinet are not synonyms. They differ in legal status, composition and accountability.

### 1. Council of Ministers

The Council is the wider constitutional ministry created by Articles 74 and 75. It includes all persons appointed as Union ministers and carries collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha.

Its actual size changes within the Article 75(1A) ceiling. The Constitution does not prescribe a permanent number.

### 2. Cabinet

The Cabinet is the smaller senior decision core consisting of Cabinet-rank ministers. It:

- decides major policy;
- coordinates central administration;
- settles major inter-ministerial disagreements;
- handles important legislative and financial proposals;
- shapes foreign policy and other major external-affairs decisions;
- advises the President through the responsible executive structure;
- directs crisis response; and
- gives collective political form to governmental decisions.

The wider Council does not ordinarily meet as one large body for every decision. Cabinet government therefore operates through a smaller deliberative core while responsibility legally attaches to the whole Council.

### 3. Is Cabinet constitutional?

The word “Cabinet” was absent from the original constitutional text. The **Forty-fourth Amendment** inserted an express definition for the limited purpose of **Article 352(3)**: written advice for a Proclamation of Emergency must come from the Cabinet, understood as the Council consisting of the PM and other ministers of Cabinet rank.

This does not mean the Cabinet lacked political existence before 1978. It means the Constitution later gave the term an express emergency-specific definition.

### 4. Cabinet as the “hyphen”

Bagehot described the Cabinet as the **“hyphen that joins”** the executive and legislature. Ministers sit in Parliament, lead a majority and direct administration. Fusion creates coordination but also an accountability risk:

```text
majority in Lok Sabha
        +
party whip
        +
government control of business
        ↓
Cabinet may dominate the House it is meant to answer to
```

Laski and Ramsay Muir used the language of “cabinet dictatorship” to describe this tendency. The phrase is an analytical critique, not a constitutional status.

Other classic descriptions capture different Cabinet functions: Ramsay Muir called it the steering wheel of the ship of state; Lowell, the keystone of the political arch; Marriott, the pivot of political machinery; Gladstone, a solar centre; Barker, the magnet of policy; Jennings, the constitutional core; and L.S. Amery, the central directing instrument. These quotations are memory aids, not substitutes for explaining decision, coordination and responsibility.

### 5. Parliamentary supremacy versus sovereignty

The 2024 question uses “parliamentary supremacy” to ask whether Cabinet growth has weakened Parliament’s effective control. It does not transform India into the British doctrine of legally sovereign Parliament.

India’s Parliament remains limited by:

- the written Constitution;
- federal distribution;
- fundamental rights;
- judicial review; and
- basic structure.

The Cabinet may marginalise scrutiny without acquiring legal supremacy over the Constitution.

### 6. Correctives to Cabinet dominance

- no-confidence motion;
- Question Hour and debates;
- demands for grants and cut motions;
- Department-related Standing Committees;
- CAG–Public Accounts Committee scrutiny;
- bicameral examination, except where the Constitution limits Rajya Sabha;
- judicial review; and
- elections and public accountability.

The strongest reply to the dictatorship critique is that a Cabinet remains removable and legally reviewable. The residual concern is that these correctives may be weakly used under a disciplined majority.

### 7. Kitchen or inner cabinet

A kitchen cabinet is an informal group of the PM and a few trusted colleagues, sometimes including advisers or persons outside the formal Cabinet. It may:

- permit rapid confidential discussion;
- help the PM test proposals;
- coordinate political strategy; and
- reduce the burden of a large Cabinet.

It is extra-constitutional and has no independent legal authority to replace the competent minister, Cabinet or statutory decision-maker.

The inner circle was particularly associated with the centralisation of decision-making under Indira Gandhi.

### 8. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** An inner cabinet makes formal Cabinet deliberation a ritual.

**Reply:** Informal consultation is unavoidable in political leadership, and final lawful decisions must still pass through competent institutional channels.

**Residual:** Where information, agenda and access are monopolised by an inner circle, formal collective responsibility may remain while genuine collegial deliberation declines.

### 9. UPSC application

The 2024 GS-II demand requires:

1. explain fusion and majority discipline;
2. show Cabinet control of legislative time, information and drafting;
3. distinguish effective marginalisation from legal sovereignty;
4. state parliamentary and judicial correctives; and
5. conclude with reform of scrutiny rather than an unqualified “dictatorship” claim.

### Revision notes

1. Council of Ministers is the wider constitutional ministry.
2. Cabinet is the smaller Cabinet-rank decision core.
3. Collective responsibility formally attaches to the whole Council.
4. Actual Council and Cabinet sizes are not permanently fixed.
5. “Cabinet” entered express constitutional text through Article 352(3), inserted by the Forty-fourth Amendment.
6. The Article 352 definition is emergency-specific.
7. Cabinet coordinates policy, legislation, finance, appointments and crises.
8. Bagehot’s “hyphen” describes executive-legislative fusion.
9. Cabinet dominance concerns effective scrutiny, not UK-style parliamentary sovereignty.
10. No-confidence, questions, finance, committees and courts remain correctives.
11. Kitchen cabinet is informal and extra-constitutional.
12. Informal advice cannot replace a legally competent decision-maker.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can the Cabinet dominate Parliament in practice without making Parliament legally sovereign or legally subordinate?

**Model answer:** A disciplined majority, party whip and control of legislative business may let the Cabinet manage parliamentary scrutiny. Yet Parliament and Cabinet remain subject to the Constitution, federal limits, rights and judicial review. The dominance concerns political operation, not a change in legal supremacy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use “parliamentary supremacy” in the 2024 question as proof that India follows the British doctrine of unlimited parliamentary sovereignty.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Distinguish the Council of Ministers, Cabinet and kitchen cabinet, and explain why the distinction matters for accountability.

**Model answer — 140 words:**
The Council of Ministers is the wider constitutional body under Articles 74–75 and is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. It includes all appointed Union ministers.

The Cabinet is its smaller senior decision core. It deliberates on major policy, legislation, finance, appointments and crises. Article 352(3), inserted by the Forty-fourth Amendment, expressly defines Cabinet for written Emergency advice, though Cabinet government existed by convention earlier.

A kitchen or inner cabinet is an informal circle of the Prime Minister and trusted colleagues or advisers. It may accelerate confidential consultation but has no independent legal authority.

The distinctions protect accountability. Cabinet efficiency must remain connected to the Council’s collective responsibility, while informal advice must be translated through competent ministers and lawful procedures. If the inner circle displaces formal deliberation, decisions may remain legally attributable to government while becoming less collegial and transparent.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** three accurate identities 4; Article 352(3) 2; accountability mechanism 2; informal-power qualification 1; conclusion 1.

The Cabinet cannot examine every matter at equal depth, which creates the need for specialised committees and temporary ministerial groups.

Committees solve a workload problem. They do not create a separate executive outside the Council.

### 1. Constitutional status

Cabinet Committees are **extra-constitutional**: the Constitution does not enumerate them. Their operation is supported by the governmental business-rule system.

“Extra-constitutional” means outside express constitutional enumeration. It does not mean unlawful or anti-constitutional.

### 2. Standing and ad hoc committees

- **Standing committees** handle recurring classes of government business.
- **Ad hoc committees** are created for a particular problem and may be dissolved after completing the task.

Their number, name, membership and internal allocation can change with governmental priorities. A textbook list is therefore a dated snapshot, not permanent constitutional doctrine.

### 3. What committees do

Cabinet Committees:

- reduce the Cabinet’s workload;
- permit detailed consideration;
- bring the relevant ministers together;
- coordinate cross-ministry policy;
- settle or narrow disagreement;
- take decisions within authorised business arrangements; and
- prepare matters for the full Cabinet where required.

The Cabinet system can review or retain ultimate control over committee decisions according to the applicable procedure. Specialisation therefore operates within, not against, collective government.

### 4. Membership and chairmanship

Membership varies. Committees may contain Cabinet Ministers, non-Cabinet ministers and special invitees where the subject requires them.

Do not memorise a universal chairmanship formula. The official composition document must control present-tense membership claims, and a committee’s chair should not be inferred merely from an old general rule or from the order in which members are listed.

Cabinet government is not complete when a political decision is announced. It must be assigned, consulted, authorised, recorded and implemented.

### 1. Article 77(3)

Article 77(3) authorises rules for:

- the convenient transaction of Government of India business; and
- allocation of that business among ministers.

Two rule sets perform different functions:

| Rule set | Core question |
|---|---|
| **Allocation of Business Rules, 1961** | Who handles the subject? |
| **Transaction of Business Rules, 1961** | How must the decision be processed? |

Allocation gives **jurisdiction**; transaction gives **procedure**.

### 2. Portfolio, ministry and department

- A **portfolio** is the political charge assigned to a minister.
- A **ministry** is a broad political-administrative unit.
- A **department** is a subject-based unit whose business is allocated under the rules.
- A **secretariat** supplies policy, coordination, drafting, finance and oversight support.

One minister may hold several portfolios. One ministry may contain several departments. These terms are related but not interchangeable.

### 3. Minister and Secretary

| Minister | Secretary |
|---|---|
| Political head | Administrative head and principal official adviser |
| Supplies democratic direction | Supplies continuity, options and recorded advice |
| Answers in Parliament | Ensures proper processing and lawful implementation |
| May change after election or reshuffle | Continues through the permanent civil service |

The Secretary is not a rival political authority. The minister is not entitled to unlawful obedience. Good administration requires candid recorded advice followed by faithful implementation of a lawful decision.

### 4. Central Secretariat and executive agencies

The Central Secretariat performs policy, legislative, budgetary, coordination and oversight work. Attached offices, subordinate offices, field formations, autonomous bodies, regulators and public enterprises carry varying implementation functions.

Delegating execution does not erase departmental stewardship. A minister cannot answer every operational detail personally, but the ministry remains responsible for lawful design, supervision and corrective action.

### 5. Cabinet Secretariat

The Cabinet Secretariat:

- functions directly under the Prime Minister;
- is administratively headed by the Cabinet Secretary;
- services Cabinet and Cabinet Committees;
- prepares and circulates agenda material;
- records and communicates decisions;
- monitors implementation;
- administers the Allocation and Transaction of Business Rules;
- supports inter-ministerial coordination; and
- uses Committees of Secretaries to resolve administrative differences.

It is not the Cabinet, the whole Central Secretariat or a super-ministry.

### 6. Prime Minister’s Office

The PMO is a **non-constitutional, non-statutory staff agency** supporting the Prime Minister. It processes matters requiring PM attention, supplies advice, monitors priorities and coordinates through PM-level channels.

The PMO is not:

- the Cabinet Secretariat;
- the custodian of all Cabinet records merely because it serves the PM;
- a substitute for every ministry;
- the legal holder of each department’s statutory power; or
- automatically answerable in place of the minister-in-charge.

### 7. Cabinet Secretariat versus PMO

| Cabinet Secretariat | PMO |
|---|---|
| Supports Cabinet government and cross-ministry procedure | Supports the Prime Minister personally and institutionally |
| Headed by Cabinet Secretary | Led administratively by Principal Secretary and other PMO officers |
| Administers AoB/ToB Rules | Processes PM-level priorities and advice |
| Custodian of Cabinet process and records | Not identical to Cabinet process |

Both can improve coordination. Both can contribute to excessive centralisation if routine decisions migrate upward.

### 8. The “court government” problem

**Critique:** Strong central staff may create a “court government” in which access to the PM matters more than departmental competence, and ministers become implementers of decisions formed elsewhere.

**Reply:** Cross-cutting programmes, crises and inter-ministerial disputes require apex coordination.

**Residual:** The test is whether decisions occur at the **lowest competent level**, required consultations remain real, and the responsible minister can still explain and defend the decision.

### 9. Link with Cabinet dominance

Ministries give the executive an information advantage. Government departments:

- draft Bills and delegated legislation;
- prepare budgets;
- control policy data;
- schedule much government business; and
- supply replies to Parliament.

This machinery can marginalise legislative scrutiny unless committees, questions, audit, disclosure and judicial review are effectively used.

```text
        ↓
ToB defines consultation and escalation
        ↓
Cabinet Secretariat coordinates
        ↓
minister and department remain answerable
```

### Revision notes

1. Article 77(3) supports both allocation and transaction rules.
2. AoB answers who handles a subject.
3. ToB answers how a decision travels.
4. Portfolio, ministry, department and secretariat are distinct.
5. The minister is political head; the Secretary is administrative head and principal official adviser.
6. Central Secretariat means collective policy-support machinery, not Cabinet Secretariat alone.
7. Implementation may be delegated; accountability is not erased.
8. Cabinet Secretariat functions under the PM and is headed by the Cabinet Secretary.
9. It services Cabinet, administers business rules and coordinates ministries.
10. PMO supports the PM but is non-constitutional and non-statutory.
11. PMO and Cabinet Secretariat are not synonyms.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is confusing the Allocation of Business Rules with the Transaction of Business Rules a substantive error rather than a naming mistake?

**Model answer:** Allocation determines which ministry or department owns a subject, while transaction determines consultation, escalation and approval procedure. A body may possess subject jurisdiction yet still act unlawfully or irregularly if it bypasses the required decision route.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that the office coordinating a proposal thereby acquires the legal power assigned to the responsible ministry or statutory authority.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Central coordination can cure departmental silos but also weaken ministerial responsibility. Analyse with reference to the Cabinet Secretariat and PMO.

**Model answer — 164 words:**
Modern policy crosses departmental boundaries. Article 77(3) therefore supports Allocation of Business Rules, which identify subject ownership, and Transaction of Business Rules, which require consultation and escalation.

The Cabinet Secretariat cures silos by servicing Cabinet and Cabinet Committees, administering business rules, recording decisions and using Committees of Secretaries to resolve differences. The PMO supports the Prime Minister, monitors priorities and brings unresolved or strategic matters to apex attention. Both are valuable during crises and cross-government programmes.

Centralisation becomes harmful when routine files move upward, departmental expertise is bypassed or ministers cannot explain decisions formed through informal channels. The PMO is a non-constitutional, non-statutory staff agency; it does not acquire every ministry’s statutory authority. The Cabinet Secretariat is a coordinating institution, not a super-ministry.

The appropriate test is subsidiarity with accountability: decisions should be taken at the lowest competent level, required consultations should be recorded, and the minister-in-charge should remain answerable to Parliament. Coordination should connect responsible departments, not dissolve them into an unaccountable centre.

The debate is not whether the PM is important. It is whether the Cabinet remains a genuine collective decision body or becomes an approval forum for PM-centred choices.

### 1. From “first among equals” to central leadership

“Primus inter pares” captures the PM’s formal membership in a collective ministry. It understates the office’s structural advantages:

- the PM forms the ministerial team;
- assigns and reshuffles portfolios;
- controls access to senior office;
- coordinates Cabinet agenda;
- leads the governing party;
- communicates with the President;
- advises dissolution;
- speaks for the government nationally; and
- directs central staff and crisis coordination.

Crossman and Mackintosh used “prime-ministerial government” to describe migration of power from Cabinet toward the PM.

### 2. Why party organisation matters

A PM who controls a cohesive majority can use:

- the party whip;
- anti-defection consequences;
- control of candidatures and promotion;
- government command over parliamentary time; and
- public identification of the government with the leader.

This can convert legislative support into executive dominance. Anti-defection stabilises the government but may reduce independent backbench scrutiny.

### 3. Information and agenda power

Power includes deciding:

- what reaches Cabinet;
- which alternatives are examined;
- who attends;
- when a decision is taken; and
- which institution monitors implementation.

PMO and Cabinet Secretariat coordination can increase coherence. If information reaches ministers only through a narrow central channel, collective deliberation may become formal rather than substantive.

### 4. The case for central leadership

Concentration is not inherently unconstitutional. A national executive requires:

- clear responsibility;
- rapid crisis response;
- inter-ministerial coordination;
- negotiation with States and foreign governments;
- resolution of departmental conflict; and
- communication of a coherent programme.

Diffused authority can produce delay, contradiction and blame shifting.

### 5. Cabinet and parliamentary counterweights

The PM still requires:

- ministers with political and administrative capacity;
- Cabinet agreement or at least acquiescence;
- continuing Lok Sabha confidence;
- passage of legislation and supply;
- implementation by ministries and States; and
- lawful action capable of surviving judicial review.

Parliamentary committees, questions, financial control and debate preserve accountability where party discipline does not reduce them to form.

### 6. Coalition and federal variation

Under coalition government:

- allies negotiate portfolios;
- policy may require a common programme;
- regional parties can threaten withdrawal;
- Cabinet committees may distribute influence; and
- the PM becomes a broker as much as a commander.

Under a cohesive single-party majority, the same formal office can become far more dominant.

Federal politics also matters. States implement many programmes, control important policy fields and possess independent electoral legitimacy. A Union PM cannot treat State governments as subordinate departments.

### 7. Courts and constitutional limits

Article 74(2) does not shield unlawful action. Courts may review:

- competence;
- violation of rights;
- statutory compliance;
- mala fides;
- absence of relevant material; and
- unconstitutional procedure.

Judicial review cannot create day-to-day collegiality, but it prevents political dominance from becoming legal omnipotence.

### 8. Prime-ministerial government and presidentialisation

Presidentialisation is often used to describe leader-centred elections, personalised campaigns and centralised executive communication.

The term must be qualified:

- the PM has no separately fixed national executive tenure;
- the government remains removable by Lok Sabha confidence;
- ministers remain part of Parliament;
- the PM cannot veto legislation like a US President; and
- the office depends on party and parliamentary position.

India may display presidential **style** without becoming a presidential **system**.

### 9. Strongest objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Cabinet government is now a constitutional fiction because the PM controls appointments, agenda, party and communication.

**Reply:** Cabinet, Parliament, coalition arithmetic, federal implementation, courts and elections continue to impose real constraints, though their strength varies.

**Residual:** The formal system may remain intact while the quality of internal deliberation deteriorates. The proper reform target is transparent, informed and accountable collegiality—not artificial weakness at the centre.

### 10. UPSC application

For “critically examine”:

1. define prime-ministerial government as an analytical claim;
2. show at least four concentration mechanisms;
3. explain why concentration may improve capacity;
4. present Cabinet, parliamentary, federal and judicial limits;
5. vary the verdict by majority/coalition context; and
6. conclude that arithmetic and institutional practice determine degree.

### Revision notes

1. “Prime-ministerial government” is an analytical term, not constitutional text.
2. Portfolio and personnel control create structural leverage.
3. Article 78 makes the PM the President–Council channel.
4. Party leadership adds power not enumerated in the Constitution.
5. Whip and anti-defection can convert majority into executive agenda control.
6. Information and agenda control may be as important as formal voting power.
7. Central leadership can improve coherence and crisis response.
8. Cabinet deliberation and collective responsibility remain formal counterweights.
9. Lok Sabha confidence, legislation and supply constrain the PM.
10. Coalition allies turn the PM from commander into broker.
11. State governments constrain Union implementation and political reach.
12. Judicial review tests legality despite Article 74(2).
13. Presidential style does not create a presidential system.
14. PM dominance is strongest under cohesive majority conditions.

### Concept check

**Question:** What is the decisive difference between strong prime-ministerial leadership and conversion to a presidential system?

**Model answer:** A strong PM may dominate party, Cabinet and agenda, but still depends on Lok Sabha confidence, sits within a collective ministry, shares the legislature–executive fusion and lacks a separately fixed executive mandate. Presidential style can grow without changing these structural rules.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer a change of constitutional system merely from personalised elections, a powerful PMO or a strong parliamentary majority.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** “India has moved toward prime-ministerial government in practice without abandoning cabinet government in law.” Critically examine.

**Model answer — 206 words:**
India’s constitutional form remains cabinet government. Articles 74–75 create a Prime Minister-headed Council collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Yet political practice can concentrate power within that collective executive.

The Prime Minister selects ministers, allocates portfolios, controls reshuffles, coordinates Cabinet agenda and acts as the Article 78 channel to the President. Party leadership, anti-defection discipline, command over parliamentary business, dissolution advice, PMO support and leader-centred electoral politics add further leverage. Under a cohesive majority, Cabinet may appear to ratify decisions shaped around the Prime Minister.

Concentration can improve coherence, crisis response and inter-ministerial coordination. It is not automatically unconstitutional. The danger arises when agenda and information control hollow out genuine Cabinet deliberation and Parliament becomes a voting chamber rather than a scrutiny institution.

Important limits remain. The ministry depends on Lok Sabha confidence and supply; ministers retain political weight; coalition allies bargain over policy and personnel; States control major implementation fields; courts review unlawful action; and elections impose public accountability. Article 74(2) protects confidential advice, not illegality.

Therefore, India’s legal form is constant while political reality varies. A cohesive majority produces prime-ministerial dominance; coalition arithmetic restores bargaining and collegiality. Reform should strengthen Cabinet information, parliamentary committees and reasoned departmental responsibility rather than weaken necessary national leadership.

| Axis | Indian PM | US President | British PM | Indian Chief Minister |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Executive form | Real head within parliamentary dual executive | Single elected executive head | Real head under constitutional monarchy | Real State executive under Governor |

The comparison must explain why the designs produce different incentives, not merely list labels.

### 1. Indian PM and US President: constitutional position

The US President combines head-of-State and head-of-government functions and serves a fixed term. The executive is institutionally separate from Congress. Cabinet secretaries are not legislators and do not collectively depend on House confidence.

The Indian PM is head of government within a dual executive. Ministers sit in Parliament, and the Council remains collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.

### 2. Actual authority can reverse formal expectations

The US President appears formally stronger because of:

- fixed tenure;
- separate national mandate through the presidential electoral system;
- veto power;
- command role;
- executive appointments; and
- institutional separation from Congress.

Yet separation also creates independent legislative resistance. A President may face an opposition-controlled Congress, Senate confirmation constraints, judicial review and federal limits.

An Indian PM lacks a fixed independent tenure but may command a disciplined legislative majority. When party control is cohesive, the PM can direct both executive policy and much of the legislative agenda.

> **Comparative insight:** Separation may strengthen constitutional independence while weakening day-to-day legislative control; fusion may weaken formal independence while strengthening operational authority under a stable majority.

### 3. Limits on the Indian PM

The Indian PM:

- can lose office through Lok Sabha confidence;
- cannot personally veto a Bill passed by Parliament;
- cannot govern without a Council;
- depends on party and coalition support;
- requires Parliament for legislation and supply;
- works within federal distribution; and
- remains subject to judicial review.

### 4. Limits on the US President

The US President:

- cannot dissolve the House of Representatives;
- cannot remove Congress through a confidence mechanism;
- needs Congress for legislation and appropriations;
- faces Senate advice and consent in important appointments and treaties;
- may face judicial invalidation; and
- shares power with constitutionally autonomous States.

The offices are therefore not direct functional equivalents.

### 5. India and Britain

Both follow responsible parliamentary government, but:

- India is a republic; Britain is a constitutional monarchy.
- India has a written supreme Constitution; Britain’s constitution is uncodified and Parliament is legally sovereign.
- India’s PM may belong to either House; Britain’s PM is conventionally from the Commons.
- Indian governmental action is subject to constitutional judicial review.

The similarities explain Westminster influence; the differences explain why Britain cannot be mechanically copied into an Indian answer.

### 6. State counterpart

Articles 163 and 164 broadly parallel Articles 74 and 75:

- Governor corresponds to President;
- Chief Minister corresponds to PM;
- State Council is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly;
- the six-month non-member rule appears in Article 164(4); and
- the State ministry cap is 15% of Assembly strength, with a constitutional minimum of twelve.

The parallel is not exact. Article 163 recognises constitutionally conferred gubernatorial discretion in specified fields, while the President has no similarly worded general discretion clause.

Detailed Governor, assent and State-ministry controversies require their own treatment. Here, the State parallel is used only to prevent close-option confusion.

### 8. The 2026 comparative demand

The 2026 GS-II question asks two connected tasks:

1. compare Indian separation of powers with the more rigid US presidential model; and
2. compare the **actual authority** of the Indian PM and US President.

The answer should not declare one universally stronger. Use conditional authority:

```text

→ strong legislative agenda control

US President with divided government
→ greater tenure independence but legislative bargaining

→ weaker personnel and policy control

```

The **2018 GS-II demand**—“India and USA are two large democracies. Examine the basic tenets on which the two political systems are based.”—is the broader antecedent. It requires constitutional supremacy, federalism, rights, judicial review and executive design; the 2026 demand narrows the comparative focus to separation of powers and actual executive authority.

### 9. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Comparison becomes meaningless because party systems and political contexts change.

**Reply:** Constitutional design still determines tenure, removal, membership, dissolution, veto and accountability. Context explains how those structures operate.

**Residual:** “Actual authority” must always be dated to political conditions, but the answer should compare institutional capacities rather than personalities.

### 10. Final answer line

> The Indian Prime Minister is constitutionally dependent but may be politically dominant; the US President is constitutionally independent but may be legislatively constrained.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Constitutional location of power

**Question:** Connect Articles 53, 74, 75 and 77 without describing either the President or the Prime Minister as constitutionally redundant.

**Model answer:** Article 53 supplies formal executive vesting; Article 74 requires PM-headed ministerial advice; Article 75 makes that ministry politically responsible to the Lok Sabha; Article 77 gives official action formal expression and authentication. The President preserves constitutional form and continuity, while ministers choose and defend policy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Formal vesting and political responsibility are complementary, not competing claims to personal executive ownership.

## Check 2 — Office and confidence

**Question:** A Rajya Sabha member is appointed Prime Minister after presenting coalition support but before a floor test. Which issues must be analysed separately?

**Model answer:** Membership of Rajya Sabha is constitutionally permissible. Appointment may precede proved confidence where support claims require testing. The President must rely on objective material and arrange a prompt Lok Sabha floor test. Eligibility, appointment and continuing confidence are separate questions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat Lok Sabha confidence as a requirement that the PM personally be a Lok Sabha member.

## Check 3 — Responsibility and confidentiality

**Question:** Distinguish collective responsibility, Cabinet confidentiality, the Third Schedule secrecy oath and Article 74(2).

**Model answer:** Article 75(3) makes the ministry publicly accountable as one body to the Lok Sabha. Cabinet confidentiality permits candid internal deliberation before ministers defend the final decision collectively. The Third Schedule binds each minister not to reveal official matters except as required for proper duties. Article 74(2) narrowly bars judicial inquiry into whether and what advice was tendered to the President; it does not shield every record or unlawful resulting act.

**Misconception to avoid:** These doctrines share a concern for responsible decision-making but do not create one unlimited secrecy privilege.

## Check 4 — Internal executive bodies

**Question:** Place Council, Cabinet, Cabinet Committee, kitchen cabinet, Cabinet Secretariat and PMO in their correct institutional categories.

**Model answer:** Council is the wider constitutional ministry; Cabinet is its senior decision core; Cabinet Committees are extra-constitutional ministerial mechanisms; kitchen cabinet is an informal inner circle; Cabinet Secretariat is the Cabinet-support and coordination institution; PMO is the PM’s non-constitutional, non-statutory staff agency.

**Misconception to avoid:** Shared proximity to the Prime Minister does not make these bodies legally interchangeable.

## Check 5 — Final synthesis

**Question:** What determines whether the same constitutional office appears collegial in one period and dominant in another?

**Model answer:** The formal powers remain broadly constant, but party cohesion, majority size, coalition dependence, ministerial stature, federal politics, parliamentary scrutiny, information flows and judicial intervention alter operational authority. Prime-ministerial dominance is therefore institutionally enabled but politically variable.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not explain changes in executive authority only through individual personality.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain why Article 74(2) is a confidentiality clause rather than a general immunity clause for executive action.

**Model answer — 123 words:**
Article 74(2) prevents courts from inquiring into whether ministers tendered advice to the President and what that advice contained. Its purpose is to preserve confidential consultation within the parliamentary executive.

The clause does not place every resulting action beyond judicial review. Courts may still examine whether the government possessed legal authority, followed mandatory procedure, considered relevant material, respected fundamental rights and acted without mala fides. Authentication in the President’s name under Article 77 likewise cannot cure substantive illegality.

The distinction protects two constitutional interests. Ministers can advise the head of State candidly without later disclosure of internal deliberation, while citizens retain judicial remedies against unlawful state action.

Therefore, Article 74(2) shields the advisory conversation, not the legal validity of what government ultimately does.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** exact scope of Article 74(2) 3; reviewable action categories 3; Article 77 connection 1; competing constitutional interests 2; conclusion 1.

## Final 15-mark question

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Ministerial responsibility in India is constitutionally strong but institutionally uneven. Examine.

**Model answer — 187 words:**
India’s Constitution gives ministerial responsibility a strong foundation. Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, so loss of confidence removes the entire ministry. Cabinet solidarity requires ministers to defend common policy or resign. Article 75(2) supports individual responsibility, effectively enforced by the Prime Minister through resignation, reshuffle or removal advice.

Responsibility also operates departmentally through questions, debates, grants, committees, CAG scrutiny and judicial review. Article 78 requires the PM to keep the President informed and permits individual ministerial decisions to be returned for collective consideration.

Its institutional operation is uneven. Party whips and anti-defection discipline may turn parliamentary scrutiny into majority ratification. Administrative complexity disperses decisions across civil servants, regulators and missions. Coalition ministers may criticise policy publicly, while strong PMO coordination can obscure departmental authorship. Individual resignations for administrative failure are inconsistent.

The solution is not to fragment authority. Parliament needs stronger committee scrutiny and information access; Cabinet procedure should preserve recorded deliberation; and ministers must remain visibly answerable for delegated administration.

Thus, responsibility is clear in constitutional design but depends on institutions capable of converting formal answerability into examination and consequence.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** collective doctrine 3; individual/departmental mechanisms 3; institutional weaknesses 4; named provisions 2; reforms 2; graded conclusion 1.

## Final 20-mark question

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Evaluate whether the growth of central coordinating institutions has strengthened cabinet government or accelerated prime-ministerial government in India.

**Model answer — 212 words:**
Central coordination is indispensable to contemporary cabinet government. Cross-ministry programmes, security crises and complex regulation require common priorities, consultation and implementation tracking. The Cabinet Secretariat supports Cabinet and Cabinet Committees, administers the Allocation and Transaction of Business Rules and resolves inter-departmental differences. The PMO supplies the Prime Minister with advice and monitoring capacity.

These institutions can strengthen cabinet government by improving information, preventing silos and ensuring that collective decisions are implemented. Cabinet Committees similarly permit specialised ministerial examination without burdening the full Cabinet.

They can also accelerate prime-ministerial government. When agenda, information and monitoring concentrate around the PM, routine departmental decisions may migrate upward. Ministers may become implementers of choices formed through narrow channels, while an informal inner circle eclipses Cabinet deliberation. A cohesive parliamentary majority and party discipline amplify this effect.

The distinction turns on procedure and responsibility. Coordination strengthens Cabinet when affected ministries participate, alternatives and dissent are recorded, committees have clear mandates, and the responsible minister can defend the decision in Parliament. It produces “court government” when access substitutes for competence and central staff silently exercise departmental authority.

India requires strong central leadership, but not responsibility-free centralisation. The proper model is coordinated collegiality: apex direction for genuinely cross-government matters, subsidiarity for routine decisions, and effective parliamentary scrutiny of both.

**Rubric — 20 marks:** coordination rationale 3; Cabinet Secretariat/PMO mechanisms 4; cabinet-strengthening case 3; prime-ministerial risks 4; evaluative test 3; reforms and balanced verdict 3.

# REMEDIATION

## Error 1 — “The President is the real executive because Article 53 vests power”

Article 53 states formal vesting. Articles 74–75 explain ministerial exercise and democratic responsibility.

## Error 2 — “Article 74(2) prevents courts from reviewing executive action”

It prevents inquiry into ministerial advice. Courts may still review legality, competence, procedure, rights and relevant material.

## Error 3 — “The PM must belong to Lok Sabha”

The PM may belong to either House or initially be a non-member for six consecutive months. The ministry, however, must command Lok Sabha confidence.

## Error 4 — “The Constitution creates four ministerial ranks”

The Constitution appoints ministers without prescribing such a rank classification. Cabinet Minister, Minister of State and Deputy Minister are organisational categories.

## Error 5 — “Article 88 itself gives ministers a voting right wherever they participate”

Article 88 allows participation in either House but gives no voting right by itself. Voting in one’s House follows House membership; voting at a joint sitting or in a committee depends on parliamentary or committee membership and the applicable procedure. A non-member minister cannot vote.

## Error 6 — “Cabinet and Council of Ministers are interchangeable”

Council is the wider constitutional ministry; Cabinet is the smaller senior decision core.

## Error 7 — “Cabinet Committees have a permanent list and chair pattern”

Their number, names, membership and chairs are notification-sensitive. Use the dated official document and do not infer missing details.

## Error 8 — “Extra-constitutional means unconstitutional”

Cabinet Committees, kitchen cabinet and PMO are not expressly created by the Constitution, but may operate lawfully within constitutional and statutory competence.

## Error 9 — “Ambedkar resigned over the Hindu Code Bill in 1953”

Ambedkar tendered his resignation in **1951**. Use the episode carefully as an illustration of ministerial solidarity.

## Error 10 — “A strong PM proves India has become presidential”

The PM still depends on Lok Sabha confidence and leads a collective ministry. Strong leadership or presidential style does not replace parliamentary structure.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Responsible-government spine

- Article 53 formally vests Union executive power in the President.
- Article 73 extends Union executive power to Parliament's legislative field and treaty/agreement authority.
- Article 73's proviso ordinarily preserves State execution in shared fields unless the Constitution or parliamentary law expressly provides otherwise.
- Article 74 creates the PM-headed Council to aid and advise the President.
- Forty-second Amendment made advice expressly binding.
- Forty-fourth Amendment permits one reconsideration; reconsidered advice binds.
- Article 74(2) protects whether and what advice was tendered.
- Article 74(2) does not bar review of the resulting action’s legality.
- Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible only to Lok Sabha.
- Article 77 formalises action in the President’s name and supports business rules.
- Article 78 makes the PM the constitutional information channel.

## Appointment and tenure of the Prime Minister

- Article 75(1) prescribes appointment by the President but no detailed selection method.
- Clear Lok Sabha majority: recognised leader must be appointed.
- Hung House: bounded judgment based on objective support; confidence belongs on the floor.
- Appointment may precede proof of confidence.
- Sudden death without an agreed successor may require temporary presidential judgment.
- PM may belong to either House.
- *S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda* (1996): non-member PM permissible subject to Article 75(5).
- Six consecutive months without membership ends ministerial office.
- Oaths of office and secrecy are administered by the President.
- PM has no fixed term; presidential pleasure is governed by Lok Sabha confidence.

## PM’s principal relationships

- Recommends appointment of other ministers.
- Allocates and reshuffles portfolios.
- May demand resignation or advise removal of a minister.
- Chairs and coordinates the ministry.
- PM resignation or death ends the existing ministry politically.
- Article 78: communicate Council decisions, furnish requested information, place individual ministerial matters before Council when required.
- Leads governmental policy in Parliament and may advise dissolution.
- Party leadership and majority discipline amplify constitutional position.
- Appointment influence must be stated through the procedure applicable to the named office.
- Standard appointment examples include AG, CAG, UPSC, Election Commission and Finance Commission processes, each subject to its own legal route.
- Standard coordinating-body examples include NITI Aayog, Inter-State Council, National Integration Council and National Water Resources Council; current arrangements must be verified when specifically asked.
- Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, P.V. Narasimha Rao, H.D. Deve Gowda and Narendra Modi had prior Chief Minister experience.
- Morley, Jennings, Laski and Munro are attributed analytical characterisations, not law.

## Continuity and caretaker control

- *U.N.R. Rao* (1971): Council does not disappear merely because Lok Sabha is dissolved.
- Article 74 prevents ordinary personal presidential government.
- *Shamsher Singh* (1974): formal satisfaction ordinarily operates through ministerial advice.
- Caretaker government is a situational convention, not a separate constitutional category.
- Outgoing government performs routine and urgent administration.
- It should avoid avoidable irreversible policy, appointments and partisan commitments.
- National security, safety, legal deadlines and financial stability may require major action.
- Tarkunde Committee guidance, the 1979 presidential assurance and the 1979 Calcutta High Court observation support restraint without paralysis.

## Article 75 detail

- 75(1): PM appointed by President; others on PM advice.
- 75(1A): total Union ministers including PM ≤ 15% of total Lok Sabha membership.
- 75(1B): a political-party member of either House disqualified under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule is barred from ministership for the stated period.
- Article 75(1B) bar ends on normal term expiry or earlier re-election, as constitutionally specified.
- 75(2): individual pleasure.
- 75(3): collective responsibility to Lok Sabha.
- 75(4): oaths.
- 75(5): six-month non-member rule.
- 75(6): salaries and allowances determined by Parliament.
- Ninety-first Amendment, 2003 inserted the cap and office bar.

## Article 88 precision

- Ministers and Attorney-General may speak and participate in either House, joint sitting and relevant committees.
- Article 88 gives no voting right by itself.
- Voting in one’s House follows House membership; joint-sitting and committee voting follow the applicable membership and procedural rules.
- A non-member minister may speak but cannot vote in either House, a joint sitting or a committee.

## Ministerial categories

- Constitution does not classify four ranks.
- Cabinet Ministers form senior decision core.
- Ministers of State may hold independent charge or be attached.
- Deputy Ministers traditionally assist without independent charge.
- Actual category use varies.
- Deputy Prime Minister is descriptive only; no separate constitutional powers.
- Traditional Union parliamentary secretaries were PM-appointed assistants without departments and were not Council members merely by that designation.

## Responsibility

- Collective responsibility has confidence and solidarity dimensions.
- No-confidence defeat removes the whole ministry, including Rajya Sabha ministers.
- Cabinet decisions bind ministers publicly.
- Minister who cannot defend common policy should resign.
- Ambedkar tendered resignation in **1951** in the Hindu Code Bill context.
- Individual responsibility is effectively enforced by the PM under Article 75(2).
- Departmental responsibility uses questions, finance, committees, audit and review.
- India has no British-style constitutional countersignature system of legal responsibility.
- Cabinet confidentiality enables candid deliberation and supports public solidarity after decision.
- The Third Schedule oath bars disclosure of ministerial matters except where proper discharge of duties requires it.
- Article 74(2) bars inquiry into whether and what advice was tendered to the President; it does not immunise unlawful action or every underlying record.

## Council, Cabinet and inner circle

- Council = wider constitutional ministry.
- Cabinet = smaller Cabinet-rank decision core.
- Actual sizes are not fixed.
- “Cabinet” entered express constitutional text in Article 352(3) through the Forty-fourth Amendment.
- Cabinet handles major policy, legislation, finance, appointments and crisis coordination.
- Foreign affairs and central administrative coordination are also classic Cabinet functions.
- Kitchen cabinet = informal PM-centred circle; no independent legal authority.
- Bagehot’s “hyphen” explains executive-legislative fusion.
- Muir, Lowell, Marriott, Gladstone, Barker, Jennings and Amery supply attributed descriptive vocabulary for Cabinet direction and coordination.
- Cabinet dominance is about weak scrutiny, not British-style parliamentary sovereignty.

## Business machinery

- Article 77(3) supports AoB and ToB Rules.
- AoB = who owns the subject.
- ToB = how the proposal is processed.
- Portfolio = political charge.
- Ministry = broad political-administrative unit.
- Department = allocated subject unit.
- Secretariat = policy, drafting, finance, coordination and oversight.
- Minister = political head; Secretary = administrative head and principal official adviser.
- Cabinet Secretariat functions under PM, headed by Cabinet Secretary.
- It services Cabinet, administers business rules and coordinates ministries.
- PMO supports PM; non-constitutional and non-statutory.
- PMO is not Cabinet Secretariat or a super-ministry.
- Committee of Secretaries is an official-level coordination mechanism.
- Lowest-competent-level decision plus visible ministerial accountability is the governing test.

## Prime-ministerial-government debate

- Concentrating mechanisms: portfolios, personnel, party, agenda, Article 78, dissolution advice, PMO and public leadership.
- Benefits: coherence, speed, crisis response and conflict resolution.
- Risks: Cabinet ratification, information monopoly, parliamentary marginalisation and departmental weakness.
- Constraints: collective responsibility, Cabinet stature, coalition allies, Lok Sabha confidence, Parliament, States, courts, elections.
- Single-party majority generally strengthens PM dominance.
- Coalition arithmetic generally strengthens brokerage and collegial bargaining.
- Presidential style does not convert India into a presidential system.

## Comparative rapid recall

- Indian PM: confidence-dependent but may control legislature through majority.
- US President: fixed and separately elected but may face divided Congress.
- Indian PM may belong to either House.
- British PM is conventionally from Commons.
- India has constitutional supremacy and judicial review; Britain has parliamentary sovereignty.
- Articles 163–164 provide State analogues.
- State Council is collectively responsible to Legislative Assembly.
- Article 163’s discretion language makes Governor–President comparison non-identical.

## PYQ answer routes

- 2018 India–USA: compare constitutional design, not personalities.
- 2020 parliamentary system: identify responsibility and removability.
- 2020 State minister: separate eligibility, six-month rule and RPA consequences.
- 2021 executive accountability: formal tools plus practical limitations.
- 2022 ministerial ranks/cap: Constitution versus convention; Lok Sabha ceiling.
- 2024 Cabinet dominance: fusion, whip, agenda and correctives; supremacy is not sovereignty.
- 2026 PM versus US President: formal independence versus operational authority under party conditions.
