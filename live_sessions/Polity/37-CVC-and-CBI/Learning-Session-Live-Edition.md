# CVC and CBI — Live Session Edition

India runs two anti-corruption institutions that most learners quietly assume are twins: the same kind of body, created the same way, controlled the same way. They are not. One is a statutory watchdog that can only advise. The other is a police organisation that was never given its own Act of Parliament at all. Confusing the two — their legal birth, their powers, their leash — is the single most common way marks are lost on this topic. This session untangles exactly that.

### The learner's problem

Ask most people "is the CBI a statutory body?" and they will say yes — after all, it investigates under a law, it has a Director, it looks exactly like a statutory organisation. That instinct is wrong, and UPSC exploits it relentlessly. The CBI has never had its own Act of Parliament. What it has is an executive resolution that created the *organisation*, plus a different 1946 Act that gives its investigators their *police powers*. Get this split wrong and an entire answer collapses.

### Plain-language intuition

Think of the CBI as a company that was never formally incorporated by its own charter, but whose employees happen to also hold valid police badges issued under a different, older law. The "company" (CBI) is the 1963 resolution. The "police badge" (power to search, arrest, investigate) is the Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946. The badge is real and statutory; the company wearing it is not.

The CVC, by contrast, is like a professional regulator that started as an internal departmental committee in 1964 and was later given full legal personality by its own Act — the CVC Act, 2003 — so that today its existence, composition and powers are all textually fixed in a statute, not merely described in a government order.

### Technical doctrine

**CVC (Central Vigilance Commission):** a statutory, multi-member vigilance and advisory institution. Its legal existence was created by Government resolution in 1964 (following the Santhanam Committee on Prevention of Corruption, 1962–64) and converted into statutory status by the **Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003**. It is not a constitutional body — it appears nowhere in the Constitution.

**CBI (Central Bureau of Investigation):** an organisation set up by a **Government of India (Home Ministry) resolution dated 1 April 1963**, on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation. It has **no dedicated statute of its own**. Its investigating wing is formally the Investigation and Anti-Corruption Division, and the police powers its officers exercise are drawn from the DSPE Act.

**DSPE (Delhi Special Police Establishment):** the statutory police establishment created by the **Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946**. Section 2 gives DSPE members police powers in Union Territories; later sections (covered in Lesson 6) extend that power to States subject to consent. When people loosely say "CBI has powers under the DSPE Act," what is precise is that **DSPE — the police establishment inside the CBI organisation — has those powers**, and CBI personnel exercise them as DSPE officers.

### *Navendra Kumar v Union of India* (2013) — the 1963 resolution's unresolved challenge

[FACT] The 1963 resolution just described as the CBI's legal origin has itself been judicially challenged. On **6 November 2013**, a Division Bench of the **Gauhati High Court** (Justices **I.A. Ansari and Indira Shah**), deciding a writ appeal in ***Navendra Kumar v Union of India***, **quashed the Home Ministry resolution dated 1 April 1963** that constitutes the CBI. The Court held that the resolution was never a Union Cabinet decision and was never assented to by the President, so it could at best count as "departmental instructions," not "law"; that the CBI is **neither an organ nor a part of the DSPE** and cannot be treated as a "police force" constituted under the DSPE Act, 1946; and that the CBI's actions — registering a case, arresting a person, conducting search and seizure, prosecuting an accused — therefore offended **Article 21** for want of valid "procedure established by law."

[FACT] The Union Government moved the Supreme Court at once. On **9 November 2013** — three days later, at an emergency hearing before the Chief Justice — the **Supreme Court stayed the operation of the Gauhati High Court's judgment**. The special leave petition has remained pending since, and the Supreme Court has **not yet finally ruled** on whether the Gauhati High Court's reasoning is correct.

```text
Gauhati HC, 6 Nov 2013: quashes the 1963 resolution; CBI's actions held to offend Article 21
                          |
            Union moves the Supreme Court on an SLP
                          |
       Supreme Court stays the Gauhati HC judgment (9 Nov 2013)
                          |
     A stay suspends ENFORCEMENT only — it does not affirm the resolution's validity
                          |
  SLP still pending: the CBI's foundational validity is UNRESOLVED, not settled either way
```

[LIMIT] A stay order suspends a judgment's operation; it does not erase the judgment's reasoning and it is not itself a ruling on the merits. The only accurate description of the present legal position is that the Supreme Court has not decided whether the Gauhati High Court was right — the 1963 resolution continues to operate in practice solely **because** the quashing order is under stay, not because the Supreme Court has upheld or validated it.

⚠️ **UPSC trap — three wrong formulations to avoid:**
- "The Supreme Court held the CBI unconstitutional" — wrong court; only the Gauhati High Court so held.
- "The CBI has been declared illegal/invalid," stated as a final, operative fact — ignores the unvacated stay.
- "The Supreme Court upheld/validated the 1963 resolution" — no such final ruling exists; only an interim stay does.

> 🔑 **Safe exam line:** *A High Court quashed the CBI's founding resolution in 2013; the Supreme Court stayed that quashing within days; the CBI's foundational validity remains sub judice.*

### The diagnostic test

A fast way to check any claim about CVC or CBI: ask "does this claim describe something textually written into a specific Act, or something merely organisational?"

```text
CLAIM: "CVC can issue binding disciplinary orders."
   -> Is this in the CVC Act's text? No — advice, not command (Lesson 5).
   -> FALSE as stated.

CLAIM: "CBI is a statutory body under the DSPE Act."
   -> Is the CBI organisation itself created by that Act? No.
   -> IMPRECISE: the DSPE (not the whole CBI) is the statutory part.

CLAIM: "Neither CVC nor CBI is a constitutional body."
   -> Does either appear in the Constitution's text? No.
   -> TRUE for both.

CLAIM: "The Supreme Court has declared the CBI's 1963 resolution invalid."
   -> Did the Supreme Court decide this on merits? No — only the Gauhati High Court so held, and the Supreme Court has merely stayed that judgment.
   -> FALSE as stated: conflates a stayed High Court holding with a final Supreme Court ruling.
```

### Examples and their limits

Consider a corruption complaint against a central government officer. The complaint might reach the CVC as the vigilance apex (an advisory, statutory institution), get referred for investigation to the CBI/DSPE (an executive-created organisation exercising statutory police power), and eventually be tried in a competent court (the only body that can determine guilt). Three different legal characters, three different roles, one case. The example clarifies the three-way split, but it does not mean every complaint follows this exact three-step path — Lesson 12 shows how Lokpal references complicate the routing.

### Comparison: resolution versus statute

| Dimension | Executive resolution (CBI, 1963) | Parliamentary statute (DSPE Act 1946; CVC Act 2003) |
|---|---|---|
| Creates | Organisational form, administrative business allocation | Defined legal powers, jurisdiction, safeguards |
| Can be altered by | The executive, without Parliament | Only by Parliament (or delegated rule-making within statutory limits) |
| Example here | CBI organisation itself | DSPE's police powers; CVC's composition, tenure, removal and section 8 functions |
| Risk if conflated | Overstating CBI's legal insulation; assuming it has statutory tenure/budget protection as an entity | — |

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If DSPE has statutory police powers and DSPE is inside CBI, isn't it fair to just call CBI statutory for exam purposes?"

**Reply:** No — this is exactly the shortcut UPSC traps punish. The *DSPE* (a defined statutory police establishment) having powers does not make the *CBI* (the broader organisation that also does non-DSPE administrative and economic-offence work under the same resolution-based umbrella) a statutory body. A precise answer always separates "the organisation was created by resolution" from "its investigators act with statutory police power through DSPE."

**Qualified residual:** The safest formulation, usable in any answer: *"CBI is an executive-created organisation; its core investigative powers derive from the statutory DSPE Act, 1946; CVC alone among the two is itself a statutory body, under the CVC Act, 2003."*

### UPSC application

This distinction is the most frequently tested single fact in the CVC/CBI block, appearing as a direct Prelims statement-matching trap and as the opening sentence of any Mains answer on either institution. A Mains answer that begins "Both CVC and CBI are statutory bodies" loses credibility in its first line; begin instead by naming each institution's exact legal source. A closely related trap tests whether the 1963 resolution has itself been invalidated: the correct answer is that the Gauhati High Court's 2013 quashing in *Navendra Kumar* remains under an unvacated Supreme Court stay and is legally unresolved — never cite it as a settled invalidation or a settled validation.

### Revision notes

1. CVC = statutory since the CVC Act, 2003; originated as a 1964 executive resolution.
2. CBI = executive organisation, created by a Government of India resolution dated 1 April 1963; has no dedicated Act.
3. DSPE = statutory police establishment under the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946.
4. DSPE sits inside the CBI organisation as its Investigation and Anti-Corruption Division.
5. CBI's police powers are exercised through DSPE, not through an independent "CBI Act."
6. Neither CVC nor CBI is a constitutional body.
7. The Santhanam Committee (1962–64) recommended both the CVC and the CBI.
8. "Statutory" describes a specific legal source, not organisational prestige or public importance.
9. The safest exam phrase separates organisation, statute and powers into three distinct clauses.
10. A claim that is true of DSPE is not automatically true of the whole CBI organisation.
11. *Navendra Kumar v Union of India* (2013): the Gauhati High Court quashed the 1963 resolution on 6 November 2013; the Supreme Court stayed that judgment on 9 November 2013; the CBI's foundational validity remains unresolved/sub judice — never cite it as a settled invalidation or a settled validation.

### Concept check

**Question:** A statement reads: "The CBI derives its existence and all its powers directly from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946." Is this statement accurate? Why or why not?

**Model answer:** It is inaccurate in a precise sense. The CBI *organisation* was created by a 1963 executive resolution, not by the DSPE Act. The DSPE Act, 1946 creates the statutory police establishment (DSPE) that functions as a division within the CBI and supplies its officers' police powers. So the Act supplies powers exercised within CBI, but it did not create the CBI as an organisation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "CBI's investigators have statutory police powers" as equivalent to "CBI is a statutory body." The power's statutory source and the organisation's legal origin are two separate facts that must both be stated correctly.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish precisely between the legal character of the Central Vigilance Commission and the Central Bureau of Investigation, identifying the exact legal instrument behind each.

**Model solution:** The Central Vigilance Commission is a statutory body under the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003, having originated as an executive resolution in 1964 following the Santhanam Committee. The Central Bureau of Investigation, by contrast, is an organisation created by a Government of India resolution dated 1 April 1963; it has no dedicated parliamentary statute of its own. Its investigators exercise police powers as members of the Delhi Special Police Establishment, a statutory body created by the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946, which functions as a division within the CBI. Neither institution is constitutional. The precise formulation for an answer is: CVC is statutory; CBI is executive-created; DSPE, operating within CBI, is statutory and supplies investigative police power.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** CVC's statutory source correctly named — 2; CBI's executive-resolution origin correctly stated — 2; DSPE's statutory source and its relationship to CBI correctly explained — 3; explicit statement that neither is constitutional — 1; coherent 150-word structure with no conflation of the three — 2.

### The learner's problem

Why does India's premier anti-corruption machinery look so patched-together — one wartime police unit, one 1946 Act, two separate 1960s resolutions, a Supreme Court judgment doing the work legislation should have done, and amendments as recent as 2021? Because it was never designed as a single system. It accreted, crisis by crisis. Understanding the sequence is not mere trivia — each step explains why the present law looks the way it does.

### Visual: evolution timeline

*Caption: notice that the oldest institution (DSPE, 1946) is the most statutorily secure, while the organisation most people think of first (CBI, 1963) is the least secure — it was never re-founded by Parliament even while everything around it was being codified.*

### Plain-language intuition

Picture a government building that started as a single wartime office in 1941. Over eight decades, different governments added a new statutory wing here (1946), created a new department by order rather than law there (1963 CBI, 1964 CVC), and then — only after a Supreme Court judgment forced the issue — gave one of those departments (CVC) a proper legal charter in 2003. The building has survived, but its rooms have very different legal titles. Knowing the renovation history tells you why some rooms are statutorily "owned" and others are merely "occupied."

### Technical doctrine: the full sequence

1. **1941 — Special Police Establishment (SPE):** set up by executive order during the Second World War to investigate bribery and corruption connected with War and Supply Department transactions.
2. **1943 — wartime ordinance:** gave the SPE an interim legal framework during the war.
3. **1946 — Delhi Special Police Establishment Act:** gave the SPE (renamed DSPE) continuing statutory police powers beyond the war.
4. **1963 — CBI resolution:** the Union Home Ministry's resolution dated 1 April 1963 established the CBI as a broader organisation; the DSPE became its Investigation and Anti-Corruption Division. This resolution's own constitutional validity was later challenged and remains unresolved: the Gauhati High Court quashed it in 2013, and the Supreme Court has stayed that quashing ever since (full case treatment in Lesson 1).
5. **1964 — CVC resolution:** the Government created the CVC by resolution (dated 11 February 1964) on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation; at this stage CVC was purely an executive creation, with no statutory backing.
6. **1997 — *Vineet Narain v Union of India*:** arising from concern about investigative inertia in a politically sensitive case (the "Jain hawala" matter), the Supreme Court issued continuing-mandamus directions covering CVC's statutory status, CBI Director's tenure security, and professional investigative safeguards (full detail in Lesson 10).
7. **2003 — CVC Act:** Parliament finally gave the CVC statutory status, incorporating much of the *Vineet Narain* safeguard architecture into Sections 3-19.
8. **2013-14 — Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act:** created a new statutory ombudsman with its own Inquiry Wing and Prosecution Wing, adding a fresh complaint-routing and superintendence interface over CVC and DSPE for referred matters (Lesson 12).
9. **2021 — CVC (Amendment) Act and DSPE (Amendment) Act:** amended the tenure provisions to permit conditional, reasoned, one-year-at-a-time extensions for the CBI Director (and, under the parallel amendment, the Enforcement Directorate Director), subject to an aggregate five-year ceiling.
10. **2023 — *Dr Jaya Thakur v Union of India*** (2023 INSC 616): the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the 2021 extension framework, while separately holding that particular extensions granted to the then-Director of Enforcement under that framework had violated an earlier mandamus in unrelated proceedings — a fact-specific finding that must not be read as invalidating the amendment itself (detailed in Lesson 9).

### Examples and their limits

The 1946 Act is sometimes mistakenly treated as "the CBI's founding law" because it is the oldest instrument in the chain and the only one both bodies share indirectly (CBI's investigators use DSPE power). It is a useful anchor date, but it founded DSPE, not CBI — the organisation itself waited another seventeen years for its 1963 resolution, and the vigilance apex (CVC) waited until 2003 for equivalent statutory grounding.

### Comparison: judicial pressure versus legislative codification

| Step | Driven by | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1946 DSPE Act | Wartime necessity, then continuing need for a central police establishment | Statutory police power created |
| 1963/1964 resolutions | Administrative/policy decision following Santhanam Committee | Organisational bodies created without statute |
| 1997 *Vineet Narain* | Judicial intervention after perceived investigative inertia | Court-ordered safeguards pending legislation |
| 2003 CVC Act | Legislative codification of judicial directions | CVC becomes statutory |
| 2013 Lokpal Act | Legislative anti-corruption reform agenda | New ombudsman and referral architecture |
| 2021 amendments | Legislative response to administrative/tenure concerns | Conditional extension framework |
| 2023 *Jaya Thakur* | Judicial review of the 2021 framework | Framework's validity confirmed |

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If *Vineet Narain* (1997) already fixed CBI Director tenure and CVC's role, why did Parliament need to legislate again in 2003?"

**Reply:** Judicial directions in a continuing-mandamus case bind the parties and guide administration, but they are not a permanent substitute for legislation — they can be revisited, are vulnerable to administrative drift, and do not carry the same institutional permanence as a parliamentary statute. The 2003 Act converted judicially mandated practice into codified, amendable, Parliament-owned law — which is also why the 2021 amendments could later modify the tenure rules through the ordinary legislative process rather than through fresh litigation.

**Qualified residual:** The evolution shows a recurring Indian institutional pattern — executive origin, judicial correction, legislative codification — rather than a single clean constitutional design. No stage should be treated as final; the 2021 amendments show the sequence is still active.

### UPSC application

Prelims frequently tests exact years and the executive-versus-statutory character of each step (for example, confusing the 1963 CBI resolution with a statute, or misplacing the DSPE Act's year). Mains answers on CBI's institutional weaknesses almost always need this timeline as the opening paragraph, because the "no dedicated CBI Act" critique (Lesson 10) only makes sense once the reader sees that every other institution around CBI eventually got a statute while CBI did not.

### Revision notes

1. 1941: Special Police Establishment set up by executive order (wartime bribery/corruption in War and Supply transactions).
2. 1943: wartime ordinance gave SPE an interim legal framework.
3. 1946: Delhi Special Police Establishment Act — DSPE gets continuing statutory police powers.
4. 1963 (1 April): CBI established by Home Ministry resolution; DSPE becomes its division (this resolution's own validity is unresolved since *Navendra Kumar*, 2013 — Lesson 1).
5. 1964 (11 February): CVC created by resolution, following the Santhanam Committee (1962-64).
6. 1997: *Vineet Narain v Union of India* — safeguards ordered pending legislation.
7. 2003: CVC Act — CVC becomes statutory.
8. 2013: Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act — new referral/superintendence interface.
9. 2021: CVC and DSPE amendments — conditional one-year extensions, five-year aggregate ceiling.
10. 2023: *Dr Jaya Thakur* upholds the 2021 framework (fact-specific finding on one Director's extensions kept separate).
11. CBI alone, among the institutions in this chain, has never received its own dedicated statute.
12. The sequence is "executive origin → judicial correction → legislative codification," repeating across decades.

### Concept check

**Question:** A learner claims: "The Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946 is the CBI's founding statute, since 1946 is the earliest date in this history." Evaluate this claim.

**Model answer:** The claim is inaccurate. The 1946 Act founded the DSPE — a statutory police establishment — not the CBI. The CBI organisation itself was created seventeen years later, by a 1963 Government of India resolution, and has never had its own dedicated Act; it absorbed the already-existing DSPE as a division. Earliest date in a historical chain does not mean "founding instrument of the body being asked about."

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the oldest legal instrument in an institutional history must be the founding law of the organisation most associated with it today. Trace each institution's own specific origin separately.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Trace the evolution of India's anti-corruption institutional architecture from 1941 to 2003, showing how judicial intervention shaped legislative codification.

**Model solution:** The Special Police Establishment, set up by executive order in 1941 to investigate wartime corruption, received continuing statutory footing through the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. The CBI was established only in 1963 by executive resolution, absorbing DSPE as its investigation division; the CVC followed in 1964, also by resolution, on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation. For over three decades CVC remained a purely executive creation with no statutory anchor. This changed only after *Vineet Narain v Union of India* (1997), where the Supreme Court, concerned about investigative inertia in a politically sensitive matter, issued continuing-mandamus directions demanding statutory status for CVC and tenure safeguards for the CBI Director. Parliament codified these directions through the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. The sequence illustrates a recurring pattern: executive origin, judicial correction, legislative codification.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** 1941/1946 stages correctly stated — 2; 1963/1964 executive-resolution origins correctly stated — 2; *Vineet Narain*'s role as a judicial trigger correctly explained — 3; 2003 codification correctly linked back to the judgment — 2; coherent 150-word chronological structure — 1.

### Plain-language intuition

Think of the CVC as a three-seat bench that is never required to be fully occupied — the Act says "not more than two" Vigilance Commissioners, meaning the Commission is lawful even with just the Central Vigilance Commissioner sitting alone, or with one VC. Appointment is deliberately taken out of the sole hands of the executive: a three-person committee recommends, and only then does the President formally appoint.

### Technical doctrine

**Composition (Section 3):** the Commission consists of one **Central Vigilance Commissioner**, as Chairperson, and **not more than two Vigilance Commissioners**, as Members. The ceiling is on the maximum (three total); the Act does not require both VC seats to be filled.

**Eligibility pools:** the statute draws eligible persons from two broad categories —

1. persons with experience in an All-India Service or Union civil service/post, involving vigilance, policy-making and administration, including police administration; or
2. persons with experience in specified public-sector corporate bodies and expertise in finance (including insurance and banking), law, vigilance and investigation.

A built-in diversity guardrail prevents all three seats from being drawn from a single category: **not more than two of the three appointees may belong to the same eligibility category.**

**Appointment (Section 4):** the President appoints the Central Vigilance Commissioner and the Vigilance Commissioners by warrant under hand and seal, after obtaining the recommendation of a three-member selection committee consisting of —

- the Prime Minister, as Chairperson;
- the Union Minister of Home Affairs; and
- the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or, where no Leader of Opposition is recognised, the leader of the single largest Opposition group in that House).

### Examples and their limits

Suppose only the Central Vigilance Commissioner and one Vigilance Commissioner have been appointed, with the second VC seat vacant. The Commission remains validly constituted — the statute's ceiling ("not more than two") is a maximum, not a floor. This example clarifies that vacancy is not automatically illegality, but it does not mean indefinite vacancy is desirable; prolonged vacancy can still weaken institutional capacity even where it does not breach the letter of the law (a theme returned to in Lesson 14).

### Comparison: CVC's committee versus the CBI Director's committee

| Seat | CVC selection committee (Section 4) | CBI Director committee (DSPE Act, Section 4A — Lesson 9) |
|---|---|---|
| Chairperson | Prime Minister | Prime Minister |
| Second member | Union Home Minister | Leader of Opposition, Lok Sabha (or substitute) |
| Third member | Leader of Opposition, Lok Sabha (or substitute) | Chief Justice of India or a Supreme Court judge nominated by the CJI |
| Shared member | Prime Minister | Prime Minister |

*The Home Minister sits on the CVC panel but not the CBI Director panel; the CJI/nominee sits on the CBI Director panel but not the CVC panel. This single table resolves one of the most common Prelims confusions in the entire topic.*

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "Since the Prime Minister chairs both committees, doesn't the executive effectively control both appointments anyway?"

**Reply:** The Prime Minister's presence on both does give the executive a structural advantage, and this is a fair institutional criticism (developed further in Lesson 14). But the comparison is not between "executive control" and "no executive control" — it is between unilateral executive appointment and a multi-member committee that always includes an Opposition or judicial check. The CVC committee includes an elected Opposition voice; the CBI Director committee substitutes a judicial voice for the Home Minister. Both designs dilute, without eliminating, executive discretion.

**Qualified residual:** Composition and appointment rules are independence *safeguards*, not independence *guarantees* — their value depends on how faithfully the committee process is actually followed in a given selection, a point this course does not resolve with any named allegation.

### UPSC application

For Mains, the composition/appointment facts rarely carry marks alone but are the necessary opening architecture for any institutional-independence answer.

### The learner's problem

"Four years or 65, whichever is earlier" is the headline fact everyone remembers. The part almost everyone forgets is what happens to a Vigilance Commissioner who is later appointed Central Vigilance Commissioner — does their clock restart? And on removal, people routinely conflate the dramatic Supreme-Court-inquiry route with a separate, more mundane list of direct statutory grounds. Both gaps are exam traps.

### Visual: the tenure clock

```text
ENTRY INTO OFFICE
      |
      +--> Four years in office, OR
      |
      +--> attaining age 65
      |
WHICHEVER OCCURS EARLIER -> term ends

SPECIAL RULE: Vigilance Commissioner promoted to Central Vigilance Commissioner
      |
      +--> may be appointed CVC through the ordinary statutory process
      |
      +--> BUT aggregate service as VC + CVC together cannot exceed four years
```

*Caption: "four years or 65" is a ceiling, and the VC-to-CVC promotion does not create a fresh four-year entitlement — it is capped in aggregate.*

### Technical doctrine: tenure and post-tenure bars

**Tenure:** the Central Vigilance Commissioner and each Vigilance Commissioner hold office for **four years from the date of entry, or until attaining the age of sixty-five, whichever is earlier**. The CVC is ineligible for reappointment in the Commission after ceasing office. A sitting Vigilance Commissioner may be appointed Central Vigilance Commissioner through the statutory appointment process, but their **combined service as VC and CVC cannot exceed the four-year aggregate cap**.

**Post-tenure restrictions:** on ceasing office, the CVC and VCs are barred from specified diplomatic assignments, from appointment as a Union Territory Administrator or other specified presidential appointments, and from further employment in any office of profit under the Union or a State.

### Visual: two removal routes

```text
ROUTE A: PROVED MISBEHAVIOUR OR INCAPACITY
President makes a reference
        -> Supreme Court holds an inquiry
        -> Court reports whether removal is warranted
        -> President orders removal only after that report

ROUTE B: LISTED DIRECT STATUTORY GROUNDS (no SC inquiry needed)
President may remove for:
   - adjudged insolvency
   - conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude
   - engaging in paid employment outside official duties during the term
   - infirmity of mind or body making continuation unfit, in the President's opinion
   - a financial or other interest likely to prejudice functions
```

*Caption: Route A is the high-threshold, judicially supervised path for contested allegations; Route B is a shorter list of largely self-evident, less contestable disqualifying facts. Treating Route B as requiring a Supreme Court inquiry is a common and serious error.*

[FACT] A prohibited interest in a Government of India contract or agreement — beyond the ordinary position of a shareholder in an incorporated company — is statutorily **deemed misbehaviour**, bringing it within Route A's threshold rather than being treated as an automatic Route B ground.

During the pendency of a Route A reference, the **President may suspend** the Commissioner concerned and prohibit their attendance in office.

### Plain-language intuition

Picture tenure as a countdown clock with two stop conditions (four years, or turning 65), whichever trips first. Removal, meanwhile, works like two separate doors: one door (proved misbehaviour/incapacity) is heavily guarded — you need a Supreme Court inquiry to open it. The other door (the listed direct grounds) is a simple checklist — if a Commissioner is adjudged insolvent or convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, the President can act without a fresh judicial inquiry into that specific fact, because the fact itself (insolvency, conviction) has typically already been established elsewhere.

### Examples and their limits

If a Vigilance Commissioner is credibly accused of orchestrating a specific vigilance decision for personal benefit, that is a Route A matter — it requires proved misbehaviour established through a Supreme Court inquiry before removal. If the same Commissioner is instead adjudged insolvent by a competent court, that fact alone (Route B) permits removal without a fresh Supreme Court inquiry into the insolvency, since the statute treats insolvency as a self-contained disqualifying event. The example shows the difference in process, but it does not mean Route B grounds are trivial — insolvency and moral-turpitude convictions are serious, independently adjudicated facts in their own right.

### Comparison: tenure safeguards and their limits

| Design safeguard | Independence value | Continuing limit |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed four-year/65 term | Protects against removal at executive pleasure during the term | A short maximum tenure can also reduce institutional continuity |
| VC-to-CVC aggregate cap | Prevents indefinite service through sequential appointments | Does not prevent short-term discontinuity between successive appointees |
| Supreme Court inquiry for Route A | Very high removal threshold for contested allegations | Route B grounds still permit removal without that inquiry |
| Post-tenure employment bars | Reduces inducement risk after office | Does not create budget or cadre autonomy during the term |
| Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India | Financial independence from annual budgetary votes | Operational capacity still depends on staffing and administrative support |

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If removal requires a Supreme Court inquiry, isn't the CVC's independence essentially absolute?"

**Reply:** Not quite — this overstates Route A's scope by forgetting Route B. The listed direct grounds (insolvency, moral-turpitude conviction, unauthorised paid employment, infirmity, prejudicial interest) allow removal without invoking the Supreme Court inquiry at all. The high threshold protects against a *specific* category of contested-conduct allegations; it does not immunise a Commissioner from the clearly defined disqualifying facts in Route B.

**Qualified residual:** Security of tenure is cumulative, not singular — the four-year/65 ceiling, the two carefully separated removal routes, the post-tenure bars and the Consolidated Fund charge together build independence; no single provision does all the work alone.

### UPSC application

A frequent Prelims distractor states that CVC removal "always requires a Supreme Court inquiry" — this is correct only for Route A and must be qualified against the direct Route B grounds. Mains answers on CVC's independence should state tenure, both removal routes, and the post-tenure bars together as a package, rather than naming only the headline "four years or 65" fact.

### Revision notes

1. Tenure: four years from entry, or age 65, whichever is earlier.
2. The CVC is ineligible for reappointment to the Commission after ceasing office.
3. A VC may be appointed CVC, but aggregate VC+CVC service cannot exceed four years.
4. Post-tenure bars: specified diplomatic/presidential assignments and further government office of profit.
5. Removal Route A: President's reference → Supreme Court inquiry → Court's report → President's removal order, for proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
6. Removal Route B: listed direct grounds — insolvency, moral-turpitude conviction, unauthorised paid employment, infirmity, prejudicial interest — no Supreme Court inquiry required.
7. A prohibited Government-of-India contractual interest (beyond ordinary incorporated-company shareholding) is deemed misbehaviour, falling under Route A.
8. The President may suspend a Commissioner during the pendency of a Route A reference.
9. Expenses of the Commission are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
10. Independence is cumulative — tenure, removal design, post-tenure bars and financial protection work together, and no single safeguard is complete alone.

### Concept check

**Question:** A Vigilance Commissioner is adjudged insolvent by a competent court. Must the President order a Supreme Court inquiry before removing that Commissioner?

**Model answer:** No. Adjudged insolvency is one of the listed direct statutory grounds (Route B) for removal, which does not require a Supreme Court inquiry. The Supreme Court inquiry route (Route A) applies specifically to removal for proved misbehaviour or incapacity — a different and higher-threshold category of allegation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming every removal of a CVC/VC requires a Supreme Court inquiry. Only Route A (proved misbehaviour or incapacity) does; the listed direct grounds in Route B operate independently of that inquiry.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the tenure and removal framework applicable to the Central Vigilance Commissioner and Vigilance Commissioners, distinguishing the two statutory removal routes.

**Model solution:** The Central Vigilance Commissioner and Vigilance Commissioners hold office for four years from entry, or until age sixty-five, whichever is earlier; a Vigilance Commissioner later appointed Central Vigilance Commissioner remains subject to an aggregate four-year cap across both offices. The second route lists direct statutory grounds — adjudged insolvency, conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude, unauthorised paid employment, infirmity of mind or body, or a prejudicial financial interest — permitting removal without a fresh Supreme Court inquiry. A prohibited Government-of-India contractual interest is statutorily deemed misbehaviour, placing it within the first route.

### The learner's problem

"The CVC superintends the CBI" is a sentence that sounds complete but is dangerously incomplete. Superintend over what, exactly? Can it tell the CBI how to run a specific case? And who actually does the CVC's legwork inside each Ministry — does the Commission itself visit every department? This lesson answers all three questions through Section 8, the operational heart of the CVC Act.

### Visual: Section 8 function map

*Caption: eight distinct functions, each textually bounded — none of them is "run the CBI" or "convict the accused."*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of the CVC less like a commanding officer and more like an expert auditor-cum-advisor with a narrow but real supervisory lane: it watches over how corruption cases under one specific law (the Prevention of Corruption Act) are investigated, it can ask questions and issue systemic directions about that process, but it cannot reach into an individual file and say "charge this person" or "close this case." On everything outside that one lane, the oversight job belongs to the Central Government instead (detailed further in Lesson 6).

### Technical doctrine: superintendence without case dictation

The statute contains an express proviso: the CVC's power to give directions for the discharge of its supervisory responsibility **cannot require that any investigation be done, or disposed of, in a particular manner**.

```text
PERMITTED LEVEL                         PROHIBITED LEVEL
---------------                         ----------------
systemic supervision                    "reach this specific result"
progress monitoring                     "charge this named person"
lawful general directions               "close this particular case"
sanction-delay review                   "investigate in this exact manner"
integrity/process oversight             "dispose of the case as instructed"
```

"Superintendence," in this reading, means lawful oversight of the agency and process — not remote authorship of the evidence, charges or final report in a named case. This is precisely the boundary that keeps the CVC a supervisory institution rather than a shadow investigator.

**Person and complaint coverage:** the complaint-based inquiry/investigation route under Section 8(1)(d) applies only to Section 8(2)'s covered categories — broadly, members of All-India Services serving in connection with Union affairs and Group A officers of the Central Government, plus notified levels in covered central corporations, Government companies, societies and local authorities. A complaint's seriousness does not by itself create CVC jurisdiction if the person or category falls outside this statutory gate; Lokpal-referred complaints introduce a separate interface (Lesson 12), and that route should not be collapsed into this one.

**Advice is not command:** after an inquiry, the CVC communicates its advice to the Union Government or the covered organisation. Where the recipient disagrees, the statute requires it to **record reasons in writing** and communicate them to the CVC — the advice is considered, but it is not self-executing.

```text
INQUIRY REPORT -> CVC -> ADVICE to Union/covered organisation
                              |
                   agrees -> appropriate action taken
                              |
                   disagrees -> written reasons communicated to CVC
```

**Civil-court-like inquiry powers:** for its specified inquiries, the CVC may summon and enforce attendance, examine on oath, require discovery and production of documents, receive affidavit evidence, requisition public records and issue commissions for examining witnesses or documents. These procedural powers make its inquiries more rigorous; they do not convert the Commission into a criminal trial court, a general police station, or the final judge of guilt.

**Annual accountability:** the CVC submits an annual report, including a separate part on DSPE's anti-corruption functioning, which goes to the President and is laid before both Houses of Parliament.

### CVOs — the departmental vigilance bridge

Chief Vigilance Officers (CVOs) are the vigilance nodes embedded within Ministries, departments and covered public organisations. They connect preventive vigilance (process redesign, sensitive-post rotation, procurement transparency, audit trails), complaint scrutiny, and disciplinary coordination with the organisational head, while communicating with the CVC on the vigilance dimension.

```text
                     CVC
                      |
         general vigilance supervision
                      |
                     CVO
           within Ministry / PSU / body
              /          |          \
    preventive review  complaints  disciplinary coordination
              \          |          /
                 organisational head
```

*Caption: the CVO is an internal bridge, not an independent criminal court or a miniature CBI branch with unlimited powers.*

### Examples and their limits

Suppose the CVC reviews a stalled prosecution-sanction application and advises the concerned Ministry to expedite its decision. If the Ministry disagrees — perhaps citing an incomplete internal inquiry — it must record its reasons in writing and send them to the CVC. The CVC cannot then simply override the Ministry and compel sanction; its remedy is reporting, persuasion and systemic pressure, not command. This example illustrates the advisory ceiling cleanly, but it does not mean CVC advice is toothless — repeated, documented disagreement is itself a visible institutional signal that can inform parliamentary scrutiny of the annual report.

|---|---|
| Summon and enforce attendance | A criminal trial court |
| Examine on oath | A general police station |

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If CVC's advice is not binding and its directions cannot dictate a case's outcome, what real teeth does Section 8 give it at all?"

**Reply:** Its teeth are procedural and systemic rather than coercive: civil-court-style inquiry powers to gather facts rigorously, a statutory requirement that disagreement be justified in writing (creating an accountability paper trail), parliamentary visibility through the annual report, and — within the Prevention of Corruption Act field specifically — genuine superintendence over how DSPE conducts those investigations. This is real institutional leverage even though it stops short of command authority.

**Qualified residual:** The CVC is best understood as a recommendatory vigilance apex, structurally similar in this respect to bodies like the NHRC, rather than a binding-order institution like a court or the Information Commission (which this topic does not cover in depth, but which is a useful contrast point if you have studied it).

### The learner's problem

"Does the CBI have jurisdiction everywhere in India?" is a deceptively simple question with a precise, layered answer. The honest answer is: automatically, only in Union Territories; everywhere else, only through a specific statutory sequence. This lesson builds that sequence section by section, then separates two different "who is in charge" questions that learners routinely merge — administrative superintendence and investigative superintendence.

### Visual: the DSPE jurisdiction ladder

### Plain-language intuition

Imagine DSPE's jurisdiction as a key that automatically opens every door inside Union Territories (Section 2), but needs the State's own locksmith (its consent, under Section 6) to be cut and fitted before it opens any door inside a State (after the Centre first decides, under Section 5, to extend the jurisdiction there at all). A completely separate master key exists — held by the Supreme Court and High Courts — that can open a State's door without needing that locksmith at all, but it is meant to be used sparingly (Lessons 7-8).

### Technical doctrine

**Section 2:** gives DSPE members police powers for the Union, in relation to specified offences, within the Union Territories as the statutory baseline — no further consent step is needed here.

**Section 3:** empowers the Central Government to specify, by notification, the classes of offences DSPE is to investigate.

**Section 5:** allows the Central Government to extend DSPE's powers and jurisdiction to a State area (beyond the Union Territory baseline).

**Section 6:** makes the exercise of that extended power in the State area conditional, ordinarily, on the **State Government's consent**.

```text
"CBI can investigate any case in India automatically" — FALSE (overbroad)
"CBI can never act in a State without consent" — ALSO FALSE (overbroad)

The accurate position:
Union Territory -> Section 2 baseline, no consent needed
State area -> Sections 5-6, ordinarily consent-gated
State area, exceptional -> constitutional-court direction (Lessons 7-8)
```

### Superintendence split (Section 4) — a different question

Section 4 answers "who superintends DSPE's work," which is a distinct question from "where can DSPE operate."

```text
DSPE MATTER                                SUPERINTENDENCE UNDER SECTION 4
------------                                --------------------------------
Investigation of Prevention of             Central Vigilance Commission
Corruption Act offences

Other DSPE matters                         Central Government, save as
                                            otherwise provided by law

investigation                              notwithstanding Section 4/CVC
                                            Section 8 (Lesson 12)
```

*Caption: there is no single controller for every CBI function — the controller changes with the legal subject matter.*

[ANALYSIS] Administration and investigative judgment must be kept distinct. Administrative control (budget, personnel policy, deputation, infrastructure, service conditions) can create influence risk over time, but it is not the same as a specific finding that an investigative decision in a named case was dictated. Independence is compromised when administrative leverage is used to shape case outcomes; accountability is compromised when "independence" is invoked to avoid lawful supervision, audit, courts or reasons. The constitutional objective is insulated investigation combined with answerable power.

### Examples and their limits

A bribery allegation against a central government official, investigated under the Prevention of Corruption Act, falls within CVC's superintendence (Section 4(1)) even while general administrative matters for the same investigating team — postings, budget, infrastructure — remain with the Director under the Central Government's broader oversight. The same case, if referred by the Lokpal, would additionally trigger Lokpal's special superintendence under its own Act (Lesson 12), layering a third oversight relationship onto the same investigation. The example shows superintendence can be genuinely multi-layered for a single case; it does not mean every case attracts every layer simultaneously — most ordinary DSPE matters involve only the Central Government's general Section 4(2) oversight.

### Comparison: territorial baseline across jurisdictions

| Territory | DSPE position |
|---|---|
| Union Territory | Section 2 gives DSPE members police powers directly, as the statutory baseline |
| State | Sections 5-6 ordinarily require extension by the Centre plus the State's consent |
| State, under constitutional-court direction | An independent authority route, not derived from Section 6 at all (Lessons 7-8) |

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If CVC superintends Prevention of Corruption Act investigations, doesn't that make CVC effectively CBI's administrative boss?"

**Reply:** No — this conflates two different Section 4 roles. CVC's superintendence is confined to the investigative-oversight dimension of one specific offence category; administration of DSPE as an organisation (postings, budget, infrastructure, general service conditions) remains with the Director under the Central Government's broader oversight for non-Prevention-of-Corruption-Act matters. CVC does not run DSPE's day-to-day administration even within its own superintendence field.

**Qualified residual:** The superintendence split is best summarised as "CVC for the relevant corruption-investigation field; Central Government for the rest; Director for administration throughout; Lokpal for its own referred matters" — a four-way allocation, not a single hierarchy.

### Revision notes

3. Section 5: Central Government may extend DSPE powers/jurisdiction to a State area.

7. Section 4(2): Central Government superintends other DSPE matters, subject to other statutory provisions.
8. Section 4(3): Director administers DSPE.
9. Lokpal has a special, separate superintendence power for matters it refers (Lesson 12).

### Concept check

**Question:** A State has not extended general consent to the CBI. Does this mean DSPE has no lawful way to investigate any matter connected to that State at all?

**Model answer:** No. The State-consent requirement under Section 6 governs the ordinary statutory route for extending DSPE's powers into that State area. It does not affect the Union Territory baseline under Section 2, nor does it foreclose the exceptional constitutional route by which a High Court (Article 226) or the Supreme Court (Article 32) may direct a CBI investigation independent of Section 6 consent (detailed in Lessons 7-8).

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating State consent as the only possible legal gateway for DSPE activity connected to a State. It is the ordinary statutory gateway; a separate constitutional gateway exists independently of it.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain the territorial-jurisdiction architecture of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946, and distinguish it from the Act's separate superintendence arrangement.

**Model solution:** The DSPE Act, 1946 builds territorial jurisdiction through a graded sequence. Section 3 empowers the Central Government to notify the classes of offences DSPE investigates. Section 5 permits the Central Government to extend DSPE's powers and jurisdiction to a State area, while Section 6 makes the exercise of that extended power ordinarily conditional on the State Government's consent — reflecting that police and public order are primarily State subjects. Neither "CBI has automatic nationwide jurisdiction" nor "CBI can never act without State consent" is accurate; the correct position traces territory through this specific statutory ladder, alongside an exceptional constitutional route by which courts may direct an investigation independent of consent. Separately, Section 4 answers a different question — who superintends DSPE's work, not where it may act. The Central Vigilance Commission superintends DSPE's investigation of Prevention of Corruption Act offences and connected same-trial offences; the Central Government superintends other DSPE matters, subject to special provisions such as the Lokpal Act's referral power; and the Director administers DSPE throughout. Conflating territorial jurisdiction with superintendence, or administrative control with case-specific investigative command, are the two most common errors in this area.

### The learner's problem

Having established that State consent ordinarily gates DSPE's work inside a State (Lesson 6), the natural next question is: what exactly is being consented to, and what happens when a State changes its mind partway through an investigation? Learners often assume "withdrawal of consent" instantly nullifies whatever DSPE was doing. It does not — and the precise legal reason why is a named, frequently tested holding.

### Visual: general consent versus case-specific consent

| General consent | Case-specific consent |
|---|---|
| Standing consent for defined classes/categories of cases | Consent tied to a named case, FIR, persons or facts |
| Reduces the need for repeated administrative requests | Preserves closer, case-by-case State control |
| May contain conditions or riders | Scope depends entirely on the instrument's precise terms |
| Can be prospectively withdrawn | Must be sought and evaluated for each requested case |

*Caption: "consent" is not a single undifferentiated instrument — its exact text, date, conditions and scope all matter to the legal analysis.*

### Visual: withdrawal sequence

```text
GENERAL CONSENT EXISTS
       |
CBI validly begins an investigation under that consent
       |
STATE WITHDRAWS GENERAL CONSENT
       |
       +--> FUTURE / NEW CASES: a fresh legal authority is ordinarily needed
       |
       +--> ALREADY-INSTITUTED INVESTIGATION:
            withdrawal does NOT automatically extinguish it
            (Kazi Lhendup Dorji v CBI, 1994 — a fact-specific rule,
             not a free-standing licence for new cases)
```

*Caption: withdrawal operates prospectively on new matters; it does not retroactively undo lawfully commenced work.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of general consent as a standing permission slip a State hands the CBI, saying "you may act within these defined categories without asking me each time." If the State later tears up that slip, any brand-new request from that point onward needs a fresh basis. But a case the CBI was already validly working on, under the slip that existed at the time, does not retroactively become illegal just because the slip was later torn up — the CBI can finish that specific job and submit its report.

### Technical doctrine

[FACT] In ***Kazi Lhendup Dorji v CBI*** (1994), the Supreme Court held that withdrawal of consent did **not operate retrospectively** to terminate investigations that CBI had already undertaken while consent was in force; CBI could lawfully complete those investigations and submit the statutory police report.

[LIMIT] This holding does **not** create a free-standing power to begin any *later* investigation without consent — it protects only investigations validly commenced before withdrawal, on their own facts. Converting this into "withdrawal is always legally meaningless" is a serious overstatement.

### Examples and their limits

Suppose a State grants general consent for all cases above a certain monetary threshold in 2015, and the CBI registers a case in 2019 under that consent. If the State withdraws general consent in 2020, the 2019 case may still be completed under *Kazi Lhendup Dorji*'s principle, because it was validly begun while consent operated. A brand-new 2021 allegation of the same type, arising after withdrawal, would ordinarily need a fresh legal basis — either renewed case-specific consent or a constitutional-court direction (Lesson 8). The example shows the prospective/retrospective line clearly, but real cases often involve harder questions about exactly when an investigation is considered "validly undertaken," which depends on the specific facts and the precise terms of the original consent.

### Comparison: what "consent is not absolute" actually means

| Correct meaning | Incorrect overstatement |
|---|---|
| State consent controls the ordinary DSPE Act route | State consent has no constitutional value at all |
| Existing, validly commenced investigations may survive a later withdrawal | Withdrawal is always legally meaningless |
| A separate constitutional-court route can override the ordinary consent requirement (Lesson 8) | The Union executive may simply ignore Section 6 on its own authority |
| Each consent instrument's precise scope and timing must be examined | There is no legal difference between general and case-specific consent |

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If withdrawal doesn't stop existing investigations, doesn't that make a State's withdrawal decision largely symbolic?"

**Reply:** No — withdrawal has real prospective force. It closes the standing door for any *future* case falling within the withdrawn category, forcing the CBI back to case-specific consent requests or the exceptional constitutional route for anything new. Its effect on already-validly-commenced work is narrow and fact-specific, precisely because retroactively invalidating ongoing lawful investigative work would itself be a serious rule-of-law problem — investigations conducted lawfully at the time should not become void merely because the legal environment later changed.

**Qualified residual:** State withdrawal of general consent is a genuine exercise of federal authority over future cases; it is not, and was never intended to be, a tool for retroactively unwinding investigations that were lawful when begun.

### UPSC application

This is the exact legal nuance tested by the 2021 GS-II Mains question (fully solved in Lesson 8), which asks why State consent "is not absolute." *Kazi Lhendup Dorji* is the standard citation for the prospective-withdrawal principle and is frequently paired, in a complete answer, with the constitutional-court exception from *CPDR* (Lesson 8).

### Revision notes

1. General consent is standing permission for defined classes/categories of cases; case-specific consent is tied to named facts.
2. General consent may carry conditions or riders and can be prospectively withdrawn.
3. *Kazi Lhendup Dorji v CBI* (1994): withdrawal does not retrospectively terminate investigations validly undertaken before it.
4. CBI may complete such already-commenced investigations and submit the statutory police report.
5. Withdrawal does have real prospective force — new cases after withdrawal ordinarily need a fresh legal basis.
6. The roster of States granting or withdrawing general consent changes over time; no fixed count should be memorised.
7. "Withdrawal is legally meaningless" and "withdrawal retroactively voids pending investigations" are both incorrect extremes.
8. Case-specific consent's scope depends entirely on its precise terms — always read the actual instrument, not an assumed default.
9. Withdrawal of general consent does not, by itself, foreclose the separate constitutional-court route (Lesson 8).

### Concept check

**Question:** A State withdraws general consent to the CBI in 2022. An investigation the CBI had validly begun in that State in 2020, while general consent was still in force, remains ongoing. What happens to that 2020 investigation?

**Model answer:** Under the principle in *Kazi Lhendup Dorji v CBI* (1994), the 2022 withdrawal operates prospectively and does not retrospectively terminate the investigation validly undertaken in 2020. The CBI may lawfully complete that investigation and submit its statutory police report; the withdrawal instead affects the legal basis for any new case arising after 2022.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that withdrawal of general consent automatically halts or invalidates every investigation already underway in that State. The rule is prospective, not retrospective, and is tied to the specific facts of validly commenced investigations.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the distinction between general and case-specific State consent under the DSPE Act, and the legal effect of withdrawing general consent on investigations already underway.

**Model solution:** General consent is standing permission for defined classes or categories of cases, reducing the need for repeated administrative requests, while case-specific consent is tied to a named case, FIR, persons or facts and must be separately sought each time. A State may prospectively withdraw general consent, requiring a fresh legal basis for future cases within the withdrawn category. However, in *Kazi Lhendup Dorji v CBI* (1994), the Supreme Court held that such withdrawal does not operate retrospectively: investigations validly undertaken by the CBI while consent was in force may be completed, with the statutory police report submitted in due course. This is a fact-specific, prospective-withdrawal principle, not a general licence for the CBI to begin new investigations without authority. Consent's precise scope, conditions and timing must always be read from the actual instrument rather than assumed.

No — constitutional courts hold an independent, exceptional power to direct a CBI investigation even without State consent. ### Technical doctrine

[FACT] In ***State of West Bengal v Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights*** (2010), a Constitution Bench held that a High Court, exercising its power under **Article 226**, may direct a CBI investigation in a State **without that State's consent**. [LIMIT] The Court simultaneously cautioned that this extraordinary power must be used **sparingly, cautiously and in exceptional situations** — not merely because allegations have been made against the local police. Routine transfer of cases to the CBI would itself erode the federal balance the judgment seeks to preserve.

[LIMIT] This is a narrow, fact-dependent outcome. It must never be generalised into "post-facto consent always cures an absence of consent" — the actual holding depends on the precise consent terms, the timing of when State servants became involved, whether later consent was obtained, and whether prejudice was specifically pleaded and shown.

### Technical doctrine: PE versus FIR

[FACT] In ***CBI v Thommandru Hannah Vijayalakshmi*** (2021), the Supreme Court held that a **Preliminary Enquiry (PE) is not mandatory before every corruption FIR**. If a complaint or "source information" discloses a cognizable offence, the CBI may directly register a Regular Case (FIR). A PE remains valuable and permissible in an *appropriate* case, but it **cannot be demanded by the accused as a right**, and the CBI Manual's internal guidance on PE is not itself a statute overriding this position.

[FACT] ***Lalita Kumari v Government of Uttar Pradesh*** requires FIR registration when the information discloses a cognizable offence, while preserving a limited scope for preliminary inquiry in defined categories of cases where the information itself does not clearly disclose a cognizable offence.

| Preliminary Enquiry (PE) | FIR/Regular Case |
|---|---|
| Limited threshold verification, where appropriate | Formal registration of cognizable-offence information |
| Asks whether a cognizable offence is disclosed at all | Starts the formal statutory investigation |
| Not mandatory in every corruption case | Required once the legal registration threshold is met |
| Not a mini-trial on the merits | Not itself proof that the allegation is true |
| Cannot be stretched into indefinite delay | Must be investigated fairly and lawfully |

[CURRENT] Older judgments use terminology from the Code of Criminal Procedure applicable when they were decided; current procedure must be read alongside the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 and its transition provisions. [FACT] **Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988** (inserted in 2018) governs **previous approval** before an enquiry, inquiry or investigation into an allegation relatable to a public servant's recommendation or decision taken in the discharge of official functions, subject to the section's own statutory exceptions (including cases involving arrest on the spot for accepting or attempting to accept an undue advantage).

[FACT] **Section 19 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988** separately governs **previous sanction** required before a court can take cognizance of specified offences under the Act against a public servant.

[LIMIT] Approval under Section 17A and sanction under Section 19 are not declarations of innocence or guilt; their applicability depends strictly on the statutory conditions and the facts of each case. Section 17A operates at the enquiry/investigation stage; Section 19 operates later, at the cognizance/prosecution stage — the two gates must not be merged into one.

### Current linkage: the Section 17A constitutional challenge

[CURRENT] In ***Centre for Public Interest Litigation v Union of India*** (2026 INSC 55), a two-judge Supreme Court bench delivered a **split verdict in January 2026** on Section 17A's constitutional validity. One judge held the provision **unconstitutional**, reasoning that it shields public servants from even a preliminary corruption inquiry and conflicts with the reasoning in earlier judgments that had struck down similar prior-approval requirements. The other judge **upheld** Section 17A's validity but held that the approval decision should not rest with the executive alone, favouring independent screening — for instance, by the Lokpal or State Lokayukta — rather than unilateral executive discretion. Because the two opinions were irreconcilable, the matter was **referred to the Chief Justice of India for constitution of a larger bench**.

[LIMIT] As of this session's control date, the larger bench has not ruled, and **Section 17A remains in force** exactly as it stood before the split verdict. This session teaches only that posture — approval requirements continue to apply under Section 17A, and its ultimate constitutional fate awaits an authoritative larger-bench decision. ### Plain-language intuition

Think of the safeguard gates as a sequence of checkpoints, each asking a narrower question than the last: first, is there even a lawful route into this matter at all (jurisdiction); then, does the information justify starting formal work (threshold); then, for a public servant's official decision specifically, is executive approval needed before even looking closer (Section 17A); then, once investigated, is sanction needed before a court can take up the case (Section 19); and finally, only a full trial determines guilt. Section 17A is currently a contested checkpoint — one judge thinks it is an unjustified extra barrier protecting wrongdoing, the other thinks it is a legitimate filter against harassment of honest officials, provided an independent body (not the executive alone) controls the gate.

### Examples and their limits

A complaint alleging that a senior official's official sanctioning decision was corruptly influenced would, if Section 17A applies on the facts, require prior approval before any enquiry, inquiry or investigation begins into that specific allegation — a gate separate from, and earlier than, the Section 19 sanction gate that governs whether a court can later take cognizance. The example shows the two gates operating at different stages for the same hypothetical case; it does not mean every corruption allegation triggers Section 17A — the section's own statutory exceptions, including certain on-the-spot arrest situations, remove some categories of cases from its reach entirely.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Discuss the procedural safeguards governing the registration of corruption cases and the initiation of investigation against public servants, with reference to the current constitutional debate over Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act.

**Model solution:** Indian anti-corruption procedure layers several sequential safeguards. At the threshold stage, *CBI v Thommandru Hannah Vijayalakshmi* (2021) clarified that a Preliminary Enquiry is not mandatory before every corruption FIR — a Regular Case may be registered directly where information discloses a cognizable offence, consistent with the registration duty recognised in *Lalita Kumari*; an accused cannot demand a PE as a right. Where the allegation concerns a public servant's recommendation or decision taken in discharge of official functions, Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 requires previous approval before enquiry, inquiry or investigation begins, subject to statutory exceptions such as certain on-the-spot arrest situations. A later, separate gate — Section 19 — requires previous sanction before a court can take cognizance of specified offences. Section 17A's own constitutional validity is presently unsettled: in *Centre for Public Interest Litigation v Union of India* (2026 INSC 55), a two-judge bench split in January 2026, one judge finding the provision unconstitutional as an undue shield for corrupt officials, the other upholding it while insisting that approval be screened independently rather than solely by the executive. The matter stands referred to the Chief Justice of India for a larger bench, and Section 17A continues to operate pending that ruling. The design reflects a genuine tension between protecting investigative urgency and protecting honest officials from harassment — a tension the pending larger-bench decision will have to resolve.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** PE/FIR doctrine (*Thommandru*, *Lalita Kumari*) correctly explained — 4; Section 17A requirement and its statutory exception noted — 3; Section 19 correctly distinguished as a separate, later gate — 2; current Section 17A litigation accurately and cautiously stated, with no predicted outcome — 5; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

A fourth institution now enters the picture: the Lokpal. ### Technical doctrine: the three CVC/CBI touchpoints

**Touchpoint 1 — preliminary-inquiry routing to CVC:** for complaints concerning the specified categories of public servants, the Lokpal framework routes certain preliminary inquiries to the CVC. The CVC reports back on specified categories to Lokpal, while proceeding under the CVC Act itself for other specified categories, as the governing framework provides.

**Touchpoint 2 — bounded superintendence over DSPE for referred matters:** where a matter is referred by the Lokpal to DSPE for investigation, the Lokpal framework gives it **superintendence and direction over that referred matter**, operating notwithstanding the DSPE Act's own Section 4 and the CVC Act's own Section 8. Crucially, the **same non-dictation principle you learned in Lessons 5-6 applies here too** — Lokpal, like CVC, cannot require that a referred investigation be conducted, or disposed of, in a particular manner.

**Touchpoint 3 — CVO as a shared vigilance node:** CVOs (Lesson 5) remain the departmental vigilance bridge regardless of whether a particular complaint travels through the ordinary CVC Section 8 route or the Lokpal referral route — their preventive and disciplinary-coordination functions are not duplicated or replaced by the Lokpal interface; they simply continue to feed into whichever oversight channel a specific matter is proceeding through.

```text

                     |                              |
         general Section 8           referred matters: Lokpal
         vigilance supervision        superintendence applies,
                     |                 subject to the same
                    CVO                 non-dictation limit
           within Ministry/PSU/body
              /          |          \
    preventive review  complaints  disciplinary coordination
              \          |          /
                 organisational head
```

*Caption: the CVO sits at the centre of departmental vigilance regardless of which higher-level channel — ordinary CVC supervision or Lokpal referral — a specific complaint is travelling through.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of the Lokpal not as a new boss standing above CVC and CBI in a single chain of command, but as a separate statutory body that can, for certain complaints it decides to take up, *borrow* CVC's preliminary-inquiry machinery or *direct* DSPE's investigative machinery for that specific referred matter — while both CVC and DSPE continue to exist, and to operate under their own Acts, for everything that does not come through the Lokpal route. It is a parallel entry point into the same investigative and vigilance infrastructure, not a merger of institutions.

### Examples and their limits

A complaint against a covered central public servant could reach CVC either directly (ordinary Section 8 route, Lesson 5) or via the Lokpal (if the complainant goes to Lokpal and Lokpal decides to route the preliminary inquiry to CVC). In the second scenario, CVC's eventual report travels back to the Lokpal rather than simply closing under the Act's standalone process. The example shows that the *entry point* can differ for what looks like a similar underlying complaint, while the *downstream investigative machinery* (CVC's inquiry powers, DSPE's police powers) remains the same institution operating under its own law — only the referral source and ultimate reporting line changes.

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If Lokpal can direct DSPE investigations and supervise CVC's preliminary inquiries, doesn't that make Lokpal the real top authority over the whole anti-corruption system?"

**Reply:** Only for the specific matters it actually takes up and refers — its superintendence is bounded to referred matters, operates alongside (not instead of) CVC's and DSPE's own statutory roles for everything else, and is itself subject to the same non-dictation limit that protects investigative judgment from case-result commands. Lokpal is best understood as an additional, parallel statutory entry point with defined referral powers, not a general supervisory apex replacing CVC's Section 8 role or DSPE's ordinary Section 4 superintendence.

**Qualified residual:** The CVC-CBI-Lokpal-CVO relationship is a **legally allocated workflow** — different statutes assigning different roles for different categories of complaints — rather than a single unified chain of command with one body always on top.

### UPSC application

Prelims distractors sometimes claim Lokpal is "CBI's permanent administrative superior" — this overstates a bounded, referred-matter-only superintendence into a general hierarchy, and should be corrected using the distinction taught here. ### Concept check

**Question:** Does the Lokpal's power to direct a DSPE investigation for a referred matter make the Lokpal a routine administrative superior of the CBI for all its work?

**Model answer:** No. The Lokpal's superintendence and direction apply only to the specific matters it has referred to DSPE for investigation, operating notwithstanding DSPE Act Section 4 and CVC Act Section 8 for those referred matters alone. ### Technical doctrine: the structural causes of the "caged parrot" critique

[ANALYSIS] The 2013 courtroom criticism (Lesson 10) has institutional causes, not merely political ones, and this session's teaching across Lessons 1-13 supplies the specific mechanisms:

1. **No own statute (Lesson 1):** the CBI organisation rests on a 1963 executive resolution, never re-founded by a dedicated Act, unlike CVC after 2003.
2. **Executive administrative dependence (Lesson 6):** budget, staffing and postings for the organisation remain substantially executive-controlled, even though case-specific investigative judgment is legally distinct from administrative control.
3. **Government-sanction requirements (Lesson 11):** Sections 17A and 19 of the PC Act insert approval/sanction gates before certain investigations or prosecutions can proceed — necessary due-process protections, but also potential points of delay or leverage.
4. **Split superintendence (Lesson 6):** CVC superintends only the Prevention of Corruption Act field; the Central Government superintends the rest, fragmenting single-point accountability for the organisation as a whole.

[ANALYSIS] The **consent regime** (Lessons 6-8) compounds this: it gives State governments a lever that can shield or expose officials depending on which party controls the State relative to the Centre — the identical instrument reads as "federal safeguard" or "obstruction of justice" depending on the political alignment of the moment, which is why this session deliberately avoids freezing any particular State's current consent status (Lesson 7).

[ANALYSIS] **Multiplicity** (Lesson 13) — CVC, CBI, Lokpal, ED, SFIO, FIU and State agencies all potentially touching one transaction — creates genuine overlap and turf friction, which is exactly why a clearer coordination charter is a recurring reform demand rather than a settled achievement.

|---|---|
| Consent/federalism (Lessons 6-8) | State consent under Section 6 is a real federal feature, but *CPDR* keeps the balance by letting constitutional courts order a CBI probe without consent, exceptionally — so withdrawal limits the executive route, not the judiciary's. |
| CBI autonomy (Lessons 1, 9-10, this lesson) | The CBI is structurally exposed by construction, not accident: an organisation without its own statute, administratively dependent on the executive, with sanction/approval gates layered on top — a dedicated CBI Act is the structural fix most consistently proposed, though it is not itself a complete solution without the other reform levels. |
| CVC's reach (Lessons 4-5) | Statutory status and fixed tenure gave the CVC independence but not command authority; advice that binds no one, and superintendence limited to one offence field, cap what a vigilance apex can achieve alone. |
| Current Section 17A debate (Lesson 11) | A genuinely live, unresolved tension between protecting honest officials from harassment and avoiding an executive-controlled shield for the dishonest — currently before a larger bench, with no predicted outcome taught here. |

### Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** "If the structural problems are this well understood and this well documented, why has a dedicated CBI statute never actually been enacted?"

**Reply:** Enacting such a statute requires resolving genuinely hard, contested design questions — how much federal consultation is required, how an independent budget line would be audited, how case-allocation transparency can coexist with investigation secrecy, and how a new law's drafting would itself become a politically contested process given each government's own incentives regarding the agency's independence. The absence of legislation reflects political-institutional difficulty in resolving these trade-offs, not an absence of understanding that a problem exists.

**Qualified residual:** This session's considered position, consistent with its canonical sources, is that CVC-CBI reform should protect evidence-based investigation from improper power while preserving federal consent, constitutional judicial review, due process, reasoned supervision and parliamentary accountability — a genuinely multi-dimensional objective, not a single-lever fix.

### UPSC application

"The CBI is a caged parrot — examine the structural causes and suggest reforms" and "multiplicity of anti-corruption bodies: synergy or turf war?" are the two standard synthesis-style Mains demands this lesson is built to answer directly, each requiring the full cross-lesson architecture (legal identity, consent, Director safeguards, Section 17A, Lokpal interface, comparison matrix) rather than any single fact in isolation.

### Revision notes

1. Independence tools: fixed Director tenure, high-powered selection, committee transfer control, case-allocation insulation, professional cadre.
2. Accountability tools: statutory jurisdiction limits, CVC/Lokpal supervision, judicial review, reasoned decisions, parliamentary reporting.
3. The "caged parrot" critique has four traceable structural causes: no own CBI statute, executive administrative dependence, PC Act approval/sanction gates, and split superintendence.
4. The consent regime's political-alignment sensitivity is a structural feature of the federal design, not a flaw unique to any particular government.
5. Multiplicity (CVC, CBI, Lokpal, ED, SFIO, FIU, State agencies) creates genuine coordination friction alongside legitimate parallel jurisdiction.
6. The reform ladder spans five levels: process, personnel, federalism, finance/reporting, legislation — no single level alone is a complete fix.
7. A dedicated CBI statute is the most consistently proposed structural reform, but remains a proposal, not enacted law.
8. Durable reform must protect evidence-based investigation from improper power while preserving federal consent, judicial review, due process and parliamentary accountability together.
9. This session's final verdict scaffolds consolidate the consent/federalism, CBI-autonomy, CVC-reach and Section-17A themes into reusable one-paragraph positions.

### Concept check

**Question:** A learner argues: "Since the structural causes of CBI's autonomy problem are well known, a dedicated CBI statute alone would completely resolve the institution's independence and accountability challenges." Evaluate this claim.

**Model answer:** The claim overstates what a single legislative reform can achieve. A dedicated CBI statute would address the organisational-status gap identified in Lesson 1 and could, if well drafted, strengthen budgetary and personnel independence. But the reform ladder taught in this lesson shows that durable independence-with-accountability also requires process-level transparency (case allocation, recusals), personnel stability, federal consent protocols that respect Section 6, and robust parliamentary reporting — a statute alone does not automatically deliver any of these without complementary institutional design.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "give the CBI its own Act" as a complete, self-sufficient reform. It is the most consistently proposed single step, but this session's own sources are explicit that every autonomy reform needs a reciprocal accountability design alongside it.

### Original Mains question

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** "The CBI's autonomy problem is structural, not merely political, and no single reform can resolve it." Critically examine this statement and propose a reform framework addressing independence and accountability together.

**Model solution:** The statement is substantially correct. The CBI's structural exposure traces to at least four distinct, interacting causes: it remains an executive-created organisation without its own statute, unlike the CVC after 2003; it depends on the executive for budget, staffing and postings, even though investigative judgment in specific cases is legally distinct from administrative control; Sections 17A and 19 of the Prevention of Corruption Act insert approval and sanction gates that, while protecting honest officials, can also delay investigations; and superintendence itself is split, with the CVC responsible only for the Prevention of Corruption Act field and the Central Government responsible for the rest. These causes compound a further federal complication: the State-consent regime under Sections 5-6 of the DSPE Act gives State governments a lever whose political reading shifts with alignment between the Centre and the State concerned. A durable reform framework must therefore operate on five levels together: transparent, auditable case-allocation processes; a stable, adequately diverse specialist cadre with protected tenure; predictable, statute-respecting federal consent protocols; an independently audited functional budget alongside stronger aggregate parliamentary reporting; and, at the legislative level, a debated dedicated CBI statute defining mandate and accountability. No single level — least of all legislation alone — can substitute for the others; genuine reform requires all five operating as a coherent, mutually reinforcing system rather than a slogan-level fix.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** Four structural causes of the autonomy problem correctly identified and explained — 8; federal/consent complication correctly linked — 3; all five reform-ladder levels named and explained — 6; explicit rejection of "single reform suffices" framing — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks test connections across multiple lessons rather than a single lesson's content. Attempt each before reading the model answer.

### Cross-lesson check 1 (Lessons 1, 6)

**Question:** A statement reads: "CBI is a statutory body because its officers exercise powers under the DSPE Act, 1946, which also governs CBI's territorial jurisdiction in the States." Identify what is accurate and what is imprecise in this statement.

**Model answer:** The DSPE Act's role in territorial jurisdiction (Sections 2, 5 and 6) is accurately described. What is imprecise is calling the *CBI* statutory on this basis: the DSPE Act creates and empowers the DSPE, a statutory police establishment functioning as a division within the CBI; it does not create the CBI organisation itself, which rests on a 1963 executive resolution. "Powers exercised under a statute" and "organisation created by a statute" are different claims that this statement incorrectly merges.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that an organisation's officers exercising statutory powers automatically makes the whole organisation a statutory body.

### Cross-lesson check 2 (Lessons 3, 9)

**Question:** Name one member who sits on both the CVC's selection committee and the CBI Director's selection committee, and one member who sits on only one of the two.

**Model answer:** The Prime Minister sits on both committees. The Union Home Minister sits only on the CVC's selection committee (Section 4, CVC Act); the Chief Justice of India, or a Supreme Court judge nominated by the CJI, sits only on the CBI Director's selection committee (Section 4A, DSPE Act). The Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or the statutory substitute) sits on both committees as well.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the two committees are identical in composition merely because both are headed by the Prime Minister.

### Cross-lesson check 3 (Lessons 7, 8)

**Question:** A State withdraws general consent to the CBI in 2023. In 2024, the Supreme Court directs a CBI investigation in that same State under Article 32 in an unrelated matter. Are these two legal events in tension with each other?

**Model answer:** No. The 2023 withdrawal affects the ordinary statutory route under Sections 5-6 of the DSPE Act for new cases going forward (Lesson 7). The 2024 Supreme Court direction arises from an entirely separate, constitutional basis recognised in *CPDR* (2010) — the Court's own Article 32 power, exercised exceptionally, independent of Section 6 consent (Lesson 8). The two routes coexist without contradiction because they rest on different legal foundations.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating withdrawal of consent as if it could also bind or limit a constitutional court's independent Article 32/226 power.

### Cross-lesson check 4 (Lessons 5, 12)

**Question:** Does the Lokpal's bounded superintendence over a referred DSPE matter follow a different legal principle from the CVC's ordinary Section 8 superintendence regarding case-result dictation?

**Model answer:** No, it follows the same principle. Just as the CVC's Section 8 superintendence cannot require that a DSPE investigation be conducted, or disposed of, in a particular manner, the Lokpal's superintendence over matters it has referred to DSPE is subject to the identical non-dictation limit. Both oversight relationships preserve investigative judgment in named cases while allowing systemic, process-level supervision.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that a new oversight body (Lokpal) must have broader command powers than CVC's already-studied, textually bounded superintendence.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These three questions are distinct from every lesson-local Mains question and from the verified PYQs above. Each carries its own quantified rubric and strict word ceiling.

### Synthesis Mains — 10 marks, strict ceiling 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the superintendence arrangements applicable to DSPE investigations from the territorial-jurisdiction rules applicable to DSPE's exercise of power, and explain why these are two separate legal questions.

**Model solution:** Superintendence (DSPE Act, Section 4) answers "who oversees DSPE's work" — the Central Vigilance Commission for Prevention of Corruption Act investigations, the Central Government for other matters, and the Director for administration throughout, with Lokpal's bounded superintendence applying only to matters it has referred. Territorial jurisdiction (Sections 2, 5 and 6) answers an entirely different question — "where may DSPE lawfully act" — through the Union Territory baseline, extension to States, and State consent, supplemented by the exceptional constitutional-court route. A DSPE investigation squarely within CVC's superintendence field could still be territorially unlawful without proper consent or court direction, and a territorially authorised investigation could still raise a separate superintendence question about who oversees its conduct. Conflating the two produces confused, imprecise answers.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Superintendence correctly explained as the "who oversees" question — 4; territorial jurisdiction correctly explained as the separate "where" question — 4; explicit statement that the two can vary independently — 1; coherent 150-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 15 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "Every independence safeguard built into India's anti-corruption architecture is paired with an accountability limit." Examine this statement with reference to the CVC and the CBI Director.

**Model solution:** The statement holds across both institutions studied in this session. For the CVC, statutory status and a fixed four-year/sixty-five tenure protect against arbitrary removal, but the Commission's advice is not binding, and its superintendence over DSPE is confined to the Prevention of Corruption Act field — independence in tenure is paired with a real ceiling on command authority. Removal itself splits into two routes: a high-threshold Supreme Court inquiry for proved misbehaviour or incapacity, and a separate list of direct statutory grounds requiring no such inquiry, so insulation from casual removal is paired with genuine accountability for clearly defined disqualifying facts. For the CBI Director, a protected two-year minimum tenure and committee-gated transfer insulate the office from premature displacement, as confirmed in *Alok Kumar Verma* (2019), which held that even informally divesting the Director of powers has transfer-equivalent effect. Yet the 2021 extension framework, upheld in *Dr Jaya Thakur* (2023), caps continuation at an aggregate five years and requires annual, reasoned, committee-recommended justification rather than automatic renewal. In each case, a genuine independence safeguard coexists with an equally genuine accountability boundary, confirming that India's design deliberately avoids granting either value without a corresponding limit on the other.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** CVC tenure/removal safeguard-limit pairing correctly explained — 5; CVC advisory/superintendence limit correctly explained — 3; CBI Director tenure/transfer safeguard correctly explained with *Alok Kumar Verma* — 4; extension-framework limit correctly explained with *Jaya Thakur* — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 20 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** Construct a reasoned position on whether India's anti-corruption institutional design — spanning CVC, CBI/DSPE, State consent and the Lokpal interface — should be judged primarily by its legal architecture or by its practical functioning, drawing on the case law and current developments studied in this session.

**Model solution:** Legal architecture and practical functioning must be judged together, because each studied theme in this session shows architecture creating only the possibility of good functioning, not its guarantee. The CVC's statutory status, won only after *Vineet Narain* (1997), gives it genuine procedural tools, yet its advisory, non-binding character means functioning still depends on how faithfully covered organisations respond to its advice. The CBI's dependence on a 1963 resolution rather than a dedicated statute is itself a functioning risk, compounded by administrative dependence that *could* permit improper influence even where no specific instance is alleged. The consent regime under Sections 5-6 shows architecture (a federal safeguard) whose practical meaning shifts with political alignment, while *CPDR*'s exceptional judicial-override power shows courts correcting functioning gaps the architecture alone cannot close. Most tellingly, the currently unresolved Section 17A litigation (2026 INSC 55) shows that even settled-looking architecture can face live constitutional contest, with real consequences for how investigations function meanwhile. The soundest position is that architecture sets the boundaries of what accountable functioning is even possible, but functioning — administrative capacity, political restraint, judicial vigilance, and now the eventual larger-bench ruling on Section 17A — determines whether those boundaries are honoured in practice. Studying one without the other, as this session has shown throughout, produces an incomplete answer.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** CVC architecture-versus-functioning point correctly made with *Vineet Narain* — 5; CBI's resolution-based status linked to functioning risk — 4; consent regime's political-alignment sensitivity correctly explained — 4; *CPDR* correctly used as a functioning-gap corrective — 3; Section 17A litigation correctly used as evidence that architecture itself can remain unsettled — 3; coherent, reasoned 250-word structure — 1.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### 1. Legal identity (Lesson 1)

- CVC: statutory, CVC Act 2003; began as a 1964 executive resolution.
- CBI: executive organisation, 1 April 1963 resolution; no dedicated statute.
- DSPE: statutory police establishment, DSPE Act 1946; functions as a division inside CBI.
- Neither CVC nor CBI is a constitutional body.
- *Navendra Kumar v Union of India* (2013): Gauhati High Court quashed the 1963 resolution (6 November 2013); Supreme Court stayed that judgment (9 November 2013); the CBI's foundational validity remains unresolved — never cite as a settled invalidation or a settled validation.
- Safest exam phrase: "CBI is executive-created; its core investigative powers derive from the statutory DSPE Act; CVC alone is itself statutory."

### 2. Evolution (Lesson 2)

- 1941 SPE (executive order, wartime corruption) → 1943 wartime ordinance → 1946 DSPE Act → 1963 CBI resolution → 1964 CVC resolution → 1997 *Vineet Narain* → 2003 CVC Act → 2013 Lokpal Act → 2021 amendments → 2023 *Jaya Thakur*.
- Recurring pattern: executive origin → judicial correction → legislative codification.
- CBI alone, in this chain, has never received its own dedicated statute; its 1963 founding resolution's own validity is itself unresolved, stayed on appeal since 2013 (Lesson 1).

### 3. CVC composition and appointment (Lesson 3)

- Composition: one CVC (Chairperson) + not more than two VCs (Members); maximum strength three; vacancy in a VC seat does not invalidate the Commission.
- Eligibility: Union/All-India Service vigilance-administration experience, or specified public-sector/finance/law/investigation expertise; not more than two appointees from one category.
- Appointment: President, by warrant under hand and seal, on recommendation of a committee of the Prime Minister (Chair), Union Home Minister, and Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or the statutory single-largest-Opposition-group substitute).

### 4. CVC tenure and removal (Lesson 4)

- Tenure: four years or age 65, whichever is earlier; VC-to-CVC aggregate cap of four years.
- Post-tenure bars: specified diplomatic/presidential assignments; further government office of profit.
- Removal Route A: President's reference → Supreme Court inquiry → Court's report → President's removal order (proved misbehaviour/incapacity); suspension permitted during the inquiry.
- Removal Route B: direct statutory grounds — insolvency, moral-turpitude conviction, unauthorised paid employment, infirmity, prejudicial interest — no Supreme Court inquiry needed.
- A prohibited Government-of-India contractual interest is deemed misbehaviour (Route A).

### 5. CVC functions and CVOs (Lesson 5)

- Section 8: superintendence (PC Act field only), bounded directions, reference/complaint-based inquiry (Section 8(2) covered categories), investigation/sanction progress review, advice, vigilance-administration supervision.
- Statutory proviso: no particular-manner investigation or disposal may be dictated.
- Advice is not binding; disagreement requires written reasons communicated to CVC.
- Civil-court-like inquiry powers (summons, oath examination, document discovery, record requisition) without becoming a criminal court.
- Annual report, with a separate DSPE part, to the President and both Houses.
- CVOs: departmental vigilance bridge — preventive and disciplinary-coordination roles, not independent investigative authorities.

### 6. CBI/DSPE organisation and jurisdiction (Lesson 6)

- Section 2: UT baseline; Section 3: Central Government notifies offence classes; Section 5: extension to State area; Section 6: State consent ordinarily required.
- Neither "automatic nationwide jurisdiction" nor "never without consent" is accurate.
- Section 4 superintendence split: CVC (PC Act investigations), Central Government (other matters), Director (administration), Lokpal (bounded, for referred matters).
- Administrative control is distinct from, and not proof of, case-specific investigative dictation.

### 7. State consent (Lesson 7)

- General consent: standing permission for defined categories, may carry riders, can be prospectively withdrawn.
- Case-specific consent: tied to named facts; scope depends on the exact instrument.
- *Kazi Lhendup Dorji* (1994): withdrawal operates prospectively; validly begun investigations may be completed.
- The roster of States granting/withdrawing consent changes over time and is deliberately not frozen in this session.

### 8. Constitutional-court exception (Lesson 8)

- *CPDR* (2010): SC (Article 32)/HC (Article 226) may direct a CBI investigation without State consent — exceptional, sparing, cautious use required.
- *Fertico* (2020): a fact-specific result on its own consent-rider/timing/prejudice facts; not a universal "post-facto consent cures everything" rule.
- *State of West Bengal v Union of India* (2024): rejected a preliminary maintainability objection only; the consent dispute's merits remain undecided by that judgment.
- Owned PYQ: 2021 GS-II Q11 (Explain, 15 marks, 250 words) — answer approach only, mapped here and in the PYQ-linkage section above.

### 9. CBI Director (Lesson 9)

- Appointment: Central Government, on the Section 4A committee's recommendation (PM, LoP/substitute, CJI/nominee); a separate, earlier CVC-chaired panel prepares the eligible-officer shortlist.
- Tenure: two-year protected minimum; transfer needs the Section 4A committee's prior consent.
- 2021 amendment: conditional, reasoned, one-year-at-a-time extensions, aggregate five-year ceiling; not automatic.
- *Alok Kumar Verma* (2019): divesting powers has transfer-equivalent effect; cannot bypass committee protection by relabelling.
- *Dr Jaya Thakur* (2023 INSC 616): 2021 framework upheld; a separate, fact-specific finding on one officeholder's particular extensions kept distinct from the framework's own validity.
- Director of Prosecution and SP-and-above posts follow distinct, separately specified appointment routes.

### 10. *Vineet Narain* and "caged parrot" (Lesson 10)

- *Vineet Narain* (1997): continuing-mandamus directions — statutory CVC, CVC's anti-corruption supervision, Director's minimum tenure, committee transfer control, Director's work-allocation freedom, CBI Manual compliance — later codified in the CVC Act and DSPE amendments.
- "Caged parrot" (2013): courtroom criticism during coal-block proceedings; context/illustration only, never a binding legal test.

### 11. Investigation lifecycle and the current linkage (Lesson 11)

- *Thommandru* (2021): PE is not mandatory before every corruption FIR; cannot be demanded as a right.
- *Lalita Kumari*: FIR registration mandatory once a cognizable offence is disclosed, with limited preliminary-inquiry scope preserved.
- Section 17A (PC Act): previous approval before enquiry/inquiry/investigation into a public servant's official recommendation/decision, subject to statutory exceptions.
- Section 19 (PC Act): previous sanction before a court takes cognizance — a separate, later-stage gate.
- Current linkage: *Centre for Public Interest Litigation v Union of India* (2026 INSC 55) — January 2026 split verdict on Section 17A's validity, referred to the Chief Justice of India for a larger bench; Section 17A remains in force as of this session's control date; no outcome is predicted.

### 12. Lokpal interface (Lesson 12)

- Preliminary-inquiry routing to CVC for specified categories; CVC reports back to Lokpal on some categories while continuing to process others independently.
- Bounded superintendence over DSPE for referred matters, notwithstanding DSPE Act Section 4/CVC Act Section 8, subject to the same non-dictation principle.
- CVOs continue their role regardless of which channel a complaint travels through.
- This session does not teach Lokpal's own composition, eligibility or full jurisdiction — that belongs to a separate topic.

### 13. Comparison map and 2026 Prelims PYQ (Lesson 13)

- Full comparison matrix across CVC, CBI/DSPE, Lokpal, ED, NIA, State ACB/police — distinct statute, domain, power and output for each.
- Frequent conflations: CBI/ED, CBI/NIA, Lokpal-as-superior, CVC-as-enforcer, State-ACB-as-subordinate.
- Owned PYQ: 2026 Prelims GS-I matching question — answer-neutral; checking method (organisation + function + ministry, verified independently per row) taught, no answer stated.

### 14. Independence, accountability, reform (Lesson 14)

- Independence tools and accountability tools operate as paired columns, not a single scale.
- Four structural causes of the "caged parrot" critique: no own CBI statute, administrative dependence, PC Act approval/sanction gates, split superintendence.
- Reform ladder: process, personnel, federalism, finance/reporting, legislation — all five needed together.
- Verdict scaffolds on consent/federalism, CBI autonomy, CVC's reach and the current Section 17A debate consolidate the entire topic's evaluative positions.
