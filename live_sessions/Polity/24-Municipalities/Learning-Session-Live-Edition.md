Urban local government means governance of a demarcated urban area through locally elected representatives. Its case rests on **proximity**, **place-based coordination** and **accountability**. A blocked drain, unsafe street, uncollected waste or erroneous property assessment is visible locally; the resident should be able to identify an institution responsible for correction.

The 7 September 2026 decentralisation editorial provides a current anchor for this distinction: constitutional recognition has not by itself completed the transfer of **funds, functions and functionaries** to Panchayats and Municipalities. That is why the lesson tests actual authority and accountability rather than treating the presence of elections as proof of self-government.

### 1. Self-government is more than a field office

Three arrangements must be distinguished:

| Arrangement | Who decides? | Accountability |
|---|---|---|
| Deconcentration | State department through local office | Upward to department |
| Delegation | Agency performs assigned task | To delegating authority |
| Devolution | Elected local institution receives authority, resources and responsibility | Downward to residents plus legal oversight |

A municipal office that only executes State instructions is local administration, not full local self-government. Conversely, municipal autonomy is not sovereignty: municipalities remain creatures of the Constitution, State legislation and lawful supervision.

### 2. Why the urban problem is distinctive

Cities generate strong spillovers. A road in one ward changes traffic in another; upstream land use changes downstream flooding; one jurisdiction's waste may be processed elsewhere. Urban governance therefore requires both **neighbourhood voice** and **city-region coordination**.

The local case also has limits. Small municipalities may lack engineers, planners or a viable tax base. Local elites may capture contracts. Land and infrastructure decisions may impose costs on vulnerable residents. Decentralisation therefore needs reservation, disclosure, audit, professional capability, appellate remedies and State-wide standards.

### 3. Evolution

```text
1688 Madras municipal corporation
   ↓
1726 Bombay and Calcutta corporations
   ↓
1870 Lord Mayo: financial decentralisation
   ↓
1882 Lord Ripon: local self-government resolution
   ↓
1907–09 Royal Commission on Decentralisation
   ↓
1919 local self-government becomes a transferred provincial subject
   ↓
1935 local government becomes a provincial subject
   ↓
1989 65th Amendment / Nagarpalika Bill fails in Rajya Sabha
   ↓
1990 revised bill lapses
   ↓
1991 modified bill introduced
   ↓
1992 74th Amendment enacted
   ↓
1 June 1993 Part IX-A commences
```

Lord Ripon's 1882 Resolution is commonly called the **Magna Carta of local self-government**, but colonial municipalities remained constrained by official control, narrow representation and weak finance. Post-independence municipal reform likewise depended on State statutes until repeated weakness produced the demand for constitutional status.

The 1989, 1990 and 1991–92 sequence matters. It reveals the constitutional compromise eventually adopted: a national democratic floor for municipal existence, elections and inclusion, while leaving substantial operational design to State legislatures.

### UPSC application

- Define self-government through authority, not merely geographic proximity.
- Use the evolution only to explain why ordinary State law proved insufficient.
- Qualify the democratic claim: proximity improves information but can also localise capture.

### Revision notes

1. Urban local government is area-based governance through elected representatives.
2. Local government is in Entry 5 of the State List.
3. Deconcentration is not devolution.
4. Urban services create inter-ward and inter-jurisdictional spillovers.
5. Madras received the first municipal corporation in 1688.
6. Lord Ripon's 1882 Resolution is the classic local-self-government milestone.
7. The 1919 Act made local self-government a transferred provincial subject.
8. The 1935 Act treated it as a provincial subject.
9. The 65th Amendment/Nagarpalika Bill failed in 1989.
10. The 74th Amendment entered into force on 1 June 1993.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a State department operating inside a city not equivalent to urban self-government?

**Model answer:** Physical location does not determine devolution. Urban self-government requires elected local authority over defined functions, resources and accountability. A State field office remains answerable upward and may deliver a service without giving city residents control over priorities or leadership.

**Misconception to avoid:** Local self-government does not mean municipal sovereignty; State law and constitutional standards continue to bind it.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the democratic and administrative case for urban local self-government.

**Illustrative response:** Urban local self-government connects area-based public services with democratic accountability. Dense settlements share roads, drainage, water, waste systems, land markets and public spaces; decisions in one ward create effects elsewhere. A municipality can combine local information with city-wide coordination better than isolated departmental offices. Elected councillors also provide a visible forum through which residents can question priorities, taxation and service failures.

Yet proximity alone is insufficient. Small municipalities may lack professional staff and revenue, while local elites may capture land and contracts. State-wide standards, reservation, audit, judicial remedies and technical support are therefore necessary. The proper principle is subsidiarity with safeguards: decide locally what can be handled locally, while higher levels finance equalisation, protect rights and coordinate metropolitan spillovers. Urban self-government is thus democratic devolution within a constitutional and federal framework, not either bureaucratic decentralisation or local sovereignty.

**Quantified rubric (10):** definition/distinction 2; urban coordination logic 3; democratic accountability 2; limits and safeguards 2; qualified conclusion 1.

Once urban self-government is justified, the next difficulty is legal durability: proximity and accountability mean little if a State can redesign or suspend the institution at will. That problem leads directly to the constitutional floor created by the 74th Amendment.

### Visual first: the constitutional map

```text
74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992
              │
              ├── Part IX-A: Articles 243P–243ZG
              ├── Twelfth Schedule: 18 matters
              └── commencement: 1 June 1993

Article 243P definitions
   ↓
243Q–V democratic institution
   ↓
243W–Z functions, finance, accounts
   ↓
243ZA–ZG elections, scope, planning, transition, court bar
```

The amendment constitutionalised municipalities. It did not write one uniform municipal code for India. It created a **constitutional floor** that State law must respect.

### 1. What constitutional status changed

Before Part IX-A, a State could create, supersede or redesign municipalities solely through ordinary legislation. After constitutionalisation:

- municipal categories, electoral structure, reservation and tenure are constitutionally protected;
- a State Election Commission and State Finance Commission become part of the architecture;
- planning committees receive constitutional recognition;
- courts can enforce constitutional limits, although Article 243ZG regulates electoral challenges.

The result is best described as **guaranteed institutional existence with State-dependent empowerment**.

### 2. Article 243P: definitions that control later provisions

| Term | Constitutional meaning |
|---|---|
| Committee | A committee constituted under Article 243S |
| District | A district in a State |
| Metropolitan area | Area with population of **ten lakh or more**, in one or more districts, comprising two or more municipalities or panchayats or contiguous areas, specified by the Governor |
| Municipal area | Territorial area of a municipality notified by the Governor |
| Municipality | Institution of self-government constituted under Article 243Q |
| Panchayat | Panchayat constituted under Article 243B |
| Population | Population as ascertained at the last preceding census whose relevant figures have been published |

These definitions prevent loose reasoning. A metropolitan area is not simply any famous city. It requires the constitutional population threshold and notification. A municipal area is not every settlement that census methodology calls urban.

### 3. Mandatory and enabling provisions

| Constitutional floor | State-law space |
|---|---|
| Three municipal categories | Exact classification and nomenclature |
| Direct election of territorial seats | Chairperson's election and many additional representations |
| Reservation minimums | Rotation and lawful additional backward-class reservation |
| Five-year duration | Detailed dissolution procedure |
| SEC control | Election law and petition machinery |
| SFC review | Tax assignment, grants and implementation |
| DPC/MPC minimum elected fractions | Remaining composition and procedure |

The recurring verb matters. Article 243W says the legislature **may, by law, endow** municipalities with powers. Article 243X similarly enables State-authorised taxation. Constitutional recognition therefore does not automatically transfer all functions or taxes.

### 4. The “third tier” qualification

Municipalities are commonly called the third tier. This is useful but must be handled carefully. They are constitutionally recognised governments, yet legislative competence over local government remains substantially State-centred. Their powers are not a constitutionally exclusive legislative list comparable to Union and State Lists.

### UPSC application

The strongest opening line is: **The 74th Amendment constitutionalised urban democracy but did not constitutionalise a uniform quantum of urban power.**

### Revision notes

1. The 74th Amendment added Part IX-A.
2. Part IX-A runs from Article 243P to 243ZG.
3. The Twelfth Schedule has eighteen matters.
4. Commencement was 1 June 1993.
5. Article 243P supplies controlling definitions.
6. Metropolitan area threshold is ten lakh or more.
7. Municipal area depends on Governor's notification.
8. “Population” uses the last preceding published census figures.
9. Constitutional status guarantees a floor, not uniform functionality.
10. Articles 243W and 243X are enabling rather than self-executing.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can municipalities be constitutionally protected yet remain unevenly empowered?

**Model answer:** Part IX-A mandates core democratic institutions, tenure, reservation, electoral machinery and planning structures. But State law determines much of functional assignment, taxation, personnel and operational design. States can therefore comply with constitutional form while differing sharply in actual devolution.

**Misconception to avoid:** The Twelfth Schedule is not an exclusive municipal legislative list that transfers eighteen matters automatically.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “The 74th Amendment created a constitutional floor, not a uniform model of city government.” Examine.

**Illustrative response:** The 74th Amendment created Part IX-A, Articles 243P–243ZG, and the Twelfth Schedule, thereby protecting urban local government from purely discretionary statutory existence. The floor is substantial: Article 243Q requires municipal institutions; Articles 243R–243V structure representation, reservation and continuity; Article 243ZA places elections under the State Election Commission; Article 243Y applies the State Finance Commission; and Articles 243ZD–243ZE constitutionalise integrated planning.

Uniformity nevertheless ends at this floor. Article 243W allows State legislatures to endow functions; it does not automatically vest all eighteen Twelfth Schedule matters. Article 243X likewise requires State authorisation for taxes, duties, tolls and fees. Chairperson selection, committee details, personnel systems, local audit and the balance between elected councils and commissioners vary under State laws.

This flexibility accommodates different settlement sizes and federal diversity. Its cost is uneven self-government: development authorities, water boards or State cadres may retain the budget, staff and decision even where a municipal function is formally listed. Thus the amendment securely constitutionalised representation and continuity, but functional, fiscal and administrative power remains State-dependent. The constitutional project succeeds institutionally while remaining incomplete operationally.

**Quantified rubric (15):** constitutional floor 5; State-law variation 4; mechanism/examples 3; balanced federal assessment 2; verdict 1.

A constitutional floor still needs a rule for deciding which settlement receives which urban institution. The next lesson therefore asks how Article 243Q classifies transitional, smaller and larger urban areas—and why one industrial exception cannot be mistaken for a fourth municipal type.

### Visual first: classification ladder

```text
Rural settlement
      ↓ transition
NAGAR PANCHAYAT
      ↓ greater urban scale
MUNICIPAL COUNCIL
      ↓ larger urban scale
MUNICIPAL CORPORATION

Separate branch:
industrial establishment provides/proposes municipal services
      ↓ Governor considers constitutional factors
INDUSTRIAL TOWNSHIP NOTIFICATION
      ↓
No municipality need be constituted for that area or part
```

### 1. Three constitutional types

Article 243Q provides:

- **Nagar Panchayat** for a transitional area moving from rural to urban;
- **Municipal Council** for a smaller urban area;
- **Municipal Corporation** for a larger urban area.

The Constitution does not prescribe one national population slab for each. The Governor specifies the area, having regard to:

1. population;
2. population density;
3. revenue generated for local administration;
4. percentage of employment in non-agricultural activities;
5. economic importance; and
6. other factors considered fit.

This is why national counts, uniform thresholds and State-specific nomenclature should not be generalised without current State law.

### 2. The industrial-township proviso

Where an industrial establishment in an urban area or part thereof provides or proposes to provide municipal services, the Governor may, considering the area's size and the services being provided or proposed and other factors, specify it as an **industrial township**. No municipality need then be constituted for that area.

This is an exception to ordinary municipal constitution, not a fourth constitutional species of municipality.

### 3. Three close but different categories

| Category | Nature | Constitutional position |
|---|---|---|
| Census town | Statistical classification for census purposes | Not automatically a municipality |
| Nagar Panchayat | Article 243Q urban municipality for transitional area | Elected constitutional local body |
| Industrial township | Notified exception where industrial establishment provides/proposes services | No municipality need be constituted |

Administrative practice can lag behind functional urbanisation. A settlement may acquire dense non-farm employment while remaining under rural government. The correct solution is not to call every such settlement a municipality, but to apply transparent statutory and constitutional criteria and manage assets, staff, taxes, representation and entitlements during transition.

### 4. Why classification affects rights and services

Reclassification changes:

- the elected forum: Gram Sabha/Panchayat to ward/council arrangements;
- tax and user-charge exposure;
- building and land-use regulation;
- service standards and technical agencies;
- access to rural or urban programme categories;
- representation and the scale of the electorate.

Thus boundary notification is not a purely cartographic act.

### UPSC application

Use Article 243Q in rural–urban merger answers while keeping the institutions distinct: Part IX governs rural Panchayats, whereas Part IX-A supplies the constitutional route into urban municipal government.

### Revision notes

1. Nagar Panchayat is urban, not a village panchayat.
2. Municipal Council serves a smaller urban area.
3. Municipal Corporation serves a larger urban area.
4. The Constitution fixes factors, not uniform national slabs.
5. Non-agricultural employment is one classification factor.
6. Industrial township is an exception, not a fourth municipality.
7. The industrial establishment must provide or propose municipal services.
8. A census town is statistical, not automatically constitutional.
9. Reclassification changes representation, regulation, finance and entitlements.
10. State-specific claims require current State-law verification.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a census town, a Nagar Panchayat and an industrial township not be treated as synonyms?

**Model answer:** A census town is a statistical category. A Nagar Panchayat is an Article 243Q municipality for a transitional area. An industrial township is a notified exception where no municipality need be constituted because an industrial establishment provides or proposes municipal services.

**Misconception to avoid:** Industrial township status does not add a fourth municipality alongside Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council and Municipal Corporation.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the constitutional logic and governance risks of rural-to-urban reclassification.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243Q recognises urbanisation as a transition rather than a binary event. The Governor may specify a Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council or Municipal Corporation after considering population, density, local revenue, non-agricultural employment and economic importance. This permits institutions to follow the actual scale and character of settlement.

Reclassification can improve integrated water, sanitation, roads, land-use control and access to urban finance. It can also raise taxes, remove the Gram Sabha forum, alter programme eligibility, transfer assets and dilute the voice of formerly rural residents. A census label alone is therefore insufficient. States need published criteria, consultation, service and fiscal impact assessment, staff and record transfer, transitional representation and continuity of entitlements. Article 243Q supports timely urban government, but lawful classification must be matched by a fair transition process.

**Quantified rubric (10):** Article 243Q factors 3; benefits 2; risks 3; transition safeguards 1; conclusion 1.

Constituting the right municipal type settles the area, but not who speaks or votes within it. The next problem is representative design: wards, additional members, chairpersons and the neighbourhood-level committee through which a large municipality can remain locally answerable.

## Lesson 4 — Articles 243R–243S: Composition, Wards, Chairpersons and Ward Committees

### Visual first: representation from ward to council

```text
Municipal area
   ├── Ward 1 ─ elected councillor ┐
   ├── Ward 2 ─ elected councillor ├─ MUNICIPAL COUNCIL
   ├── Ward 3 ─ elected councillor ┘
   │
   ├── permitted additional representation under State law
   │     experts: no vote in municipal meetings
   │     specified MPs/MLAs
   │     specified RS/Legislative Council members
   │     chairpersons of committees other than Ward Committees
   │
   └── if population ≥ 3 lakh → Ward Committee(s)
```

### 1. Direct election is the democratic base

Article 243R requires all territorial seats to be filled by direct election from wards. The municipality's territorial area is divided into constituencies called wards.

State law may provide representation for:

- persons with special knowledge or experience in municipal administration, **without voting rights in municipal meetings**;
- Lok Sabha and State Assembly members representing constituencies wholly or partly covering the municipal area;
- Rajya Sabha and Legislative Council members registered as electors within it;
- chairpersons of committees constituted under Article 243S(5), other than Ward Committees.

State law also determines how the municipal chairperson is elected.

### 2. Why experts have no municipal vote

Expert participation can improve technical deliberation, but a nominated expert lacks the same territorial mandate as an elected councillor. The no-vote rule preserves the elected character of municipal decision-making while allowing advice.

### 3. Ward Committees under Article 243S

Ward Committees must be constituted within the territorial area of a municipality with a population of **three lakh or more**. A committee may cover one or more wards. State law determines:

- composition;
- territorial area;
- manner of filling seats.

Article 243S then supplies a democratic minimum that State law cannot erase:

- every municipal councillor representing a ward within the Ward Committee's territorial area is a member of that committee;
- if the committee consists of one ward, that ward's councillor is its chairperson;
- if it consists of two or more wards, the committee's members elect one of the councillor-members representing those wards as chairperson.

State law may also create additional committees.

### 4. Ward Committee is not the whole solution

A Ward Committee can:

- reveal street-level priorities;
- monitor local works;
- scrutinise sanitation and maintenance;
- connect residents to councillors;
- reduce the distance created by large city councils.

But its effectiveness depends on meeting regularity, accessible information, staff responsiveness, a small discretionary budget or clear referral power, inclusive participation and closure of grievances. A committee without records or administrative response may become a consultation ritual.

### UPSC application

For a ward-democracy answer, show the complete chain:

```text
voice → agenda → budget/work order → implementation → disclosure → correction
```

Participation that ends before budget or correction is incomplete accountability.

### Revision notes

1. Territorial seats are directly elected from wards.
2. Experts may be represented but cannot vote in municipal meetings.
3. Specified legislators may be represented under State law.
4. Chairperson election is State-law determined.
5. Ward Committees are mandatory at population three lakh or more.
6. One Ward Committee may comprise one or more wards.
7. Every councillor representing a constituent ward is a committee member.
8. A one-ward committee is chaired by that ward's councillor.
9. In a multi-ward committee, members elect one councillor-member as chairperson.
10. State law controls the remaining composition and seat-filling design.
11. Additional committees are constitutionally permitted.
12. Ward voice needs administrative and fiscal linkage.
13. Ward Committee performance cannot be generalised nationally without State evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** What democratic problem does the no-vote rule for nominated municipal experts solve?

**Model answer:** It allows technical knowledge to enter deliberation without letting unelected persons displace the voting authority of territorially elected representatives. Advice is included while the council's democratic mandate is preserved.

**Misconception to avoid:** Article 243R does not prohibit all additional representation; it specifically denies nominated experts a vote in municipal meetings.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess whether Ward Committees can make large-city government genuinely participatory.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243S addresses the democratic distance created by large municipal areas by requiring Ward Committees in municipalities with populations of three lakh or more. Because one committee may cover one or several wards, it can translate neighbourhood information into city government and give residents a forum closer than the full council.

Its potential is significant. Ward Committees can identify maintenance failures, scrutinise local works, monitor sanitation, expose exclusion and create a public record against which councillors and officials are questioned. They also complement Article 243R's ward-based direct election.

However, constitutional existence does not guarantee influence. State laws determine composition and seat-filling; committees may lack predictable meetings, public data, staff support, fiscal discretion and a binding response process. Participation can also be dominated by organised or affluent groups unless timings, languages, accessibility and representation are designed inclusively.

Effective Ward Committees need published agendas and minutes, ward-level service and expenditure dashboards, time-bound departmental replies, participatory prioritisation of a defined local budget and escalation to standing committees or the council. They should deepen representative government, not create an unelected parallel executive. Properly connected to finance and administration, they can make metropolitan democracy continuous; otherwise, they remain consultative shells.

**Quantified rubric (15):** Article 243S design 3; participatory mechanisms 4; constraints 4; reforms 3; verdict 1.

Representation can be territorially direct yet still exclude social groups or be interrupted by dissolution. The next lesson therefore connects inclusive entry, fixed tenure, timely renewal and lawful eligibility into one continuous democratic cycle.

## Lesson 5 — Articles 243T–243V: Reservation, Tenure, Dissolution and Disqualification

Progress: 5 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: inclusion, continuity and eligibility

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

- **Book context:** Articles 243T–243V, the complete constitutional treatment and local-government case-law controls checked.
- **CA Search:** “Supreme Court OBC reservation local bodies triple test 2026”
- **CA Found:** “Supreme Court Seeks Maharashtra Local Body Poll Timeline, Separates OBC Reservation Issues” — 21 July 2026 — The Bar Bulletin.

### Visual first: the democratic continuity chain

```text
Inclusive entry (243T)
      ↓
fixed institutional life (243U)
      ↓
timely electoral renewal
      ↓
lawful eligibility (243V)
      ↓
continuous representative municipality
```

### 1. Reservation under Article 243T

Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion, as nearly as may be, to their population in the municipal area. These seats may rotate among constituencies.

The women's seat floor has two layers:

- not less than one-third of SC/ST reserved seats must be reserved for SC/ST women;
- not less than one-third of the total directly elected seats, including SC/ST women's seats, must be reserved for women and may rotate.

Article 243T(4) is different. It requires offices of municipal chairpersons to be reserved for SCs, STs and women, but leaves the **manner of that reservation entirely to State legislation**. The Constitution does not itself prescribe a proportional SC/ST formula for chairperson offices or a one-third women's quota among chairperson offices.

State law may provide reservation for backward classes. Unlike constitutionally population-linked SC/ST reservation, such reservation is controlled by the judicial **triple test**: a dedicated empirical commission, local-body-wise determination of need and compliance with the aggregate ceiling identified by the Court.

Article 243T(5) links the SC/ST reservation under clauses (1) and (2), and SC/ST representation in chairperson offices under clause (4), to the period specified in Article 334. The proviso expressly protects women's reservation under clauses (3) and (4) from that cessation rule. As on **1 October 2026**, the Article 334 period for SC/ST legislative reservation is eighty years from the Constitution's commencement and has not expired; it runs to January 2030 unless the Constitution is amended again.

### 2. Duration and dissolution under Article 243U

Every municipality continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting unless sooner dissolved under law. It must receive a reasonable opportunity of being heard before dissolution.

Election must be completed:

- before expiry of the five-year duration; or
- within six months of dissolution.

If the remainder of the dissolved body's term is less than six months, an election for that short remainder is unnecessary. A reconstituted municipality serves only the unexpired remainder, not a fresh five-year term. A legal amendment cannot dissolve a functioning municipality before its five-year term merely by changing the law.

### 3. Disqualification under Article 243V

A person is disqualified if disqualified under the law governing State-legislature elections or under State municipal law. But a person who has attained **twenty-one years** cannot be disqualified merely for being below twenty-five. Questions are decided by the authority and procedure State law provides.

### 4. Case-law safeguards

- ***Kishansing Tomar v Municipal Corporation of Ahmedabad* (2006):** timely municipal elections are a constitutional mandate; ordinary administrative delay cannot defeat Article 243U.
- ***K. Krishna Murthy v Union of India* (2010):** political reservation for backward classes requires distinct constitutional justification and empirical care.
- ***Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v State of Maharashtra* (2021):** operationalised the triple-test discipline.

### UPSC application

Reservation opens the door; it does not by itself transfer executive power, staff, money or social agency. A strong answer distinguishes **descriptive representation** from **substantive authority**.

### Revision notes

1. SC/ST seats are population-linked within the municipal area.
2. At least one-third of SC/ST reserved seats go to SC/ST women.
3. At least one-third of all directly elected seats go to women.
4. State law determines the manner of SC/ST/women reservation in chairperson offices.
5. Article 243T itself fixes no proportional or one-third chairperson-office formula.
6. Clause (5) links SC/ST reservation to Article 334 but expressly preserves women's reservation.
7. Backward-class reservation is enabling, not automatic.
8. The triple test requires empirical and local-body-wise justification.
9. Municipal duration is five years from the first meeting.
10. A municipality must be heard before dissolution.
11. Election follows before expiry or within six months after dissolution.
12. The short-remainder exception applies below six months.
13. A reconstituted body serves the unexpired remainder.
14. Twenty-one years protects eligibility against the ordinary twenty-five-year rule.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does an election after premature dissolution not give the new municipality a fresh five-year term?

**Model answer:** Article 243U preserves the original constitutional cycle. A municipality constituted after premature dissolution serves only the remainder of the dissolved body's term, preventing dissolution from being used to reset tenure strategically.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Election within six months” is not a licence for a routine six-month administrator period after every normal expiry; elections must be completed before the scheduled term ends.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine how Articles 243T–243V combine social inclusion with institutional continuity.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243T constitutionalises inclusive entry. SC/ST seats are reserved broadly in proportion to their municipal population; at least one-third of SC/ST reserved seats and at least one-third of all directly elected seats are reserved for women. For chairperson offices, Article 243T(4) requires reservation for SCs, STs and women but leaves its manner to State law; it does not itself impose a proportional SC/ST formula or a one-third women's chairperson quota. Clause (5) links SC/ST reservation to Article 334 while expressly preserving women's reservation. State law may add backward-class reservation, but *K. Krishna Murthy* and *Vikas Gawali* require empirical justification, local-body-wise calibration and judicially mandated limits.

Inclusion would be hollow if municipalities could disappear. Article 243U therefore fixes a five-year duration, requires a pre-dissolution hearing and mandates election before normal expiry or within six months of premature dissolution. The short-remainder exception and unexpired-term rule prevent manipulation of the electoral calendar. *Kishansing Tomar* reinforces timely elections against ordinary administrative excuses.

Article 243V completes the design by connecting municipal disqualification to law while protecting candidates who have attained twenty-one years from the ordinary twenty-five-year legislative threshold.

The architecture is powerful but not self-executing. Reservation may change who occupies office without changing who controls staff or budgets; elections may be timely yet councillors remain weak. Social inclusion and continuity are therefore necessary constitutional conditions, while substantive empowerment requires functions, finance, personnel and protection against proxy control.

**Quantified rubric (15):** reservation precision 5; tenure/dissolution precision 4; disqualification 2; cases 2; qualification 2.

An inclusive and continuously elected council may still lack meaningful work. The next question is functional: which urban responsibilities can State law entrust to municipalities, and why does listing eighteen matters not itself transfer staff, money or operational control?

### 1. Article 243X

The State legislature may by law:

- authorise a municipality to levy, collect and appropriate taxes, duties, tolls and fees;
- assign to it taxes, duties, tolls and fees levied and collected by the State, subject to conditions and limits;
- provide grants-in-aid from the State Consolidated Fund;
- create municipal funds and regulate crediting and withdrawal.

The word **authorise** is decisive. A municipality cannot invent a tax merely because local revenue is desirable.

### 2. Article 243Y and the SFC

Article 243Y applies the State Finance Commission constituted under Article 243I **every fifth year** to municipal finances. It recommends to the Governor:

- principles governing State–municipality distribution of net proceeds and allocation among municipalities;
- taxes, duties, tolls and fees that may be assigned;
- grants-in-aid from the State Consolidated Fund;
- measures to improve municipal financial position;
- other matters referred in the interest of sound municipal finance.

The Governor lays the recommendations and an explanatory memorandum on action taken before the State legislature.

### 3. The two-commission chain

```text
Local accounts and needs
      ↓
State Finance Commission: State–local settlement
      ↓
State action and legislative scrutiny
      ↓
Union Finance Commission, Article 280(3)(c):
measures to augment State Consolidated Fund
to supplement municipal resources
```

Article 243Y concerns the State Finance Commission’s municipal review, whereas Article 280 establishes the Union Finance Commission and clause 280(3)(c) supplies the municipal-augmentation link. The two constitutional levels must not be conflated.

### 4. Why the SFC is often the missing bridge

The SFC can connect functions to predictable revenue. The bridge fails when:

- constitution is delayed;
- municipal accounts are incomparable or late;
- recommendations lack a fiscal map;
- State action memoranda are weak;
- accepted transfers are not predictable;
- functions and staff are ignored.

An SFC recommendation is also not self-executing. State government and legislature must act.

### UPSC application

Do not frame municipal finance as “own revenue versus grants.” Unequal cities need both accountable own-source effort and equalising transfers.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Article 243X not itself give every municipality an identical taxing power?

**Model answer:** It authorises State legislatures to confer municipal taxing, assignment, grant and fund arrangements. The usable tax base therefore depends on State law, conditions and administrative capacity rather than arising uniformly from the Constitution.

**Misconception to avoid:** The State Finance Commission and Union Finance Commission are not interchangeable; they operate at different fiscal levels.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Assess the State Finance Commission's role in municipal fiscal autonomy.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243Y extends to municipalities the State Finance Commission that Article 243I requires every fifth year. It reviews the distribution of State tax proceeds, assignment of taxes and fees, grants-in-aid and measures needed to improve municipal finances. Its report and the Governor's action memorandum must be placed before the State legislature. The SFC can therefore convert Article 243X's broad fiscal channels into a periodic, evidence-based State–local settlement.

Its practical influence is often weaker than its constitutional design. Delayed constitution, poor municipal accounts, weak technical support, selective acceptance and unpredictable release break the link between functions and finance. Higher Finance Commission grants under Article 280(3)(c) can support accounts, services and revenue reform, but cannot determine State tax assignment or place municipal staff under council control.

Reform requires the next SFC to be constituted before the previous award expires, a permanent data and secretariat unit, comparable accrual and budget records, transparent formulae, a reasoned action memorandum and disclosure of implementation. SFC analysis must also cost devolved functions rather than divide money without examining responsibility.

The SFC is thus indispensable but not sufficient. Municipal autonomy needs authorised and administered own revenues, equalising transfers, expenditure discretion and accountable capacity alongside a timely fiscal commission.


### 1. Property tax

Property tax is the central municipal own-tax instrument in the standard literature. Its strengths are a visible local base and a link between urban land/property value and local services.

Its performance depends on:

- complete property coverage;
- current valuation;
- lawful and transparent rates;
- limited and reasoned exemptions;
- billing and collection;
- appeal and correction;
- public linkage between revenue and service.

GIS can improve discovery and mapping, but a digital parcel map is not automatically a lawful assessment. Ownership, occupancy, use, valuation, notice and appeal still require due process.

### 2. User charges and fees

Water, sewerage, sanitation, parking, licences and markets may generate non-tax revenue. Charges can support maintenance and reveal service cost, but full cost recovery may exclude poor households. A defensible design combines lifeline access or targeted subsidy with metering, transparent tariffs and better service.

### 3. Grants

Grants perform at least three functions:

- **equalisation:** compensate for unequal tax bases;
- **minimum standards:** support water, sanitation or other basic services;
- **incentives:** reward accounts, revenue effort or service outcomes.

The Sixteenth Finance Commission's 2026–31 recommendations are dated policy, not constitutional constants. FC-XVI Table 10.5 recommends **₹4,35,236 crore for rural local bodies and ₹3,56,257 crore for urban local bodies**, out of ₹7,91,493 crore in total—approximately **55:45** rural–urban. Gujarat, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh and Uttarakhand had recommended a 60:40 rural–urban allocation in their memoranda; that State proposal is not the Commission's total-award ratio. The Commission did use 60:40 only for the combined **basic and performance components**, while the urban total also includes a ₹56,100 crore special-infrastructure component and a ₹10,000 crore urbanisation premium. Within the rural and urban basic/performance pools, the split is 80:20. Conditions concern duly constituted bodies, accounts, SFC action and relevant own-source-revenue effort.

### 4. Municipal bonds

A municipal bond borrows from investors for capital expenditure and must be repaid with interest. It can:

- match long-lived assets with long-term finance;
- impose disclosure and financial discipline;
- diversify beyond grants and bank loans.

It cannot repair an unviable revenue base. Creditworthy borrowing requires audited accounts, predictable cash flows, lawful approvals, project preparation and debt-service capacity. Pooled finance may help smaller bodies share transaction costs and credit support.

SEBI's 2015 municipal-debt regulations form the market framework. The 8 July 2026 amendment and 11 August 2026 circular are current dated controls; they include operational changes for privately placed securities and pooled-finance arrangements. They do not convert municipal debt into own revenue.

### 5. The fiscal accountability loop

```text
resident pays locally
      ↓
municipality discloses use
      ↓
service becomes visible
      ↓
resident demands correction
      ↓
revenue legitimacy improves
```

Grant dependence can weaken this loop by orienting accountability upward, but own revenue without transparency can also become coercive. Both require audit and citizen remedies.

1. Property tax is a key municipal own-tax instrument.
2. Coverage, valuation, exemptions and collection are separate reform problems.
3. GIS supports assessment but does not replace legal due process.
4. User charges should distinguish cost recovery from universal access.
5. Grants equalise unequal municipal capacity.
6. Conditional grants can improve accounts but reduce discretion.

8. A bond is debt, not revenue or a grant.

12. Strong finance joins adequacy, autonomy, equity and accountability.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can municipal bonds not substitute for property-tax and transfer reform?

**Model answer:** Bonds finance capital by borrowing; they require repayment from future cash flows. Without reliable own revenue, transfers, accounts and debt-service capacity, borrowing merely shifts weakness forward and can endanger essential services.

**Misconception to avoid:** A successful bond issue is not proof that all municipalities can or should borrow from markets.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Design a balanced municipal-finance strategy for Indian cities.

**Illustrative response:** A balanced strategy must combine autonomy, equalisation and prudence. First, States should provide clear Article 243X authority for property tax, fees and user charges. Municipalities should widen property coverage, update valuation, rationalise exemptions and create transparent appeal systems. GIS can improve discovery, but lawful assessment and citizen correction remain essential.

Second, user charges should fund maintainable services without excluding vulnerable households. Lifeline supply or targeted subsidy is preferable to either free universal provision or unaffordable full-cost pricing.

Third, timely State Finance Commissions should cost devolved functions and recommend predictable transfers. Union Finance Commission grants can support minimum services, accounts and revenue effort; the Sixteenth Commission's 2026–31 design is useful as a dated incentive framework, not a permanent formula.

Fourth, municipal bonds should finance prepared, long-lived projects only where audited accounts and stable cash flows support debt service. Pooled finance can assist smaller bodies. SEBI's July–August 2026 changes strengthen the contemporary regulatory route, but debt cannot replace own revenue.

Finally, budgets, liabilities, contracts and service outcomes must be publicly linked. The aim is not municipal self-financing at any cost; it is a credible mix in which local payment strengthens accountability, grants protect equity and borrowing remains sustainable.

Every revenue instrument creates a traceability problem: who recorded the liability, spent the money and can be replaced when accountability fails? That leads to the linked disciplines of accounts, audit, electoral control and the judicial rules governing election disputes.

### 1. Article 243Z: accounts and audit

State legislatures may provide by law for maintenance and audit of municipal accounts. The Constitution does not make the CAG the routine primary auditor of every municipality.

The practical architecture may involve local-fund audit institutions, State departments, chartered accountants under law and CAG technical guidance or statutory audit where public-finance law permits. The safe boundary is:

- Article 243Z leaves primary design to State law;
- the CAG's wider constitutional/statutory mandate applies only where the relevant legal conditions are met;
- technical guidance is not identical to direct audit responsibility.

Good audit requires common accounting standards, asset and liability registers, timely closure, public reports and corrective action. An audit report without recovery, disciplinary action or process reform is incomplete accountability.

### 2. Article 243ZA: State Election Commission

The superintendence, direction and control of electoral rolls and all municipal elections vest in the State Election Commission. State legislatures may make election law subject to the Constitution.

Article 243ZA imports the Article 243K framework, linking municipal electoral autonomy with the local-government SEC.

***Fouziya Imtiaz Shaikh* (2021)** held that the office requires independence; a serving government officer holding another State office cannot simply be given additional charge as SEC. ***Kishansing Tomar* (2006)** protects the election timetable.

### 3. Article 243ZG: the electoral bar

Two rules operate:

1. the validity of a law relating to delimitation or seat allotment under Article 243ZA cannot be questioned in court;
2. a municipal election can be questioned only through an election petition to the authority and in the manner State law provides.

The purpose is electoral continuity. It prevents ordinary litigation from repeatedly interrupting the process. It does not license unconstitutional delay, destroy all judicial review or validate a body that refuses to hold elections.

### 4. Why accounts and elections belong together

Election makes rulers replaceable; accounts make decisions traceable. A councillor cannot be held meaningfully accountable if the resident cannot see the ward allocation, contract, bill, asset and service outcome.

```text
                       ├── spatial planning
                       ├── shared water/natural resources
                       ├── integrated infrastructure
                       ├── environmental conservation
                       └── available financial/other resources
                                   ↓
Chairperson forwards plan to State Government
```

### 1. Composition

Every State must constitute a District Planning Committee at district level. State law provides its composition, election method, planning functions and chairperson selection.

At least **four-fifths** of the total members must be elected by and from:

- elected members of the district-level Panchayat; and
- elected members of municipalities in the district.

Their representation is proportional to the ratio of rural and urban populations in the district. Four-fifths is a constitutional minimum elected fraction, not the entire composition formula.

### 2. Planning duty

The DPC consolidates Panchayat and municipal plans and prepares a draft development plan for the district as a whole. It must consider:

- matters of common interest, including spatial planning;
- sharing of water and other physical and natural resources;
- integrated infrastructure;
- environmental conservation;
- extent and type of available resources, financial or otherwise;
- consultation with institutions and organisations specified by the Governor.

The chairperson forwards the draft plan to the State Government.

### 3. Consolidation is not stapling

Mechanical compilation cannot resolve competing land use, water allocation or road alignments. Genuine consolidation requires:

1. common data and maps;
2. identification of inter-jurisdictional effects;
3. prioritisation under a resource envelope;
4. negotiation between rural and urban bodies;
5. a reasoned integrated plan.

### 4. The DPC’s constitutional and operational role

Article 243ZD places the DPC in Part IX-A even though it integrates Panchayat and municipal plans. Its constitutional task is preparation of a district-wide draft plan; whether that plan improves services depends on budgets, departments and implementation aligning with it.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a DPC more than a committee for collecting local plans?

**Model answer:** Article 243ZD requires a district-wide draft plan that addresses common spatial, resource, infrastructure and environmental interests within available resources. This demands reconciliation and prioritisation, not merely attaching separate local plans.

**Misconception to avoid:** The DPC is not the district administration and its four-fifths rule is a minimum elected fraction, not a claim that every member is locally elected.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate the District Planning Committee as the constitutional bridge between rural and urban development.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243ZD gives the District Planning Committee a distinctive integrative mandate. It consolidates Panchayat and municipal plans and prepares a draft development plan for the whole district. At least four-fifths of members are elected by and from elected district-Panchayat and municipal members, proportionate to the rural–urban population ratio. This gives integration a representative rather than purely bureaucratic base.

The DPC's substantive value lies in common-interest planning. Peri-urban roads, watersheds, waste facilities, markets and environmental systems cross local boundaries. Article 243ZD expressly requires attention to spatial planning, shared physical and natural resources, integrated infrastructure, environmental conservation and available resources.

In practice, committees may merely compile schemes, lack a planning secretariat or operate after State departments have fixed investments. Rural and urban plans may also use incompatible data and timelines. The chairperson's duty to forward the draft plan does not itself guarantee budget authority.

Reform needs a common geospatial and fiscal baseline, professional support answerable to the committee, early disclosure of departmental investment, public consultation and a reasoned State response where the draft is altered. The DPC is the correct constitutional bridge, but it becomes effective only when the planning cycle and resource decisions actually pass through it.

### 1. Threshold and definition

Article 243P defines a metropolitan area as one with population of **ten lakh or more**, spanning one or more districts and consisting of two or more municipalities or panchayats or contiguous areas, specified by the Governor.

### 2. Composition

Every metropolitan area must have an MPC. State law determines composition, election, representation of Union/State agencies, planning and coordination functions and chairperson selection.

At least **two-thirds** of members must be elected by and from:

- elected members of municipalities; and
- chairpersons of Panchayats in the metropolitan area.

Representation follows the ratio of the population of municipalities to Panchayats in the metropolitan area.

### 3. Draft development plan

The MPC must consider:

- plans prepared by municipalities and Panchayats;
- coordinated spatial planning;
- sharing of water and other physical/natural resources;
- integrated infrastructure;
- environmental conservation;
- Union and State objectives and priorities;
- investments likely from Union/State agencies;
- available financial and other resources;
- consultation specified by the Governor.

The chairperson forwards the draft plan to the State Government.

### 4. Why metropolitan government is difficult

Economic and ecological systems exceed municipal boundaries, while electoral mandates remain local. A metropolitan body must coordinate without making municipalities meaningless.

Parastatals may possess technical skill and large capital budgets. Their defence is scale and expertise. The objection is fragmented democratic accountability: the resident votes for a councillor but a separate authority controls land, water or transport. The resolution is not necessarily abolition, but transparent assignment, municipal representation, integrated plans and clear answerability.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the MPC include Panchayat chairpersons even though it is a metropolitan institution?

**Model answer:** Metropolitan areas may include peri-urban Panchayat territory and cross rural–urban boundaries. Including Panchayat chairpersons allows the plan to account for shared land, water, infrastructure and transition effects rather than treating the metropolitan core in isolation.

**Misconception to avoid:** An MPC is not automatically a metropolitan executive government controlling every agency, budget and service.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** “Indian metropolitan areas are planned by multiple agencies but not necessarily through metropolitan democracy.” Discuss.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243ZE creates the Metropolitan Planning Committee to reconcile fragmented urban regions. For every metropolitan area—constitutionally ten lakh or more and Governor-specified—at least two-thirds of MPC members must be elected by and from municipal elected members and Panchayat chairpersons, proportionate to their populations.

Its plan must integrate local plans, spatial coordination, shared water and natural resources, infrastructure, environmental conservation, Union–State priorities and expected public investment. This is designed to place metropolitan coordination within an elected constitutional chain.

Actual authority is often dispersed among development authorities, water and sewerage boards, transport bodies, housing agencies and mission-specific SPVs. Such bodies may offer expertise and scale, but each controls a fragment of land, finance or staff. Residents then cannot identify one elected forum responsible for the metropolitan outcome.

Reform should make the MPC the mandatory forum for the integrated spatial-investment framework, require parastatals to disclose and align capital plans, provide a professional secretariat and publish deviations by the State. Municipalities should retain local service accountability, while the MPC resolves cross-boundary systems. Metropolitan democracy does not require one giant municipality; it requires that coordination itself becomes representative, transparent and consequential.

A metropolitan plan can coordinate space and investment only if institutions can execute it. The next learning problem is therefore internal municipal operation: how councils, mayors, commissioners, committees and personnel systems divide authority and responsibility.

### Visual first: the operating triangle

```text

   ↓ elect

   │

   └── COMMISSIONER / CHIEF OFFICER ─ administration, staff, execution

Core design question:
```

### 1. Council

The council is the deliberative representative body. Depending on State law, it adopts budgets, by-laws, policies and resolutions; scrutinises expenditure; and represents wards.

### 2. Mayor or chairperson

The mayor symbolises city-wide political leadership and presides over the corporation. Election mode, tenure and executive authority vary by State. Some arrangements produce a strong mayor; others leave the office mainly ceremonial or short-tenured.

The directly elected mayor debate is therefore not only about electoral method. It asks whether the elected city-wide leader receives:

- stable tenure;
- budget influence;
- committee coordination;
- staff oversight;
- accountability to the council;
- protection against concentration of power.

### 3. Commissioner or chief municipal officer

The commissioner is generally the administrative executive under State municipal law, commonly State-appointed. The office implements council decisions, manages administration and supervises service delivery. Professional continuity is valuable, but a commissioner accountable primarily upward can weaken downward democratic control.

The best distinction is:

```text
political direction and public accountability
                    +
professional administration and legality
                    =
effective municipal executive
```

Neither elected amateurism nor bureaucratic substitution is adequate.

### 4. Standing committees

Large councils need specialised committees for finance, taxation, public works, health or other fields. Committees can deepen scrutiny and distribute workload, but opaque delegation can also move important decisions away from the full council. Membership, powers, minutes and reporting should be public.

### 5. Personnel systems

| System | Control | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate | Each municipality | loyalty and autonomy | uneven capacity |
| Unified | State-wide municipal cadre | mobility and professional standards | upward control |
| Integrated | Municipal and State personnel in common service | flexible deployment | local accountability diluted |

No one system is universally best. The test is whether staff capability, career protection and local answerability are aligned.

1. Council is the representative deliberative wing.
2. Mayor/chairperson design varies by State.
3. Commissioner is the administrative executive under State law.
4. Direct election alone does not create a strong mayor.
5. Stable tenure and authority must match responsibility.
6. Committees specialise council work.
7. Committee transparency prevents hidden delegation.
8. Separate personnel favours local loyalty.
9. Unified personnel favours professional mobility.
10. Integrated personnel links municipal and State services.
11. Professional expertise and democratic control are complementary.
12. Avoid national generalisation from one State municipal act.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the mayor–commissioner issue better understood as an accountability problem than as a personality conflict?

**Model answer:** The issue concerns institutional allocation of budget, staff, implementation and public responsibility. If the elected mayor is blamed but the appointed commissioner controls execution—or vice versa—authority and accountability diverge regardless of the individuals involved.

**Misconception to avoid:** A directly elected mayor is not automatically effective unless tenure, powers, council relations and administrative control are coherently designed.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate the mayor–commissioner model of urban administration.

**Illustrative response:** The mayor–commissioner model seeks to combine democratic leadership with professional administration. The elected council and mayor articulate priorities, adopt budgets and remain publicly answerable. The commissioner supplies continuity, legality, technical coordination and staff management.

Its weakness arises when responsibility and authority separate. A mayor may face city-wide electoral expectations yet lack stable tenure, budget influence or staff control. A State-appointed commissioner may control execution while remaining primarily answerable upward. Standing committees and parastatals can further fragment the chain. The result is blame without command and administration without direct local accountability.

A wholly political executive, however, may not solve technical and integrity problems. Large cities require professional planners, engineers, accountants and procurement systems. Reform should therefore define reserved political and administrative domains, give elected leadership a stable mandate and meaningful budget role, protect the commissioner's duty to law, place service standards before the council and create transparent dispute resolution. Standing committees should scrutinise specialised sectors with public minutes.

The appropriate model is not “mayor versus commissioner” but a dual executive with clear authority, professional competence and one visible democratic accountability chain. State variation makes institutional design—not one national election formula—the proper focus.

### 1. Cantonment Board

A Cantonment Board administers civilian municipal needs in a cantonment area under central cantonment legislation and the Ministry of Defence framework. The Cantonments Act, 2006 replaced the 1924 Act and provides a partly elected, partly ex officio structure and municipal-type obligatory/discretionary functions.

Its defence setting, central legal basis and mixed composition distinguish it from an Article 243Q municipality. Similar functions do not create identical constitutional status.

The 2026 Jan Vishwas event illustrates the boundary. The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2026 amended the Cantonments Act through its Schedule, and Ministry of Defence S.R.O. 7(E) commenced those listed amendments on 22 May 2026. This changed the governing central statute; it did **not** convert Cantonment Boards into Part IX-A municipalities.

### 2. Notified Area Committee and Town Area Committee

The textbook taxonomy describes a Notified Area Committee for a fast-developing or otherwise important area not yet constituted as a municipality; it is created by notification and is generally nominated. A Town Area Committee is a limited, semi-municipal body for a small town under State law.

Names and continued use vary by State. Do not transform this descriptive taxonomy into a claim that every State currently has all eight types.

### 3. Township and port institution

A public-enterprise township may provide civic amenities through enterprise administration without an elected municipal council. Port institutions combine port management with civic functions under their governing central law. Neither should be casually counted as a fourth Article 243Q municipality.

### 4. Special-purpose agencies and parastatals

Development authorities, water/sewerage boards, housing boards and transport bodies perform functions that overlap the municipal field. Their justification is expertise, scale and capital coordination. Their risks are:

- fragmented budgets;
- unclear citizen forum;
- land and service decisions outside council scrutiny;
- duplication;
- upward rather than local accountability.

Reform can use service agreements, municipal representation, plan conformity, shared data and legislative reporting without assuming every specialist body must disappear.

### Revision notes

1. Cantonments are not Article 243Q municipalities.
2. Cantonment administration rests on central law and Defence control.
3. Municipal-type functions do not prove municipal constitutional status.
4. A Notified Area Committee is generally nominated in the textbook model.
5. A Town Area Committee has limited civic functions.
6. Public-enterprise townships may lack elected members.
7. Port bodies combine port and civic roles.
8. Special-purpose agencies are function-based.
9. State-specific current institutional names require verification.
10. The classic eight-type taxonomy is descriptive, not a fixed national count.
11. Parastatals can add expertise and fragment accountability simultaneously.
12. Agency reform should clarify authority, finance and citizen remedy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a Cantonment Board not a municipality under Article 243Q even though it performs municipal functions?

**Model answer:** Constitutional identity depends on legal source and institutional design, not functional resemblance alone. Cantonment Boards operate under central cantonment law and Defence administration, whereas Article 243Q municipalities are constituted under the Part IX-A State-law framework.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Urban local body” in descriptive writing does not mean every urban institution belongs to the same constitutional category.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish constitutional municipalities from other urban institutions.

**Illustrative response:** Article 243Q constitutionally recognises three municipalities: Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council and Municipal Corporation. Their territorial seats, reservation, tenure, SEC elections and fiscal review operate within Part IX-A.

Other urban bodies may perform similar services without sharing that status. Cantonment Boards arise under central cantonment law and Defence administration. Notified or Town Area Committees depend on State statutory arrangements; enterprise townships may be bureaucratically managed; port bodies combine port and civic functions. Development authorities and water boards are function-specific parastatals rather than general elected governments.

The distinction matters for accountability. A resident may elect a councillor but find that land, water or transport is controlled elsewhere. Specialist agencies can provide scale and expertise, yet their plans and budgets should be aligned with elected municipal institutions through representation, disclosure and service agreements. Functional similarity must not obscure constitutional and democratic difference.

**Quantified rubric (10):** Article 243Q category 2; cantonment distinction 2; other institutions 3; accountability analysis 2; conclusion 1.

### 1. From three Fs to functionality

Funds, functions and functionaries are inputs. **Functionality** means that the institution can reliably convert them into outcomes. That requires:

- defined service standards;
- preventive maintenance;
- procurement and contract management;
- asset registers;
- trained frontline staff;
- grievance closure;
- ward-level verification;
- correction after audit.

### 2. Ward accountability

A resident experiences government through a tap, drain, road, light, permit, birth certificate or waste route. Ward accountability should therefore publish:

- responsible agency and officer;
- service standard;
- ward budget and works;
- contractor and completion status;
- complaint age and closure;
- reasons for rejection or delay.

Ward Committees can verify priorities and outcomes, but should not become exclusive clubs. Women, informal workers, tenants, slum residents, persons with disabilities and linguistic minorities need accessible participation.

### 3. Smart Cities and SPVs

The Smart Cities Mission used city-level special-purpose vehicles for project implementation. An SPV can concentrate expertise, procurement and execution. Its democratic risk is bypass: a company board may control project money outside normal council committees. The correct assessment is conditional:

- Does the project fit the municipal and metropolitan plan?
- Is the council informed and able to scrutinise?
- Are assets and operating liabilities transferred transparently?
- Who answers after mission funding ends?

### 4. AMRUT 2.0

AMRUT 2.0, launched in 2021, focuses on urban water security, water supply, sewerage/septage, water-body rejuvenation and green spaces through State and city action plans, reforms and capacity support. It is a scheme framework, not constitutional transfer of the Twelfth Schedule.

It describes the IIRS/ISRO application as supporting field-data collection and attribute vetting under AMRUT 2.0; its version 1.8.0 listing was updated on 22 September 2026. The evidentiary value is operational traceability, not proof that a project was completed or that municipal authority was devolved.

### 5. NUDM and digital public infrastructure

The National Urban Digital Mission promotes shared, interoperable digital building blocks; UPYOG supports municipal applications such as property tax, water/sewerage and grievance services.

Digital systems can:

- standardise workflows;
- improve traceability;
- reduce repeated software costs;
- enable performance comparison;
- widen online access.

But they can also exclude residents lacking connectivity, encode incorrect property or beneficiary data, centralise surveillance or create vendor dependence. Offline assistance, privacy, security, open standards, correction rights and elected oversight are essential.

4. Ward data should connect money, work and outcome.

**Question:** Why can a fully digitised municipal service still be democratically weak?

**Model answer:** Digitisation may make a workflow fast and traceable while leaving priorities, data correction, budget control or grievance escalation outside elected and citizen oversight. Technology improves administration only when authority, inclusion and accountability are also designed.

**Misconception to avoid:** A dashboard entry marked “closed” is not conclusive evidence that the resident's service problem was actually resolved.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically examine the role of missions, SPVs and digital platforms in municipal service delivery.

**Illustrative response:** Urban missions can overcome capacity and investment bottlenecks. AMRUT 2.0 supports water security, sewerage, water-body rejuvenation and reform; Smart Cities SPVs can concentrate procurement and project expertise; NUDM and UPYOG can standardise property-tax, water and grievance workflows across municipalities.

These instruments, however, are not substitutes for the 74th Amendment. A scheme does not transfer Article 243W authority, and an SPV can shift budget and implementation outside council scrutiny. Digital platforms may reproduce incorrect records, exclude offline residents, weaken privacy or create vendor dependence. A dashboard can measure transactions without proving water quality, waste clearance or grievance satisfaction.

The correct integration is constitutional rather than competitive. Projects should conform to municipal, DPC and MPC plans; councils should approve or scrutinise major commitments; assets and future operating liabilities should be disclosed; ward committees should verify outcomes; and digital systems should provide correction, appeal, offline support, security and open interoperability.

Missions and platforms are valuable capacity multipliers when they strengthen elected institutions. When they become parallel chains answerable mainly upward, they deliver projects while weakening urban self-government.

### 1. The main challenges

**Functional fragmentation:** land, water, transport and infrastructure may remain with State agencies or SPVs.

**Fiscal weakness:** narrow or poorly administered property tax, limited discretionary revenue, delayed SFC processes and tied grants constrain choice.

**Functionary gap:** municipal responsibility may not be matched by control over engineers, planners and frontline staff.

**Leadership ambiguity:** mayor, council, committees and commissioner may have overlapping or mismatched authority.

**Planning failure:** DPCs and MPCs may lack timely data, staff, investment control or consequence.

**Participation deficit:** Ward Committees may exist without budgets, records or inclusive attendance.

**Capacity inequality:** smaller bodies cannot easily maintain specialist cadres or market access.

**Parallelism:** missions can build assets while bypassing ordinary municipal accountability.

### 2. A sequenced reform programme

1. **Classify transparently:** use Article 243Q factors and phased rural–urban transition.
2. **Map activities:** identify exact tasks, assets and agency boundaries for all relevant Twelfth Schedule matters.
3. **Align staff:** establish accountable municipal cadres or binding service-control arrangements.
4. **Strengthen finance:** improve property coverage/valuation, equitable user charges, timely SFCs and predictable transfers.
5. **Borrow prudently:** use bonds or pooled finance for prepared capital projects with repayment capacity.
6. **Make planning consequential:** connect DPC/MPC plans to departmental and parastatal investments.
7. **Clarify executive power:** give elected leadership stable authority with professional administrative safeguards.
8. **Deepen ward accountability:** publish budgets, works, service standards and grievance closure.
9. **Integrate missions:** subordinate SPV projects to constitutional plans and council scrutiny.
10. **Close the audit loop:** accounts, audit, legislative/council review and corrective action.

### 3. PYQ synthesis

The direct 2023 demand asks whether States have empowered ULBs functionally and financially. The best thesis is graded: representation and continuity are constitutionally secured; function, finance, personnel and planning remain uneven.

The 2020 and 2022 cross-demands require moving from the three Fs to functionality and judging the extent of grassroots change. The 2024 merger demand requires criteria rather than advocacy. The 2024 objective question tests exact constitutional Parts. The 2026 women's-reservation question begins with local bodies before moving to legislative reservation.

### 4. A balanced verdict

The amendment did not fail: it made elected municipal institutions, reservation, tenure, SEC elections, SFC review and planning committees part of the constitutional order. Nor did it complete urban decentralisation: enabling clauses and State control permit agencies, staff and finance to remain elsewhere.

14. The final verdict is constitutional success with incomplete operational self-government.

### Concept check

**Question:** What single test best reveals whether a city is self-governed rather than merely governed?

**Model answer:** Trace one service from legal function to budget, staff, decision, execution, disclosure and citizen remedy. If these powers are dispersed among agencies not answerable to the elected council, municipal self-government is incomplete even when elections occur regularly.

**Misconception to avoid:** The 74th Amendment should be judged neither as total failure nor as completed devolution; its institutional gains and operational gaps must be assessed separately.

### Lesson Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** “India's cities are governed, but not fully self-governed.” Critically examine and suggest reforms.

**Illustrative response:** The 74th Amendment made urban democracy constitutionally durable. Part IX-A provides municipalities, direct ward elections, reservation, five-year tenure, SEC elections, SFC review and DPC/MPC planning. These are real gains: city government can no longer be treated only as revocable administrative convenience.

Yet Article 243W and Article 243X are enabling. States may retain spatial planning, water, transport, staff and buoyant revenue through departments, development authorities, boards and SPVs. Weak property-tax administration, delayed SFCs and tied grants limit fiscal choice. The mayor may carry public responsibility while a State-appointed commissioner or parastatal controls execution. Ward Committees, DPCs and MPCs may exist without consequential budgets or planning authority.

Reform should begin with transparent Article 243Q classification and complete activity mapping of Twelfth Schedule functions. Funds and staff must follow responsibility. Property tax needs wider coverage, fair valuation and appeals; equalising transfers remain necessary; bonds should finance only prepared assets. DPC/MPC plans should bind or at least require reasoned conformity from parastatals. Stable elected leadership, professional cadres, inclusive Ward Committees, public accounts and grievance closure should complete the chain.

India has constitutional municipalities but uneven municipal government. The objective is not agency abolition or municipal isolation; it is integrated expertise under a visible elected accountability structure.

**Quantified rubric (20):** constitutional gains 4; functional/fiscal/personnel diagnosis 7; balanced agency analysis 2; sequenced reforms 5; verdict 2.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Block A — Lessons 1–4: foundation and democratic structure

**Cumulative question:** What did constitutionalisation add beyond ordinary State municipal laws?

**Concise model answer:** It entrenched a national floor of municipal categories, direct territorial election, reservation, tenure, electoral machinery, fiscal review and planning institutions, while leaving substantial operational detail to State law.

**Cumulative question:** Distinguish a census town, industrial township and Nagar Panchayat.

**Concise model answer:** The first is statistical, the second a notified exception where no municipality need be constituted, and the third an elected Article 243Q municipality for a transitional area.

## Block B — Lessons 5–9: inclusion, functions, finance and accountability

**Cumulative question:** Why must Articles 243W, 243X, 243Y and 243Z be read together?

**Concise model answer:** Functions require authorised revenue and transfers; fiscal arrangements require periodic review; and all expenditure requires accounts, audit and correction. Separating them creates unfunded or unaccountable mandates.

**Cumulative question:** What is the constitutional logic connecting SEC independence and Article 243ZG?

**Concise model answer:** The SEC protects autonomous and timely administration of elections, while Article 243ZG prevents ordinary litigation from derailing the process and channels election disputes into the prescribed petition route.

## Block C — Lessons 10–12: scope and planning

**Cumulative question:** Compare DPC and MPC in one precise statement.

**Concise model answer:** DPC integrates Panchayat and municipal plans across a district with at least four-fifths elected members in the rural:urban ratio; MPC plans a Governor-specified metropolitan area with at least two-thirds elected members in the municipality:Panchayat population ratio.

**Cumulative question:** Why is exclusion from Part IX-A not the same as absence of local service needs?

**Concise model answer:** Constitutional exclusion protects special territorial governance, but residents still require water, sanitation and planning. The institutional solution must fit and preserve protected structures rather than assume no urban governance problem exists.

## Block D — Lessons 13–16: operations, services and reform

**Cumulative question:** How should expertise and democratic control be reconciled in urban government?

**Concise model answer:** Professional commissioners, cadres and parastatals may supply continuity and scale, but their plans, budgets and performance must fit constitutional planning and remain visible to elected councils and residents.

**Cumulative question:** Give a minimum reform sequence for municipal self-government.

**Concise model answer:** Classify transparently, map activities, align staff, authorise and administer revenue, strengthen SFC transfers, make DPC/MPC plans consequential, clarify executive power, empower Ward Committees and close the accounts-audit-grievance loop.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Solved 10-marker

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish constitutional recognition from functional empowerment of municipalities.

**Model solution:** Constitutional recognition refers to Part IX-A's protected institutional floor: Article 243Q municipalities, directly elected wards, reservation, five-year tenure, SEC elections, SFC review and DPC/MPC planning. It prevents urban local bodies from depending entirely on ordinary executive discretion.

Functional empowerment asks a different question: who controls the task, staff, money, assets and decision? Article 243W allows States to endow Twelfth Schedule functions; it does not transfer them automatically. Development authorities may retain spatial planning, boards may control water, and State cadres may control personnel. Article 243X also requires State-law fiscal authorisation.

Thus a city can possess elected constitutional form while lacking operational command. Genuine empowerment requires activity mapping, accountable functionaries, predictable finance, consequential planning and audit-linked citizen remedy. The 74th Amendment secured the platform; State-level devolution determines how much government stands on it.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both terms, uses exact Articles, explains the causal gap and gives a qualified conclusion.

**Rubric (10):** recognition 3; empowerment 3; mechanisms/evidence 3; verdict 1.

## Solved 15-marker

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the constitutional and fiscal case for strengthening property tax in Indian municipalities.

**Model solution:** Article 243X permits State legislatures to authorise municipal taxes and fees, while Article 243Y provides SFC review. Within this architecture, property tax is attractive because immovable urban property is locally visible and its value is linked partly to roads, water, planning and amenities supplied by public institutions. A stronger tax can provide discretionary revenue and tighten the resident–service accountability loop.

The problem is not simply low rates. Incomplete property coverage, outdated valuation, exemptions, weak billing, disputed records and poor collection reduce yield and legitimacy. GIS can discover parcels, but lawful assessment still needs correct ownership/use data, notice, appeal and privacy safeguards. A tax perceived as arbitrary will invite evasion rather than accountability.

Reform should widen the base before raising burdens; use transparent valuation and periodic updating; publish exemptions; integrate building permissions and service records; provide simple correction and appeal; and disclose ward-wise use of revenue. Vulnerable households may require calibrated relief rather than blanket exemption.

Property tax cannot finance every city equally. Poorer municipalities need SFC and Union Finance Commission equalisation, while user charges and grants serve different purposes. Strengthening property tax is therefore fiscally and democratically desirable, but only within a fair, service-linked and redistributive municipal-finance system.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects constitutional authority, instrument design, implementation defects, due process, equity and a bounded verdict.

**Rubric (15):** constitutional basis 3; fiscal case 3; implementation diagnosis 4; reforms/equity 3; conclusion 2.

## Solved 20-marker

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Critically assess whether DPCs and MPCs can overcome fragmented urban and regional planning.

**Model solution:** Articles 243ZD and 243ZE create representative planning bridges. The DPC consolidates rural and urban plans for the district with at least four-fifths elected membership in the rural:urban population ratio. The MPC prepares a metropolitan draft plan with at least two-thirds elected membership in the municipality:Panchayat ratio. Both must consider spatial planning, shared resources, infrastructure and environmental conservation; the MPC additionally integrates Union–State priorities and expected investment.

Their logic directly addresses fragmentation. Water, transport, waste, housing and land markets cross ward and municipal boundaries. Elected integration can connect local plans to a larger resource envelope.

Yet constitutional committees may remain weak if development authorities, departments and SPVs decide land and capital investment separately. Compilation after budgets are fixed is not planning. Committees may also lack data, professional staff, public participation or power to require reasons when their draft is altered.

Reform should synchronise local and departmental planning calendars, provide independent secretariats, mandate disclosure of parastatal investments, use common geospatial and fiscal data, and require a reasoned State response. DPC/MPC plans should guide statutory spatial and infrastructure decisions while preserving municipal responsibility for local services.

These committees can overcome fragmentation only if representative coordination becomes consequential. Their constitutional design is adequate as a bridge; the missing elements are authority, timing, capacity and budget alignment.

**Why this earns marks:** It states exact fractions and functions, explains the causal promise, identifies institutional failure and gives an implementable reform architecture.

**Rubric (20):** constitutional precision 5; fragmentation logic 4; critical limits 5; reforms 4; verdict 2.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional identity and evolution

- Urban local government is governance of a demarcated urban area through elected representatives.
- Local government belongs to Entry 5, State List.
- Historical milestones: Madras 1688; Bombay/Calcutta 1726; Mayo 1870; Ripon 1882; 1919 transferred subject; 1935 provincial subject.
- 65th Amendment/Nagarpalika Bill failed in 1989; 1990 bill lapsed; 1991 bill became the 74th Amendment.
- Part IX-A commenced on **1 June 1993**.
- Part IX-A = **Articles 243P–243ZG**; Twelfth Schedule = **18 matters**.

## Article register

- **243P:** definitions; metropolitan area = ten lakh or more; population = last preceding published census.
- **243Q:** Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council, Municipal Corporation; industrial-township exception; notification factors.
- **243R:** territorial seats directly elected; experts may be represented without vote; legislators/committee chairpersons as State law provides; chairperson route by State law.
- **243S:** Ward Committees in municipalities with population three lakh or more; constituent-ward councillors are members; a one-ward committee is chaired by that councillor, while a multi-ward committee elects one councillor-member as chair; additional committees allowed.
- **243T:** population-linked SC/ST seats; one-third SC/ST reserved seats for SC/ST women; one-third total directly elected seats for women; State law determines the manner of SC/ST/women chairperson reservation, with no constitutional proportional or one-third chairperson-office formula; clause (5) links SC/ST reservation to Article 334 while expressly preserving women's reservation; backward-class reservation enabling.
- **243U:** five years; hearing before dissolution; election before expiry or within six months; short-remainder exception; reconstituted body serves remainder.
- **243V:** State-election-law and State-municipal-law disqualifications; twenty-one-year protection.
- **243W:** State-law endowment for municipal self-government, plans and schemes, plus authority for Article 243S Committees carrying conferred responsibilities; Twelfth Schedule not automatic.
- **243X:** authorised municipal levies; assigned State revenue; grants; municipal funds.
- **243Y:** the Article 243I SFC constituted every fifth year reviews municipal finance; report and action memorandum before legislature.
- **243Z:** State-law accounts and audit.
- **243ZA:** SEC controls rolls and municipal elections.
- **243ZB:** UT application; Governor means Article 239 Administrator, and State-legislature references mean the Legislative Assembly for a UT having one; Presidential adaptations permitted.
- **243ZC:** Scheduled/tribal exclusions; Darjeeling protection; parliamentary extension route.
- **243ZD:** DPC; at least four-fifths elected; rural:urban ratio; integrated district draft plan.
- **243ZE:** MPC; at least two-thirds elected; municipality:Panchayat ratio; metropolitan draft plan.
- **243ZF:** inconsistent laws at most one year; existing municipalities generally continue to term.
- **243ZG:** delimitation/seat-allotment bar; election-petition route.

- Property tax: coverage → valuation → rate/exemption → bill → collect → appeal → disclose service use.

- SEC, not ECI, runs municipal elections.
- Article 243ZG channels, rather than abolishes, electoral adjudication.

## Operations and service-delivery diagnosis

- Council = deliberation, policy, budget and oversight.
- Mayor/chairperson = political leadership; actual power and tenure vary by State.
- Commissioner/chief officer = administration and implementation under State law.
- Standing committees = specialised scrutiny; require public minutes and reporting.
- Personnel may be separate, unified or integrated.
- Three Fs are inputs; functionality additionally needs standards, maintenance, capacity, procurement, records, ward verification and correction.
- Smart Cities SPV, AMRUT 2.0 and NUDM are implementation/capacity tools, not constitutional substitutes.
- Digital record ≠ verified outcome.
