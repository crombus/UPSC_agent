# Central and State Information Commissions (CIC and SIC) — Live Session Edition

You file an RTI application asking a ministry why a road contract went to a particular firm. Thirty days pass. Silence. You appeal to a senior officer in the same department — unsurprisingly, nothing changes. Where do you go next, and why does the answer matter more than the one file you wanted?

That single question opens the entire topic. The Right to Information Act, 2005 did not just create a right to ask; it created a chain of officers, clocks, appeals and an independent adjudicator at the end of the chain — the Information Commission — whose own independence, powers and limits decide whether the right to know is real or merely printed in a statute. This session builds that chain lesson by lesson: from the constitutional root of the right, through the request-and-appeal machinery, into the institutional design of the CIC and SIC, their adjudicatory powers and limits, the exemptions that bound disclosure, the live privacy-versus-transparency fight now before the Supreme Court, and the accountability verdict the UPSC has already tested once and will test again.

### Why this question comes first

Before you memorise a single section number, settle one classification question that silently decides how every other answer must be framed: **is the Central Information Commission a constitutional body, like the Election Commission, or something else?** Get this wrong, and an otherwise well-informed answer loses its anchor paragraph.

```text
Is CIC/SIC mentioned in the Constitution?           NO
Is CIC/SIC created by an Act of Parliament?         YES — RTI Act, 2005
Does a constitutional provision explain WHY
the right it enforces exists?                       YES — Article 19(1)(a)
```

**The resolution:** CIC and SIC are **statutory, quasi-judicial bodies** — they exist because Parliament created them in 2005, not because the Constitution names them. But the *right* they adjudicate is older and deeper than the statute: courts had already read a **"right to know"** into Article 19(1)(a)'s freedom of speech and expression, years before the Act was ever drafted.

### The doctrine, step by step

1. **Article 19(1)(a)** guarantees freedom of speech and expression. A citizen who cannot find out what the government is doing cannot meaningfully speak about it, criticise it, or vote on it. Courts therefore read an implicit **right to know** into this provision.
2. ***State of U.P. v. Raj Narain* (1975)** is the foundational case. The Supreme Court held that the people have a right to know every public act of their public functionaries, because India runs a "government of responsibility" — officials must answer to a public that is entitled to be informed, subject only to legitimate, narrowly construed secrecy claims (for instance, certain matters of State security).
3. ***S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981)** — the Judges' Transfer case — went further, treating **open government** as the democratic norm and secrecy as the exception needing justification, again tracing the logic back to Article 19(1)(a).
4. **The RTI Act, 2005 codifies — it does not create — this right.** That distinction is not academic: the Act gives the right teeth (sections, timelines, officers, a Commission, penalties), but the right would remain recognisable, in attenuated form, through Article 19(1)(a) even if the Act were repealed tomorrow. Conversely, the Act's procedure, exemptions and forums bind the Commission; the Commission cannot override the statute merely by invoking the Constitution.

```text
CONSTITUTION (Article 19(1)(a))
   supplies: the normative "why" — a right to know
          |
CASE LAW (Raj Narain, S.P. Gupta)
   supplies: judicial recognition, before any statute existed
          |
RTI ACT, 2005
   supplies: the practical "how" — definitions, PIOs, timelines,
             appeals, exemptions, Commission, penalties
```

### Example: watch the distinction do real work

Suppose a question asks: *"Can Parliament repeal the RTI Act tomorrow without violating the Constitution?"* A learner who thinks the right is purely constitutional will answer "no" flatly. The correct, qualified answer: Parliament *can* repeal the statute (it is ordinary legislation, not a constitutional entrenchment), but the core right to know under Article 19(1)(a) would survive in a weaker, non-codified form, and total repeal without any replacement mechanism could itself invite a constitutional challenge for failing to give effect to a recognised fundamental-rights dimension. The example shows exactly where the "constitutional root, statutory machinery" distinction earns marks: it lets you hold two true things — the Act is ordinary law, and the right has constitutional depth — without contradiction.

### Comparison: what CIC/SIC are not

| Institution | Source | Character | Typical output |
|---|---|---|---|
| CIC / SIC | RTI Act, 2005 | statutory, quasi-judicial appellate and complaint body | binding appellate decision, compensation, personal penalty |
| Supreme Court / High Court | Constitution | constitutional court | binding judgment or writ |
| Election Commission | Article 324 | constitutional body | binding orders on elections |
| NHRC / SHRC | Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 | statutory, recommendatory | recommendations, reports (ordinarily non-binding) |

*Why the comparison matters:* UPSC regularly tests whether a candidate can correctly classify a body. CIC/SIC is the clearest possible trap for "statutory bodies that feel important enough to be constitutional" — resist that instinct every time.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If the right is constitutional, why should the Commission's jurisdiction be limited by an ordinary statute at all — shouldn't constitutional rights always prevail?"

**Reply:** A constitutional right explains *why* access to information deserves protection; it does not by itself create officers, timelines, or an adjudicatory body. Parliament had to design the actual machinery, and in doing so it also had to draw the machinery's boundaries — definitions of what counts as information, who counts as a public authority, and which categories are exempt. The Commission's authority comes entirely from the Act that created it; it cannot expand its own jurisdiction by appeal to the Constitution, though a litigant can still approach a constitutional court separately if a statutory limit itself appears unconstitutional. The qualified residual: the constitutional right is the reason the statute cannot be gutted into meaninglessness, not a licence for the Commission to act beyond what the statute permits.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, "Statutory, regulatory and various quasi-judicial bodies."
- **Common trap:** calling CIC/SIC "constitutional" because they "protect a fundamental right." The right is constitutional; the body is statutory.
- **Answer-use:** open any CIC/SIC Mains answer needing a constitutional anchor with the two-line chain — Article 19(1)(a) → *Raj Narain*/*S.P. Gupta* → RTI Act, 2005 codifies — before moving to the Act's specific machinery.
- **Probable framing:** "Trace the constitutional basis of the right to information" or an opening sentence inside a larger autonomy/effectiveness question.

### Revision notes

1. CIC/SIC are **statutory**, created by the RTI Act, 2005 — never call them constitutional bodies.
2. The right to information's constitutional root is **Article 19(1)(a)** — freedom of speech and expression.
3. ***State of U.P. v. Raj Narain* (1975)** — foundational "right to know" case; open government flows from accountability to the public.
4. ***S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981)** — open government as the democratic default; secrecy as the justified exception.
5. The Act **codifies, not creates**, the right — repeal would weaken, not necessarily extinguish, the underlying constitutional claim.
6. The Commission's own jurisdiction is bounded by the statute, not expanded by invoking the Constitution directly.
7. Contrast with the Election Commission (constitutional, Article 324) and NHRC/SHRC (statutory, recommendatory) to avoid classification errors.
8. Exam framing almost always opens a long-answer question — use this chain as a reliable opening paragraph, not as a standalone answer.
9. "Codifies, not creates" is the single most reusable phrase for this entire topic's constitutional dimension.
10. Mnemonic: **R-S-G** — **R**aj Narain starts it, **S.P. Gupta** strengthens it, **G**azette notification of the RTI Act operationalises it.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "The RTI Act, 2005 does not create the right to information; it codifies a right the courts had already read into Article 19(1)(a), and in doing so builds the first statutory machinery capable of enforcing it."

### Concept check

**Question:** A student writes: "CIC is a constitutional body because it protects a fundamental right." Is this correct? Why or why not?

**Model answer:** No. Protecting a right does not make a body constitutional — only its *source* does. CIC is created by the RTI Act, 2005 (an ordinary statute), so it is a statutory, quasi-judicial body. The right it protects has a constitutional root in Article 19(1)(a), recognised in *Raj Narain* and *S.P. Gupta*, but the body itself exists only because Parliament legislated it into existence in 2005.

**Misconception to avoid:** Conflating the source of a *right* with the source of the *institution* that enforces it. Many statutory bodies enforce constitutionally grounded rights (for example, consumer commissions and Article 21-linked claims) without themselves becoming constitutional bodies.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "The Right to Information Act, 2005 codifies a right the Constitution already implied." Examine this statement with reference to the constitutional basis of the right to information.

**Model solution:** The right to information is not textually present in the Constitution but was judicially read into Article 19(1)(a)'s freedom of speech and expression. In *State of U.P. v. Raj Narain* (1975), the Supreme Court held that a democratic electorate must know the acts of its public functionaries, since responsible government presupposes an informed citizenry; secrecy survives only as a narrow, justified exception. *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981) reinforced open government as the constitutional default. The RTI Act, 2005 therefore did not invent this right; it converted a judicially recognised but procedurally toothless right into an enforceable system — defining information, designating officers, fixing timelines, and creating the CIC/SIC as adjudicators. The Act's constitutional pedigree also explains why its core access guarantee, though statutory, cannot be casually diluted without raising a live constitutional question.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Constitutional root correctly identified (Article 19(1)(a)) — 2; *Raj Narain* holding stated accurately — 3; *S.P. Gupta* linked to open-government norm — 2; "codifies, not creates" distinction explicitly drawn — 2; coherent 150-word structure with no case-law fabrication — 1.

**Why the next lesson follows:** Once you know *why* the right exists and *what kind* of body enforces it, the next question is mechanical and definitional: what exactly does this right let you ask for, and from whom? That is where the RTI Act's core vocabulary — information, record, public authority — takes over.

### The learner's problem

You want to RTI a cooperative bank that gets a government subsidy, and separately you want to RTI your own school-board result sheet. Both requests sound alike — "give me a document a government-linked body holds" — but one almost certainly succeeds and the other is far more contested. Why? Because two different gates control access: first, *is the body even covered* (the public-authority test), and second, *is the material even "information" in the statutory sense* (the existing-record test). Lesson 1 fixed the constitutional frame; this lesson fixes the vocabulary that every later lesson assumes you already know.

### Four definitions, precisely

| Term | Statutory meaning | Why it is tested |
|---|---|---|
| **Information** (s. 2(f)) | Material in any form — records, documents, memos, e-mails, opinions, advice, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models, and electronic data; also information about a private body that a public authority can legally access under any other law | Defines the outer boundary of what can ever be sought |
| **Record** (s. 2(i)) | Any document, manuscript or file; microfilm/microfiche/facsimile copy; reproduction of an image from a microfilm; and material produced by a computer or any other device | Clarifies that "record" is a *format* category inside the broader "information" |
| **Right to information** (s. 2(j)) | Access to information held by or under the control of a public authority — inspection of work/documents/records, taking notes/extracts/certified copies, taking certified samples, and obtaining information in electronic form (printouts, diskettes, and the like) | Tells you *what remedy* the right actually delivers, not merely that a right exists |
| **Public authority** (s. 2(h)) | Any authority or body established by/under the Constitution, by Parliament, by a State legislature, or by notification/order of the appropriate government — **plus** any body owned, controlled or **substantially financed**, directly or indirectly, by government funds, **plus** a non-government organisation substantially financed, directly or indirectly, by the appropriate government | The single most litigated definition in the Act |

### Visual — the definition funnel

```text
MATERIAL IN ANY FORM  (section 2(f) "information")
           |
HELD BY OR UNDER THE CONTROL OF A PUBLIC AUTHORITY
           |

           |

           =

```

### The existing-record versus new-creation trap

This is the single most common procedural misunderstanding candidates carry into the exam: **RTI is a records-access right, not an interrogation right.** A public authority must give you a document it already holds (or can legally access); it does not have to create a fresh explanation, write a new opinion, answer a hypothetical "why", or collate data it has never compiled.

***CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay* (2011)** is the controlling case. The Supreme Court held that **evaluated answer books are "information"** accessible under the Act — a student may inspect or obtain a copy of her own evaluated script, subject to any applicable exemption and procedure. But the judgment simultaneously drew the boundary that controls almost every other "can I RTI this?" question: a public authority is **not obliged to create information that does not exist**, to interpret or explain a decision beyond its recorded reasons, to furnish an opinion/advice it was never asked to record, or to collect and collate data it does not already hold in that form.

```text
RTI MAY REQUIRE                       |  RTI DOES NOT ORDINARILY REQUIRE
---------------------------------------|---------------------------------------
existing file noting                  |  a newly written justification
recorded answer script / marks        |  a fresh expert re-evaluation opinion
held database extract (as it exists)  |  creation of a database that doesn't exist
inspection / certified copy           |  answering a hypothetical "why" question
```

### The public-authority test: beyond mere registration

Section 2(h) is deliberately broad on its face — "owned, controlled or substantially financed" — but broad words invite a harder question: *how much* control or finance is "substantial"? This is exactly the gap the canonical teaching notes for this topic leave thin, so it is taught here in full.

***Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank Ltd. v. State of Kerala* (2013) 16 SCC 82, decided 7 October 2013.** Cooperative societies registered under the Kerala Cooperative Societies Act argued they were private bodies outside RTI's reach; the Kerala State Information Commission had treated them as public authorities merely because they were registered and regulated under a State law. The Supreme Court disagreed with that shortcut and laid down the controlling test:

1. **Mere registration or statutory regulation is not "control."** A Registrar of Cooperative Societies supervising compliance, auditing accounts, or holding a general power of inspection is *regulatory oversight*, not the "control" section 2(h) demands.
2. **"Control" must be deep, pervasive and direct** — government must be able to direct the body's actual management and affairs, not merely monitor compliance with a licensing law.
3. **"Substantially financed" needs a genuinely significant, recurring financial relationship** — not an incidental subsidy, a one-off grant, or ordinary tax concessions available to similar bodies generally.
4. Applying this, most ordinary cooperative societies — autonomous in management, financed mainly by their own members, merely *regulated* (not *controlled*) by the Registrar — fall **outside** section 2(h) and are not public authorities merely for being registered under a cooperative law.

```text
                 IS THE BODY A "PUBLIC AUTHORITY" UNDER s.2(h)?
                                   |
          +------------------------+------------------------+
          |                                                 |
   REGULATORY OVERSIGHT ONLY                  DEEP, PERVASIVE, MANAGERIAL CONTROL
   (licensing, audit, inspection)              (government effectively runs the body)
          |                                                 |
      NOT "control" --- Thalappalam                   "control" satisfied
                                   |
                    "SUBSTANTIALLY FINANCED"?
                    recurring + significant, not incidental
                                   |
                         test applied case-by-case
```

**Why *Thalappalam* belongs here, not as a footnote:** without this case, a learner is left believing that section 2(h) is satisfied by almost any government connection — a dangerously over-inclusive reading that UPSC traps exploit directly ("a cooperative bank regulated by the RBI/Registrar is automatically a public authority" is a classic false statement). *Thalappalam* supplies the precise discriminating test: regulation is not control, and finance must be substantial, not incidental.

### Comparison: three near-neighbour fact patterns

| Fact pattern | Public authority? | Controlling reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| A ministry department | Yes | Directly established under/by the Constitution or law |
| An autonomous cooperative society, lightly regulated by a Registrar, self-financed by members | No (ordinarily) | *Thalappalam* — regulation is not control; member finance is not government finance |
| An NGO run almost entirely on a recurring government grant covering most of its budget | Yes (ordinarily) | Falls within the "substantially financed NGO" limb of section 2(h) |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If regulation alone is not control, doesn't that let heavily government-dependent bodies escape RTI simply by avoiding the word 'control' in their founding documents?"

**Reply:** *Thalappalam* does not protect such bodies — it protects bodies whose government relationship is genuinely regulatory and whose finance is genuinely their own. Where finance is in fact recurring and substantial, or where government can and does direct day-to-day management (appointing office-bearers, approving budgets in a binding rather than advisory way, or similar deep involvement), the test is satisfied regardless of the label used. The qualified residual: *Thalappalam* prevents *over-inclusion* (treating every regulated private body as public), but it does not create an escape hatch for bodies that are, in substance, government-run or government-funded.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, RTI machinery and the scope of statutory rights.
- **Common trap:** "Any NGO that gets a government grant is a public authority" — wrong; the grant must be *substantial*, and the *CBSE*/`Thalappalam` pairing tests both the information-scope boundary and the authority-scope boundary respectively.
- **Answer-use:** when a question gives a fact pattern about a cooperative, trust, or NGO, run the two-step *Thalappalam* test (control depth, then finance substantiality) before concluding.
- **Probable framing:** "Is the office of X a public authority under the RTI Act?" style application questions, or a direct "discuss the scope of 'public authority'" demand.

### Revision notes

1. **Information** (s. 2(f)): material in any form, broadly defined, including legally accessible private-body information.
2. **Record** (s. 2(i)): a format sub-category — files, documents, microforms, computer-produced material.
3. **Right to information** (s. 2(j)): inspection, notes/extracts/certified copies, certified samples, electronic access — a *remedy*, not an abstract entitlement.
4. **Public authority** (s. 2(h)): constitutional/statutory/notified bodies, **plus** owned/controlled/substantially-financed bodies, **plus** substantially-financed NGOs.
5. *CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay* (2011): evaluated answer scripts are "information"; but no duty to create new information, opinion, or explanation.
6. *Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank v. State of Kerala* (2013) 16 SCC 82: regulation ≠ control; finance must be substantial, recurring and significant, not incidental.
7. The two cases solve two *different* gates: *CBSE* gates the **material** ("is this information at all?"); *Thalappalam* gates the **body** ("is this even a public authority?").
8. Mnemonic: **"Regulate ≠ Run."** A Registrar who merely regulates does not "run" (control) the body.
9. Do not assume State-wise uniformity — SICs apply the same statutory test, but factual findings on control/finance vary by entity.
10. Exam-safe phrase: "substantially financed" requires a significant and recurring — not incidental — financial relationship.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "RTI tests two different gates before any document is handed over: is the *body* a public authority (*Thalappalam*'s control/finance test), and is the *material* existing information rather than a demand for a new explanation (*CBSE*'s records-not-interrogation rule)."

### Concept check

**Question:** A Registrar of Cooperative Societies regularly audits a cooperative bank's accounts and can inspect its records at any time. Does this alone make the bank a "public authority" under section 2(h)?

**Model answer:** No. Under *Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank v. State of Kerala* (2013), routine regulatory supervision — audit and inspection powers under a Registrar's statutory oversight — is not "control" within the meaning of section 2(h). Control must be deep, pervasive and managerial; mere regulatory compliance monitoring does not meet that threshold. Unless the bank is also substantially financed by government or government effectively directs its management, it remains outside section 2(h).

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating any statutory regulatory relationship (licensing, audit, inspection) as equivalent to "control." *Thalappalam* explicitly separates supervisory regulation from the managerial control the Act actually requires.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** With reference to the Right to Information Act, 2005, examine the tests laid down by courts for determining (a) when a body is a "public authority," and (b) what counts as "information." Use relevant case law.

**Model solution:** Section 2(h) defines "public authority" expansively — bodies established by the Constitution or law, plus bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by government, plus substantially financed NGOs. In *Thalappalam Service Cooperative Bank v. State of Kerala* (2013), the Supreme Court refused to read this expansively in every case: cooperative societies regulated by a Registrar under a cooperative societies Act were not automatically public authorities merely because of statutory registration and oversight. The Court held that "control" must be deep, pervasive and direct — capable of governing day-to-day management — and that mere regulatory supervision, audit or inspection powers do not satisfy this test. Similarly, "substantially financed" requires a significant, recurring financial relationship, not an incidental grant or ordinary concession. On the separate question of what counts as "information," *CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay* (2011) held that evaluated answer scripts are information accessible under the Act, since they are existing records held by the authority. However, the Court clarified that a public authority is not obliged to create new information, offer a fresh opinion, or answer interrogatory "why" questions beyond its recorded reasons. Together, the two cases discipline RTI's scope on both axes: *Thalappalam* prevents an over-expansive reading of "public authority," while *Aditya Bandopadhyay* prevents RTI from becoming a general interrogation tool. A correct answer must apply both tests before concluding that a request is maintainable.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Section 2(h) definition stated accurately — 2; *Thalappalam* holding and control/finance test explained — 5; *CBSE* holding and records-not-interrogation principle explained — 5; explicit distinction between the two tests' different functions — 2; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

**Why the next lesson follows:** Knowing what counts as information and who counts as a public authority tells you *what* can be asked and *of whom*. The next question is temporal and structural: must a public authority wait to be asked at all, or does the Act itself demand transparency by default? That is section 4's proactive-disclosure mandate.

### The learner's problem

If every citizen had to file a request merely to learn a ministry's budget, its organisational chart, or the criteria for a subsidy, the RTI system would drown in entirely avoidable paperwork. Section 4 exists to prevent exactly that: it asks public authorities to publish the *predictable* information before anyone has to ask.

### The doctrine

**Section 4(1)** requires every public authority to: maintain its records in a manner and form that facilitates the right to information; catalogue and index records within a reasonable time; computerise records where feasible, for wide dissemination; and **(1)(b)** publish, within 120 days of enactment (an obligation that continues as a standing duty), a long list of particulars — organisation, functions and duties; powers and duties of officers; the decision-making procedure including channels of supervision and accountability; norms for discharging functions; rules, regulations, instructions and manuals used by employees; categories of documents held; arrangements for public consultation; boards, councils and committees constituted; a directory of officers and employees; monthly remuneration; budget allocated to each agency; manner of execution of subsidy programmes; particulars of concessions/permits/authorisations; details of information available in electronic form; facilities available for citizens to obtain information; and the names, designations and contact particulars of PIOs.

**Section 4(1)(c)** adds a sharper duty: publish all relevant facts while formulating important policies or announcing decisions that affect the public. **Section 4(1)(d)** requires providing reasons for administrative or quasi-judicial decisions to affected persons — a reason-giving duty that exists independently of any RTI request.

**Section 4(2)** states the guiding philosophy directly: every public authority must, as a **continuing endeavour**, take steps to provide the **maximum suo motu** disclosure to the public at regular intervals through various means, so that the public has **minimum resort** to filing individual requests.

```text
SECTION 4 DISCLOSURE  ------------------------------+
 (records, budgets, reasons, beneficiaries)          |
                                                      v
                                   FEWER AVOIDABLE INDIVIDUAL REQUESTS
                                                      |
             REQUEST  ->  PIO  ->  APPEALS  ->  COMMISSION  -+
```

### Clusters, so the long list becomes usable

| Cluster | Typical content | Accountability payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | organisation, functions, duties | locate the responsible unit without a request |
| Authority | powers, procedures, norms | test legality and delay against a published standard |
| Rules and records | manuals, categories of documents, past decisions | compare actual conduct with the stated standard |
| Money | budgets, subsidy execution, concessions | trace allocation and beneficiary logic |
| People | staff directory, remuneration, named PIOs | identify the officer actually responsible |
| Participation | consultation arrangements, boards/committees | assess how much public voice is designed in |

### Example and its limit

A ministry uploads its annual budget as a scanned PDF of a printed ledger, in English only, with no search function. Formally, section 4 is satisfied — the figures are "published." Substantively, almost nothing is gained: a citizen in a non-English-speaking area, or one without specialist software, cannot actually use it. **The limit of the example:** publication alone is not transparency; accessibility, searchability, timeliness, local-language availability and genuine discoverability determine whether disclosure does real accountability work or merely satisfies a checkbox.

```text
PUBLISHED?  ->  UPDATED?  ->  SEARCHABLE?  ->  ACCESSIBLE + LOCAL-LANGUAGE?
                                                        |
                                    LINKED TO AN ACTUAL DECISION / BUDGET / OFFICER?
                                                        |
                                             USABLE TRANSPARENCY
```

### Comparison: prevention versus remedy

| Layer | Trigger | What it buys the citizen |
|---|---|---|
| Section 4 (prevention) | nothing — it is a standing duty | information without having to ask, appeal, or wait |
| Request/appeal (remedy) | an individual's unmet need | a specific, enforceable answer to one question |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "Section 4 looks like a vague aspirational clause — 'maximum suo motu disclosure' and 'continuing endeavour' are not enforceable standards the way a 30-day clock is."

**Reply:** The objection is partly right and identifies a genuine design weakness: section 4 itself carries no direct penalty for non-compliance comparable to section 20's personal fine on a PIO for request-level defaults. But the weakness is one of *enforcement intensity*, not *doctrinal emptiness*. A citizen can still file a section 18 complaint about systemic non-disclosure, the Commission can direct section 4 compliance under its section 19(8) remedial powers in an appeal, and the annual reporting duty under section 25 is meant to surface implementation gaps to the legislature. The qualified residual: section 4 is real law with real remedial hooks, but its soft, standard-based phrasing does make it the weakest-enforced part of the Act relative to the hard clocks and penalties governing individual requests.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, transparency and accountability mechanisms.
- **Common trap:** treating section 4 as optional "good practice" rather than a statutory duty with its own clauses and a reasoned-decision requirement under 4(1)(d).
- **Answer-use:** any "RTI effectiveness" or "reduce RTI backlog" answer should propose stronger section 4 compliance as a structural reform, not merely faster request processing.
- **Probable framing:** "Proactive disclosure is more important to RTI's success than the volume of individual applications" — discuss/analyse-type questions.

### Revision notes

1. Section 4(1)(a): maintain, catalogue, index and (where feasible) computerise records.
2. Section 4(1)(b): publish a detailed list — organisation, powers, procedure, norms, manuals, budgets, subsidies, concessions, staff directory, remuneration, PIOs, and more.
3. Section 4(1)(c): publish relevant facts when formulating important policies or announcing decisions affecting the public.
4. Section 4(1)(d): give reasons for administrative/quasi-judicial decisions to affected persons.
5. Section 4(2): maximum suo motu disclosure as a continuing endeavour, to minimise resort to individual requests.
6. Section 4 is the **prevention layer**; requests and appeals are the **remedy layer**.
7. Formal publication (an unreadable scan) is not the same as usable transparency (searchable, updated, accessible, local-language).
8. Section 4 carries no section-20-style personal penalty; enforcement relies on complaints, Commission directions under section 19(8), and section 25 reporting.
9. Best Mains line: "Section 4 converts transparency from individual litigation into an administrative default."
10. Mnemonic: **"Index, Inform, Involve"** — index records, inform the public proactively, involve the public in consultation disclosures.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "Section 4 is designed so that most citizens never need to file a request at all — it fails not when a request is filed, but when a request becomes necessary for something that should already have been public."

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is section 4(2)'s "minimum resort to the use of this Act to obtain information" considered the real test of section 4's success, rather than the length of the published list in section 4(1)(b)?

**Model answer:** A long published list under section 4(1)(b) can still fail citizens if it is inaccessible, outdated, or unsearchable — the list is a formal requirement, not a guarantee of use. Section 4(2)'s own stated standard is outcome-based: disclosure succeeds only if it actually reduces the number of individual requests citizens must file. That makes usability — searchability, accessibility, timeliness — the real measure, not the mere existence of a published catalogue.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that satisfying the listed clauses of section 4(1)(b) automatically achieves section 4's purpose. Compliance with the list and genuine transparency are related but distinct; the Act's own stated goal in section 4(2) is the proper success test.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** "Proactive disclosure under Section 4 is more important to the success of the Right to Information Act than the volume of individual applications processed." Discuss.

**Model solution:** Section 4 requires public authorities to catalogue records, publish organisational, procedural, financial and personnel particulars, disclose facts behind important policy decisions, and give reasons for administrative decisions — pursuing "maximum suo motu disclosure" as a continuing endeavour so that citizens have "minimum resort" to individual requests. This converts transparency into an administrative default rather than a citizen-by-citizen struggle, reduces repetitive applications, lowers appellate backlog, and enables social audit even by those who never file a request. However, section 4 carries no direct penalty comparable to section 20, so compliance is often weak, and formal publication (unreadable scans, stale data) frequently substitutes for usable transparency. A balanced view: section 4 is the structurally more important layer, but it needs stronger enforcement — periodic audits and Commission directions — to deliver on its promise, rather than individual applications remaining RTI's primary engine.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Section 4 duties accurately listed (at least three clauses) — 3; "maximum suo motu disclosure / minimum resort" standard correctly quoted or paraphrased — 2; causal argument for why prevention matters more than volume — 2; enforcement weakness honestly flagged — 2; balanced conclusion within 150 words — 1.

**Why the next lesson follows:** Proactive disclosure reduces, but never eliminates, the need to ask directly. The next lesson follows a request through the machinery that activates the moment someone actually does ask: the PIO, the APIO, the clocks, and what happens when nobody answers in time.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

You know *what* you can ask for (Lesson 2) and that transparency should ideally already exist (Lesson 3). Now: concretely, who receives your request, how long do they have, what happens if they are the wrong office, and what happens if nobody replies at all?

### The officer map

| Officer | Core role | Liability point |
|---|---|---|
| CPIO / SPIO (Central/State Public Information Officer) | receives and decides the request; must render reasonable assistance | personally liable to a section 20 penalty for unjustified default |
| CAPIO / SAPIO (Assistant PIO) | receives applications/appeals and forwards them | filing through an APIO **adds five days** to the response clock |
| Assisting officer | a PIO may seek help from another officer to dispose of a request | that officer is **deemed a PIO** for contravention purposes |
| First Appellate Authority | an officer senior in rank to the PIO within the same public authority | reviews the PIO's decision; distinct from the Commission |

### The request itself

A request may be made in writing or electronic form, in English, Hindi, or the official language of the area, accompanied by the prescribed fee. If a person cannot write the request, the PIO must **render reasonable assistance** to reduce the oral request to writing. Critically, **the applicant need not disclose any reason** for the request or any personal details beyond what is necessary for contact — RTI is a right, not a justified favour.

### The statutory clocks

```text
EVENT                              CLOCK
---------------------------------------------------------
section 6(3) transfer              not later than 5 days
ordinary decision                  30 days
life-or-liberty information        48 hours
filed through APIO                 +5 days added
third-party decision overall       40 days (see Lesson 15)
first appeal filing                ordinarily 30 days
first-appeal disposal              30 days; up to 45 with written reasons
second appeal filing               ordinarily 90 days
```

**Transfer (section 6(3)):** if the requested information is held by, or is more closely connected to the functions of, another public authority, the receiving authority must transfer the application (or the relevant part) to the correct authority **as soon as practicable, and not later than five days**, with immediate intimation to the applicant.

**Decision and the "life or liberty" exception (section 7(1)):** the ordinary decision period is **30 days** from receipt. Where the information concerns the **life or liberty of a person**, the period collapses to **48 hours** — the Act treats urgency as a distinct category, not merely a faster version of the ordinary clock.

**Deemed refusal (sections 7(2), 7(6)):** if the PIO fails to decide within the prescribed time, the request is treated as **refused** — this "deemed refusal" is what makes the clock enforceable rather than aspirational: silence is not a safe option for the authority, because silence itself becomes an appealable decision. Where the authority misses its own deadline, any information eventually furnished must be provided **free of charge**.

**Fee (Central RTI Rules, 2012):** the application fee is Rs 10, with further, separately calculated charges for copying, electronic media, or inspection beyond the first hour, and a statutory exemption for applicants below the poverty line. A further-fee intimation must disclose the calculation and the applicant's right to seek review of that calculation; the days between the fee intimation and its payment are **excluded** from the 30-day computation. **State Rules may differ** in form and amount — never assume the Central fee schedule applies uniformly nationwide.

```text
CITIZEN
  |
  +--> CPIO / SPIO ----------------------------+
  |                                             |
  +--> CAPIO / SAPIO -- forwards (+5 days) -----+
                                                |
                                 DECISION within 30 days
                                 (48 hours if life/liberty)
                                                |
                              NO DECISION = DEEMED REFUSAL
                                                |
                                       FIRST APPEAL
```

### Example and its limit

A citizen files through an APIO in a remote taluka office because no CPIO sits there. The Act adds five days precisely to absorb this forwarding delay — a design choice that protects citizens in areas with thin administrative presence from being penalised for using the access point available to them. **Limit:** the five added days apply to the *computation of the decision clock*; they do not excuse the CPIO from eventually deciding, nor do they restart the clock if the APIO delays forwarding beyond what is reasonable.

### Comparison: fee-related traps

| Fee issue | Correct rule |
|---|---|
| BPL applicant | exempt from application fee under the statutory proviso |
| Authority misses the deadline | information must be given free of charge |
| Unreasonable fee demanded | a listed ground for a section 18 complaint (Lesson 6) |
| Central fee schedule | Rs 10 application fee under the 2012 Rules — a Central default, not a universal one |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "A 30-day clock sounds enforceable, but if a PIO simply ignores the request, what actually forces compliance beyond the applicant filing yet another appeal?"

**Reply:** The objection correctly identifies that the clock alone does not self-execute — the applicant must still invoke the appellate machinery. But "deemed refusal" is precisely the design feature that prevents silent stonewalling from being cost-free: a non-response is legally treated as an adverse decision the moment the clock lapses, which immediately opens the first-appeal route without needing to prove that a decision was *actually* made. Combined with the free-of-charge consequence for delayed information and the personal section 20 penalty risk for the PIO, the statute builds escalating pressure against doing nothing. The qualified residual: enforcement still depends on the applicant's willingness to appeal, and on commissions and appellate officers actually using their penalty powers (a theme returned to in Lesson 13 and Lesson 18).

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, RTI procedure — a frequent source of close-option Prelims-style statements even though no direct Prelims PYQ is routed to this file (see the PYQ ledger note in the final arc).
- **Common trap:** confusing the APIO's role (forward only) with the PIO's role (decide); or assuming all States use the Rs 10 Central fee.
- **Answer-use:** a procedural "trace the RTI request from filing to decision" demand should move through PIO/APIO → transfer → decision/deemed refusal, citing exact day-counts.
- **Probable framing:** short factual/applied questions embedded inside a larger procedural or effectiveness essay.

### Revision notes

1. CPIO/SPIO decides; CAPIO/SAPIO only receives and forwards (+5 days added).
2. An assisting officer is deemed a PIO for contravention purposes.
3. Requests may be written/electronic, in English, Hindi, or the local official language; oral requests must be reduced to writing with PIO assistance.
4. No reasons or unnecessary personal details may be demanded from the applicant.
5. Section 6(3) transfer: as soon as practicable, not later than 5 days, with immediate intimation.
6. Ordinary decision clock: 30 days; life-or-liberty clock: 48 hours.
7. Deemed refusal: silence past the clock is treated as refusal, triggering appeal rights.
8. Delayed information must be furnished free of charge.
9. Central application fee: Rs 10 (2012 Rules); BPL applicants exempt; State rules may differ.
10. Fee-calculation days (intimation to payment) are excluded from the 30-day clock.
11. Mnemonic: **"5-30-48"** — five days to transfer, thirty days to decide, forty-eight hours if life or liberty is at stake.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "The Act's clocks work because silence is not neutral — a PIO who does nothing has still made a decision the Act calls 'deemed refusal,' and that decision is immediately appealable."

### Concept check

**Question:** A CPIO in Delhi receives a request that actually concerns records held by a different ministry. What must the CPIO do, and within what time?

**Model answer:** Under section 6(3), the CPIO must transfer the application (or the relevant part of it) to the public authority that holds the information or whose functions are more closely connected with it, as soon as practicable and not later than five days from receipt, and must immediately intimate the applicant about the transfer.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the CPIO can simply reject the request as "not maintainable" because the information belongs elsewhere. The Act requires affirmative transfer with prompt notice to the applicant, not a dead-end rejection.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the statutory clocks governing a Right to Information request, and discuss why "deemed refusal" is central to making these clocks enforceable.

**Model solution:** The RTI Act sets layered timelines: transfer a misdirected application within five days (section 6(3)); decide an ordinary request within 30 days; decide a life-or-liberty request within 48 hours (section 7(1) proviso). Filing through an Assistant PIO adds five days. If the authority misses the applicable period, section 7(2) read with section 7(6) deems the request refused, and delayed information, once furnished, must be free of charge. This "deemed refusal" mechanism is central: inaction is not a pending matter but an adverse decision that immediately opens the first-appeal route, forcing the system to keep moving even when an officer chooses not to act. Without deemed refusal, silence could functionally defeat the right; with it, the clock becomes the enforceable floor of the access guarantee.

**Why the next lesson follows:** A decision — or a deemed refusal — is not the end of the story. The Act gives the citizen a structured route to challenge it: first within the same department, and then, if that fails, to an independent Commission. That two-tier appeal structure, and exactly how binding its final step is, is the next lesson.

## Lesson 5

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

A PIO refuses your request, or simply lets the clock run out. You now have two more chances before the trail goes cold — but they are not identical in power, forum, or finality. Confusing them is one of the most common structural errors in an RTI answer.

### The two-tier structure

**First appeal (section 19(1)):** lies to an officer **senior in rank to the PIO** within the **same public authority**, ordinarily within **30 days** of the decision or of the expiry of the time allowed for decision (deemed refusal). The First Appellate Authority (FAA) must dispose of the appeal within **30 days**, extendable to **45 days** with recorded reasons in writing.

**Second appeal (section 19(3)):** lies to the **CIC or SIC**, ordinarily within **90 days** of the date the first-appeal decision should have been given or was actually given. This is the point at which the matter leaves the department entirely and reaches an institutionally independent adjudicator.

```text
PIO DECISION / DEEMED REFUSAL
          |
FIRST APPEAL — senior officer, SAME authority
          |   (30 days to file; 30-45 days to decide)
          |
SECOND APPEAL — CIC / SIC, INDEPENDENT of the authority
          |   (ordinarily 90 days to file)
          |
BINDING DECISION
```

### Onus and hearing

**Section 19(5)** places the **burden of proving that the denial of the request was justified squarely on the PIO** — not on the applicant to prove entitlement. This reverses the ordinary informational disadvantage a citizen faces: the party that already holds the record, and that chose to withhold it, must justify that choice, rather than the citizen having to prove a negative.

Where a request involves third-party information, that party must ordinarily be given a hearing opportunity before the appellate authority decides (developed fully in Lesson 15).

### Binding force — and its boundary

**Section 19(7)** makes the Commission's decision in a second appeal **binding**. This is one of RTI's most distinctive design choices: unlike many recommendatory oversight bodies, the Information Commission's order compels compliance rather than merely suggesting it.

But "binding" is not the same as "beyond review." **Section 23** bars ordinary civil courts from entertaining suits or proceedings against orders made under the Act — but this procedural bar cannot, and does not, oust the **constitutional judicial review** jurisdiction of the High Courts (Article 226) or the Supreme Court (Article 32/136). A public authority dissatisfied with a Commission order may seek a writ; it cannot simply ignore the order or relitigate it in an ordinary civil suit.

```text
PIO DECISION           -> operative within the department -> reviewable by first appeal
FIRST APPEAL DECISION  -> operative                        -> reviewable by second appeal
CIC/SIC DECISION       -> BINDING under section 19(7)      -> reviewable only by
                                                               constitutional writ jurisdiction
```

### Example and its limit

A ministry refuses a request citing "administrative inconvenience" — not a recognised section 8 exemption at all. At first appeal, the senior officer (from the same department) upholds the refusal, perhaps out of institutional sympathy. At second appeal, the Commission — institutionally outside that department — can scrutinise the claim on its merits, applying section 19(5)'s onus: the PIO must now actually justify the refusal with a real exemption, not a vague administrative preference. **Limit of the example:** the Commission's independence improves scrutiny, but it does not guarantee a particular outcome — if a genuine section 8 exemption does apply, the Commission can and should uphold the refusal; "binding" protects the correctness of the process, not a presumption in the applicant's favour.

### Comparison: first appeal versus second appeal

| Dimension | First appeal (s. 19(1)) | Second appeal (s. 19(3)) |
|---|---|---|
| Forum | senior officer, same public authority | CIC / SIC — institutionally independent |
| Filing window | ordinarily 30 days | ordinarily 90 days |
| Disposal window | 30 days; up to 45 with reasons | no fixed statutory outer limit; subject to case backlog |
| Bindingness | operative within department, itself appealable | binding under section 19(7), subject only to constitutional review |
| Onus | still rests with the PIO under general principle | expressly placed on the PIO by section 19(5) |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If the first appeal stays inside the same department, isn't it structurally toothless — why not let every refusal go straight to the Commission?"

**Reply:** The first appeal has genuine value even though it lacks the Commission's independence: it resolves a meaningful share of disputes quickly and cheaply, closer to the record-holder, without burdening an already backlogged Commission with every single refusal. It also creates a documented, reasoned departmental position that sharpens the second appeal's issues if the matter proceeds further. The qualified residual: the first appeal's quality depends heavily on how seriously the senior officer takes the review — where it becomes a rubber stamp, its value shrinks toward zero, and the real adjudicatory work shifts almost entirely to the Commission, with correspondingly higher pendency (a theme developed further in Lesson 18).

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, appellate architecture of statutory bodies.
- **Common trap:** assuming the second appeal lies to "a higher officer" rather than to an institutionally separate Commission; or forgetting that section 19(5) reverses the onus onto the PIO.
- **Answer-use:** any question on RTI's "teeth" should cite section 19(7)'s binding force *and* its judicial-review boundary in the same breath — overclaiming finality is a common scoring loss.
- **Probable framing:** "Distinguish the first and second appeal mechanisms under the RTI Act" or an embedded procedural paragraph inside a larger effectiveness question.

### Revision notes

1. First appeal: senior officer, same authority, ordinarily 30 days to file, 30-45 days to decide.
2. Second appeal: CIC/SIC, ordinarily 90 days to file — the point of institutional independence.
3. Section 19(5): the PIO bears the onus of proving denial was justified.
4. Section 19(7): Commission decisions in second appeal are binding.
5. Section 23 bars ordinary civil suits against RTI orders but does not oust constitutional writ jurisdiction.
6. "Binding" protects process integrity, not a guaranteed pro-disclosure outcome.
7. First appeal's practical value depends on how independently the senior officer actually reviews the PIO's decision.
8. Mnemonic: **"30 — 30/45 — 90"** for filing/disposal windows across the two appeals.
9. Do not confuse appeal (reviewing a denial/non-decision) with complaint (Lesson 6's distinct route).
10. Exam-safe phrase: "binding, yet constitutionally reviewable" captures the precise legal position without overclaiming finality.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "The RTI appeal structure reverses the usual burden — the officer who withheld the record must justify that choice — and ends in a Commission decision that is binding on the department but never immune from constitutional judicial review."

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does placing the onus on the PIO under section 19(5) matter more in an RTI appeal than it might in an ordinary civil dispute?

**Model answer:** In an ordinary dispute, the party seeking relief often must prove its claim because both sides have roughly equal access to the relevant facts. In an RTI dispute, the applicant structurally cannot know what the withheld record contains or why it was withheld — only the PIO holds that information. Placing the onus on the PIO corrects this informational asymmetry: the party who already controls the record must justify withholding it, rather than requiring the applicant to disprove a refusal about material they have never seen.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the general "whoever asserts must prove" rule of ordinary litigation applies unmodified to RTI appeals. Section 19(5) is a deliberate, express departure from that default, precisely because of the applicant's informational disadvantage.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Critically examine the structure of appeals under the Right to Information Act, 2005, and assess whether their binding force makes the Information Commission's decision truly final.

**Model solution:** The RTI Act creates a deliberately two-tiered appellate structure. A first appeal under section 19(1) lies to an officer senior in rank to the PIO within the same public authority, ordinarily within 30 days, to be disposed of within 30 days (extendable to 45 with written reasons). A second appeal under section 19(3) lies to the CIC or SIC, ordinarily within 90 days, shifting the matter to an institutionally independent adjudicator. Section 19(5) places the onus of justifying any denial squarely on the PIO, correcting the applicant's structural informational disadvantage, since only the record-holder knows what was withheld and why. Section 19(7) declares the Commission's decision binding, and section 19(8) equips it with systemic remedial powers — ordering access, appointing PIOs, directing record-management reform, awarding compensation and recommending penalties. This genuinely distinguishes RTI from recommendatory oversight bodies. However, "binding" is not synonymous with "final": section 23 only bars ordinary civil suits against RTI orders; it cannot and does not oust the constitutional writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and Supreme Court. A public authority can still challenge a Commission order via Article 226 or 32/136. The first appeal's actual value also depends on institutional will — where a senior officer defers reflexively to the PIO's decision, functional independence shifts entirely to the second appeal, increasing Commission backlog. The appellate structure is therefore legally robust but only as effective as the independence actually exercised at each tier and the Commission's own staffing capacity.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Both appeal tiers accurately described with timelines — 4; section 19(5) onus-reversal explained with reasoning — 3; section 19(7)/19(8) binding force and remedial powers explained — 3; section 23 versus constitutional review boundary correctly drawn — 3; critical assessment of first-appeal practical weakness — 1; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

**Why the next lesson follows:** Appeal is not the only route into the Commission's jurisdiction. The Act also allows a distinct "complaint" under section 18 — and conflating the two is one of the most litigated confusions in RTI practice, resolved definitively by the Supreme Court in *State of Manipur*.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

You could not even *submit* an RTI request — perhaps no PIO was designated at the office you approached, or the PIO refused to accept your application outright. Is that a "denial" you appeal, or something else entirely? The Act gives you a separate route for exactly this kind of problem: the section 18 complaint.

### The doctrine

**Section 18(1)** allows a complaint to the CIC/SIC where a person:

1. was unable to submit a request because no PIO had been appointed, or the PIO/APIO refused to accept the application or appeal;
2. was refused access to requested information;
3. was not given a response within the time limits;
4. was required to pay a fee considered unreasonable;
5. believes the information received was incomplete, misleading or false; or
6. faces any other matter relating to requesting or obtaining access to records.

On receiving a complaint, if there are **reasonable grounds**, the Commission may **initiate an inquiry** — and while inquiring, it wields the civil-court powers detailed in Lesson 11.

### Why the distinction is tested so hard

A complaint and a second appeal can look alike — both reach the same Commission — but they solve **different procedural problems**. An appeal reviews a *decision* (or deemed refusal) already made somewhere in the PIO/FAA chain. A complaint addresses *obstruction of the access system itself* — situations where the ordinary request-and-appeal chain could not even properly begin, or was abused along the way (unreasonable fee, false information).

***Chief Information Commissioner v. State of Manipur* (2011)** settled the point candidates most often get wrong. The Supreme Court held that **section 18's complaint jurisdiction is not an automatic substitute for the section 19 appellate disclosure remedy.** A person who wants the Commission to *order disclosure* of specific withheld information should ordinarily pursue the statutory appeal route (first appeal, then second appeal), because that is the route the Act designs for reviewing a denial on its merits and compelling release. A section 18 complaint, by contrast, is aimed at the *access-system failure* itself — it can trigger an inquiry, civil-court-style evidence-gathering, and section 20 consequences, but it does not convert into a disclosure order merely because the complainant also wants the withheld document.

```text
INFORMATION NEEDED
      |
REQUEST TO PIO
      |
decision / no decision
      |
FIRST APPEAL (senior officer)
      |
SECOND APPEAL TO CIC/SIC --------> disclosure / compliance remedy

ACCESS SYSTEM OBSTRUCTED?
no PIO appointed / APIO refuses / false information / unreasonable fee / delay
      |
SECTION 18 COMPLAINT -------------> inquiry + statutory consequences
                                     (not an automatic disclosure order)
```

### Example and its limit

A citizen approaches a small municipal office; no PIO has ever been designated there. She cannot even file a request, let alone appeal a decision that was never made. Section 18(1)(a) is designed exactly for this: she complains directly to the Commission about the structural failure to designate a PIO at all. **Limit of the example:** once the Commission inquires and (say) directs the authority to designate a PIO and accept her application, she may still need to go through the ordinary request-decision-appeal chain to actually obtain the specific document — the complaint fixed the system failure; it did not, by itself, guarantee her the document on the merits.

### Comparison: complaint versus appeal

| Dimension | Section 19 appeal | Section 18 complaint |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | denial, non-decision, or dissatisfaction with a decision | listed access-system failures (no PIO, refusal to accept, delay, unreasonable fee, false/incomplete information) |
| Ordinary sequence | request → first appeal → second appeal | direct complaint on a listed ground, without needing a prior appeal |
| Disclosure remedy | yes — the Commission can order access on the merits | not a substitute disclosure route per *State of Manipur* |
| Inquiry powers | appellate adjudication under section 19 | express civil-court inquiry powers under section 18 |
| Penalty exposure | section 20 may still arise | section 20 may still arise |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If both routes can ultimately trigger a section 20 penalty against the PIO, doesn't the complaint/appeal distinction collapse in practice?"

**Reply:** The two routes can converge on a shared *consequence* (the section 20 penalty) without collapsing into the same *remedy*. The distinction that *State of Manipur* protects is about which forum is designed to adjudicate the *disclosure question itself* — the appeal route, with its appellate structure, onus-reversal under section 19(5), and section 19(8) compliance powers, is purpose-built to decide "should this specific information be released?" A complaint inquiry is purpose-built to decide "did the access system fail this person, and should that failure carry consequences?" The qualified residual: a Commission handling a complaint is not barred from also directing disclosure where the facts plainly warrant it, but *State of Manipur* prevents complainants from treating section 18 as a shortcut that bypasses the appellate structure whenever that is more convenient.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, remedial architecture of statutory bodies — a recurring source of close-option traps.
- **Common trap:** describing section 18 as simply "another name for appeal" or claiming a complaint always secures disclosure.
- **Answer-use:** whenever a question gives a fact pattern ("no PIO was designated," "fee was unreasonable," "information was false"), route it to section 18; whenever the pattern is "my request was refused/ignored and I want the document," route it to section 19.
- **Probable framing:** "Distinguish complaint and appeal under the RTI Act with reference to case law" or an applied fact-pattern question.

### Revision notes

1. Section 18(1) lists six complaint grounds: no PIO appointed, refusal to accept application/appeal, refusal of access, no timely response, unreasonable fee, incomplete/misleading/false information (plus a residual "any other matter").
2. On reasonable grounds, the Commission may inquire into a complaint with civil-court powers.
3. *Chief Information Commissioner v. State of Manipur* (2011): complaint is not an automatic substitute for the section 19 disclosure appeal.
4. Appeal reviews a decision/non-decision on the merits; complaint addresses access-system obstruction.
5. Both routes can trigger section 20 penalty exposure for the PIO.
6. A complaint can still lead to an inquiry-level direction, but the designed route for ordering disclosure is the section 19 appeal.
7. Classic fact-pattern cue: "no PIO available" or "false information given" → complaint; "my request was denied/ignored and I want the document" → appeal.
8. Mnemonic: **"Complaint fixes the system; appeal fixes the answer."**
9. The case is routinely tested as a direct "what does *State of Manipur* hold" factual question.
10. Section 20 consequences are shared across both routes — do not treat penalty exposure as a distinguishing feature.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "A section 18 complaint asks whether the access system itself failed you; a section 19 appeal asks whether a specific denial was justified — and *State of Manipur* holds that the first route is not a shortcut around the second."

### Concept check

**Question:** A citizen's RTI request was ignored for 40 days with no reply. Should she file a section 18 complaint or a section 19 appeal to get the information she originally asked for?

**Model answer:** She should pursue the section 19 route. Silence past the statutory clock is "deemed refusal" under section 7(2)/7(6), which is an appealable decision — she should file a first appeal to the senior officer, and if unresolved, a second appeal to the CIC/SIC, since this route is designed to secure the specific disclosure on its merits. A section 18 complaint would be appropriate only if her underlying problem were an access-system failure (for example, no PIO being available to receive the request at all), not a straightforward non-response to a validly filed request.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "nothing happened for a long time" as automatically a section 18 complaint ground. Delay past the statutory clock is deemed refusal — the Act routes that specifically through the appeal chain, not the complaint chain, per *State of Manipur*'s reasoning on remedy allocation.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish a complaint under Section 18 from a second appeal under Section 19 of the RTI Act. Why does getting this distinction right matter in practice?

**Model solution:** Section 18 permits a complaint to the CIC/SIC on listed grounds — no PIO designated, refusal to accept an application, denial of access, delayed response, unreasonable fee, or incomplete/false information — allowing the Commission to inquire with civil-court powers. A second appeal under section 19(3) reviews a specific denial or non-decision on its merits, places the onus on the PIO under section 19(5), and can yield a binding disclosure order under sections 19(7)-(8). In *Chief Information Commissioner v. State of Manipur* (2011), the Supreme Court held that a complaint is not an automatic substitute for the section 19 disclosure remedy. The distinction matters because misrouting a grievance wastes time: a complainant wanting a specific document should use the appellate chain, purpose-built for disclosure, while one facing systemic obstruction should use section 18, which targets the access system's own failures, not a single denial's merits.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Section 18 grounds accurately listed (at least three) — 3; section 19 appeal structure and onus correctly described — 3; *State of Manipur* holding correctly stated — 2; practical significance of the distinction explained — 1; coherent within 150 words — 1.

**Why the next lesson follows:** Every remedy so far — request, appeal, complaint — eventually lands at the same destination: the Commission itself. Before its powers can be examined further, you need to know who actually sits on it and how they get there. That is the Central Information Commission's own composition and appointment.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

An institution is only as independent as the process that fills its seats. Before judging whether the CIC is "independent enough," you need the bare mechanics: how many members, who picks them, and what happens if the normal picking process has a gap (for instance, no recognised Leader of Opposition)?

### The doctrine

**Section 12** requires the Central Government to constitute the Central Information Commission by notification in the Official Gazette. The Commission consists of the **Chief Information Commissioner (CIC)** and **such number of Central Information Commissioners, not exceeding ten**, as the government considers necessary.

**Section 12(3)** fixes the appointing process: the **President** appoints the Chief and other Commissioners, acting on the **recommendation of a three-member committee** consisting of:

1. the **Prime Minister**, as Chairperson;
2. the **Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha**; and
3. a **Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister**.

**The LoP-deeming rule:** where no Leader of Opposition has been formally recognised in the Lok Sabha (for instance, because no single opposition party crosses the recognition threshold), the statutory **explanation deems the leader of the single largest opposition group** in the House to be the Leader of Opposition for the purpose of this committee. This prevents the appointment process from stalling merely because of a parliamentary arithmetic technicality.

**General superintendence, direction and management** of the Commission vests in the Chief Information Commissioner, who is assisted by the other Information Commissioners; the Commission is expressly stated to act **autonomously**, without being subject to directions from any other authority, in exercising its powers and performing its functions under the Act.

```text
                  PRIME MINISTER
                     (Chairperson)
                    /            \
                   /              \
      LOK SABHA LoP            UNION CABINET MINISTER
   (or deemed leader of the    (nominated by the
    largest opposition group)   Prime Minister)
                    \            /
                     \          /
                   RECOMMENDATION
                        |
                    PRESIDENT
                        |
         CHIEF INFORMATION COMMISSIONER
                        +
      up to TEN Information Commissioners
```

### Example and its limit

Suppose a Lok Sabha term has no party crossing the seat threshold ordinarily required for formal LoP recognition. Without the deeming rule, the three-member committee would effectively become a two-member committee dominated entirely by the executive (PM plus a minister nominated by the PM) — a serious independence concern. The deeming rule prevents this collapse by substituting the largest opposition group's leader. **Limit of the example:** the deeming rule guarantees an opposition *voice* on the committee, but it does not guarantee that voice can outvote a PM-plus-nominated-minister majority; the committee structure remains, numerically, executive-weighted even with the deeming rule operating correctly.

### Comparison: what the appointment design achieves and what it does not

| Design feature | Value delivered | Residual limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory three-member committee | removes pure, unstructured executive discretion | still a 2-to-1 executive-leaning numerical majority |
| LoP participation (actual or deemed) | guarantees a non-executive voice in every appointment | one voice can be outvoted; it cannot block a recommendation alone |
| "Up to ten" ceiling | flexibility to size the Commission to workload | a ceiling, not a guaranteed filled strength — vacancies remain possible |
| Autonomous-function language | signals functional independence in day-to-day work | does not, by itself, insulate tenure or pay (addressed in Lesson 9) |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "A committee where the Prime Minister chairs, nominates one of the three members, and faces only a single opposition voice is not meaningfully different from a purely executive appointment."

**Reply:** The objection correctly flags that the committee is numerically executive-leaning, and this is a genuine, honestly acknowledged structural weakness. But "not meaningfully different" overstates the case: the opposition member's presence creates a documented, recorded participant in every selection, capable of placing dissent on record, raising public scrutiny of a contested choice, and (historically) prompting litigation or public pressure around opaque selections. The qualified residual: the committee design is a meaningful, though incomplete, check — it reduces unilateral executive appointment to a *recorded, three-party process with a built-in opposition seat*, even though it cannot, by its numbers alone, prevent an executive-preferred choice from ultimately prevailing.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, appointment processes of statutory bodies — frequently compared across institutions (CBI Director panel, Lokpal selection, Election Commissioners).
- **Common trap:** placing the Chief Justice of India or the Lok Sabha Speaker on the CIC selection committee — both belong to *other* institutional panels, not this one.
- **Answer-use:** state the three-member committee precisely by office, not generically as "a committee"; the LoP-deeming rule is a frequently tested, easily forgotten detail.
- **Probable framing:** direct factual recall ("who appoints the CIC?") or comparative institutional-design questions.

### Revision notes

1. CIC = Chief Information Commissioner + up to ten Central Information Commissioners (a ceiling, not a mandatory full strength).
2. Constituted by Central Government notification in the Official Gazette (section 12).
3. Appointed by the **President** on the recommendation of a three-member committee.
4. Committee: **Prime Minister (Chair) + Lok Sabha LoP + a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM**.
5. LoP-deeming rule: if no LoP is formally recognised, the leader of the single largest opposition group is deemed LoP for this committee.
6. General superintendence and management vest in the Chief, assisted by the Commissioners.
7. The Commission acts autonomously in exercising its statutory powers — but this is function-level autonomy, distinct from tenure/pay independence (Lesson 9).
8. Headquarters ordinarily at Delhi; other offices require prior Central Government approval.
9. Do not add the Chief Justice, the Speaker, or the Leader of the House to this committee — they belong to other appointment panels entirely.
10. Mnemonic: **"PM chairs, LoP checks, Minister assists"** — three roles, three functions.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "The CIC's appointing committee gives the opposition a guaranteed, recorded seat at the table — a genuine check on unilateral executive appointment, even though the committee's numbers still lean toward the executive."

### Concept check

**Question:** If no party in the Lok Sabha has enough seats for its leader to be formally recognised as Leader of Opposition, does the CIC appointment process stall?

**Model answer:** No. The statutory explanation to section 12(3) deems the leader of the single largest opposition group in the Lok Sabha to be the Leader of Opposition for the specific purpose of this appointment committee, ensuring the three-member structure — and the opposition voice within it — remains intact regardless of formal recognition status.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the committee becomes a two-member, purely executive body whenever no LoP is formally recognised. The deeming rule is specifically designed to prevent exactly that outcome.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Describe the composition and appointment process of the Central Information Commission. How does the Act address the absence of a formally recognised Leader of Opposition?

**Model solution:** The Central Information Commission, constituted by Central Government notification under section 12, consists of a Chief Information Commissioner and up to ten Central Information Commissioners. The President appoints all members on the recommendation of a three-member committee comprising the Prime Minister as Chairperson, the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. General superintendence and management vest in the Chief, assisted by the other Commissioners, and the Commission is statutorily described as functioning autonomously. Where no Leader of Opposition has been formally recognised in the Lok Sabha — for instance, where no single party crosses the recognition threshold — the Act's explanation deems the leader of the single largest opposition group to serve on this committee instead, preserving a non-executive voice in every appointment rather than allowing the process to default into a purely executive exercise.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Composition (Chief + up to ten) stated accurately — 2; appointing authority (President) and committee's three offices named precisely — 4; LoP-deeming rule explained correctly — 3; coherent within 150 words — 1.

**Why the next lesson follows:** The Centre's design is only half the architecture. States need their own Commissions to hear appeals against State public authorities — and the Act builds the State Information Commission as a careful mirror of the Central design, with a few differences worth getting exactly right.

### The learner's problem

If the Centre has a CIC, does every State automatically get an identical SIC by simple analogy? Mostly yes — but "mostly" is exactly where UPSC likes to place its traps, and the eligibility/disqualification rules that apply to *both* bodies are a separate, equally testable layer.

### The doctrine — SIC composition and appointment

**Section 15** requires every **State Government** to constitute a **State Information Commission** by Gazette notification. It consists of the **State Chief Information Commissioner** and **such number of State Information Commissioners, not exceeding ten**, as considered necessary — an exact numerical mirror of the Central design.

**Section 15(3)** fixes the appointing process: the **Governor** appoints them on the recommendation of a three-member committee consisting of:

1. the **Chief Minister**, as Chairperson;
2. the **Leader of Opposition in the Legislative Assembly**; and
3. a **State Cabinet Minister nominated by the Chief Minister**.

The same **largest-opposition-group deeming rule** applies where no Assembly LoP is formally recognised. One precise limitation: in a **bicameral State**, the **Legislative Council's Leader of Opposition is not part of this committee** — only the Assembly LoP (or deemed leader) sits on it.

```text
                 CHIEF MINISTER
                    (Chairperson)
                   /            \
                  /              \
     ASSEMBLY LoP              STATE CABINET MINISTER
  (or deemed leader of the     (nominated by the
   largest opposition group)    Chief Minister)
                   \            /
                    \          /
                  RECOMMENDATION
                       |
                   GOVERNOR
                       |
      STATE CHIEF INFORMATION COMMISSIONER
                       +
     up to TEN State Information Commissioners
```

### The symmetry table — and the one asymmetry that matters most

| Head | CIC | SIC |
|---|---|---|
| Appointing authority | President | Governor |
| Committee chair | Prime Minister | Chief Minister |
| Opposition member | Lok Sabha LoP (or deemed leader) | Legislative Assembly LoP (or deemed leader) |
| Minister member | Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM | State Cabinet Minister nominated by the CM |
| Maximum other Commissioners | ten | ten |
| **Removal authority** | **President** | **Governor** |

The removal-authority row deserves special emphasis because it is the single most exploited comparison trap in this entire topic: **it is tempting, but wrong, to assume the SIC follows the NHRC/SHRC pattern, where even a State Human Rights Commission member is removed only by the President.** For the SIC, removal genuinely tracks appointment — the **Governor** both appoints *and* removes State Information Commissioners. This is explored fully in Lesson 10, but the distinction must be flagged the moment SIC composition is taught, because it is so frequently conflated with the SHRC's asymmetric rule.

```text
SIC:  Governor appoints  -->  Governor removes   (symmetric)
SHRC: Governor appoints  -->  PRESIDENT removes  (asymmetric — do not confuse the two)
```

### Eligibility and disqualification — common to CIC and SIC alike

**Eligibility:** appointees to either Commission (Chief or member, Central or State) must be persons of **eminence in public life** with wide knowledge and experience in **law, science and technology, social service, management, journalism, mass media, or administration and governance**. The statute deliberately seeks multidisciplinary competence rather than a judicial monopoly or a single professional pipeline.

**Disqualification:** a person cannot be appointed, and cannot continue, if they are a **Member of Parliament or of any State/UT Legislature**, hold **any other office of profit**, are **connected with any political party**, **carry on any business**, or **pursue any profession**.

```text
ELIGIBLE IF: eminence + wide knowledge/experience in
law | science-technology | social service | management |
journalism | mass media | administration-governance
                      |
                      v
DISQUALIFIED IF: legislature membership | office of profit |
                 political-party connection | business | profession
```

### Example and its limit

A retired senior civil servant with deep administrative experience is an obvious eligible candidate under the "administration and governance" head. A sitting Member of the Legislative Assembly who also happens to have "wide experience in governance" is nonetheless **disqualified outright** by the legislature-membership bar — eligibility and disqualification operate as independent gates, and satisfying the eligibility criteria never cures a disqualification. **Limit of the example:** disqualification bars apply at the moment of appointment and, by the statute's design, during service as well — a Commissioner who later joins a political party or takes up another office of profit would fall foul of the same bar, feeding into the removal grounds developed in Lesson 10.

### Comparison: design rationale versus residual risk

| Feature | Intended value | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|
| Multidisciplinary eligibility | varied, non-judicial-monopoly understanding of governance and records | criteria remain fairly open-textured in practice |
| No political-party connection | impartiality in adjudicating against the government of the day | prior affiliations before appointment may still shape outlook |
| No business/profession | reduces conflicts of interest | post-tenure career incentives are not addressed by this bar |
| Committee-based appointment | political accountability plus an opposition voice | numerically executive-leaning, as in Lesson 7 |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If eligibility is this broad — six entire professional fields — doesn't the criterion do almost no gatekeeping work at all?"

**Reply:** The breadth is a deliberate design choice, not an oversight: the Commission adjudicates disputes touching governance, administration, technology, media and social service alike, so a narrow single-discipline requirement (say, a judicial background only) would under-equip it for the Act's actual subject matter. The genuine gatekeeping happens less through the eligibility list and more through the **disqualification** bars (no legislature seat, no office of profit, no party connection, no business or profession), which do real, enforceable work by excluding candidates with live political or commercial conflicts. The qualified residual: breadth of eligible background is a strength for competence diversity, but it does not by itself guarantee pluralism in *actual* appointments, which still depends on how the appointing committee exercises its discretion within the eligible pool — a theme connected to the independence debate in Lessons 9-10 and 18.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, comparative institutional design (CIC/SIC vis-à-vis NHRC/SHRC, Lokpal, Election Commission).
- **Common trap:** importing the SHRC's President-only removal rule onto the SIC; or omitting the bicameral-State qualification that only the Assembly LoP sits on the SIC committee.
- **Answer-use:** any comparative question listing CIC/SIC alongside NHRC/SHRC should explicitly flag the removal-authority symmetry difference as the highest-yield distinguishing fact.
- **Probable framing:** "Compare the composition of the CIC and SIC" or a close-option statement testing the removal-authority trap directly.

### Revision notes

1. SIC = State Chief Information Commissioner + up to ten State Information Commissioners (section 15).
2. Constituted by State Government Gazette notification; appointed by the **Governor**.
3. Committee: **Chief Minister (Chair) + Assembly LoP (or deemed leader) + a State Cabinet Minister nominated by the CM**.
4. The Legislative Council's LoP is **not** on this committee even in a bicameral State.
5. **Removal of SIC members is by the Governor** — symmetric with appointment, **unlike the SHRC**, whose members only the President can remove.
6. Eligibility (both CIC and SIC): eminence plus experience in law, science-technology, social service, management, journalism/mass media, or administration-governance.
7. Disqualification (both): legislature membership, office of profit, political-party connection, business, or profession.
8. Eligibility and disqualification are independent gates — meeting eligibility never cures a disqualification.
9. The "up to ten" ceiling, LoP-deeming rule, and autonomous-function language all apply identically to the SIC as to the CIC.
10. Mnemonic: **"Mirror the Centre, but mind the Governor"** — composition mirrors the CIC almost exactly, except that the Governor both appoints and removes.

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "The SIC is the CIC's structural mirror at the State level — same ceiling, same committee logic, same eligibility and disqualification rules — with exactly one asymmetry worth memorising precisely: the Governor both appoints and removes, unlike the SHRC's President-only removal."

### Concept check

**Question:** A State Cabinet Minister argues that because the Governor appoints SIC members, the same logic used for the SHRC should mean only the President can remove them. Is this correct?

**Model answer:** No. Unlike the SHRC, where appointment and removal are split between the Governor (appoints) and the President (removes), the SIC's appointment and removal are symmetric: the Governor both appoints and removes State Information Commissioners, subject to the same proved-misbehaviour/incapacity and Supreme Court inquiry safeguards that apply to the Centre. The SHRC's asymmetric rule does not carry over to the SIC.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming all "Governor-appointed" statutory commissioners share the SHRC's President-only removal rule. This is one of the most common comparative traps in Polity — verify the removal authority for each body independently rather than assuming uniformity.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Compare the composition and appointment of the Central and State Information Commissions. Identify the one point at which their institutional design is not a perfect mirror of each other.

**Model solution:** Both Commissions mirror each other: the CIC comprises a Chief and up to ten Central Information Commissioners, appointed by the President on a committee of the PM, the Lok Sabha LoP (or deemed leader), and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM. The SIC comprises a State Chief and up to ten State Information Commissioners, appointed by the Governor on a parallel committee — the CM, the Assembly LoP (or deemed leader), and a State Cabinet Minister nominated by the CM. Both share identical eligibility (eminence in specified fields) and disqualification bars (legislature membership, office of profit, party connection, business or profession). The genuine asymmetry worth flagging lies in contrast with a different institution: unlike the SHRC, whose members the Governor appoints but only the President removes, SIC members are both appointed and removed by the Governor — symmetric, unlike the SHRC.

### The learner's problem

An adjudicator who reviews the government's own secrecy decisions is only as independent as the process that decides their *term* and *pay*. Before 2019, Parliament itself fixed those terms, in the Act. After 2019, who fixes them — and does that change actually matter, or is it just a change of paperwork?

### Before 2019 — the original design

As originally enacted, the RTI Act fixed, in the statute itself: a term of **five years** (or until age 65, whichever came earlier) for the Chief and other Commissioners, at both Central and State levels; and **salary parity** — the Central Chief Information Commissioner's salary and conditions were pegged to the **Chief Election Commissioner's**, a Central Information Commissioner's to an **Election Commissioner's**, and the corresponding State-level officers to the **Chief Secretary** of the State.

### What the 2019 Amendment actually changed

The **RTI (Amendment) Act, 2019** removed these fixed, Parliament-enacted provisions and replaced them with a delegation: tenure, salary, allowances and other service conditions for both Central and State Information Commissioners would henceforth be **"such as may be prescribed" by the Central Government** — that is, decided through **executive rule-making**, not through the Act itself.

Acting on that delegation, the **2019 service-condition Rules** currently prescribe:

| Head | Current rule under the 2019 Rules |
|---|---|
| Term | **three years** from entry into office (for Central Chief, Central ICs, State Chief and State ICs alike) |
| Maximum age | **65** — this ceiling remains directly in the Act itself, untouched by the amendment |
| Reappointment | a Chief **cannot** be reappointed as Chief; an IC **cannot** be reappointed as IC, but **may be appointed as Chief**; the **aggregate** tenure as IC **and** Chief together cannot exceed **five years** |
| Pay (Central Chief Information Commissioner) | Rs 2,50,000 per month, fixed |
| Pay (Central Information Commissioner) | Rs 2,25,000 per month, fixed |

### The autonomy argument, precisely stated

The headline "five years became three years" is the easy half of the story and, on its own, actually understates the real concern. The deeper structural point is this: **a safeguard that used to live in an Act of Parliament — amendable only by Parliament, through open legislative process — now lives in Rules the Central Government can itself prescribe and revise.** The body whose job is to review the government's own secrecy decisions now has its tenure, pay and residuary service conditions fixed by that same government's rule-making power, not by the legislature as a separate, harder-to-change check.

```text
COMMISSION REVIEWS GOVERNMENT DISCLOSURE DECISIONS
                    |
CENTRAL EXECUTIVE NOW PRESCRIBES THE COMMISSION'S OWN
TERM + PAY + RESIDUARY SERVICE CONDITIONS, BY RULE
                    |
[reasoned concern] structural dependence / possible chilling effect
                    |
[counter-safeguard] committee-based appointment, age ceiling,
non-reappointment, and protected removal all survive unchanged
```

**Residuary control:** the 2019 Rules further provide that any service-condition question not expressly covered is decided by the Central Government, whose decision is binding, and the Central Government retains a power of relaxation — reinforcing, in the fine print, just how much of a Commissioner's working life now runs through executive rule rather than parliamentary statute.

### Example and its limit

Two Information Commissioners are appointed the same year — one at the Centre, one in a State. Before 2019, both could point to an identical, Parliament-fixed five-year term and salary parity with a named constitutional-level office, regardless of which government was in power. After 2019, both serve a three-year term and a fixed rupee salary that the **same executive they adjudicate against** can, in principle, revise through ordinary rule-making, without needing to return to Parliament at all. **Limit of the example:** a change in Rules is not instantaneous or invisible — rule-making is still subject to procedural requirements and public scrutiny, and no evidence suggests arbitrary mid-term changes have actually been used to pressure sitting Commissioners. The concern is about *structural* dependence and incentive, not a documented instance of abuse.

### Comparison: survived versus weakened

| Safeguard | Status after 2019 |
|---|---|
| Committee-based appointment with opposition participation | **survived**, unchanged |
| Statutory maximum age of 65 | **survived**, unchanged — still directly in the Act |
| Non-reappointment rule | **survived**, unchanged |
| Protected removal (Supreme Court inquiry for misbehaviour/incapacity) | **survived**, unchanged (Lesson 10) |
| Fixed five-year term in the Act | **weakened** — now "as prescribed," currently three years by Rule |
| Salary parity with a constitutional-level office | **weakened** — now a fixed rupee figure set by Rule, not statutory parity |
| Core service-condition rule-making authority | **shifted** from Parliament (in the Act) to the Central Government (by Rules) |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "The government could argue this is mere administrative rationalisation — a statutory body need not claim the same tenure protections as a constitutional one, and nothing in the 2019 change touches appointment or removal."

**Reply:** This is the strongest available counter-position, and a balanced answer must state it fairly: appointment (committee-based, with opposition participation), the age ceiling, non-reappointment, and protected removal after a Supreme Court inquiry all remain exactly as before — the 2019 change did not touch any of them. A statutory body genuinely need not mirror a constitutional body's every protection. The qualified residual, however, is that this defence addresses *breadth* of safeguards, not their *source*: even accepting every surviving safeguard at full strength, the specific safeguards that were changed — tenure and pay — are precisely the two levers through which day-to-day dependence on the executive is most directly felt, and moving them from Act to Rule is a real, not merely cosmetic, shift in who controls them.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

Formal removal protections look reassuring on paper — a Commissioner cannot be sacked on a whim. But what if a government never needs to remove anyone, because it simply never *appoints* replacements when seats fall vacant? Is an empty chair not a much quieter, much more effective way to weaken a Commission than any formal removal ever could be?

### The doctrine — removal

**Removing authority:** the **President** removes a Central Chief/Information Commissioner; the **Governor** removes a State Chief/State Information Commissioner (confirming the symmetric SIC rule flagged in Lesson 8).

**The protected route — proved misbehaviour or incapacity:** removal on this ground requires the President or Governor to make a **reference to the Supreme Court**; only after the Supreme Court's **inquiry and report** finding the charge proved may the removal order follow. **During the pendency of that inquiry**, the President or Governor may **suspend** the Commissioner and, if considered necessary, prohibit their attendance at office, until the inquiry report is received and an order is passed.

**The direct-ground route — no Supreme Court inquiry required:** removal may follow a direct order where the Commissioner:

1. is **adjudged an insolvent**;
2. is **convicted of an offence** which, in the relevant authority's opinion, involves **moral turpitude**;
3. engages in any **paid employment outside the duties of office**;
4. is, in the relevant authority's opinion, **unfit to continue by reason of infirmity of mind or body**; or
5. has **acquired such financial or other interest** as is likely to affect prejudicially their official functions.

A Commissioner's interest in a **government contract or agreement**, beyond the ordinary shareholder interest in an incorporated company, is itself **deemed misbehaviour** warranting the inquiry route.

```text
ALLEGATION AGAINST A COMMISSIONER
      |
      +--> proved misbehaviour / incapacity
      |         |
      |    President/Governor REFERS to Supreme Court
      |         |
      |    SC INQUIRY + REPORT finds charge proved
      |         |
      |    removal order follows
      |         (suspension possible throughout the inquiry)
      |
      +--> listed direct ground (insolvency, moral-turpitude
              conviction, paid outside work, infirmity,
              prejudicial interest)
                |
           direct order by President/Governor — no SC inquiry needed
```

### The quieter problem: vacancy as de facto weakening

Formal removal is genuinely hard — deliberately so, to protect Commissioners from arbitrary executive displeasure. But a government that wants to weaken a Commission does not need to remove anyone at all: it can simply **delay filling vacancies** as terms expire or seats fall open, leaving the Commission understaffed, backlogged, and slower to deliver the very remedy the Act promises.

***Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India* (2019)** is the controlling authority on exactly this dimension. The Supreme Court addressed **prolonged vacancies** in the Central Information Commission and several State Commissions, and directed the government toward a **timely and transparent appointment process** — including initiating the selection process sufficiently in advance of an impending vacancy, and placing relevant particulars of the selection process (such as the composition of search/selection mechanisms and shortlisted candidates, subject to appropriate safeguards) in the public domain, rather than filling seats through an opaque, last-minute process.

```text
SEAT FALLS VACANT (retirement / end of term)
            |
NO TIMELY, TRANSPARENT SELECTION PROCESS INITIATED
            |
PROLONGED VACANCY
            |
FEWER COMMISSIONERS AVAILABLE TO HEAR SECOND APPEALS
            |
GROWING BACKLOG  ->  LONGER WAIT FOR DISCLOSURE
            |
STATUTORY RIGHT BECOMES PRACTICALLY SLOWER,
EVEN THOUGH NO FORMAL REMOVAL EVER OCCURRED
```

### Example and its limit

A Central Information Commissioner's three-year term ends; no selection process begins for several months afterward. No one has been "removed" — formally, nothing adverse has happened to anyone. Yet the Commission now operates with one fewer adjudicator, second appeals queue longer, and citizens who already waited through request and two appeal tiers face a further, invisible delay at the very last stage. **Limit of the example:** *Anjali Bhardwaj* addresses the *process* failure (delay, opacity) and directs timely, transparent appointment; it cannot, by itself, compel a government to actually appoint a specific person by a specific date, nor does judicial direction alone guarantee sustained compliance over time — which is precisely why vacancy remains a live, recurring concern rather than a problem solved once by a single judgment.

### Comparison: removal versus vacancy as independence threats

| Threat | Mechanism | Formal/visible? | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arbitrary removal | President/Governor acting without cause | highly visible — would attract immediate scrutiny | Supreme Court inquiry requirement for misbehaviour/incapacity |
| Prolonged vacancy | simply not appointing replacements in time | easy to overlook, rarely attracts the same scrutiny | *Anjali Bhardwaj* direction toward timely, transparent process |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If removal is this well-protected, doesn't that prove the Act already secures Commissioner independence adequately?"

**Reply:** Protected removal genuinely secures independence *for sitting Commissioners against arbitrary dismissal* — that part of the objection is correct and should not be understated. But it says nothing about the independence of the *institution* when seats are simply left empty rather than filled with Commissioners who would then enjoy that protection. The qualified residual: formal removal protection and timely appointment are two *different* independence guarantees, and a government uninterested in confrontational removal can still weaken the Commission through appointment delay alone — which is exactly the gap *Anjali Bhardwaj* was decided to address, and exactly why "removal is hard" cannot be read as "independence is secure."

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, independence and accountability of statutory bodies — this lesson supplies the "implementation gap" counter-evidence frequently needed in a 2019-Amendment autonomy answer (Lesson 9) or the final synthesis (Lesson 18).
- **Common trap:** discussing only formal removal protections as "independence," while ignoring vacancy/appointment-delay as an equally real, arguably more common, independence threat.
- **Answer-use:** pair the removal-is-hard point with the vacancy-is-easy point whenever a question asks you to "critically evaluate" or "critically examine" independence — this pairing is what earns the "critically" credit.
- **Probable framing:** embedded within a larger independence/effectiveness essay; occasionally a direct "discuss the Supreme Court's intervention on Information Commission vacancies" question.

**Question:** Why can a Commission be practically weakened without a single Commissioner ever being formally removed?

**Model answer:** Because prolonged vacancy achieves a similar practical effect to removal without triggering any of the formal removal safeguards. If the government delays initiating a transparent selection process after a seat falls vacant, the Commission simply operates understaffed — hearing fewer second appeals, building backlog, and delivering the Act's remedy more slowly — even though no Commissioner was dismissed and no misbehaviour inquiry ever occurred. *Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India* (2019) specifically targeted this appointment-delay pathway.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating "Commissioners cannot be arbitrarily removed" with "the Commission's independence/effectiveness is fully secure." Appointment-delay is a distinct, separately significant threat that protected removal does nothing to address.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** "Protected removal safeguards the independence of sitting Information Commissioners, but says nothing about the independence of an understaffed Commission." Examine, with reference to relevant judicial intervention.

**Model solution:** The RTI Act builds a genuinely protective removal process: the President removes Central Commissioners and the Governor removes State Commissioners, but only after a Supreme Court inquiry establishes proved misbehaviour or incapacity for the general ground, with suspension possible meanwhile; a narrower set of direct grounds — insolvency, moral-turpitude conviction, paid outside employment, infirmity, or prejudicial financial interest, including deemed misbehaviour from certain government-contract interests — permits removal without that inquiry. However, this safeguard addresses only one threat to independence: it says nothing about a government simply declining to fill vacancies promptly as terms expire. In *Anjali Bhardwaj v. An understaffed Commission hears fewer second appeals, accumulates backlog, and delays the very disclosure the Act promises, even though formally no Commissioner has been removed and no misbehaviour inquiry has occurred. ## Lesson 11

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — section 18(2)-(3)'s civil-court powers cross-checked against the canonical owners for the exact listed powers and their stated boundary.
🔍 CA Search: `"CIC civil court powers inquiry limits judicial review 2025 2026"`
📰 CA Found: No material new development changing the settled "civil-court powers, not a civil court" boundary found.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

A phrase like "the Commission has civil-court powers" can sound, misleadingly, like "the Commission is a civil court." It is not — and the gap between the two is exactly where a careless answer loses marks.

### The doctrine

**Section 18(2):** where there exist **reasonable grounds**, the Commission may **initiate an inquiry** in respect of a complaint received under section 18(1) (the grounds catalogued in Lesson 6).

**Section 18(3):** while inquiring, the Commission has the **same powers as are vested in a civil court** while trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, specifically concerning:

1. **summoning and enforcing the attendance** of persons, and compelling them to give oral or written evidence **on oath** and to produce documents or things;
2. **requiring the discovery and inspection** of documents;
3. **receiving evidence on affidavit**;
4. **requisitioning** any public record or copy from any office or court;
5. **issuing commissions** for the examination of witnesses or documents; and
6. any other matter prescribed.

**The record-access guarantee:** the Commission may **examine any record** covered by the Act that is under the public authority's control, and **no such record may be withheld from the Commission on any ground** — a deliberate design feature preventing an authority from resisting the Commission's own scrutiny by invoking the very exemption whose applicability is in dispute.

```text
                  CIVIL-COURT TOOLKIT (section 18(3))
     --------------------------------------------------------
     summon/compel attendance        -> secure testimony
     evidence on oath                -> formalise fact-finding
     discovery/inspection             -> locate relevant documents
     affidavit evidence               -> receive sworn material
     requisition public records       -> overcome departmental withholding
     commissions for witnesses/docs   -> examine where direct attendance is impractical
```

### Power is not identity

```text
CIVIL-COURT POWERS FOR THE LISTED INQUIRY MATTERS
                        ≠
A CIVIL COURT WITH GENERAL JURISDICTION
```

This single inequality is the lesson's core teaching point. "Civil-court powers" means the Commission has borrowed a specific **evidence-gathering toolkit** from civil procedure — the ability to compel testimony, demand documents, and examine records that a reluctant authority might otherwise withhold. It does **not** mean the Commission has become a civil court in the general sense. The Commission **cannot**:

- **convict** anyone of any offence, or impose criminal sanctions;
- award **punitive or general tort-style damages** as though exercising plenary civil jurisdiction;
- **decide the merits of the underlying dispute** that happens to generate the RTI request (for instance, whether a pension claim is actually valid, or whether a tender was wrongly awarded) — it can order the *information* about that dispute to be disclosed, but it cannot itself adjudicate the dispute;
- **strike down a law** or declare a provision unconstitutional.

```text
RTI REQUEST: "Give me the pension file and the reasons recorded for rejecting my claim."
                    -> Commission CAN compel disclosure of the file/reasons

UNDERLYING GRIEVANCE: "My pension claim itself was wrongly rejected — sanction it."
                    -> competent service/grievance/court route, NOT the Commission
```

### Example and its limit

A whistleblower complains that a department is refusing to produce a specific set of inspection reports, citing vague "confidentiality." Using its civil-court powers, the Commission can summon the responsible officer, require production of the reports for its own examination (even over the department's resistance), and take sworn evidence about why the reports were withheld. **Limit of the example:** if those reports reveal that the officer behaved corruptly, the Commission can order *disclosure* of the reports and, separately, trigger section 20 consequences against a defaulting PIO if the refusal itself was unjustified — but it cannot *prosecute* the officer for corruption; that step belongs to vigilance, Lokpal, or criminal-investigation machinery entirely outside the RTI Act.

### Comparison: Commission inquiry versus a true civil court

| Dimension | Commission (section 18 inquiry) | Civil court |
|---|---|---|
| Source of power | RTI Act, 2005, borrowing a CPC-style toolkit | Constitution/statute establishing general civil jurisdiction |
| Subject-matter reach | RTI access-system complaints only | the full range of civil disputes within its jurisdiction |
| Can it convict/punish criminally? | No | A criminal court can; an ordinary civil court generally cannot either, but the comparison underscores the Commission's narrower remit |
| Can it decide the underlying merits (pension, tender, etc.)? | No — only the information about it | Yes, if that is the properly framed cause of action before it |
| Remedy | disclosure, compliance, compensation, penalty, discipline (Lessons 12-13) | the full range of civil remedies within its jurisdiction |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If the Commission can summon witnesses, compel sworn evidence, and requisition any record over an authority's resistance, functionally that already looks like a full court — why insist on the distinction?"

**Reply:** The toolkit's strength is real and should not be minimised — it is precisely what prevents an uncooperative department from stonewalling the Commission's own fact-finding. But a strong evidence-gathering toolkit answers a narrow question — *can the Commission find out what actually happened and what a record actually says?* — while jurisdiction over the underlying dispute answers a much broader question — *can the Commission decide whether a government action was itself lawful, fair, or correct on the merits?* The Act answers the first question emphatically yes and the second question no. The qualified residual: the distinction matters most exactly where a request is a thin wrapper around an unresolved service or contractual grievance — recognising the Commission's jurisdictional limit there prevents a misdirected RTI complaint from being mistaken for the actual remedy the complainant needs.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, powers and limits of a quasi-judicial statutory body.
- **Common trap:** describing the Commission as "equivalent to a civil court" without qualification, or assuming it can grant the underlying service benefit a request indirectly concerns.
- **Answer-use:** cite the exact section 18(3) toolkit (summon, oath, discovery, affidavit, requisition, commissions) to earn factual marks, then immediately state the boundary ("but it cannot decide the underlying dispute / convict / strike down a law") to earn analytical marks.
- **Probable framing:** "Discuss the powers of the Information Commission while inquiring into a complaint" or an applied fact pattern testing jurisdictional limits.

### Revision notes

1. Section 18(2): inquiry on "reasonable grounds" following a section 18(1) complaint.
2. Section 18(3) toolkit: summon/compel attendance and oath-based evidence; discovery/inspection of documents; affidavit evidence; requisition of public records; commissions for witnesses/documents; any other prescribed matter.
3. No RTI-covered record may be withheld from the Commission on any ground during its own examination.
4. "Civil-court powers" is a borrowed evidence-gathering toolkit, not a conversion into a civil court with general jurisdiction.
5. The Commission cannot convict, cannot award punitive/tort-style damages, cannot decide the underlying service/contractual merits, and cannot strike down a law.
6. The Commission can order disclosure of information *about* an underlying dispute without adjudicating that dispute itself.
7. Corruption or criminal wrongdoing revealed through an inquiry routes to vigilance/Lokpal/criminal-investigation machinery, not Commission prosecution.
8. The record-access guarantee (no withholding "on any ground" from the Commission itself) is distinct from, and prior to, any exemption analysis applied to the *public* (Lessons 14-15).
9. Mnemonic: **"Borrowed toolkit, not a borrowed bench."**
10. This lesson's boundary point pairs naturally with Lesson 12's binding-force boundary — both teach "strong power, bounded jurisdiction."

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "The Commission's civil-court powers are a toolkit for finding out the truth about a withheld record, not a licence to adjudicate the underlying dispute, convict wrongdoing, or strike down a law."

### Concept check

**Question:** A department refuses to let the Commission itself examine a file during a section 18 inquiry, citing section 8(1) confidentiality. Can it do this?

**Model answer:** No. The Act expressly provides that any record covered by the Act and under the public authority's control may be examined by the Commission, and no such record may be withheld from the Commission on any ground. The department's section 8(1) exemption argument may be relevant to what is eventually *disclosed to the public/complainant*, but it cannot be used to prevent the Commission's own internal examination of the record while determining that very question.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming a section 8 exemption claim can block the Commission's own access to a record. The exemption analysis governs disclosure to the applicant, not the Commission's power to examine the record in order to decide the dispute.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain the civil-court powers available to the Information Commission under Section 18, and clarify the limits of these powers.

**Model solution:** Under section 18(3), while inquiring into a complaint on reasonable grounds, the Commission exercises civil-court powers — summoning and compelling attendance, oral/written evidence on oath, discovery and inspection of documents, affidavit evidence, requisitioning public records, and issuing commissions to examine witnesses or documents. No Act-covered record may be withheld from the Commission's own examination on any ground. These powers are a borrowed evidence-gathering toolkit, not general civil jurisdiction: the Commission cannot convict anyone, cannot award tort-style punitive damages, cannot adjudicate the underlying service, contractual or policy dispute behind the request, and cannot strike down a law. It can compel disclosure of information about a dispute without deciding the dispute itself, routing corruption or criminal findings to vigilance or prosecutorial machinery instead.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Section 18(3) toolkit listed with at least four specific powers — 4; record-access guarantee stated — 2; jurisdictional limits (no conviction, no underlying-merits adjudication, no striking down law) stated — 3; coherent within 150 words — 1.

**Why the next lesson follows:** Civil-court powers equip the Commission to find facts during a *complaint* inquiry. The Commission's most consequential power, however, operates in the *appeal* — where it can bind a department to actually disclose information, reform its practices, or compensate a citizen. That binding appellate power, and precisely where judicial review still reaches it, is the next lesson.

## Lesson 12

Progress: 12/18 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Section 19 appellate powers, binding force, Section 19(8) remedies and the judicial-review boundary

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — sections 19(7)-(8) and section 23 cross-checked against the canonical owners for the exact remedial list and the review-boundary language.
🔍 CA Search: `"RTI Commission order judicial review writ High Court 2025 2026"`
📰 CA Found: No fresh development displacing the settled binding-yet-reviewable position.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

Lesson 5 established that a second-appeal decision is "binding." But binding on whom, to do exactly what — and binding against every further challenge, or only against ordinary litigation? This lesson supplies the precise remedial menu and the precise outer boundary.

### The doctrine — the remedial menu under Section 19(8)

In deciding an appeal, the Commission has the power to **require the public authority to take any steps necessary to secure compliance** with the Act, including:

1. providing **access to information**, in a particular form if required;
2. **appointing a Central/State Public Information Officer**, where none exists;
3. **publishing** certain information or categories of information;
4. making **necessary changes to practices** relating to the maintenance, management and destruction of records;
5. **enhancing training** provisions for officials on RTI;
6. providing an **annual report** compliance direction; and
7. requiring the authority to **compensate** the complainant for any loss or other detriment suffered.

Separately, the Commission may **impose any of the penalties** under section 20, or **reject the application**.

```text
                  SECTION 19(8) REMEDY WHEEL
                   ------------------------
                        PROVIDE ACCESS
                             |
  APPOINT PIO ------ SECURE COMPLIANCE ------ PUBLISH INFORMATION
                             |
  RECORD-PRACTICE REFORM ---+--- TRAINING ---+--- ANNUAL-REPORT COMPLIANCE
                             |
                 COMPENSATION / PENALTY / REJECTION
```

**Why this matters more than a single disclosure order:** the remedy wheel shows the Commission's strongest systemic power is not merely "release this one document" — it can also correct the **underlying administrative architecture** that produced the refusal in the first place: appointing a missing PIO, forcing record-management reform, or mandating training. This is what separates RTI's adjudicator from a body that can only resolve one dispute at a time without touching the system that keeps generating similar disputes.

### Binding force and its boundary

**Section 19(7):** the decision of the Commission in a second appeal is **binding**.

**Section 23:** no court shall entertain any **suit, application or other proceeding** in respect of any order made under the Act, and no such order shall be called in question otherwise than by way of an appeal under the Act itself.

**The precise boundary:** section 23's bar operates against **ordinary civil litigation** re-opening a Commission's order as though it were an ordinary civil dispute. It does **not**, and constitutionally **cannot**, oust the **writ jurisdiction** of the High Courts under **Article 226** or the Supreme Court under **Article 32/136** — constitutional judicial review of an administrative/quasi-judicial order is not "a suit, application or other proceeding" of the kind section 23 contemplates, and a privative clause cannot extinguish a court's constitutionally conferred review jurisdiction.

```text
PIO DECISION            -> operative within the department -> reviewable by first appeal
FIRST APPEAL DECISION    -> operative                       -> reviewable by second appeal
CIC/SIC DECISION         -> BINDING (section 19(7))          -> NOT reviewable by ordinary
                                                                  civil suit (section 23) BUT
                                                                  reviewable by constitutional
                                                                  writ jurisdiction (Art. 226/32)
```

### Example and its limit

A public authority, dissatisfied with a Commission order directing disclosure of a sensitive internal report, cannot file an ordinary civil suit in a district court asking that court to "re-decide" the RTI dispute — section 23 forecloses exactly that route. It *can*, however, petition the jurisdictional High Court under Article 226 arguing that the Commission's order is, say, perverse, beyond jurisdiction, or in breach of natural justice. **Limit of the example:** a writ petition is not a routine second-guessing mechanism — High Courts ordinarily exercise restraint over specialised tribunal/Commission findings of fact, intervening chiefly for jurisdictional error, perversity, or procedural unfairness, not merely because the authority disagrees with the outcome.

### Comparison: three ways to describe "binding," only one of them accurate

| Claim | Accurate? | Correct position |
|---|---|---|
| "Binding means final — no court can ever touch it." | No | section 23 bars ordinary suits, not constitutional writ review |
| "Binding means advisory — authorities can simply ignore it." | No | section 19(7) makes it legally binding, enforceable through compliance mechanisms and, ultimately, contempt-style consequences for non-compliance where applicable |
| "Binding, yet constitutionally reviewable." | **Yes** | the precise, examinable position |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If a dissatisfied authority can always approach the High Court, doesn't 'binding' become a weak word in practice — effectively just another, slower appeal?"

**Reply:** Writ review is available, but it is not equivalent to another ordinary appeal: High Courts do not routinely reweigh the facts or substitute their own view of the exemption analysis; they intervene on recognised, narrower judicial-review grounds. In the great majority of cases where no such ground exists, the Commission's order simply stands as the final word a public authority must comply with. The qualified residual: writ review is a genuine, constitutionally necessary safety valve against a Commission acting outside its jurisdiction or unfairly, but it is not — and is not meant to be — a routine escape hatch from an otherwise correctly decided disclosure order.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, the "teeth" of a statutory quasi-judicial body and the constitutional limits on ouster/privative clauses generally — a theme that recurs across several Polity institutions.
- **Common trap:** stating binding force without the judicial-review qualification (overclaiming finality), or conversely treating "writ review is possible" as proof the Commission's order has no real force (underclaiming bindingness) — both are scoring errors in opposite directions.
- **Answer-use:** always pair "binding under section 19(7)" with "subject to constitutional judicial review, not ordinary suits under section 23" in the same sentence.
- **Probable framing:** "How binding are the Information Commission's orders?" or an applied question about a dissatisfied authority's available remedy.

### Revision notes

1. Section 19(8) remedy menu: access/disclosure, PIO appointment, publication, record-practice reform, training, annual-report compliance, compensation.
2. Section 20 penalty/rejection remain available alongside the section 19(8) menu.
3. The remedy wheel's systemic power (record-practice reform, training, PIO appointment) is as significant as any single disclosure order.
4. Section 19(7): Commission decisions in second appeal are binding.
5. Section 23: bars ordinary civil suits/proceedings against RTI orders; channels challenge only "by way of an appeal under this Act" for matters the Act itself routes that way.
6. Section 23 cannot oust constitutional writ jurisdiction (Article 226/32) — a privative clause cannot extinguish constitutionally conferred review power.
7. "Binding, yet constitutionally reviewable" is the only fully accurate one-line description.
8. Writ review is narrower than an ordinary appeal — courts intervene for jurisdictional error, perversity or procedural unfairness, not mere disagreement.
9. Compensation (to the complainant) and penalty (on the PIO) both sit within this remedial architecture but serve distinct purposes (sharpened further in Lesson 13).
10. Mnemonic: **"Binding closes the department's door, not the Constitution's.")**

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "A Commission order is binding under section 19(7) and shielded from ordinary civil suits by section 23 — but it has never been, and constitutionally could never be, shielded from the High Courts' and Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction."

### Concept check

**Question:** Can a public authority that disagrees with a Commission's disclosure order simply refuse to comply, treating the order as merely advisory?

**Model answer:** No. Section 19(7) expressly makes the Commission's decision in a second appeal binding; it is not advisory. A dissatisfied authority's only lawful route to resist the order is to challenge it through constitutional judicial review (a writ petition in the appropriate High Court, or the Supreme Court), since section 23 bars ordinary civil suits against RTI orders. Simply ignoring a binding order is not a legally available option.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "subject to judicial review" as equivalent to "not really binding." The two are compatible: an order is binding and enforceable unless and until a constitutional court, exercising its own distinct jurisdiction, sets it aside.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the remedial powers available to the Information Commission in a second appeal, and assess the precise meaning and limits of the binding force of its decisions.

**Model solution:** Section 19(8) equips the Commission with a wide remedial menu in deciding a second appeal: it may require a public authority to provide access to information (in a specified form), appoint a Public Information Officer where none exists, publish categories of information, reform its record-management practices, enhance staff training, ensure annual-report compliance, and compensate the complainant for loss or detriment suffered. Separately, it may impose a section 20 penalty on the defaulting officer or reject the application. This remedial breadth matters because it allows the Commission to address the systemic administrative failure behind a refusal, not merely the single withheld document. Section 19(7) makes the Commission's decision binding, and section 23 bars ordinary civil suits or proceedings from reopening that decision, channelling challenge only through the Act's own appellate mechanism where applicable. However, section 23's bar cannot, and does not, extinguish the constitutional writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226 or the Supreme Court under Article 32/136 — a privative clause in an ordinary statute cannot oust constitutionally conferred judicial review. In practice, this means a Commission order is enforceable and must be complied with unless a competent constitutional court, exercising its narrower review jurisdiction (typically for jurisdictional error, perversity, or breach of natural justice, rather than mere disagreement with the outcome), sets it aside. The accurate formulation is therefore "binding, yet constitutionally reviewable" — neither absolutely final nor merely advisory.

## Lesson 13

📚 Book context: Queried — sections 19(8)(b) and 20(1)-(2) cross-checked against the canonical owners for the exact rupee figures, burden allocation and target of each consequence.
🔍 CA Search: `"RTI Section 20 penalty use statistics under-utilisation 2025 2026"`
📰 CA Found: Commentary on under-use of penalty powers is long-standing and general; no single verifiable statistic is frozen here to avoid stale-figure risk.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

"The Commission can fine the ministry" is a sentence that sounds plausible and is simply wrong. RTI's accountability design separates three distinct consequences, aimed at three distinct targets, for three distinct reasons — confusing any two of them is a near-guaranteed loss of marks.

### The three tools, precisely

**1. Compensation (section 19(8)(b))** — a **public-authority**-level remedy. In deciding an appeal, the Commission may require the public authority to **compensate the complainant** for any loss or other detriment suffered as a consequence of the authority's conduct. This is **restorative**: it repairs the applicant's injury caused by wrongful RTI administration (for example, costs or losses suffered because of an unreasonable, prolonged denial).

**2. Monetary penalty (section 20(1))** — a **personal, PIO-level** consequence. Where the Commission finds that the **CPIO/SPIO**, **without any reasonable cause**, refused to receive an application, did not furnish information within the time specified, **malafide** denied the request, knowingly gave **incorrect, incomplete or misleading** information, destroyed requested information, or obstructed furnishing in any manner, the Commission **shall impose a penalty of Rs 250 per day** until the application is received or the information is furnished, **subject to a maximum of Rs 25,000**. The PIO must be given a **reasonable opportunity of being heard** before the penalty is imposed, and the **burden of proving reasonable and diligent conduct lies on the PIO**.

**3. Disciplinary recommendation (section 20(2))** — a **service-discipline** consequence for **persistent default**. Where the Commission is satisfied that a PIO has, without reasonable cause and persistently, failed to act as required, it **recommends disciplinary action** against that officer under the service rules applicable — but the Commission itself does **not** act as the disciplinary authority; the competent service authority carries that process forward.

```text
TARGET AND PURPOSE, SIDE BY SIDE
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
LOSS TO THE APPLICANT          -> compensation, paid by the PUBLIC AUTHORITY
LISTED PIO DEFAULT             -> personal monetary PENALTY on the PIO
                                   (Rs 250/day, capped at Rs 25,000)
PERSISTENT PIO DEFAULT         -> DISCIPLINARY RECOMMENDATION,
                                   carried forward by the service authority
```

### The penalty gate, step by step

```text
LISTED DEFAULT (refusal / delay / malafide denial /
                false-incomplete-misleading info /
                destruction / obstruction)
          +
NO REASONABLE CAUSE
          +
OPPORTUNITY OF HEARING GIVEN TO THE PIO
          +
PIO FAILS TO DISCHARGE THE REASONABLE-DILIGENCE BURDEN
          =
Rs 250 PER DAY, MAXIMUM Rs 25,000
```

Each element of the gate is a genuine precondition, not a formality: a delay caused by a demonstrably reasonable circumstance (say, a sudden, documented systems failure) does not automatically attract the penalty; the PIO must still be heard; and it is the **PIO**, not the Commission, who carries the burden of showing their conduct was reasonable and diligent — a deliberate mirror of the onus-reversal already seen in the appeal context (Lesson 5).

### Example and its limit

A PIO deliberately gives an applicant an incomplete file, omitting a page that would have been inconvenient to disclose. The Commission, on finding this was done **knowingly**, imposes the maximum Rs 25,000 personal penalty and separately recommends disciplinary action if this is part of a documented pattern of persistent defaults by the same officer. **Limit of the example:** the penalty targets *this specific PIO personally*; it does not fine the ministry as an institution, and it does not, by itself, compensate the applicant for any loss suffered — that would require a separate section 19(8)(b) compensation direction against the public authority, decided on its own facts.

### Comparison: the three-consequence matrix

| Consequence | Target | Purpose | Statutory basis | Cap/mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Compensation | public authority | repair applicant's loss/detriment | section 19(8)(b) | no fixed statutory cap; tied to actual loss |
| Monetary penalty | PIO, personally | deter listed access defaults | section 20(1) | Rs 250/day, max Rs 25,000 |
| Disciplinary recommendation | PIO, via service authority | address persistent default | section 20(2) | carried forward under applicable service rules |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "A maximum penalty of Rs 25,000 on an individual officer seems too small to meaningfully deter a well-resourced department from simply tolerating occasional non-compliance."

**Reply:** This is a fair and frequently raised critique, and it should not be dismissed. The penalty's deterrent value depends less on the rupee cap (which is genuinely modest, especially against inflation since 2005) and more on **how consistently and visibly** it is actually imposed — a personal, reputationally significant finding against a named officer, even at a capped amount, can still meaningfully alter institutional behaviour if applied reliably. The qualified residual: the statutory design is sound in principle (personal, reasoned, hearing-based, burden-reversed), but its real-world deterrent strength depends heavily on *implementation* — consistent use by Commissions — which is a persistent, legitimate implementation critique rather than a drafting flaw in the Act itself (developed further in Lesson 18).

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, accountability tools of a statutory body — frequently tested through a "match the consequence to its target" style question.
- **Common trap:** writing "the Commission fines the ministry" (wrong target), or treating compensation and penalty as interchangeable or cumulative in every case (wrong — they answer different questions and may or may not both arise on the same facts).
- **Answer-use:** always name the target explicitly — "personal penalty on the PIO" versus "compensation paid by the public authority" — to show precise command of the provision.
- **Probable framing:** "Distinguish compensation, penalty and disciplinary action under the RTI Act" or an applied fact-pattern question.

### Revision notes

1. Compensation (s. 19(8)(b)): public authority pays the complainant for loss/detriment — restorative.
2. Monetary penalty (s. 20(1)): personal, on the CPIO/SPIO — Rs 250/day, capped at Rs 25,000 — deterrent.
3. Disciplinary recommendation (s. 20(2)): for persistent default, carried forward by the competent service authority — disciplinary.
4. The PIO bears the burden of proving reasonable, diligent conduct once a listed default is alleged.
5. A hearing opportunity for the PIO is mandatory before penalty.
6. Listed penalty-triggering defaults: unreasonable refusal to receive the application; delay beyond section 7(1); malafide denial; knowingly incorrect/incomplete/misleading information; destruction of requested information; obstruction of furnishing.
7. The three consequences can arise together or separately, depending on the facts — they are not mutually exclusive, but they are also not automatically cumulative.
8. The Commission recommends, but does not itself conduct, the disciplinary process under section 20(2).
9. The statutory cap (Rs 25,000) is modest and unrevised since 2005 — a frequently cited implementation-weakness point, distinct from any drafting defect.
10. Mnemonic: **"Authority repairs, officer pays, service disciplines."**

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "RTI separates restorative, deterrent and disciplinary consequences deliberately — the public authority repairs the applicant's loss, the PIO personally pays the penalty, and only the service authority, on the Commission's recommendation, disciplines the officer."

### Concept check

**Question:** An applicant suffers a financial loss because a PIO's unjustified three-month delay forced her to miss a tender deadline. Can the Commission simply impose a personal Rs 20,000 penalty on the PIO and treat that as also compensating her loss?

**Model answer:** No. The personal penalty under section 20(1) and compensation under section 19(8)(b) serve different purposes and have different payers: the penalty punishes the PIO personally and is paid to the government, not the applicant, while compensation for the applicant's actual loss or detriment must be separately directed against the public authority under section 19(8)(b). The Commission would need to consider both remedies independently on the facts, not treat the personal penalty as satisfying the applicant's compensation claim.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming a personal penalty on the PIO automatically compensates the applicant. The penalty is paid as a statutory fine, not transferred to the applicant; compensation is a separate, distinctly targeted remedy against the public authority.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how compensation, monetary penalty and disciplinary recommendation operate as three distinct accountability tools under the RTI Act.

**Model solution:** The RTI Act separates restorative, deterrent and disciplinary consequences. Under section 19(8)(b), the Commission may direct the public authority to compensate a complainant for loss or detriment from wrongful RTI administration — a restorative remedy. Under section 20(1), it may impose a personal penalty of Rs 250 per day, capped at Rs 25,000, on the CPIO/SPIO for listed defaults without reasonable cause — refusal, delay, malafide denial, knowingly false/incomplete information, destruction, or obstruction — after a hearing, with the burden of proving reasonable, diligent conduct on the PIO. Under section 20(2), persistent default triggers a disciplinary recommendation, carried forward by the competent service authority, not the Commission. These tools cannot be merged: compensation repairs the applicant, penalty deters the officer personally, and discipline addresses repeated misconduct through the service hierarchy.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Compensation correctly targeted at the public authority — 3; penalty correctly targeted at the PIO personally, with figures — 3; disciplinary recommendation correctly described as service-authority-led — 2; explicit statement that the three are distinct, non-interchangeable tools — 1; coherent within 150 words — 1.

Those exemptions — and the public-interest override that can still defeat them — are the next lesson.

## Lesson 14

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The learner's problem

Disclosure is the Act's default rule, but no transparency regime can be absolute — national security, ongoing investigations, and genuinely confidential commercial or personal relationships all deserve some protection. The hard question is never "should any exemption exist," but "how do you stop 'confidential' from becoming a magic word that defeats disclosure by mere assertion?"

### The doctrine — Section 8(1)'s nine clauses

Section 8(1) lists protected categories, each requiring **genuine harm analysis**, not a bare label:

| Clause | Protected interest |
|---|---|
| (a) | sovereignty, integrity, security, strategic/scientific/economic interests, foreign relations, or incitement to an offence |
| (b) | information expressly forbidden by a court/tribunal, or whose disclosure would constitute contempt |
| (c) | information that would cause a breach of parliamentary/State-legislature privilege |
| (d) | commercial confidence, trade secrets or intellectual property, where disclosure would harm a third party's competitive position (subject to a public-interest override) |
| (e) | information available to a person in a **fiduciary relationship**, unless disclosure is in larger public interest |
| (f) | information received in confidence from a foreign government |
| (g) | information that would endanger the life or physical safety of any person, or identify a confidential source of assistance for law enforcement/security purposes |
| (h) | information that would impede the **investigation, apprehension or prosecution** of offenders |
| (i) | Cabinet papers, including records of deliberations of the Council of Ministers, Secretaries and other officers (subject to time-based disclosure once the decision is complete) |
| (j) | personal-information clause — taught separately and in full at Lesson 16, since its text itself is currently contested law |

**Section 8(2)** is the Act's single most powerful pro-transparency lever: it permits access **notwithstanding anything in the Official Secrets Act, 1923 and any of the section 8(1) exemptions**, where the **public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests**. This is the **public-interest override**.

**Section 8(3)** creates a **twenty-year rule**: information exempt under several clauses nonetheless becomes disclosable after twenty years from the date of the relevant occurrence, **except** continuing protection under clauses (a), (c) and (i).

**Section 9** separately permits rejection where access would involve an **infringement of copyright subsisting in a person other than the State** — a distinct, narrower ground from section 8.

**Section 10 — severability:** where a record contains both exempt and non-exempt material, and the non-exempt part can be **reasonably severed**, access must be given to that severable, non-exempt part, with the exempt portion redacted and reasons recorded.

```text
EXEMPTION-ANALYSIS LADDER
--------------------------------
WHICH CLAUSE IS INVOKED?
         |
WHAT PROTECTED INTEREST, AND WHAT GENUINE HARM
FROM THIS SPECIFIC DISCLOSURE?
         |
CAN THE NON-EXEMPT PART BE SEVERED (section 10)?
         |
DOES SECTION 8(2) PUBLIC INTEREST OUTWEIGH THE HARM?
         |
REASONED DECISION, WITH RECORDED JUSTIFICATION
```

### *RBI v. Jayantilal N. Mistry* (2015) — regulators cannot hide behind a generic fiduciary label

The Reserve Bank of India resisted disclosing inspection reports and related information about banks it regulates, arguing a **fiduciary relationship** with those banks under section 8(1)(e). The Supreme Court rejected this as a blanket shield: a **regulator acting in the public interest**, overseeing regulated entities under statutory powers, is not automatically in a "fiduciary relationship" with those entities merely because it holds sensitive information about them. The RBI's own mandate is to protect depositors and the public interest, not the regulated banks' commercial reputations — so it could not invoke section 8(1)(e) as a generic, across-the-board secrecy claim; any exemption still had to be justified on its own specific facts.

```text
"WE HOLD SENSITIVE INFORMATION ABOUT A REGULATED ENTITY"
                        |
          DOES THIS, BY ITSELF, CREATE A FIDUCIARY RELATIONSHIP?
                        |
         NO — Jayantilal Mistry: regulatory oversight in the
              public interest is not automatically "fiduciary";
              the label must be justified on the actual facts,
              not asserted as a blanket shield
```

### Example and its limit

A bank regulator refuses to disclose an inspection report on a struggling bank, asserting "fiduciary relationship" without further explanation. Applying *Jayantilal Mistry*, the correct analysis asks: does the regulator's relationship with the bank actually have the characteristics of a fiduciary relationship (trust reposed for the *bank's* benefit), or is the regulator simply performing a public oversight function in which depositors' and the public's interests, not the bank's, are paramount? **Limit of the example:** *Jayantilal Mistry* does not abolish section 8(1)(e) or make every regulator's record automatically public — a genuine fiduciary relationship (for instance, a bank's duty of confidence to an individual depositor's account details) remains protectable; the case targets the *regulator-regulated* relationship specifically, not fiduciary claims generally.

### Comparison: label versus test

| Claim as asserted | Correct analytical question |
|---|---|
| "This is confidential, full stop." | What specific harm does disclosure cause, under which named clause? |
| "We have a fiduciary relationship with the regulated entity." | Is this genuinely a relationship of trust for the *entity's* benefit, or public-interest oversight for depositors/the public? (*Jayantilal Mistry*) |
| "Part of this file is sensitive." | Can the sensitive part be severed (section 10), disclosing the rest? |
| "This is commercially confidential." | Does section 8(2)'s public interest outweigh the competitive harm claimed? |

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** "If regulators cannot claim a blanket fiduciary shield, doesn't this risk discouraging regulated entities from being candid with their regulators, fearing eventual public disclosure?"

**Reply:** This is a real institutional-design tension the judgment had to weigh, and it should not be waved away. But the Court's answer was not "no confidentiality is ever possible" — it was that the *label* "fiduciary" cannot be invoked mechanically; a regulator can still justify withholding specific material on the facts, under the appropriate clause (for instance, genuine commercial confidence under clause (d), weighed against section 8(2)), if the harm is real and demonstrated. The qualified residual: the judgment disciplines *lazy, blanket* secrecy claims by public regulators without eliminating the possibility of a genuinely justified, fact-specific exemption — candour with a regulator is protected by the Act's harm-and-override analysis, not by an automatic fiduciary label.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, exemption architecture and regulator transparency — a recurring theme connecting RTI to financial/economic governance questions.
- **Common trap:** treating "confidential" or "fiduciary" as self-executing exemptions rather than claims requiring harm analysis and (for fiduciary claims specifically) the *Jayantilal Mistry* test.
- **Answer-use:** any exemption-analysis answer should move through the full ladder (clause → harm → severability → public-interest override) rather than stopping at "the clause was invoked, so it is exempt."
- **Probable framing:** "Discuss the exemptions under Section 8 of the RTI Act" or an applied regulator-transparency fact pattern.

1. Section 8(1) lists nine protected-interest clauses (a)-(j); clause (j) is taught separately in Lesson 16 due to its currently contested text.
2. Section 8(2): the public-interest override — disclosure permitted notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act and section 8(1), where public interest outweighs harm.
3. Section 8(3): twenty-year disclosure rule, except continuing protection under clauses (a), (c) and (i).
4. Section 9: copyright-infringement ground — distinct from, and narrower than, section 8.
5. Section 10: severability — disclose the reasonably severable non-exempt part even where another part is exempt.
6. *RBI v. Jayantilal N. Mistry* (2015): a regulator cannot invoke a blanket "fiduciary relationship" claim to shield information about entities it regulates in the public interest.

8. "Confidential" and "fiduciary" are claims to be tested, not self-executing exemptions.
9. The twenty-year rule has an exception set — not every exemption expires automatically; (a), (c) and (i) can continue to apply.
10. Mnemonic: **"Name the clause, prove the harm, try to sever, then weigh the public interest."**

> 🔑 **Answer line:** "Section 8 exemptions are not magic words — a regulator cannot shield information about the entities it regulates merely by invoking 'fiduciary relationship,' as *Jayantilal Mistry* makes clear; every claim must survive a harm, severability and public-interest analysis."

### Concept check

**Question:** A telecom regulator refuses to disclose inspection data on a regulated company, citing section 8(1)(e)'s fiduciary exemption without further explanation. Is this sufficient under *RBI v. Jayantilal Mistry*?

**Model answer:** No. *Jayantilal Mistry* holds that a regulator exercising statutory oversight in the public interest is not automatically in a fiduciary relationship with the entities it regulates merely because it holds sensitive information about them. The regulator must justify the exemption on the actual facts — showing a genuine relationship of trust for the regulated entity's benefit, or an alternative applicable clause with demonstrated harm — rather than invoking "fiduciary relationship" as a blanket, self-executing shield.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming any regulator-regulated relationship automatically qualifies as "fiduciary" under section 8(1)(e). The relationship's purpose (public oversight versus genuine private trust) determines whether the label actually applies.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the exemption architecture of Section 8 of the RTI Act, and discuss, with reference to case law, why "confidentiality" claims by regulators require close scrutiny.

**Model solution:** Section 8(1) lists nine categories of protected interest — security and sovereignty, court-forbidden material, legislative privilege, commercial confidence, fiduciary relationships, foreign-government confidences, life/safety and confidential sources, investigation/prosecution interests, and Cabinet papers — each requiring genuine harm analysis rather than bare assertion. Section 8(2), the public-interest override, permits disclosure notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act and any section 8(1) clause where the public interest in disclosure outweighs the protected harm; section 8(3) adds a twenty-year disclosure rule, subject to continuing protection for security, privilege and Cabinet-paper categories. Section 10 requires severance of non-exempt material even within an otherwise exempt record. This architecture is deliberately calibrated rather than absolute, and nowhere is careful scrutiny more necessary than where a regulator invokes a "fiduciary relationship" to withhold information about entities it regulates. In RBI v. Jayantilal N. Mistry (2015), the Supreme Court rejected precisely this manoeuvre: the Reserve Bank's statutory relationship with banks it supervises exists for the public interest and depositor protection, not as a private trust for the banks' benefit, so it could not claim a blanket fiduciary exemption under section 8(1)(e) to withhold inspection reports. The judgment shows that labels like "confidential" or "fiduciary" are claims to be tested against the specific facts and the harm actually threatened, not self-executing exemptions — a principle that applies equally to any public authority tempted to assert a protected category without demonstrating genuine, specific harm.

### The learner's problem

You request a contract document that names a private vendor's pricing structure as confidential business information. The vendor is not the public authority you asked, and was never a party to your request — yet the decision directly affects them. Does the Act let them have any say at all? And separately: certain government bodies are listed as entirely outside the Act's reach — does that exclusion ever bend?

### Section 11 — third-party procedure (not an exemption)

**Section 11** applies when a PIO **intends to disclose** information that relates to, or was supplied by, a third party, and that the third party has treated as **confidential**. The procedure:

1. the PIO must give the third party **written notice within five days** of receiving the request, inviting representations;
2. the third party has **ten days** from receipt of that notice to make representations;
3. except for **trade or commercial secrets protected by law**, disclosure **may** still be allowed where the **public interest in disclosure outweighs** any possible harm or injury to the third party;
4. the PIO must take a decision **within forty days** of the original request (accounting for the notice-and-representation process), and must communicate the decision, including appeal rights, to the third party.

```text
REQUEST RECEIVED
      |
NOTICE TO THE THIRD PARTY WITHIN 5 DAYS
      |
THIRD-PARTY REPRESENTATION WINDOW: 10 DAYS
      |
PIO BALANCES THE LAW, THE HARM AND THE PUBLIC INTEREST
      |
DECISION WITHIN 40 DAYS OF THE ORIGINAL REQUEST
      |
THIRD PARTY IS ALSO INFORMED OF APPEAL RIGHTS
```

**The critical classification point:** section 11 is **procedure, not an independent exemption**. It does not, by itself, block disclosure; it only guarantees the third party a chance to be *heard* before the PIO (applying the genuine section 8 exemptions, where applicable) decides. The third party is not given an **absolute veto** — the PIO (and, on appeal, the First Appellate Authority or Commission) still makes the final, reasoned decision, which may favour disclosure even over the third party's objection where public interest genuinely outweighs the claimed harm.

| Proposition | Correct? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Section 11 itself exempts information | No | it is a consultation procedure, not an exemption |
| The third party must ordinarily be heard where applicable | Yes | the statutory representation opportunity is real |
| The third party has an absolute veto | No | the PIO/appellate authority decides on the merits |
| Public interest may still support disclosure | Yes | the express statutory balancing survives the third party's objection |

### Section 24 — security and intelligence organisations, and the corruption/human-rights provisos

**Section 24(1)** excludes the RTI Act's application to intelligence and security organisations **specified in the Second Schedule** (Central-level bodies such as certain intelligence and specialised security agencies), and to information furnished by such organisations to the government — **subject to a crucial exception**: information relating to allegations of **corruption and human-rights violations** is **not** excluded.

For human-rights-violation information specifically, disclosure is permitted only **with the approval of the Central Information Commission**, and must be provided within **forty-five days** from the date of the request (a longer, specially calibrated clock reflecting the sensitivity involved). States may similarly notify their own security organisations for an equivalent State-level exclusion, with the SIC playing the analogous approval role.

**Amendment mechanism:** the appropriate government may add to or remove organisations from the relevant Schedule by Gazette notification, subject to the notification being laid before Parliament/the State Legislature.

```text
LISTED INTELLIGENCE / SECURITY ORGANISATION
                   |
           GENERAL EXCLUSION FROM THE ACT
                   |
        +----------+-----------+
        |                      |
  CORRUPTION ALLEGATION   HUMAN-RIGHTS-VIOLATION ALLEGATION
  carve-out: NOT excluded  carve-out: NOT excluded, BUT
                            requires Commission approval +
                            45-day clock
```

### Section 22 — the overriding effect

**Section 22** gives the RTI Act an **overriding effect**: its provisions apply **notwithstanding anything inconsistent** contained in the **Official Secrets Act, 1923**, and notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law, or any instrument having effect by virtue of any law other than the RTI Act itself.

**The precise limit of "overriding":** section 22 resolves *inconsistency with other laws* in RTI's favour; it does **not** mean the RTI Act overrides **its own internal exemption scheme**. Sections 8, 9 and 24 remain fully operative *within* the RTI Act's own framework — section 22 is a tool against competing *external* secrecy laws, not a tool that dissolves the Act's *internal* exemptions.

```text
EXTERNAL LAW/INSTRUMENT INCONSISTENT WITH RTI
                  |
             SECTION 22
                  |
          RTI ACT PREVAILS OVER THAT EXTERNAL INCONSISTENCY
                  |
   but RTI's OWN internal ss. 8, 9 and 24 still fully apply
```

### Example and its limit

### The historical clause (before 13 November 2025)

Before its substitution, section 8(1)(j) exempted: **"information which relates to personal information the disclosure of which has no relationship to any public activity or interest, or which would cause unwarranted invasion of the privacy of the individual, unless the Central Public Information Officer or the State Public Information Officer or the appellate authority, as the case may be, is satisfied that the larger public interest justifies the disclosure of such information."** It also carried a **proviso**: information that could not be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature could not be denied to any person either.

This historical clause built a **multi-factor test**: (i) does the information relate to public activity or interest; (ii) would disclosure cause an *unwarranted* invasion of privacy; (iii) even if so, does a larger public interest justify disclosure anyway; and (iv) is there a Parliament/Legislature-parity floor below which privacy could never be used to deny information.

### The current clause (from 13 November 2025)

**The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, section 44(3)** **substituted** section 8(1)(j) with new text. The **Department of Personnel and Training's own officially consolidated RTI Act (updated as on 18 November 2025)** now records clause (j) simply as: **"information which relates to personal information."**

**Commencement:** the **Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology's notification G.S.R. 843(E), dated 13 November 2025**, expressly commenced DPDP Act section 44(3) on the date of that notification's publication — **13 November 2025**. This is a staged Act: most of the DPDP Act's substantive data-protection duties and individual rights (sections 3-17 and related provisions) are separately staged for commencement around **13 May 2027**; section 44(3)'s RTI amendment was deliberately placed in the **first, earliest** commencement tranche.

```text
OLD CLAUSE (j), historical                    CURRENT CLAUSE (j), from 13 Nov 2025
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
"relates to personal information"              "relates to personal information"
   AND no relationship to public activity          (this qualifying test REMOVED)
   AND/OR unwarranted invasion of privacy           (this qualifying test REMOVED)
   UNLESS larger public interest justifies           (clause-specific override REMOVED)
   Parliament/Legislature-parity proviso              (proviso REMOVED)
```

**What this means in plain language:** the earlier clause required a PIO to actually *test* whether personal information touched public activity, whether any invasion of privacy was "unwarranted," and whether a larger public interest justified disclosure anyway, before withholding it. The current clause's bare text no longer carries these qualifying tests at the clause level — it reads as a broader, simpler exemption for anything "relating to personal information."

### The learner's problem

A statutory clause can be rewritten by Parliament in a single amendment, as Lesson 16 just showed. But is the *underlying constitutional method* for weighing transparency against privacy equally fragile — does it vanish the moment the clause it once interpreted is replaced? Or does a constitutional methodology outlive the specific statutory text it was first applied to?

### The case and its holding

***CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal* (2019)**, decided by a **Constitution Bench**, addressed whether the **office of the Chief Justice of India** is a "public authority" under the RTI Act, and, if so, how far its records (including judges' asset declarations and certain correspondence) could be disclosed.

**Holding, precisely:** the Supreme Court held that the **office of the CJI is indeed a "public authority"** within the meaning of section 2(h) — it does not enjoy a blanket immunity from the Act merely because of its position at the apex of the judiciary. At the same time, the Court held that disclosure from that office remains **subject to the Act's own balancing framework** — at the time, the then-current section 8(1)(j) test (public-activity relevance, unwarranted invasion of privacy, and the larger-public-interest override) — applied through a structured **proportionality** exercise that weighs **transparency**, **privacy**, and **judicial independence** together, rather than mechanically preferring any one value.

```text
IS THE CJI'S OFFICE A "PUBLIC AUTHORITY"?
                  |
                 YES — no blanket immunity
                  |
DOES THAT MEAN EVERY RECORD IS AUTOMATICALLY DISCLOSABLE?
                  |
                 NO — disclosure still passes through
                      the Act's balancing framework,
                      applied with judicial-independence
                      sensitivity
```

### The two-level analysis this case teaches

```text
LEVEL 1: THE STATUTE
   whichever version of section 8(1)(j) and section 8(2) is
   currently in force (Lesson 16 — text can change by amendment)

LEVEL 2: THE CONSTITUTION
   Article 19(1)(a) — the information interest
           versus
   Article 21 — the privacy interest
           reconciled through PROPORTIONALITY
   (a judicial method, not a single clause's wording)
```

**Why the distinction matters today, precisely:** *Subhash Chandra Agarwal*'s **holding on public-authority status** (the CJI's office is covered) is settled, durable constitutional law, entirely untouched by the 2025 clause amendment. Its **proportionality method** (how to weigh transparency against privacy generally) also survives as a constitutional analytical tool. What does **not** automatically survive unchanged is the *specific statutory test* the judgment was applying in 2019 — because that specific clause-level text has since been replaced (Lesson 16). **A precise answer separates these two layers**: the constitutional method endures; the statutory clause it was interpreting has moved on.

### The proportionality steps, made explicit

| Step | Question asked |
|---|---|
| Legitimate interest | what privacy or transparency interest is genuinely at stake? |
| Rational connection | does withholding (or disclosing) the information actually serve that interest? |
| Necessity | is a less restrictive step available — for instance, severability/redaction under section 10 — instead of blanket withholding or blanket disclosure? |
| Balancing | does the accountability value of disclosure justify the residual privacy cost, or vice versa? |
| Safeguards | are reasons recorded, is a hearing given, and does appeal/judicial review remain open? |

### Example and its limit

A request seeks a judge's personal asset declaration. Applying the two-level method: *first*, confirm the office holding the record is a public authority (settled by *Subhash Chandra Agarwal* — yes); *second*, apply the **current** statutory clause (Lesson 16's broadened personal-information exemption) as the threshold test; *third*, if the current clause's threshold is met, test **section 8(2)**'s public-interest override using the proportionality steps above — does the public's interest in judicial accountability (for example, verifying no undisclosed conflict of interest) outweigh the residual privacy cost of disclosing specific asset figures? **Limit of the example:** the outcome of this specific balancing exercise is genuinely fact-sensitive and cannot be predicted in the abstract — the lesson's teaching point is the *method* (two levels, proportionality steps), not a guaranteed result for any particular asset-disclosure request.

### Comparison: holding versus method versus clause

| Element of *Subhash Chandra Agarwal* | Status today |
|---|---|
| CJI's office is a "public authority" | settled, durable — unaffected by any later statutory amendment |
| Proportionality as the constitutional method for privacy-versus-transparency | durable judicial technique — continues to be the right analytical frame |
| The *specific* then-current section 8(1)(j) text the Court was applying | **superseded** by the 2025 DPDP amendment (Lesson 16) — do not quote the old clause as current law |

**Objection:** "If the specific clause the judgment applied has been replaced, doesn't that make the 2019 judgment's reasoning largely obsolete for current exam answers?"

**Reply:** The objection correctly flags a real risk — a careless answer that quotes the Court's 2019 application of the *old* clause text as though it were still the operative statutory rule would indeed be factually wrong today. ### *Kishan Chand Jain v. Union of India* (2023) — when the Commission's own functioning becomes the access problem

A petition under **Article 32** raised a problem distinct from every doctrine taught so far: **State Information Commissions ordinarily sit in a single location (typically the State capital) and conduct largely physical hearings.** For an appellant or complainant from a remote district, actually appearing in person could mean a disproportionately expensive, time-consuming journey — turning a statutory right designed to be accessible into one that, in practice, priced out exactly the citizens least able to absorb that cost.

**Decided 9 October 2023** [2023 LiveLaw (SC) 899], the Supreme Court directed that **State Information Commissions must offer hybrid hearings** — giving appellants and complainants the choice of attending **physically or virtually** — and must establish and maintain **functional digital portals** enabling online filing of complaints and second appeals, tracking of case status, and access to cause lists, daily orders and annual reports. The Court set an implementation deadline of **31 December 2023** and directed State Governments to provide the necessary financial and technical support. The judgment grounded this reform in **Articles 14, 19(1)(a) and 21** — treating accessible, technology-enabled adjudication as a **constitutional, not merely administrative, imperative** for an institution whose entire purpose is to deliver information access.

```text
SIC SITS ONLY IN THE STATE CAPITAL, PHYSICAL HEARINGS ONLY
                        |
REMOTE APPELLANT FACES DISPROPORTIONATE TRAVEL COST/TIME
                        |
Kishan Chand Jain (2023): HYBRID HEARINGS MANDATED
                        +
FUNCTIONAL DIGITAL PORTALS MANDATED
(e-filing, case-status tracking, cause lists, orders, annual reports)
                        |
ACCESS TO THE COMMISSION ITSELF BECOMES PART OF
THE RIGHT TO INFORMATION'S PRACTICAL GUARANTEE
```

**Why this case belongs at the synthesis, not earlier:** every previous lesson assumed a citizen *could* actually reach the Commission once a request reached the appeal stage. *Kishan Chand Jain* shows that assumption itself was not always true — and that modernising the Commission's own *functioning* (not its powers, not its independence from government, but its physical/technological accessibility) is itself a distinct, judicially recognised reform dimension.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks test understanding **across** lessons, after the relevant doctrine has already been taught locally. Each follows the same answer-free format as the lesson-local concept checks, but is deliberately placed here, after the full lesson sequence, rather than inside any single lesson chunk.

### Cross-lesson check 1 (spans Lessons 5, 6, 11 and 12)

**Question:** A citizen's PIO decision is silence past the 30-day clock. Trace, naming the exact provisions, which forum she should approach first, what power that forum exercises, and how "binding" that forum's final word actually is.

**Model answer:** Silence past the clock is "deemed refusal" (sections 7(2)/7(6)), which is treated as an appealable decision. She should file a **first appeal** under section 19(1) to a senior officer within the same authority (Lesson 5), and if unresolved, a **second appeal** under section 19(3) to the CIC/SIC (also Lesson 5) — not a section 18 complaint, since her problem is a denial/non-decision on the merits, not an access-system obstruction (Lesson 6, *State of Manipur*). At second appeal, the PIO bears the onus of justifying the denial (section 19(5)); if she alternatively needed the Commission's civil-court-style fact-finding powers (summons, discovery, affidavits — Lesson 11), that toolkit applies specifically to a section 18 inquiry, not to an ordinary second appeal. The Commission's second-appeal decision is binding under section 19(7), supported by the section 19(8) remedy menu, but remains subject to constitutional judicial review under Article 226/32, never to an ordinary civil suit (section 23) (Lesson 12).

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "appeal," "complaint" and "civil-court powers" as interchangeable labels for "going to the Commission." Each routes through a different provision, with a different trigger, toolkit and remedy.

### Cross-lesson check 2 (spans Lessons 7, 8, 9 and 10)

**Question:** List, in one continuous argument, every independence safeguard the RTI Act provides for a Commissioner's appointment and tenure, and every gap that survives despite those safeguards.

**Model answer:** Appointment is committee-based, with a guaranteed opposition seat (Lok Sabha/Assembly LoP or the deemed leader of the largest opposition group), chaired by the PM/CM, alongside a nominated minister (Lessons 7-8) — a genuine, if numerically executive-leaning, check. Eligibility requires multidisciplinary eminence, and disqualification bars legislature membership, office of profit, party connection, business or profession (Lesson 8). Removal requires a Supreme Court inquiry for proved misbehaviour/incapacity, with only a narrow set of direct grounds bypassing that inquiry (Lesson 10). Yet two gaps survive all of this: first, the 2019 Amendment moved tenure and pay from the Act itself to Central Rules, creating a structural-dependence concern regardless of how well the appointment and removal safeguards otherwise function (Lesson 9); second, even perfect removal protection cannot cure a government simply delaying the filling of vacant seats, the precise gap *Anjali Bhardwaj* addressed (Lesson 10).

**Misconception to avoid:** Concluding that strong appointment and removal safeguards alone prove the Commission is independent. Tenure/pay source (Act versus Rules) and appointment timeliness are separate, equally real axes of vulnerability.

### Cross-lesson check 3 (spans Lessons 2, 14, 15 and 16)

**Question:** A request seeks personal financial details about a private individual, held by a public authority that received the individual's data from a third party. Identify every gate this request must pass, in the correct order.

**Model answer:** First, confirm the holding body is actually a "public authority" under section 2(h) — applying *Thalappalam*'s control/finance test if the body's status is contested (Lesson 2). Second, confirm the material is "information" in the existing-record sense, not a demand for a newly created explanation (Lesson 2, *CBSE*). Third, if the material was supplied by or relates to a third party and is treated as confidential, follow the section 11 consultation procedure — notice within five days, representation within ten, decision within forty — remembering this is a hearing right, not a veto (Lesson 15). Fourth, apply the currently operative section 8(1)(j) personal-information exemption at the threshold, recognising its 2025 broadening (Lesson 16). Fifth, even if the clause-level exemption is engaged, test section 8(2)'s surviving public-interest override, using the proportionality method *Subhash Chandra Agarwal* supplies at the constitutional level (Lesson 17), and apply section 10 severability wherever only part of the record is sensitive (Lesson 14).

**Misconception to avoid:** Jumping straight to "it's personal information, so it's exempt" without first confirming public-authority/information-scope gates, without running the third-party procedure where applicable, and without testing the surviving public-interest override.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These three questions are additional to, and distinct from, the eighteen lesson-local Mains questions. They test synthesis across the whole topic rather than any single lesson.

### Synthesis Mains — 10 marks, strict ceiling 150 words

**Question:** Trace the complete journey of a Right to Information request from filing to a binding Commission decision, naming the key officers, timelines and remedies at each stage.

**Model solution:** A citizen files a request with a CPIO/SPIO, in writing or electronic form, without needing to state reasons; filing through an Assistant PIO adds five days to the computation. The PIO must decide within 30 days (48 hours for life-or-liberty information) or transfer a misdirected request within five days; missing the deadline is deemed refusal, and delayed information must then be free. An aggrieved applicant files a first appeal to a senior departmental officer, ordinarily within 30 days, decided within 30-45 days. If unresolved, a second appeal lies to the CIC/SIC within 90 days, where the PIO bears the burden of justifying any denial. The Commission's decision is binding, supported by remedies including disclosure, record-management reform, compensation and personal PIO penalty, subject only to constitutional judicial review, not ordinary civil suits.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** Officer roles (PIO/APIO, FAA, CIC/SIC) correctly sequenced — 3; all key clocks (5/30/48-hour/45/90 days) accurately stated — 3; deemed refusal and onus-reversal mentioned — 2; binding force and its judicial-review boundary stated — 1; coherent within 150 words — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 15 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** Compare the Central Information Commission and the State Information Commission with the National/State Human Rights Commissions on composition, removal and the character of their output. What does this comparison reveal about the design logic of statutory oversight bodies in India?

**Model solution:** Both CIC/SIC and NHRC/SHRC are statutory, not constitutional, bodies — the former under the RTI Act, 2005, the latter under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 — and both use committee-based appointment with opposition participation. Their outputs, however, diverge sharply: CIC/SIC decisions in second appeal are binding under section 19(7), backed by civil-court inquiry powers, a personal PIO penalty of up to Rs 25,000, and systemic compliance directions under section 19(8); NHRC/SHRC, by contrast, ordinarily issue recommendations and reports rather than binding orders. Removal reveals a further, easily confused asymmetry: SIC members are both appointed and removed by the Governor, a fully symmetric design, whereas SHRC members, though Governor-appointed, can be removed only by the President — an asymmetric design that must never be conflated with the SIC's rule. This comparison reveals a deliberate design logic: Parliament calibrates an oversight body's binding force to the nature of the harm it addresses — RTI's access-and-disclosure remedy was built to be directly enforceable against a specific department's specific refusal, while human-rights oversight was built around investigation, persuasion and public reporting rather than binding adjudication. Neither design is inherently superior; each reflects a judgment about what kind of institutional "teeth" the underlying right most needs. For RTI, this explains why its independence debates (Lessons 9-10, 18) focus on tenure, pay and staffing rather than on whether its orders bind at all — that much, uniquely among Indian oversight bodies, was never in doubt.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** Statutory (not constitutional) status for both correctly stated — 2; binding-versus-recommendatory contrast accurately drawn — 4; SIC-versus-SHRC removal asymmetry correctly and precisely stated — 4; design-logic argument (teeth calibrated to remedy type) articulated — 4; coherent 250-word structure — 1.

### Synthesis Mains — 20 marks, strict ceiling 250 words

**Question:** "The Right to Information Act gives citizens a legally powerful remedy, but its real-world strength depends on institutions and practices the statute cannot fully guarantee." Critically evaluate this statement, covering independence, access and the current privacy debate.

**Model solution:** The Act's legal architecture is genuinely strong: a reversed onus on the PIO in appeal, binding second-appeal decisions with systemic compliance powers, civil-court evidence-gathering powers in complaint inquiries, and a personal, deterrent penalty regime — none of this is in serious doubt. Yet three documented gaps show that statutory strength alone cannot guarantee outcomes. First, independence: the RTI (Amendment) Act, 2019 moved Commissioners' tenure and pay from Parliament's Act to Central Rules, creating a structural dependence on the very executive the Commission reviews, even though appointment, age ceiling and protected removal survived unchanged — precisely the tension the 2020 GS-II Mains question tested. Second, functioning: formal powers mean little if seats stay vacant (Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India, 2019, on appointment delay) or if citizens cannot physically or technologically reach a hearing (Kishan Chand Jain v. Union of India, 2023, mandating hybrid hearings and digital portals for State Commissions). Third, scope: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023's amendment to Section 8(1)(j), commenced 13 November 2025, has broadened the personal-information exemption at the clause level, shifting more analytical weight onto the surviving Section 8(2) public-interest override — a change currently under constitutional challenge, with the Supreme Court having declined an interim stay and referred the matter to a larger bench, with no final disposition yet recorded. Together, these show that RTI's statutory teeth are necessary but not sufficient; sustained institutional capacity, accessible functioning and a careful, evolving privacy-transparency balance are equally indispensable to the right's practical delivery.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** Legal strengths accurately summarised — 4; 2019 Amendment/independence gap correctly explained and linked to the 2020 PYQ — 4; *Anjali Bhardwaj* and *Kishan Chand Jain* both correctly cited for their distinct axes — 6; DPDP/privacy current status accurately and cautiously stated (commencement date, surviving override, pending litigation, no final outcome asserted) — 5; coherent, non-fabricated 250-word structure — 1.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

### A. Identity and constitutional anchor

CIC/SIC are statutory, quasi-judicial bodies under the RTI Act, 2005 — never constitutional bodies. The right they enforce has a constitutional root in Article 19(1)(a), recognised in *Raj Narain* (1975) and *S.P. Gupta* (1981); the Act codifies, rather than creates, this right.

### B. Four core definitions

Information (section 2(f)): material in any form, broadly defined. Record (section 2(i)): a format sub-category. Right to information (section 2(j)): inspection, copies, extracts, samples, electronic access — a remedy, not an abstract entitlement. Public authority (section 2(h)): constitutional/statutory/notified bodies, plus owned/controlled/substantially-financed bodies, plus substantially-financed NGOs — with *Thalappalam* (2013) supplying the precise control/finance discrimination test, and *CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay* (2011) separately supplying the existing-record/no-creation-duty test.

### C. Section 4 — the prevention layer

Cataloguing, computerisation, a detailed publication list (organisation, powers, procedure, norms, budgets, subsidies, remuneration, PIOs), policy-fact disclosure, reasons for decisions, and "maximum suo motu disclosure" as a continuing endeavour to minimise resort to individual requests.

### D. The access route and its clocks

PIO decides; APIO forwards only (+5 days). Transfer within 5 days; ordinary decision within 30 days; life/liberty within 48 hours; deemed refusal on missed deadlines, with free information thereafter. Fee: Rs 10 under the Central Rules, subject to State variation and a BPL exemption.

### E. Appeals and complaint

First appeal: senior officer, same authority, 30/30-45 days. Second appeal: CIC/SIC, ordinarily 90 days, PIO bears the onus, decision binding under section 19(7), reviewable only by constitutional writ (not ordinary suits, per section 23). Section 18 complaint addresses access-system obstruction, not an automatic disclosure substitute (*State of Manipur*, 2011).

### F. CIC and SIC institutional design

CIC: Chief + up to 10 ICs, President appoints on a PM + Lok Sabha LoP + Union Minister committee. SIC: State Chief + up to 10 SICs, Governor appoints on a CM + Assembly LoP + State Minister committee. Both share eligibility (multidisciplinary eminence) and disqualification (no legislature seat, office of profit, party connection, business, profession) rules. **SIC removal is by the Governor — symmetric, unlike the SHRC's President-only removal.**

### G. The 2019 Amendment and removal architecture

Pre-2019: fixed 5-year term and Election-Commission/Chief-Secretary salary parity, in the Act itself. Post-2019: tenure/pay "as prescribed" by Central Rules — currently 3 years, fixed rupee pay figures, with Central residuary and relaxation control. Removal: President (Centre)/Governor (State), via Supreme Court inquiry for proved misbehaviour/incapacity, or direct grounds (insolvency, moral-turpitude conviction, paid outside work, infirmity, prejudicial interest) without that inquiry. *Anjali Bhardwaj* (2019) separately addresses prolonged vacancy/appointment delay.

### H. Powers and their limits

Section 18 inquiry: civil-court evidence-gathering toolkit (summons, oath, discovery, affidavits, requisition, commissions), bounded by "power is not identity" — no conviction, no tort-style damages, no adjudication of the underlying service/contractual dispute, no striking down a law. Section 19 appeal: binding decision, section 19(8) remedy wheel (access, PIO appointment, publication, record reform, training, compensation), bounded by constitutional judicial review surviving section 23's bar on ordinary suits.

### I. Accountability tools

Compensation (public authority pays the applicant, section 19(8)(b)) — restorative. Personal penalty (PIO pays, Rs 250/day up to Rs 25,000, section 20(1)) — deterrent, burden on the PIO. Disciplinary recommendation (service authority acts, section 20(2)) — disciplinary, for persistent default.

### J. Exemptions, severability and third parties

Section 8(1): nine protected-interest clauses, each requiring genuine harm analysis. Section 8(2): the public-interest override, surviving every clause including the amended (j). Section 8(3): twenty-year rule, with continuing exceptions for (a), (c), (i). Section 9: copyright ground. Section 10: severability. Section 11: third-party consultation procedure — a hearing right, never a veto. *Jayantilal Mistry* (2015): a regulator cannot invoke a blanket fiduciary shield over entities it regulates in the public interest.

### K. Security bodies and the overriding effect

Section 24: excludes listed intelligence/security organisations generally, but preserves corruption and (Commission-approved, 45-day) human-rights-violation information. Section 22: overrides inconsistent external laws (including the Official Secrets Act, 1923), never the Act's own internal sections 8, 9 and 24.

### L. Privacy — historical, current and constitutional

Historical section 8(1)(j): public-activity/unwarranted-invasion tests plus a larger-public-interest override and a Parliament-parity proviso. Current section 8(1)(j), from the DPDP Act, 2023's section 44(3), commenced 13 November 2025: a simpler, broader "relates to personal information" text, with the qualifying tests removed from the clause itself — though section 8(2)'s general override survives untouched. As independently verified today, the amendment is under constitutional challenge (notice issued 16 February 2026, no interim stay, referred to a larger bench, hearing activity through at least 7 August 2026, no final ruling located) — teach the operative text as current law without asserting any particular outcome. *Subhash Chandra Agarwal* (2019): the CJI's office is a public authority, and proportionality (legitimate interest, rational connection, necessity, balancing, safeguards) remains the durable constitutional method for weighing transparency against privacy, surviving the clause-level amendment even though its specific then-current clause does not.

### M. Independence, access and reform

Independence spans appointment, tenure/pay, removal, staffing and decisional power — no single axis is the whole story. *Anjali Bhardwaj* (2019) targets vacancy/appointment delay; *Kishan Chand Jain* (2023) targets physical/technological hearing accessibility, mandating hybrid hearings and digital portals for State Commissions by 31 December 2023, grounded in Articles 14, 19(1)(a) and 21. Graded verdict: RTI is legally strong, not toothless; its practical weaknesses are capacity- and practice-constrained, and reform should target implementation — statutory tenure restoration, transparent advance selection, sustained access infrastructure, published compliance data — rather than rewriting the Act's core powers.
