# Union and Territory — Live Session Edition

India's map is constitutionally flexible, but the Union itself is not optional. This session
builds that proposition from the exact text of Articles 1–4, then follows the legal machinery
through reorganisation, cession, territorial history, renaming and federal consequences.

### Visual — Article 1's two territorial expressions

```text
ARTICLE 1 — TERRITORIAL STRUCTURE

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     TERRITORY OF INDIA                       │
│                                                              │
│  ┌────────────────────┐  ┌────────────────┐  ┌─────────────┐ │
│  │ Territories of     │  │ Union          │  │ Acquired    │ │
│  │ the States         │  │ Territories    │  │ territories │ │
│  └──────────┬─────────┘  └────────────────┘  └─────────────┘ │
└─────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
              │
              ▼
      ┌────────────────────┐
      │   UNION OF INDIA   │
      │   = States only    │
      └────────────────────┘

TERRITORY OF INDIA = States + Union Territories + acquired territories
UNION OF INDIA     = States only
```

The visual prevents the first major error: **Union of India** and **territory of India** are
not interchangeable. States are constituent units of the federal arrangement. Union
Territories and acquired territories belong to India, but they are not States merely because
they lie within Indian territory.

### 1. Begin with the constitutional sentence

Article 1(1) states:

> **India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.**

The provision answers two questions at once:

1. **What is the country's constitutional name?** India, that is Bharat.
2. **What is the form of its territorial association?** A Union of States.

The combined name was a Constituent Assembly settlement between members preferring the modern
international name “India” and those preferring the civilisational name “Bharat”. Article 1
does not create two countries or give governments a choice between two constitutional
identities.

### 2. Why “Union” rather than “Federation”?

B.R. Ambedkar explained the significance through two propositions:

```text
Indian federation was not formed by a compact among sovereign States
                              +
Constituent States possess no constitutional right to secede
                              ↓
                    “Union of States”
```

This does **not** mean that India lacks federal features. Legislative, administrative and
financial powers are constitutionally distributed between the Union and the States. “Union”
instead establishes that the federation is not dissoluble at the will of a constituent unit.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** India is federal in the distribution of constitutional power, but
> “Union” denies that its States created the federation by compact or may leave it by choice.

The phrase later supports the description **“an indestructible Union of destructible States.”**
The Union cannot be dismantled by State secession, while Parliament can constitutionally alter
a State's area, boundary, name or even continued territorial form.

### 3. Article 1's three territorial categories

Article 1(3) says that the territory of India comprises:

| Category | Meaning | Constitutional significance |
|---|---|---|
| Territories of the States | Areas belonging to the States listed in the First Schedule | Constituent units sharing federal power |
| Union Territories | Territories specified in the First Schedule | Centrally administered under the Part VIII framework, with variations |
| Territories that may be acquired | Territory India may lawfully acquire | Allows territorial expansion without pretending every acquired area is immediately a State |

The **First Schedule** identifies the States and Union Territories and describes their
territorial extent. It is therefore a legal map, not merely a list of names.

### 4. Acquired territory is a category, not an automatic form of government

As a sovereign State, India may acquire territory through modes recognised by international
law, including cession by agreement. Standard classifications also include purchase, gift,
lease, plebiscite, occupation of territory not under a recognised ruler, conquest and
subjugation as historical modes. These are descriptive legal categories, not a claim that
forceful acquisition is a normal contemporary policy.

Acquisition answers **how territory enters Indian sovereignty**. It does not by itself decide
whether that territory will be:

- administered directly;
- constituted as a Union Territory;
- merged with a State; or
- admitted or established as a State.

That second question requires legislation and the appropriate constitutional route.

### 5. The First Schedule and differentiated arrangements

States form one constitutional class, but uniform membership does not eliminate all
differentiation. Part XXI provisions for certain States and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules for
specified areas create forms of asymmetric accommodation. Those arrangements do not change the
Article 1 classification: a State remains a State, even when special safeguards apply.

The Part XXI State-specific group comprises Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur,
Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa and Karnataka. Their detailed
safeguards belong to the Special Provisions topic; here the list shows that differentiated
treatment does not create a fourth Article 1 territorial category.

Likewise, a Union Territory may have an elected legislature without thereby becoming a State.
Administration under Articles 239–241 belongs to the separate Union Territories topic; here the
important point is categorical.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** If Parliament can later change States, calling India a federal Union is empty.

**Reply:** Federalism concerns the constitutional distribution of power while a State exists;
Article 3 concerns the territorial identity of the units themselves. The Constitution protects
State governmental spheres, especially through the Seventh Schedule and judicial review, but
does not guarantee any particular State's boundaries forever.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Union of India versus territory of India; three Article 1 categories; First
  Schedule.
- **Mains:** Use Article 1 as the opening to answers on territorial reorganisation or
  Union-weighted federalism.
- **Trap:** “Union” does not mean a unitary Constitution, and “territory of India” is not limited
  to States.

### Revision notes

1. Article 1 deals with the name and territorial character of the polity.
2. “India, that is Bharat” is one constitutional name expressed through two formulations.
3. India is described as a Union, not as a compact-created federation.
4. States possess no constitutional right to secede.
5. The territory of India has three categories.
6. Union of India is narrower than territory of India.
7. The First Schedule identifies States, UTs and their territorial extent.
8. Acquired territory does not automatically become a State.
9. Federal distribution and territorial alterability address different constitutional questions.
10. Article 1 supplies the premise for Articles 2–4.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a territory belong to India without being a constituent State of the Union?

**Model answer:** Article 1 distinguishes the wider “territory of India” from the narrower Union of States. Union Territories and acquired territories fall within Indian sovereignty, but Statehood depends on the constitutional and legislative status assigned to them. Territorial belonging therefore does not automatically confer the federal position of a State.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat every part of Indian territory as a State or assume that a Union Territory with a legislature has thereby entered the State category.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain the constitutional significance of describing India as a “Union of
States”.

**Full model:**

Article 1 describes “India, that is Bharat” as a Union of States. The expression has both
integrative and federal significance. According to Ambedkar's explanation, the Indian
federation was not created by an agreement among sovereign States, and no State possesses a
right to secede. The Union is therefore indestructible.

This does not make India purely unitary. The Constitution distributes legislative,
administrative and financial power between the Union and States, and federalism has judicial
protection. Yet Article 3 permits Parliament to form States and alter their areas, boundaries
or names without requiring their consent. The Constitution protects State governmental
authority but not the permanence of a particular territorial outline.

The expression also differs from “territory of India”, which includes States, Union
Territories and acquired territories. Thus, “Union” combines federal power-sharing with
national indissolubility: India is a federation whose constituent map remains constitutionally
adaptable.

### Visual — choose the constitutional doorway

```text
                         PROPOSED TERRITORIAL CHANGE
                                    │
                 ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
                 │                                     │
    Admit or establish a new State          Rework territory already within India
                 │                                     │
             ARTICLE 2                               ARTICLE 3
                 │                                     │
      terms and conditions fixed by         form / enlarge / diminish /
               Parliament                    alter boundary / alter name
```

This is a functional decision tree. The Constitution's text should be read first; the familiar
“outside versus inside” formula is a helpful doctrinal shorthand, not language printed in
Article 2.

### 1. The exact power in Article 2

Article 2 provides:

> Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and
> conditions as it thinks fit.

It contains two verbs:

| Verb | Standard explanation |
|---|---|
| Admit | Bring into the Union a State already existing as a political entity |
| Establish | Create a new State that did not previously exist in that form |

The standard textbook and judicial understanding associates Article 2 with a State not formed
merely by internally rearranging existing Indian units. However, the article itself does not
say “foreign”, “outside the existing map” or “acquired territory”. An exam-safe answer should
separate constitutional text from doctrinal explanation.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Article 2 speaks in the language of entry or establishment; Article
> 3 speaks in the language of internal territorial surgery.

### 2. Article 3 begins where Article 2 ends

Article 3 concerns the formation of new States and alterations to existing territorial units
within India. It is the ordinary machinery for:

- separating territory from a State;
- uniting States or parts of States;
- uniting territory to part of a State;
- changing area;
- changing boundaries; and
- changing names.

The distinction is therefore not “new State versus old State” in ordinary speech. A State newly
created by dividing an existing State is legally an **Article 3 formation**, despite being new
on the political map.

### 3. Why acquisition is not identical to admission

Three ideas must remain separate:

```text
ACQUISITION        = India obtains sovereignty over territory
ADMISSION          = an existing State enters the Union under Article 2
ESTABLISHMENT      = Parliament establishes a new State under Article 2
INTERNAL FORMATION = Parliament redraws Indian units under Article 3
```

Territory may first be acquired and then receive an administrative status through legislation.
Puducherry, Goa, Daman and Diu, and Dadra and Nagar Haveli illustrate that acquisition and
constitutional incorporation can occur in stages.

### 4. Sikkim shows why simplistic formulas fail

Sikkim's constitutional journey used more than one device:

1. It had a protectorate relationship with India after 1947.
2. The Thirty-fifth Amendment, 1974 created an experimental “associate State” status through
   former Article 2A and the former Tenth Schedule.
3. A 1975 referendum favoured ending the Chogyal institution and full integration.
4. The Thirty-sixth Amendment, 1975 made Sikkim a full State, amended the First and Fourth
   Schedules, inserted Article 371F and repealed Article 2A and the old Tenth Schedule.

This was not a routine Article 3 bifurcation of an existing State. It belongs with admission and
constitutional integration, while its special institutional settlement requires constitutional
amendment.

### 5. Comparison with Article 3 procedure

| Feature | Article 2 | Article 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Core function | Admit or establish new States | Internally reorganise existing Indian units |
| Express State-reference proviso | No equivalent proviso in Article 2 | Yes, when a State's area, boundary or name is affected |
| Terms | Parliament determines terms and conditions | Parliament chooses among enumerated territorial changes |
| Article 4 support | Yes | Yes |

Article 4 applies to laws under both Articles 2 and 3. It permits required Schedule changes and
incidental consequences without deeming the law an Article 368 amendment.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** The outside/inside formula is so common that it may be written as the literal
meaning of Article 2.

**Reply:** It is a useful standard explanation, but constitutional accuracy requires saying
that Article 2 itself authorises admission or establishment and that the beyond-existing-map
idea is doctrinal interpretation. This small qualification distinguishes precise answers from
memorised slogans.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** A newly created State carved from an existing State is Article 3, not Article 2.
- **Mains:** Use the distinction before discussing acquisition, Sikkim or internal
  reorganisation.
- **Trap:** Do not equate acquisition of territory with immediate Statehood.

### Revision notes

1. Article 2 uses “admit” and “establish”.
2. Parliament fixes the terms and conditions under Article 2.
3. Admission conventionally concerns an already existing political entity.
4. Establishment conventionally concerns a State not previously existing in that form.
5. The “outside” formula is doctrinal shorthand, not the article's literal wording.
6. Article 3 controls internal territorial readjustment.
7. A politically new State may still be an Article 3 State.
8. Acquisition concerns sovereignty; admission concerns constitutional membership.
9. Sikkim followed an exceptional constitutional sequence.
10. Article 4 supports laws under both Articles 2 and 3.

### Concept check

**Question:** A new State is created by separating districts from an existing Indian State. Is Article 2 attracted merely because the resulting State is “new”?

**Model answer:** No. The legal question is not whether the political unit is new in ordinary language but whether it is formed by internal readjustment of Indian territory. Separation of territory from an existing State falls under Article 3. Article 2 concerns admission or establishment on a different constitutional footing.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use the everyday meaning of “new State” to replace the functional distinction between Articles 2 and 3.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish the constitutional purposes of Articles 2 and 3.

**Full model:**

Articles 2 and 3 give Parliament different territorial powers. Article 2 permits admission or
establishment of new States on terms Parliament determines. Standard doctrine associates it
with entry or establishment beyond internal rearrangement, although the Constitution does not
literally use “outside India”.

Article 3 is the machinery of internal reorganisation. Parliament may form a State by
separation or union, increase or diminish a State's area, alter boundaries, or alter a name. A
new State carved from an existing State is therefore created under Article 3, not Article 2.

The distinction also separates acquisition from status. India may acquire territory, but
legislation determines whether it becomes a UT, joins a State or receives Statehood. Article 4
authorises necessary Schedule and consequential changes outside Article 368. Thus, Article 2
governs admission or establishment; Article 3 governs internal reconstruction.

### Visual — the five-spoke territorial wheel

```text
                              ARTICLE 3
                                  │
       ┌──────────────┬───────────┼───────────┬──────────────┐
       │              │           │           │              │
   FORM A STATE   INCREASE AREA  DIMINISH   ALTER         ALTER
                                  AREA      BOUNDARY       NAME
       │
 separation / union of States or parts / territory joined to a State
```

Article 3 is not a vague general power. It is an enumerated set of territorial operations.
Learning the separate verbs helps solve close-option questions and prevents cession from being
smuggled into “diminish”.

### 1. Form a new State

Parliament may form a new State by:

- separating territory from any State;
- uniting two or more States;
- uniting parts of States; or
- uniting any territory to a part of a State.

Explanation II adds that the formation power includes forming a new State or Union Territory
by uniting a part of a State or Union Territory to another State or Union Territory.

The Constitution (Eighteenth Amendment) Act, 1966 inserted the two Explanations. This is a
useful Prelims fact, but the operative distinction between the clauses and proviso is more
important than the amendment number.

This language makes Article 3 adaptable. It can create a State, a Union Territory or a composite
arrangement through internal territorial combination.

### 2. Increase or diminish a State's area

These clauses change the quantity of territory assigned to a State. They may operate alongside
formation or boundary alteration, but they are conceptually distinct:

```text
AREA question     → How much territory belongs to the State?
BOUNDARY question → Where does the legal line run?
```

A transfer of territory from one Indian State to another diminishes one and increases the
other. It remains internal reorganisation because Indian sovereignty continues over the land.

### 3. Alter boundaries

Boundary alteration changes the legal frontier between units. It may involve a small strip of
land without creating a new State. The constitutional concern is the line separating units,
not necessarily the total existence of either unit.

This distinction becomes important in external boundary settlements. Article 3 can alter
internal boundaries; it does not authorise surrender of Indian sovereignty to another country.

### 4. Alter the name of a State

Article 3 expressly includes alteration of a State's name. Name changes such as Madras to Tamil
Nadu, Mysore to Karnataka, Uttaranchal to Uttarakhand and Orissa to Odisha were made through
parliamentary legislation.

Renaming is legally significant because the State is named in the First Schedule and numerous
laws may require adaptation. Yet a name change does not create a new State or change the
constitutional character of Statehood.

### 5. “State” includes a Union Territory — but not everywhere

Explanation I draws a careful distinction:

| Part of Article 3 | Meaning of “State” |
|---|---|
| Clauses (a)–(e) | Includes a Union Territory |
| Proviso requiring reference for views | Does **not** include a Union Territory |

Therefore, Parliament's substantive reorganisation power extends to Union Territories, but the
constitutional requirement to obtain the views of a legislature applies to an affected State,
not to a Union Territory. A UT legislature has no Article 3 consultation entitlement merely
because it exists.

### 6. What Article 3 does not itself settle

Article 3 tells us what Parliament may do, but several questions require other rules:

- **How is the Bill introduced?** The proviso supplies the procedure.
- **How are Schedules and representation updated?** Article 4 supplies the answer.
- **Can territory be ceded abroad?** *Berubari* says Article 3 is insufficient.
- **How is a Union Territory administered after formation?** Part VIII and the relevant statute
  answer that question.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** If “diminish area” is unlimited, Parliament may transfer territory abroad.

**Reply:** Constitutional terms are read within the structure of sovereignty. Article 3 is an
internal reorganisation provision. The Supreme Court's *Berubari* opinion drew the line:
diminution within India is not cession from India.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Memorise all five verbs and both Explanations.
- **Mains:** Show that Article 3 can change territorial identity but is not a foreign-relations
  cession power.
- **Trap:** In clauses (a)–(e), State includes UT; in the proviso it does not.

### Revision notes

1. Article 3 contains five express powers.
2. Formation may occur through separation or several forms of union.
3. Explanation II expands the formation combinations involving States and UTs.
4. Increase and diminution concern quantity of State territory.
5. Boundary alteration concerns the location of the legal line.
6. Name alteration does not create a new State.
7. Internal transfer preserves Indian sovereignty.
8. “State” includes UT in clauses (a)–(e).
9. “State” excludes UT in the consultation proviso.
10. Article 3 does not authorise foreign cession.
11. Administration after formation belongs to other constitutional/statutory provisions.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Explanation I allow Parliament to reorganise a Union Territory but not require referral of the proposal to its legislature?

**Model answer:** Explanation I deliberately gives “State” an inclusive meaning in Article 3's substantive clauses and a narrower meaning in the proviso. Parliament may therefore form or alter Union Territories, but the special consultation requirement protects affected States only. A UT legislature, where one exists, does not receive a constitutional veto or mandatory-reference right under Article 3.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not carry the inclusive definition of “State” mechanically from clauses (a)–(e) into the proviso.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Examine the scope of Parliament's substantive powers under Article 3.

**Full model:**

Article 3 gives Parliament five internal territorial powers. It may form a State through
separation or union; increase or diminish area; alter boundaries; and alter a name.
Explanation II permits combinations of parts of States and Union Territories.

The provision is flexible but not unlimited. Explanation I includes Union Territories within
“State” for the substantive clauses, while excluding them from the proviso on legislative
consultation. Article 3 also operates within Indian sovereignty. An internal transfer may
diminish one State and enlarge another, but *Berubari* establishes that diminution does not
include cession to a foreign country.

Article 3 does not govern later UT administration or every consequential adjustment; Article 4
and specific statutes perform those tasks. It is broad internal map-making power, not
unrestricted power over sovereignty.

## Lesson 4 — Consultation is mandatory; consent is not

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual — the Article 3 legislative route

```text
Proposal affects a State's area, boundary or name
                         ↓
President sends proposal to the State Legislature
                         ↓
Legislature expresses views within allowed time
                         ↓
Time expires; views may support, oppose or suggest changes
                         ↓
President recommends introduction of the Bill
                         ↓
Bill introduced in either House of Parliament
                         ↓
Parliament may accept, reject or modify the State's views
                         ↓
Ordinary legislative passage + Presidential assent
```

The process gives the affected State a constitutional voice, not a constitutional veto.

### 1. Prior Presidential recommendation

No Article 3 Bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament except on the recommendation
of the President. This is a condition of introduction, not merely a formality to be supplied
after passage.

The President acts within the parliamentary system. The requirement ensures Union-executive
screening of territorial proposals; it does not create an independent personal map-making
power.

### 2. Reference to the affected State Legislature

Where the proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of a State, the President must refer
the proposal to that State Legislature for expressing its views. The reference specifies:

- a period for response; or
- a further period allowed by the President.

The Bill may proceed after that period expires. The Constitution does not require a favourable
resolution.

### 3. Views are not consent

Three propositions must be separated:

| Proposition | Legal position |
|---|---|
| Must the State be heard? | Yes, where the proviso applies |
| Must the State agree? | No |
| Is Parliament bound by the State's suggested terms? | No |

Parliament may enact the change even when the affected Legislature opposes it. The President is
also not bound to accept the views received.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Article 3 constitutionalises consultation but withholds concurrence:
> a State may speak about its territorial future, but it cannot veto Parliament's decision.

### 4. No fresh reference for every parliamentary amendment

The standard rule associated with *Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay* is that a fresh reference
to the State Legislature is not required every time Parliament amends the Bill. Otherwise, the
consultative step could become an endless cycle in which every legislative change triggered a
new State process.

This does not authorise Parliament to replace the referred proposal with an unrelated measure.
It means the State's views are consultative within a parliamentary legislative process rather
than a form of treaty negotiation.

### 5. The Union Territory exception

Because “State” in the proviso excludes a Union Territory, no Article 3 reference is
constitutionally necessary for a UT legislature. Parliament can reorganise a UT even when it
has an elected Assembly.

This reflects the deeper distinction between Statehood and UT self-government. A UT Assembly
derives a limited legislative sphere from the Constitution or statute, while Parliament retains
plenary power over Union Territories.

### 6. Federal criticism

The procedure creates an obvious tension:

```text
FEDERAL VALUE                         UNION-HOLDING VALUE
State identity and democratic voice  National capacity to settle territorial demands
               \                     /
                \                   /
                 Article 3: views, not consent
```

**Criticism:** A Centre-dominated Parliament may alter a State against the wishes of its elected
Legislature, weakening federal trust.

**Reply:** A State veto could immobilise settlement of linguistic, ethnic, administrative or
security demands, especially when an existing State benefits from denying separation.

**Balanced assessment:** The legal power is unilateral, but durable reorganisation normally
requires political consultation beyond the constitutional minimum. Constitutional permission
does not make unilateralism politically wise.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Prior recommendation; State views; specified period; no State consent; no UT
  reference.
- **Mains:** Use consultation-versus-concurrence as evidence of India's Union-weighted
  federalism.
- **Trap:** Do not say the President must obtain State consent before recommending the Bill.

### Revision notes

1. An Article 3 Bill may be introduced in either House.
2. Introduction requires prior Presidential recommendation.
3. An affected State Legislature must receive a reference for views.
4. The President specifies or extends the response period.
5. The process may continue once that period expires.
6. State views are not binding on the President or Parliament.
7. State consent is constitutionally unnecessary.
8. A fresh reference is not required for every parliamentary amendment.
9. UT legislatures have no mandatory-reference right under the proviso.
10. The rule demonstrates both constitutional consultation and structural centralisation.
11. Political legitimacy may demand more consultation than the legal minimum.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State Legislature unanimously rejects a Bill proposing division of the State. What legal effect does that resolution have under Article 3?

**Model answer:** The resolution records the State's constitutionally required views but does not veto the Bill. After the reference period has run, Parliament may proceed and may accept, reject or modify those views. The legal requirement is consultation, not concurrence, although political legitimacy may favour negotiation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not convert a mandatory opportunity to express views into a requirement of ratification.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** “Article 3 gives States a voice over reorganisation but not control over it.”
Discuss the constitutional logic and federal implications of this arrangement.

**Full model:**

Article 3 combines procedural consultation with substantive parliamentary supremacy. A Bill
affecting a State's area, boundary or name requires the President's recommendation. Before that
recommendation, the proposal must be referred to the affected Legislature for views within a
specified or extended period. However, neither the President nor Parliament is bound by those
views, and State consent is unnecessary. *Babulal Parate* also supports the rule that every
parliamentary amendment need not be referred afresh.

The design is Union-weighted. It prevents an existing State from vetoing a legitimate demand
for separation or administrative change. Andhra, Maharashtra–Gujarat, Haryana and later States
could have faced permanent obstruction if the parent unit possessed a constitutional veto.

Yet the same rule creates a federal deficit. A State's territorial identity may be transformed
against the position of its elected Legislature, while a Union Territory need not even be
consulted under the proviso. Legal competence can therefore outpace democratic legitimacy.

The arrangement is best understood as a constitutional minimum. Parliament retains final
authority to preserve national adaptability, but cooperative federalism requires negotiation,
reasoned accommodation and transition safeguards. Article 3 makes reorganisation possible;
political consultation makes it durable.

## Lesson 5 — Article 4: constitutional consequences without Article 368

Progress: 5/11 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Schedule changes, representation and ordinary legislation

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual — one law, several consequences

```text
LAW UNDER ARTICLE 2 OR ARTICLE 3
                 │
      ┌──────────┼───────────┬────────────────────────┐
      │          │           │                        │
 First Schedule  Fourth      Parliamentary / State    Supplemental,
 territorial     Schedule    representation           incidental and
 entries          RS seats    adjustments              consequential rules
      └──────────┴───────────┴────────────────────────┘
                              ↓
      Not deemed an amendment for Article 368 purposes
                              ↓
             Ordinary legislative process / simple majority
```

Article 4 prevents every reorganisation from requiring a separate constitutional-amendment
exercise.

### 1. The First Schedule consequence

The First Schedule records the States, Union Territories and their territorial extent. A law
creating or altering a unit would be incomplete if the constitutional map continued to show the
old position. Article 4 therefore authorises the reorganisation law itself to make the necessary
First Schedule amendments.

### 2. The Fourth Schedule consequence

The Fourth Schedule allocates seats in the Council of States. Territorial change may affect
representation. Article 4 authorises necessary Fourth Schedule amendments so that the political
map and federal chamber do not contradict each other.

### 3. Supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions

Article 4 reaches beyond changing two lists. Parliament may include provisions it considers
necessary for implementing the territorial change, including representation in:

- Parliament; and
- the Legislature or Legislatures of affected States.

Reorganisation statutes may consequently address matters such as:

- allocation of sitting members;
- continuation or adaptation of laws;
- division of assets and liabilities;
- services and administrative cadres;
- courts and institutional jurisdiction;
- authorisation of expenditure; and
- transition dates.

These provisions are not decorative. A border can be redrawn in one sentence, but government
cannot function unless institutions, personnel, law and representation cross the transition.

### 4. Why this is outside Article 368

Article 4(2) expressly declares that no law of this kind shall be deemed an amendment of the
Constitution for the purposes of Article 368.

Therefore:

| Territorial law under Articles 2–4 | Article 368 amendment |
|---|---|
| Ordinary legislative process | Constituent-power procedure |
| Simple majority of members present and voting | Special majority; sometimes State ratification |
| May amend First/Fourth Schedules as necessary | May amend constitutional provisions generally, subject to basic structure |
| No special State-ratification requirement | Ratification required for specified federal provisions |

The Constitution does not literally insert the phrase “simple majority” into Article 4. The
simple-majority result follows because the measure is ordinary legislation and is expressly
excluded from Article 368.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Article 4 converts territorial flexibility into administrative
> workability by allowing one ordinary law to redraw the map, representation and transition
> machinery together.

### 5. Is Article 4 a loophole in the amendment procedure?

**Objection:** Parliament can amend constitutional Schedules by simple majority, so Article 368
is easily bypassed.

**Reply:** Article 4 is a specific constitutional authorisation limited to laws under Articles
2 and 3 and to changes necessary or consequential to those laws. It is not a general power to
rewrite the Constitution under the label of reorganisation.

**Limit:** Foreign cession remains outside this route because it is not an internal Article 3
operation. Nor can incidental provisions become a pretext for unrelated constitutional change.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** First Schedule, Fourth Schedule, representation, ordinary law, outside Article
  368.
- **Mains:** Link flexibility to rapid territorial accommodation and also to State fragility.
- **Trap:** A law changes constitutional text but is still not deemed an Article 368 amendment.

### Revision notes

1. Article 4 supports laws under both Articles 2 and 3.
2. It authorises necessary First Schedule amendments.
3. It authorises necessary Fourth Schedule amendments.
4. It covers representation in Parliament and affected Legislatures.
5. Supplemental provisions complete the statutory scheme.
6. Incidental provisions support the central territorial operation.
7. Consequential provisions manage transition effects.
8. Article 4 laws are not deemed amendments for Article 368.
9. They therefore follow ordinary legislation and simple-majority voting.
10. “Simple majority” is a procedural consequence, not the literal phrase of Article 4.
11. The authority is limited to the Article 2/3 territorial purpose.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can a law alter the First Schedule and still not be a constitutional amendment under Article 368?

**Model answer:** Article 4 itself authorises laws under Articles 2 and 3 to make the Schedule changes necessary for territorial reorganisation and expressly says that such laws are not deemed constitutional amendments for Article 368 purposes. The Constitution therefore creates a specific ordinary-legislation route for this limited class of textual change.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that every statute changing words in a constitutional Schedule must use Article 368.

### Original Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain how Article 4 makes India's territorial Constitution both flexible and
workable.

**Full model:**

Article 4 completes Articles 2 and 3. A reorganisation law may amend the First Schedule to
record the new map and the Fourth Schedule to adjust Rajya Sabha representation. It may also
contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions, including legislative
representation changes.

One statute can therefore manage the boundary and transition: members, laws, assets,
liabilities, services and administration may be adapted.

Article 4(2) further declares that these changes are not deemed amendments for Article 368.
They consequently pass through the ordinary legislative process by simple majority rather than
special majority and State ratification.

The flexibility is bounded. Article 4 supports valid Article 2 or 3 laws; it cannot justify
unrelated alteration or foreign cession.

### Visual — the sovereignty test

```text

      ┌─────────────┴─────────────────┐
      │                               │
```

The decisive question is not whether a map line moves. It is whether territory legally under
Indian sovereignty is being transferred to a foreign sovereign.

### 1. Acquisition and cession point in opposite directions

| Concept | Direction of sovereignty |
|---|---|
| Acquisition | Territory enters Indian sovereignty |
| Cession | Indian territory leaves Indian sovereignty |
| Internal reorganisation | Sovereignty remains Indian; only the internal unit changes |
| Boundary settlement | The parties identify the correct boundary rather than transfer recognised territory |

Article 1 anticipates acquisition by including “such other territories as may be acquired”.
Article 3 governs internal reorganisation. Neither proposition answers the constitutional
requirements for cession.

### 2. The pre-*Berubari* Assam–Bhutan episode, 1951

The **Assam (Alteration of Boundaries) Act, 1951** altered Assam's boundary by ceding a strip of
territory comprised in that State to Bhutan. It is an important chronological caution because
Parliament enacted it before the Supreme Court clarified the constitutional law of foreign
cession in the 1960 *Berubari* opinion.

The 1951 Act must not be used as present authority for the proposition that ordinary Article 3
legislation can cede Indian territory. The later *Berubari* rule supplies the governing
doctrine: acknowledged Indian territory may be transferred to a foreign sovereign only through
constitutional amendment under Article 368. The Assam Act records pre-clarification
parliamentary practice, not an exception to that rule.

### 3. The *Berubari Union* opinion, 1960

The proposed implementation of the 1958 Indo-Pakistan agreement concerning Berubari Union and
enclaves led to a Presidential reference under Article 143.

The Supreme Court concluded that:

1. Article 3's power to diminish a State's area concerns internal reorganisation.
2. It does not include the power to cede Indian territory to a foreign State.
3. Cession requires amendment of the Constitution under Article 368.

The Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960 supplied constitutional authority for the
contemplated territorial transfer by modifying the First Schedule. It is unsafe to say that
every contemplated Berubari transfer was thereby completed. Litigation and political
developments prevented notification of parts relating to former East Pakistan; later
India–Bangladesh arrangements completed the unresolved framework.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** *Berubari* placed sovereignty, not acreage, at the centre of the
> test: Parliament may diminish a State internally by ordinary law, but it may not diminish
> India externally without constitutional amendment.

### 4. *Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India*, 1969

The later ruling distinguished cession from settlement of a boundary dispute. Where an
agreement merely identifies or demarcates the boundary—because the location of the line is
uncertain—it does not necessarily transfer territory already recognised as Indian. Executive
action may therefore implement the settlement without constitutional amendment.

This is not an executive power to disguise cession. The legal character depends on the
substance of the arrangement.

### 5. The India–Bangladesh Land Boundary settlement

The Constitution (100th Amendment) Act, 2015 implemented the 1974 Land Boundary Agreement and
the 2011 Protocol. It modified First Schedule descriptions concerning Assam, West Bengal,
Meghalaya and Tripura.

The arrangement included:

- India transferring **111 enclaves** to Bangladesh;
- Bangladesh transferring **51 enclaves** to India;
- treatment of adverse possessions; and
- demarcation of an undemarcated border stretch.

Because sovereignty over identified territories was exchanged, constitutional amendment was
used consistently with *Berubari*.

### 6. The chronology behind the 100th Amendment

```text
Radcliffe Award, 1947
        ↓
Bagge Award, 1950
        ↓
Nehru–Noon Agreement, 1958
        ↓
Berubari opinion + Ninth Amendment, 1960
        ↓
India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement, 1974
        ↓
2011 Protocol
        ↓
100th Amendment, 2015
```

The long sequence shows why cession questions cannot be reduced to one judgment or amendment.
Diplomacy, precise ground identification and constitutional authority may arise at different
stages.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** If the executive may settle a boundary, the Article 368 protection is illusory.

**Reply:** *Maganbhai* applies when the agreement identifies the boundary rather than surrenders
acknowledged Indian territory. Courts can examine substance over labels. Where sovereignty is
actually transferred, *Berubari* and Article 368 remain applicable.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** Article 3 versus Article 368; Ninth versus 100th Amendment; enclave numbers.
- **Mains:** Use the sovereignty test and explain the constitutional-democratic reason for a
  higher threshold.
- **Trap:** Do not say all international boundary arrangements require amendment, or that
  Article 3 authorises foreign cession.

### Revision notes

1. Acquisition brings territory into India; cession sends Indian territory out.
2. Internal reorganisation preserves Indian sovereignty.
3. The Assam (Alteration of Boundaries) Act, 1951 ceded a strip from Assam to Bhutan before
   *Berubari* clarified the constitutional rule.
4. The 1951 Act is historical practice, not a present exception to *Berubari*.
5. *Berubari* arose from a 1960 Presidential reference.
6. Article 3 diminution does not include foreign cession.
7. Cession requires the Article 368 route.
8. The Ninth Amendment supplied authority for the contemplated transfer.
9. It did not complete every contemplated transfer to former East Pakistan.
10. *Maganbhai* distinguished genuine boundary settlement from cession.
11. A boundary settlement may be implemented by executive action.
12. The 100th Amendment implemented the India–Bangladesh enclave exchange.
13. India transferred 111 enclaves and received 51.
14. The real test is loss of sovereignty, not movement of a line on paper.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why may one international boundary agreement require Article 368 while another may be implemented by executive action?

**Model answer:** The constitutional route depends on substance. If acknowledged Indian territory is transferred to a foreign sovereign, the arrangement is cession and requires amendment under *Berubari*. If the agreement merely identifies or demarcates an uncertain boundary without surrendering recognised Indian territory, *Maganbhai* permits executive implementation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not classify every external boundary adjustment as either automatically executive or automatically a constitutional amendment without applying the sovereignty test.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish internal territorial reorganisation, foreign cession and settlement of
a boundary dispute under the Constitution.

**Full model:**

The three operations may alter maps but have different constitutional characters. Internal
reorganisation under Article 3 redistributes territory among Indian units while sovereignty
remains with India. Parliament may form, enlarge, diminish, rename or alter boundaries through
ordinary legislation supported by Article 4.

Foreign cession transfers territory under Indian sovereignty to another State. In the
*Berubari Union* Presidential reference (1960), the Supreme Court held that Article 3's power
to diminish a State does not include cession. Constitutional amendment under Article 368 is
required. The Assam (Alteration of Boundaries) Act, 1951 is a pre-*Berubari* statutory episode,
not authority against that later rule. The Ninth Amendment supplied authority for the contemplated Indo-Pakistan transfer,
while the 100th Amendment (2015) implemented the India–Bangladesh enclave exchange, under which
India transferred 111 enclaves and received 51.

By contrast, *Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India* (1969) held that executive action
may settle a genuine boundary dispute. Such a settlement identifies the correct line rather
than surrendering acknowledged Indian territory.

The decisive test is sovereignty. Internal redistribution changes the constituent unit;
boundary settlement clarifies the frontier; cession removes territory from the Republic.
Constitutional amendment is reserved for the last because democratic authority must match the
irreversible loss of sovereignty.

The central idea is sequential: **accession, merger, administrative consolidation and
constitutional incorporation were related but distinct processes**.

### 1. The problem created by the lapse of paramountcy

The Indian Independence Act, 1947 ended British paramountcy over princely States. Paramountcy
did not automatically pass to India or Pakistan. Rulers formally faced the options of joining
India, joining Pakistan or attempting independence.

The exact number of princely units varies with the counting date and whether estates,
principalities and administrative categories are included:

| Source convention | Figure |
|---|---:|
| Polity textbook convention | 552 within India's geographical boundaries; 549 joined initially |
| Broader post-Independence history formulation | More than 560 princely States |

These are not safely combined into one supposedly timeless “correct” number. In an answer,
write **“more than 560 princely States”** unless specifically citing the textbook's 552/549
convention.

### 2. The accession instrument

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as Home and States Minister, and V.P. Menon through the States
Department led the integration effort. The initial Instrument of Accession transferred
competence over:

1. Defence;
2. External Affairs; and
3. Communications.

Its narrowness was strategic. These were functions that an isolated princely State could not
credibly secure in the conditions of Partition, yet the limited transfer made immediate
accession politically easier than instant administrative absorption.

### 3. The three famous difficult cases were not identical

| Variable | Junagadh | Hyderabad | Jammu and Kashmir |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ruler's position | Accession to Pakistan | Sought independence | Delayed choice |
| Population/geography issue | Hindu-majority; non-contiguous with Pakistan | Hindu-majority; landlocked within India | Muslim-majority; strategically exposed |
| Trigger | Ruler–population and contiguity conflict | Stalemate and Razakar violence | Tribal invasion from Pakistan in October 1947 |
| Principal route | Plebiscite, February 1948 | Police Action/Operation Polo, September 1948 | Instrument of Accession and military defence |

The table is a comparison engine, not a complete history of each dispute. The constitutional
lesson is that territorial integration used persuasion, legal instruments, popular
consultation and force in different combinations.

### 4. Accession was not the end of integration

Rapid accession addressed sovereignty and basic Union subjects. The second stage involved:

- merger of smaller States;
- creation of unions of States;
- administrative consolidation;
- adaptation of laws and services; and
- democratisation.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** The princes acceded to a Union in 1947; their subjects entered a
> common constitutional Republic in 1950.

This is why a list of signed Instruments cannot substitute for explaining integration.

### 5. The Constitution's fourfold territorial classification

At commencement, the Constitution used four categories:

| Category | Broad composition | Number in the textbook account |
|---|---|---:|
| Part A States | Former Governors' Provinces | 9 |
| Part B States | Former princely States or unions with legislatures | 9 |
| Part C States | Chief Commissioners' Provinces and some former princely units; centrally administered | 10 |
| Part D territory | Andaman and Nicobar Islands | 1 |

The total was 29 units. This classification reflected different routes into the Republic, but
it was transitional. The States Reorganisation Act and Seventh Amendment of 1956 abolished the
Part A/B/C/D structure and replaced it with States and Union Territories.

### 6. What belongs here and what does not

This lesson uses integration only to explain the constitutional territorial starting point.
Detailed political history of popular movements, privy purses, democratisation within each
State and the later Kashmir conflict requires the dedicated history or special-provisions
topics. The present focus is the passage from fragmented sovereignty to Article 1's map.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Integration was simply a top-down achievement of Patel and Menon.

**Reply:** Their statecraft was decisive, but rulers also faced popular movements, insecurity,
economic dependence and the practical impossibility of sovereign isolation. Integration was
negotiated under unequal pressures rather than produced by one instrument alone.

  Princely States.”
- **Answer approach only:** Define the two-stage process; organise administrative and
  socio-cultural problems separately; use differentiated cases; qualify the success narrative.

1. British paramountcy lapsed; it did not transfer automatically.
2. Exact princely-State totals vary by classification and date.
3. “More than 560” is the safest general formulation.
4. Patel and V.P. Menon led the States Department process.
5. Accession initially covered three subjects.
6. Junagadh, Hyderabad and Kashmir presented different problems.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to say that signing Instruments of Accession completed the territorial integration of India?

**Model answer:** Accession initially transferred limited sovereign functions, principally Defence, External Affairs and Communications. Integration also required mergers, administrative consolidation, adaptation of laws and services, democratisation and eventual incorporation into one constitutional Republic. Accession began the process; it did not exhaust it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not collapse legal accession, territorial merger and constitutional incorporation into one event.

### Original Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Explain how the integration of princely States converted legal independence into
territorial and constitutional unity.

**Full model:**

Independence did not automatically create a territorially unified India. British paramountcy
lapsed on 15 August 1947, leaving more than 560 princely States formally outside automatic
successor sovereignty. Patel and V.P. Menon used the States Department and a limited Instrument
of Accession covering Defence, External Affairs and Communications to secure rapid entry into
the Union.

The process succeeded because it was sequenced. Limited accession was offered when isolation
was militarily and economically impractical; merger and administrative consolidation followed
after collective princely resistance had become impossible. The difficult cases required
different methods: a 1948 plebiscite in Junagadh, Operation Polo in Hyderabad, and accession
after the October 1947 tribal invasion in Jammu and Kashmir.

Yet signatures did not complete integration. Smaller units had to be merged, laws and services
adapted, and representative government extended. At constitutional commencement, the Part
A/B/C/D classification still reflected different historical routes. The Constitution
nevertheless placed them within one Republic, while the 1956 settlement later removed the
transitional classification.

Thus, integration joined statecraft to constitutionalism: accession established Union
sovereignty, administrative consolidation made it governable, and the Republic transformed
former princely subjects into equal citizens.

The sequence explains the policy reversal: agitation established the principle before the

### 1. Why the founding leadership initially resisted

Most colonial provinces were products of conquest and administrative convenience rather than
linguistic coherence. The national movement had recognised linguistic organisation, but
Partition made the leadership fear that identity-based territorial claims could endanger unity.

The **Linguistic Provinces Commission, 1948**, chaired by **S.K. Dhar**, recommended
administrative convenience rather than language as the basis of reorganisation. The surname is
also rendered “Dar” in some accounts; **Dhar** is used here.

The Congress then appointed the **JVP Committee**:

- Jawaharlal Nehru;
- Vallabhbhai Patel; and
- Pattabhi Sitaramayya.

It had no chairman and advised against immediate linguistic reorganisation.

### 2. Andhra changed the political calculation

Potti Sriramulu's fast for a Telugu State and his death in December 1952 triggered intense
agitation. Reliable accounts differ over whether the fast is described as 56 or 58 days.
The duration is therefore not used as an examination fact here. The reliable sequence is:

- fast began in October 1952;
- Sriramulu died in December 1952;
- Andhra State was created in October 1953.

Andhra was the **first linguistic State**, created before the States Reorganisation Commission
reported. It was formed by separating Telugu-speaking areas from Madras State.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Andhra did not implement the SRC's recommendation; it created the
> political fact that the SRC was asked to generalise.

### 3. The Fazl Ali Commission

The States Reorganisation Commission was appointed in December 1953:

| Member | Role |
|---|---|
| Fazl Ali | Chair |
| K.M. Panikkar | Member |
| H.N. Kunzru | Member |

It broadly accepted language as a basis but rejected the formula **“one language–one State.”**
Its four major considerations were:

1. preservation and strengthening of national unity and security;
2. linguistic and cultural homogeneity;
3. financial, economic and administrative considerations; and
4. planning and promotion of welfare.

Language was therefore important but not exclusive.

The Commission recommended **16 States and 3 centrally administered territories**. The
Government accepted the framework with minor modifications rather than reproducing every
recommended unit. The enacted settlement therefore produced **14 States and 6 Union
Territories**.

### 4. The settlement of 1956

The States Reorganisation Act, 1956 and the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956:

- abolished the old Part A/B/C/D distinction;
- implemented a modified settlement of **14 States and 6 Union Territories** with effect from
  1 November 1956;
- merged and reorganised multiple former units; and
- established Union Territories as a distinct constitutional-administrative class.

The statutory and constitutional instruments worked together. The Act redrew territory; the
Seventh Amendment altered the wider constitutional architecture made obsolete by the old
classification.

#### Compact merger map of the 1956 settlement

| Territories or units combined/reassigned | Result in 1956 |
|---|---|
| Travancore-Cochin + Malabar district of Madras + Kasargode of South Canara | Kerala |
| Andhra State + Telugu-speaking areas of Hyderabad State | Andhra Pradesh |
| Madhya Bharat + Vindhya Pradesh + Bhopal | Madhya Pradesh |
| Saurashtra + Kutch | Bombay State |
| Coorg | Merged into Mysore State |
| Patiala and East Punjab States Union (PEPSU) | Merged into Punjab |
| Ajmer | Merged into Rajasthan |
| Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands, detached from Madras | New Union Territory |

This map shows why “14 States and 6 UTs” is not enough by itself. The settlement simultaneously
created, enlarged, merged and reclassified units through several distinct territorial
operations.

### 5. Why 1956 was not the final map

The bilingual Bombay State remained contested. The Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960 created:

- Maharashtra; and
- Gujarat, conventionally counted as the fifteenth State.

Punjab was reorganised in 1966:

- Punjabi-speaking areas remained Punjab;
- Hindi-speaking areas formed **Haryana, the seventeenth State**, on 1 November 1966;
- hill areas were joined to the Union Territory of Himachal Pradesh, which became the
  **eighteenth State** on 25 January 1971; and
- Chandigarh became a Union Territory serving as the shared capital.

The settlement followed the Punjabi Suba movement and the Shah Commission's territorial
recommendations. It cannot be reduced to language alone because religious identity, hill-region
administration and the capital question also shaped the result.

These cases reveal that language interacts with capital-city claims, religion, administrative
viability and regional politics.

The Andhra sequence must also remain precise. Andhra State was created in 1953 from
Telugu-speaking areas of Madras, with Kurnool as capital and its High Court at Guntur. In 1956,
Telugu-speaking areas of Hyderabad State were joined to Andhra to create the enlarged Andhra
Pradesh, with Hyderabad as capital. Telangana's separation in 2014 later altered that
settlement.

### 6. Did linguistic reorganisation weaken India?

**Criticism at the time:** Linguistic States might convert cultural identity into separatism.

**Evidence for the reply:**

```text
recognition of language
        ↓
administration, education and politics become more accessible
        ↓
regional elites gain a stake within the federation
        ↓
demand for cultural recognition is separated from demand for sovereignty
        ↓
federal legitimacy strengthens
```

**Residual costs:** linguistic minorities within States, disputed borders and capitals, and
“sons of the soil” politics remained. Reorganisation managed linguistic identity; it did not
eliminate regional inequality or every identity conflict.

nativist “sons of the soil” politics persisted. Language also could not resolve tribal or
backward-region demands. Nevertheless, linguistic reorganisation largely contained rather
than multiplied secession: it made the federal map responsive while keeping national
sovereignty indivisible.

### 1. Territorial acquisition followed by UT incorporation

| Territory | Prior colonial control / transition | Constitutional step |
|---|---|---|
| Dadra and Nagar Haveli | Portuguese rule; liberated in 1954 | Tenth Amendment, 1961 made it a UT |
| Goa, Daman and Diu | Acquired from Portugal in 1961 | Twelfth Amendment, 1962 made one UT; Goa became a State in 1987 |

These examples reinforce Lesson 2: acquisition and constitutional status are distinct steps.
Between liberation and formal UT incorporation, Dadra and Nagar Haveli was administered by an
administrator chosen locally. Puducherry's components are Puducherry, Karaikal, Mahe and Yanam.

### 2. Nagaland and the Northeast

Nagaland was formed from the Naga Hills–Tuensang area and became the sixteenth State on
1 December 1963 under the State of Nagaland Act, 1962. The Thirteenth Amendment, 1962 inserted
Article 371A and supplied special constitutional safeguards. State formation and the special
provision are related but legally distinct instruments.

In 1972:

- Manipur became the nineteenth State;
- Tripura became the twentieth State;
- Meghalaya became the twenty-first State;
- Mizoram became a Union Territory; and
- Arunachal Pradesh, previously NEFA, became a Union Territory.

Meghalaya had first received an autonomous-State or sub-State arrangement within Assam through
the Twenty-second Amendment, 1969. That intermediate device did not satisfy the demand for full
Statehood.

Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh later became the twenty-third and twenty-fourth States in 1987.
Mizoram's transition followed the 1986 Mizoram Peace Accord. Goa became the twenty-fifth State
in the same year.

### 3. Sikkim's exceptional admission sequence

Sikkim became the twenty-second State in 1975 through the Thirty-sixth Amendment after the
short-lived associate-State experiment under the Thirty-fifth Amendment. Its route differs
from carving a State from an existing Indian State and should not be forced into the ordinary
Article 3 narrative.

### 4. The three States of 2000

| State | Parent State | Effective date/year route |
|---|---|---|
| Chhattisgarh, twenty-sixth | Madhya Pradesh | Madhya Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000 |
| Uttarakhand, twenty-seventh | Uttar Pradesh | Uttar Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000; originally named Uttaranchal |
| Jharkhand, twenty-eighth | Bihar | Bihar Reorganisation Act, 2000 |

Their creation reflected administrative, developmental, cultural and regional demands rather
than a single linguistic formula. Smaller-State arguments therefore require evidence on both
governance gains and transition costs.

### 5. Telangana

The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014 created Telangana as the twenty-ninth State by
separating territory from Andhra Pradesh. The example demonstrates Article 3's contemporary
operation and the political importance of regional inequality, identity and resource claims.

The ordinal “twenty-ninth State” describes the historical sequence at creation. India's present
State count is lower because the former State of Jammu and Kashmir was reorganised into two
Union Territories in 2019.

### 2. Delhi's NCT status was more than a rename

The Sixty-ninth Amendment Act, 1991 inserted Articles 239AA and 239AB. Delhi was redesignated

The arrangement became operational in 1992. Therefore:

```text
1991 = constitutional amendment enacted
1992 = NCT legislative arrangement becomes operational
```

Calling this only “Delhi was renamed NCT” hides the institutional change. Delhi remained a
Union Territory with a special constitutional design; it did not become a full State.

### 3. Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, 2019

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, effective 31 October 2019, created:

- **Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir with a Legislature**; and
- **Union Territory of Ladakh without a Legislature**.

Ladakh comprises the Leh and Kargil districts identified by the Act. The 2019 constitutional
orders superseded the 1954 application order, extended the Constitution of India to Jammu and
Kashmir and produced Article 370's present inoperative position. Detailed Article 370 doctrine
belongs to the Special Provisions topic.

The First Schedule State entry for Jammu and Kashmir was deleted, and UT entries were added.
This changed the national count from 29 States and 7 UTs immediately before the reorganisation
to 28 States and 9 UTs after it.

### 6. The single current linkage — J&K's continuing transition

The official MHA page continued to describe J&K as a UT with Legislature and Ladakh as a UT

retained the same statutory arrangement.

1. recorded the Union's assurance that J&K Statehood would be restored;
2. directed that restoration take place “at the earliest and as soon as possible”;
3. upheld the formation of Ladakh as a Union Territory under Article 3(a) read with
   Explanation I; and
4. left open the broader question whether Article 3 permits extinguishing a State's character
   by converting the whole State into one or more Union Territories.

This nuance is essential. The Court did **not** conclusively uphold the full proposition that
Parliament may always extinguish Statehood under Article 3.

```text
       Article 3 + Article 4         Seventh Schedule + courts
                 │                         │

                              │
```

The apparent paradox dissolves once territorial identity and governmental power are separated.

### 1. “Indestructible Union of destructible States”

India is “indestructible” because:

- States did not create it through a sovereign compact;
- States have no right to secede; and
- no State Legislature can dissolve the Union.

States are “destructible” in the limited territorial sense that Parliament may:

- divide or unite them;
- enlarge or diminish them;
- change boundaries;
- change names; or
- create a Union Territory through constitutionally permitted reorganisation.

The phrase is analytical shorthand, not wording found in Article 1.

### 2. Why this remains federal

Territorial alterability does not make State governments Union departments. Federalism rests on
the Constitution's division of legislative and executive authority, constitutional supremacy,
an independent judiciary and bicameral representation. *S.R. Bommai* recognised federalism as
a basic feature.

Therefore two propositions coexist:

| Territorial proposition | Governmental proposition |
|---|---|
| Parliament controls State boundaries | Union and States exercise constitutionally allocated powers |
| State consent is unnecessary under Article 3 | The Union cannot ordinarily exercise all State power |
| A State's map is not guaranteed forever | Federalism remains a basic constitutional feature |

### 3. Comparison with the United States

The standard contrast is:

```text
INDIA: “indestructible Union of destructible States”
USA:   “indestructible Union of indestructible States”
```

The comparison highlights that American federal arrangements generally require State consent
for boundary changes affecting States, whereas India's Article 3 does not. It should not be
used to claim that every American State institution is stronger or that India's system is
non-federal.

### 4. Advantages of territorial flexibility

1. **Accommodation:** Linguistic and regional demands can be absorbed within the Union.
2. **Conflict management:** Peace settlements may receive territorial or Statehood expression.
3. **Administrative reform:** Very large or uneven units may be reorganised.
4. **National continuity:** Territorial adaptation occurs without a secession mechanism.
5. **Democratic responsiveness:** Parliament can answer movements that a parent State opposes.

Linguistic reorganisation is the strongest historical evidence that conceding a State can
strengthen rather than fracture national unity.

### 5. Risks and criticisms

1. **Central dominance:** A State cannot legally protect its own boundaries through a veto.
2. **Partisan use:** A Union majority may frame territorial policy through short-term politics.
3. **Transition costs:** Capital, water, debt, services, assets and representation may remain
   contested.
4. **Minorities within new States:** Creating a majority unit may create new internal
   minorities.
5. **Category downgrade:** State-to-UT conversion raises questions of representative democracy
   and federal equality.

The J&K litigation demonstrates the last risk, but its current facts were already treated in
Lesson 10 and are not repeated as a second current linkage.

### 6. Legal power versus constitutional statesmanship

Article 3 sets a low legal threshold compared with Article 368:

```text
LEGAL SUFFICIENCY
President's recommendation + State views + ordinary parliamentary majority
                              ↓
POLITICAL DURABILITY
negotiation + transition safeguards + fiscal settlement + minority protection
```

The Constitution permits Parliament to proceed without State consent. Constitutional
statesmanship asks whether the change has sufficient democratic legitimacy and a workable
settlement.

> **Answer-grabbing line:** Article 3 makes India's map adaptable; federal prudence determines
> whether that adaptability becomes accommodation or domination.

### 7. Integrated unfamiliar-question method

For any new question, apply five tests:

1. **Category:** State, UT or acquired territory?
2. **Operation:** admission, formation, area, boundary, name, acquisition or cession?
3. **Procedure:** President's recommendation, State views, parliamentary majority?
4. **Consequence:** First/Fourth Schedule, representation, services and transition?
5. **Evaluation:** accommodation, centralisation, sovereignty or federal legitimacy?

This method converts memorised facts into an executable answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can Indian States be territorially “destructible” while federalism remains part of the Constitution's basic structure?

**Model answer:** Territorial destructibility concerns Parliament's power to alter the identity, area or boundaries of constituent units under Article 3. Federalism concerns the constitutional distribution of governmental power between the Union and States while those units exist. Alterable units can therefore exercise protected federal powers; the concepts operate at different levels.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat permanence of boundaries as the only test of federalism or basic-structure protection as a guarantee of every existing State's territorial form.

### Original Mains practice — 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Critically evaluate the proposition that Articles 1–4 create an “indestructible
Union of destructible States”.

**Full model:**

The proposition captures India's combination of national indissolubility and territorial
flexibility. Article 1 calls India a Union of States. The federation was not created by a
compact among sovereign States, and no State has a right to secede; the Union is therefore
indestructible.

States are territorially “destructible” because Article 3 permits Parliament to form units,
increase or diminish areas, alter boundaries and change names. The President must obtain the
affected State Legislature's views, but consent is unnecessary. Article 4 allows consequential
First and Fourth Schedule changes through ordinary legislation outside Article 368.

This flexibility has integrative value. Andhra, the 1956 settlement, Maharashtra–Gujarat,
Northeastern Statehood and the States of 2000 show that identity and development demands can be
accommodated without conceding sovereignty.

However, the design is strongly centralised. A parliamentary majority may alter a State against
its Legislature's position, transition disputes may burden citizens, and complete conversion
of a State into UTs raises deeper federal-democratic concerns. *In re Article 370* upheld
Ladakh's separation but left the broader extinction question open.

The phrase therefore needs qualification. States are not Union departments: constitutional
power distribution and *S.R. Bommai* protect federalism as a basic feature. India's design
guarantees the Union and federal government, but not any State's permanent map. Its legitimacy
depends on using legal flexibility through consultation and accommodation.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks combine lessons without introducing four-option questions.

## Check 1 — Route selection

**Question:** India acquires territory under an international agreement and later wishes to
merge it with an existing State. Identify the separate constitutional questions.

**Model answer:** Acquisition first concerns how sovereignty over the territory enters India.
The territory then falls within Article 1(3)(c). Its later status requires legislation:
admission or establishment may engage Article 2 depending on the political form, while merger
with an existing Indian State is an internal Article 3 operation. Article 4 can make necessary
Schedule and consequential changes. Acquisition itself should not be confused with immediate
Statehood.

## Check 2 — Procedure and consequence

**Question:** Parliament proposes to rename and alter the boundary of a State whose Legislature
opposes the measure. Can the Bill proceed, and what constitutional changes may the final law
contain?

**Model answer:** The proposal must be referred to the State Legislature for views within the
specified or extended period, and introduction requires the President's recommendation. The
State's opposition is not a veto; Parliament may proceed after consultation. Under Article 4,
the law may amend the First and Fourth Schedules and include representation and other
supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions. It follows the ordinary legislative
route rather than Article 368.

## Check 3 — Sovereignty test

**Question:** State the legal test separating Article 3 diminution from foreign cession and a
genuine boundary settlement.

**Model answer:** Article 3 diminution redistributes territory while Indian sovereignty
continues. Foreign cession transfers acknowledged Indian territory to another sovereign and,
under *Berubari*, requires Article 368. A genuine boundary settlement identifies or demarcates
an uncertain frontier rather than surrendering recognised Indian territory; *Maganbhai*
therefore permits executive implementation in that situation.

## Check 4 — Federal synthesis

**Question:** Does the absence of a State veto under Article 3 prove that India is not federal?

**Model answer:** No. It proves a strong Union bias in territorial design. Federalism rests on
the constitutional division of governmental power, supremacy of the Constitution, courts and
other structural features. State boundaries are alterable, but States are not mere Union
departments while they exist. The better conclusion is that India is a Union-weighted
federation with an adaptable internal map.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These final questions are distinct from the lesson-local practice and require whole-topic
synthesis.

## Final 10-marker — 150 words

**Question:** Why are the views of a State Legislature under Article 3 constitutionally
important even though they are not binding?

**Directive decoding:** “Why” requires reasons and constitutional function, not merely the
procedure.

**Model answer:**

Article 3 requires a proposal affecting a State's area, boundary or name to be referred to its
Legislature for views before the President recommends introduction. Those views do not bind
the President or Parliament, and State consent is unnecessary.

The consultation nevertheless performs three functions. First, it gives the affected
population an institutional voice through its elected Legislature. Second, it supplies
Parliament with local information on administration, identity, resources and transition costs.
Third, it publicly records disagreement, increasing the political cost of an inadequately
reasoned reorganisation.

The non-binding character serves a different purpose. A parent State cannot permanently veto
Statehood or boundary demands raised by a region within it. Parliament retains national
capacity to resolve territorial conflict.

Thus, Article 3 separates voice from control. Consultation adds legitimacy and evidence;
parliamentary supremacy preserves adaptability. Wider federal negotiation remains desirable
where change is deeply contested.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the function of consultation, the rationale for
non-binding views and the distinction between legal sufficiency and political legitimacy.

## Final 15-marker — 250 words

**Question:** Examine whether the ease of territorial reorganisation under Articles 3 and 4 is
a strength or weakness of the Indian Constitution.

**Directive decoding:** “Examine” requires mechanism, competing effects and a reasoned
judgement.

**Model answer:**

Articles 3 and 4 make India's internal map unusually adaptable. Parliament may form States,
alter area, boundaries or names after Presidential recommendation and consultation with the
affected State Legislature. The State's views are not binding. Article 4 permits necessary
First and Fourth Schedule changes and transition provisions through ordinary legislation
outside Article 368.

This ease is a constitutional strength because it channels identity and development demands
into lawful bargaining. Andhra, the 1956 linguistic settlement, Maharashtra–Gujarat,
Northeastern Statehood and the States created in 2000 demonstrate that territorial
accommodation can deepen participation without conceding secession. A rigid veto by the parent
State could indefinitely block legitimate regional claims.

The same flexibility is a weakness when legal power substitutes for federal consent. A State
may be divided or downgraded despite its Legislature's opposition. Reorganisation also creates
disputes over capitals, water, debt, assets, services and minority protection. The unresolved
question of wholly converting a State into Union Territories shows that territorial power can
touch representative democracy and the basic federal relationship.

The design is therefore defensible but incomplete as a political ethic. Articles 3 and 4
should remain flexible legal instruments, while commissions, transparent reasons, transition
settlements and genuine consultation supply legitimacy. The ease of amendment is a strength
when used for accommodation and a weakness when used as unilateral domination.

**Why this earns marks:** It joins exact constitutional procedure to named historical evidence,
a federal objection, a reply and a graded verdict.

## Final 20-marker — 250 words

**Question:** “The Constitution protects Indian sovereignty more strongly than it protects any
particular internal map.” Analyse with reference to Articles 1–4 and relevant case law.

**Directive decoding:** “Analyse” requires breaking the claim into sovereignty, internal
territory, procedure and limits, then reconnecting them.

**Model answer:**

Article 1 establishes an indissoluble Union and distinguishes States, Union Territories and
acquired territories. No State possesses a right to secede. Yet the Constitution deliberately
withholds permanence from the internal map.

Article 3 permits Parliament to form States, increase or diminish area, alter boundaries and
change names. The affected State Legislature expresses views but cannot veto the proposal.
Article 4 allows First and Fourth Schedule amendments, representation changes and transition
provisions through ordinary law outside Article 368. Linguistic reorganisation and later
Statehood movements therefore altered constituent units without disturbing Indian sovereignty.

Case law marks the external limit. In *Berubari Union* (1960), the Supreme Court held that
Article 3 diminution does not authorise cession of Indian territory to a foreign State; loss of
sovereignty requires constitutional amendment. The Ninth Amendment supplied authority for the
contemplated transfer, and the 100th Amendment implemented the India–Bangladesh enclave
exchange. *Maganbhai* (1969), however, allowed executive implementation of a genuine boundary
settlement because identifying an uncertain line is not cession.

The hierarchy is clear: internal identity is flexible; national sovereignty receives the
higher constitutional threshold. Nevertheless, federalism limits how the power should be
understood. *In re Article 370* upheld Ladakh's UT formation but left open the broader
extinction of State character. The Constitution therefore protects the Union absolutely,
permits internal adaptation broadly, and leaves political consultation and judicial review to
guard against territorial centralism.

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates constitutional text, procedure, three case/amendment
anchors, hierarchy of values and a contemporary doctrinal limit.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional identity and territorial categories

- Article 1: “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.”
- “Union” signifies no compact-based origin and no right of State secession.
- It does not erase the federal distribution of governmental power.
- Union of India = States; territory of India = States + UTs + acquired territories.
- First Schedule = legal list and territorial description of States and UTs.
- Acquired territory is not automatically a State.

## Article 2 and Article 3

- Article 2: Parliament may admit or establish new States on terms and conditions it thinks fit.
- “Admission” conventionally concerns an existing entity; “establishment” a State not previously
  existing in that form.
- The “outside existing internal map” formula is standard doctrine, not literal constitutional
  wording.
- Article 3 = internal reorganisation.
- Five powers: form; increase area; diminish area; alter boundaries; alter name.
- Explanation I: State includes UT in clauses (a)–(e), but not in the proviso.
- Explanation II: formation includes combinations of parts of States and UTs.

## Article 3 procedure

- Bill may be introduced in either House.
- Prior recommendation of the President is mandatory.
- A proposal affecting a State's area, boundary or name must be referred for views.
- President fixes or extends the response period.
- State views are not binding; consent is unnecessary.
- *Babulal Parate*: no fresh reference for every parliamentary amendment to the Bill.
- No constitutional reference requirement for a UT legislature.

## Article 4

- Article 2/3 law may amend the First Schedule.
- It may amend the Fourth Schedule.
- It may adjust representation in Parliament and State Legislatures.
- It may contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions.
- These laws are not deemed amendments for Article 368.
- Ordinary legislative process and simple majority follow.
- The phrase “simple majority” is a consequence of Article 4(2), not its literal wording.

## Acquisition, cession and boundary

- Acquisition adds territory to Indian sovereignty.
- Internal reorganisation changes the constituent unit, not sovereignty.
- Assam (Alteration of Boundaries) Act, 1951 ceded a strip from Assam to Bhutan before the
  Supreme Court clarified the cession rule.
- The 1951 Act is a pre-*Berubari* statutory episode, not a present exception.
- *Berubari Union* (1960): Article 3 diminution does not include foreign cession.
- Cession requires Article 368.
- Ninth Amendment, 1960 supplied authority for contemplated transfer; do not claim every
  transfer was completed then.
- *Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel* (1969): genuine boundary settlement may use executive action.
- 100th Amendment, 2015 implemented the India–Bangladesh land-boundary/enclave exchange.
- India transferred 111 enclaves and received 51.
- Test: loss of acknowledged sovereignty versus identification of an uncertain boundary.

## Integration and original map

- British paramountcy lapsed on 15 August 1947.
- It did not transfer automatically to India.
- Use “more than 560 princely States”; exact counts vary.
- Local textbook shorthand: 552 within India's geographical boundaries, 549 initially joining.
- Instrument of Accession: Defence, External Affairs, Communications.
- Junagadh: plebiscite, February 1948.
- Hyderabad: Operation Polo, September 1948.
- Kashmir: October 1947 invasion followed by accession and military defence.
- Integration = accession + merger + administration + democratisation + constitutional
  incorporation.
- 1950: 9 Part A, 9 Part B, 10 Part C, one Part D territory.

## Linguistic reorganisation

- Dhar Commission, 1948: administrative convenience; “Dar” is a source spelling variant.
- JVP, 1948–49: Nehru, Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya; advised delay.
- Potti Sriramulu movement forced the Andhra decision.
- Avoid disputed 56/58-day duration.
- Andhra, October 1953 = first linguistic State.
- SRC, 1953–55: Fazl Ali, K.M. Panikkar, H.N. Kunzru.
- Accepted language; rejected one language–one State.
- Four factors: unity/security; language/culture; viability/administration; welfare/planning.
- SRC recommended 16 States and 3 centrally administered territories.
- Government accepted the framework with minor modifications.
- 1956 Act + Seventh Amendment produced 14 States and 6 UTs; old classification ended.
- Named 1956 changes: Travancore-Cochin + Malabar + Kasargode → Kerala; Andhra + Telugu areas
  of Hyderabad → Andhra Pradesh; Madhya Bharat + Vindhya Pradesh + Bhopal → Madhya Pradesh;
  Saurashtra + Kutch → Bombay; Coorg → Mysore; PEPSU → Punjab; Ajmer → Rajasthan; Laccadive,
  Minicoy and Amindivi Islands → new UT.
- Maharashtra–Gujarat, 1960; Punjab–Haryana/Chandigarh, 1966.
- Verdict: cultural recognition was accommodated without conceding sovereignty, but border,
  minority and nativist costs remained.

## Renaming and present map

- United Provinces → Uttar Pradesh, 1950.
- Madras → Tamil Nadu, 1969.
- Mysore → Karnataka, 1973.
- Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands → Lakshadweep, 1973.
- Pondicherry → Puducherry, 2006.
- Uttaranchal → Uttarakhand; 2006 Act, effective 2007.
- Orissa → Odisha, 2011.
- State name change: Article 3(e).
- Delhi's NCT redesignation = Sixty-ninth Amendment + Article 239AA, not ordinary State rename.
- J&K Reorganisation effective 31 October 2019: J&K UT with Legislature; Ladakh without.
- DNH–DD merged UT operative 26 January 2020.
- First Schedule footnote also records statutory substitution from 19 December 2019.
- **Dated 30 September 2026: 28 States and 8 UTs.**

## J&K status and federal limit

- The current linkage for this session is confined to J&K's continuing transition.
- Official status checked 30 September 2026: J&K remains UT with Legislature; Ladakh remains UT
  without Legislature.
- *In re Article 370* directed Statehood restoration at the earliest and as soon as possible.
- Ladakh's formation as a UT was upheld.
- The broader question of extinguishing a State's character through full conversion to UTs was
  left open after the Union assurance.
- Election of the UT Assembly did not restore Statehood.

## Federal answer spine

```text
Article 1: indissoluble Union
        ↓
Article 3: alterable internal units
        ↓
Article 4: ordinary-law implementation
        ↓
Federal benefit: accommodation and adaptability
        ↓
Federal risk: no State veto and possible category downgrade
        ↓
Limit: sovereignty/cession + basic federal structure + judicial review
        ↓
Verdict: legal flexibility requires political consultation
```

## Rapid examiner traps

1. Union of India is not equal to territory of India.
2. Article 2's “outside” formulation is interpretation, not text.
3. “New State” does not automatically mean Article 2.
4. State views are mandatory where applicable; consent is not.
5. UT legislature has no Article 3 proviso entitlement.
6. Article 4 law changes Schedules but is outside Article 368.
7. Article 3 cannot cede territory abroad.
8. Boundary settlement is not always cession.
9. Andhra preceded SRC and the 1956 Act.
10. Avoid a fixed princely-State count without a source caveat.
11. Avoid the disputed Sriramulu fast duration.
12. Delhi NCT is not Statehood.
13. Current count is 28/8, not the older 28/9.
14. J&K Assembly election is not Statehood restoration.
15. The Supreme Court did not conclusively decide unrestricted full State-to-UT conversion.
