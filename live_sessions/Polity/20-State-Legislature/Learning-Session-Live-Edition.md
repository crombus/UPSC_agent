# State Legislature - Live Session Edition

State legislatures convert State-level electoral consent into law, taxation, expenditure and executive accountability. Their design resembles Parliament in outline but differs at decisive points: most States have only one House, a Legislative Council is optional and weak, there is no joint sitting, and the Governor occupies a sensitive constitutional gate between a passed Bill and enforceable law.

The Governor is an integral component of the legislature but is not a member of either House. This is the first distinction to fix. A State legislature is not merely the elected chamber; constitutional functions such as summoning, prorogation and assent connect the Governor to the legislative process.

### 1. Why does the Constitution permit two designs?

India does not treat a State second chamber as a federal necessity. Every State has a Legislative Assembly, because the ministry is democratically responsible to that directly elected House. A Legislative Council exists only where the constitutional route under Article 169 has been used.

That creates two levels of necessity:

1. **Assembly - indispensable:** directly elected, controls confidence and grants.
2. **Council - optional:** indirectly elected and partly nominated, able mainly to advise and delay.

The six bicameral States are **Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Bihar and Uttar Pradesh**. Jammu and Kashmir's Council was abolished through the reorganisation framework in 2019. A statute enacted for revival in Tamil Nadu in 2010 was never brought into effect and was later repealed; Tamil Nadu is not presently bicameral.

> **Memory hook:** **KAT-BUM** - Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana; Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra.

### 2. The constitutional map

| Article group | Main subject |
|---|---|
| 168-177 | constitution, composition, duration, qualification, sessions and minister/Advocate-General participation |
| 178-187 | presiding officers and legislative secretariat |
| 188-195 | oath, voting, vacancies, disqualification, privileges and salaries |
| 196-201 | Bills, bicameral disagreement, Money Bills, assent and reservation |
| 202-207 | budget, grants, appropriation, supplementary finance and Financial Bills |
| 208-212 | procedure, financial-business regulation, language, judicial-conduct bar and judicial non-interference in procedural irregularity |

This map matters because UPSC often tests a narrow rule while expecting the candidate to place it in the larger institutional sequence.

### 3. Similarity with Parliament - and the first limit

The State pattern appears familiar: Governor/President, Assembly/Lok Sabha and Council/Rajya Sabha. Yet analogy cannot replace doctrine.

| Union | State | Decisive qualification |
|---|---|---|
| President + two Houses | Governor + one or two Houses | most States are unicameral |
| Rajya Sabha is constitutionally permanent | Council is permanent while it exists | Council itself may be abolished |
| Rajya Sabha represents the federal element | Council has mixed functional electorates | Council is not a federal chamber |
| ordinary-Bill deadlock may reach joint sitting | no State joint sitting | Assembly ultimately prevails through Article 197 |

**Limit of the analogy:** calling the Council "the Rajya Sabha of the State" is useful only for permanence and staggered retirement. It is misleading about power, composition and constitutional status.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A Governor who is neither elected by the State nor a House member should not be called part of the legislature.

**Reply:** "Part of the legislature" describes constitutional function, not membership. The Governor summons and prorogues, addresses the legislature, may promulgate ordinances when the legislature is not in session, and acts on Bills under Article 200. Democratic law-making remains centred on the elected House, while the Governor performs constitutionally bounded formal and scrutiny functions.

### UPSC application

- Never write "all States have two Houses."
- Do not list the Governor as a legislator or voting member.
- A comparison answer should begin with the shared institutional outline and then identify State-specific asymmetry.
- The phrase **optional bicameralism** captures Article 168 read with Article 169.

### Revision notes

1. Articles 168-212 govern State legislatures.
2. Every State has an Assembly; a Council is optional.
3. The Governor is an integral component, not a House member.
4. Six States are currently bicameral.
5. The Council is not a federal chamber.
6. State bicameralism is constitutionally non-uniform.
7. Articles 196-201 form the principal Bill-and-assent sequence.
8. Articles 202-207 form the financial-business sequence.
9. Articles 208-212 protect procedure but not substantive unconstitutionality.
10. Parliament-State analogies require qualification.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a Legislative Council an optional State institution rather than the State equivalent of a federal chamber?

**Model answer:** Article 168 requires an Assembly in every State but permits a Council only where one exists. Article 169 allows Parliament to create or abolish it after the Assembly's special-majority resolution. Its Article 171 constituencies represent functional and local interests, not States as federal units, and it lacks Rajya Sabha's Articles 249 and 312 powers.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer that every upper House is federal. Continuity and bicameral form do not themselves create federal representation.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain why bicameralism at the State level is optional rather than federal in character.

**Mains model answer:** Article 168 permits a State legislature to contain the Governor and either one House or two. The Assembly is indispensable because it is directly elected, controls grants and sustains the Council of Ministers. A Legislative Council, by contrast, exists only through Article 169: the Assembly must first adopt a special-majority resolution and Parliament then acts by ordinary law.

Its composition under Article 171 also lacks a federal principle. Local-body, graduate, teacher and MLA electorates choose five-sixths, while the Governor nominates one-sixth. Unlike the Rajya Sabha, it neither represents constituent States nor possesses exclusive federal powers comparable to Articles 249 and 312. It can delay an ordinary Bill but cannot defeat the Assembly, control confidence or vote grants.

Thus State bicameralism may improve revision and representation, but it remains an optional institutional choice rather than a necessary expression of Indian federalism.

The Assembly is the democratic core because voters directly choose members from territorial constituencies. Direct election explains why it dominates confidence, money and any disagreement with the Council.

### 1. Strength and exceptions

Article 170 provides the normal range of **60 to 500 members**. Smaller States operate under special constitutional or statutory exceptions. Present examples include Sikkim with 32 seats and Goa and Mizoram with 40 each.

Three numbers must not be collapsed:

| Number | Meaning |
|---|---|
| constitutional normal range | 60-500 |
| legally fixed strength of a particular Assembly | varies by State |
| working strength | may temporarily fall because of vacancies |

The Governor's former power to nominate an Anglo-Indian member to a State Assembly ceased under the **104th Constitutional Amendment**.

### 2. Territorial equality and SC/ST representation

Assembly seats are filled by direct election from territorial constituencies. The Constitution seeks a broadly comparable population-to-seat ratio within a State, subject to the operative delimitation framework. Article 332 reserves seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in State Assemblies broadly in proportion to their population.

Reservation changes who may contest a seat; it does not create a separate electorate. All eligible constituency voters participate.

### 3. The delimitation control

Readjustment is not triggered simply because the calendar reaches 2026. The constitutional freeze continues until the relevant figures of the **first census taken after 2026 have been published**. A census reference date, completion of enumeration, publication of relevant population figures, enactment or operation of delimitation law, final delimitation orders and the first election on new boundaries are distinct legal events.

```text
first census taken after 2026
             |
             v
publication of relevant figures
             |
             v
lawful delimitation/readjustment exercise
             |
             v
new constituency map and representation pattern
```

As of **1 October 2026**, the notified Census 2027 process does not itself complete publication or delimitation. No post-census State-wise seat projection should be presented as law.

### 4. Representation dilemma

**Objection:** Population-proportional readjustment may reduce the relative influence of States that achieved lower fertility.

**Reply:** An indefinite freeze also weakens vote equality where constituencies contain sharply different populations. A constitutional settlement must reconcile democratic equality, federal fairness and incentives for responsible population policy. That is a political-constitutional choice, not a number that may be guessed in advance.

### UPSC application

- Distinguish direct election, territorial constituency and reserved seat.
- Do not write that delimitation "begins automatically in 2026."
- Do not convert special minima into the actual strength of every small State.
- Link Article 170 to Article 332 and the post-2026 census-publication trigger.

### Revision notes

1. The Assembly is directly elected.
2. Article 170's normal range is 60-500.
3. Special exceptions permit smaller Assemblies.
4. Working strength can differ from sanctioned strength.
5. Anglo-Indian nomination ended under the 104th Amendment.
6. SC/ST seats are reserved under Article 332.
7. Reservation does not mean a separate electorate.
8. Constituency readjustment is tied to published census figures.
9. The first census after 2026 is a trigger stage, not automatic delimitation.
10. Representation reform must balance vote equality and federal fairness.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the end of the post-2026 freeze not by itself redraw Assembly constituencies?

**Model answer:** The constitutional threshold is not the calendar year alone. Relevant figures of the first census taken after 2026 must be published, and lawful readjustment must then occur through the applicable delimitation framework. Until that sequence is completed, existing constituency orders continue.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat 2026, a census reference date or the start of fieldwork as automatic publication and delimitation.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Why must census, delimitation and representation be treated as separate constitutional stages?

**Mains model answer:** A census produces population information; it does not itself alter a constituency. The post-freeze constitutional sequence first requires the relevant figures of the first census taken after 2026 to be published. A legally authorised readjustment must then translate those figures into constituency boundaries or seat allocation. Final orders must become operative before an election uses the new map.

This separation protects legality and democratic stability. It prevents an administrative reference date or incomplete enumeration from displacing existing representation. It also permits Parliament and the delimitation machinery to address the tension between population equality and federal fairness, especially for States that achieved lower fertility.

As of 1 October 2026, Census 2027 reference dates do not establish publication, final delimitation or new State-wise seat totals. A sound answer should therefore identify each stage, avoid automatic-2026 claims and preserve uncertainty about the eventual political settlement.

## Lesson 3 - Legislative Council composition, creation and abolition

Progress: 3/14 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Articles 169 and 171, functional electorates and institutional fragility

### Visual first: two different constitutional formulas

```text
CREATE OR ABOLISH A COUNCIL                     COMPOSE A COUNCIL
          Article 169                               Article 171
                |                                        |
Assembly special-majority resolution        1/3 local bodies
                |                           1/12 graduates
                v                           1/12 teachers
Parliament enacts ordinary law              1/3 elected by MLAs
by simple majority                          1/6 Governor-nominated
                |                                        |
not an Article 368 amendment                5/6 elected + 1/6 nominated
```

The first formula tells us why the Council is optional. The second tells us why it is representative without being directly popular or federal.

### 1. Article 169 route

The Legislative Assembly must first pass a resolution supported by:

- a majority of the total membership of the Assembly; and
- not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

Parliament may then create or abolish the Council by law. Parliament passes that law by the ordinary legislative process and a simple majority. The Constitution expressly prevents such a law from being treated as an Article 368 constitutional amendment merely because it alters connected constitutional provisions.

The Assembly resolution is necessary but does not itself create or abolish the Council. Parliament's law is also necessary.

### 2. Article 171 composition

A Council's strength may not exceed one-third of the Assembly's strength and ordinarily may not be below forty. Within those constitutional limits, its actual total strength is fixed by parliamentary law; the one-third figure is a ceiling, not an automatic seat count.

| Share | Electoral source |
|---:|---|
| about 1/3 | municipalities, district boards and other specified local authorities |
| about 1/12 | graduates of at least three years' standing in the State |
| about 1/12 | teachers of at least three years' standing in specified educational institutions |
| about 1/3 | Assembly members elect persons who are not Assembly members |
| remaining about 1/6 | Governor nominates persons with special knowledge or practical experience |

The elected categories use proportional representation by the single transferable vote. Nominated fields are literature, science, art, the cooperative movement and social service.

> **Mnemonic:** **L-G-T-M-N: Local, Graduate, Teacher, MLA, Nomination.**

Article 171(2) makes this constitutional scheme provisional rather than immutable: Parliament may by law modify or replace the composition formula. Parliament has not enacted such a replacement. This power is different from Article 169, which creates or abolishes the institution itself.

### 3. Why this design?

The Council attempts to widen representation beyond territorial party competition. Local bodies supply decentralised experience; teacher and graduate constituencies were designed to add informed voices; MLA election creates a connection with the popular House; nomination permits expertise.

The analogy has limits. Graduate and teacher electorates may look socially dated, and nomination can become patronage. Functional representation is valuable only if it improves scrutiny rather than merely providing political rehabilitation.

### 4. Worked example: Andhra Pradesh's institutional cycle

Andhra Pradesh illustrates that a Council is constitutionally reversible rather than entrenched. Its Council was created in 1957, abolished in 1985 and created again in 2007. The example establishes the two-stage logic: State political choice is expressed through the Assembly's special-majority resolution, but Parliament supplies the constitutive law. It does not mean that an Assembly resolution by itself changes bicameral status.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A chamber without direct election is democratically illegitimate.

**Reply:** Democratic legitimacy can be mediated rather than direct. Rajya Sabha elections, local governments and many constitutional bodies also use indirect routes. The stronger criticism is not indirect election itself but weak representativeness, obsolete constituency design and partisan nomination. Reforming those features could preserve a second look without claiming equality with the Assembly.

### UPSC application

- Article 169: Assembly special majority, Parliament simple majority.
- Do not call the parliamentary law a constitutional amendment.
- Council maximum is one-third of Assembly; ordinary minimum is forty.
- Parliament fixes the Council's actual strength within those constitutional limits.
- The Governor nominates one-sixth, not one-twelfth.
- Parliament may modify or replace the Article 171 composition scheme by law.
- **2021 GS-II Q14** requires both constitutional provisions and actual working; composition alone is incomplete.

2. Parliament decides through ordinary law and simple majority.
3. The Article 169 law is not an Article 368 amendment.
4. Council maximum is one-third of Assembly strength.
5. Ordinary minimum is forty; Parliament fixes the actual strength within the constitutional range.
6. Five-sixths are elected indirectly.
7. One-sixth are Governor-nominated.
8. Local bodies and MLAs each account for about one-third.
9. Graduates and teachers each account for about one-twelfth.
10. Elected categories use STV proportional representation.

### Concept check

**Question:** How do Articles 169 and 171 answer different questions about a Legislative Council?

**Model answer:** Article 169 governs whether a State has a Council: the Assembly initiates creation or abolition by special-majority resolution and Parliament legislates by ordinary law. Article 171 governs composition through local-authority, graduate, teacher, MLA-elected and nominated categories; Article 171(2) also permits Parliament to modify or replace that composition scheme.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse the Assembly's initiating role under Article 169 with power to enact the final creation or abolition law.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Functional representation in Legislative Councils can enrich scrutiny but may also reproduce exclusion. Analyse.

**Mains model answer:** Article 171 attempts to supplement territorial representation with functional voices. Local authorities elect about one-third of a Council; graduates and teachers elect one-twelfth each; MLAs elect about one-third from non-members; and the Governor nominates the remaining one-sixth for knowledge or experience in listed fields. This can bring local-government experience, professional knowledge and continuity into legislative scrutiny.

The design nevertheless reflects an older social structure. Graduate and teacher constituencies privilege narrow electorates, while indirect MLA election may reproduce party arithmetic. Nominations can reward expertise, but opaque selection can become political rehabilitation. Low turnout or weak constituency accountability further reduces representative legitimacy. Article 171(2), which permits Parliament to modify or replace the scheme, confirms that the formula was not treated as permanently complete.

Reform should update functional constituencies, publish nomination criteria, require conflict disclosures and strengthen committee work so expertise changes legislative outcomes. Direct election is not the only source of democratic value, but mediated representation must demonstrate inclusion and public purpose.

Thus the Council's case rests less on inherited categories than on whether diverse representation produces better scrutiny without entrenching privilege.

## Lesson 4 - Duration, sessions and the legal capacity to transact business

Progress: 4/14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Articles 172-177 and 188-189

### Visual first: the life cycle of legislative business

```text

      v

      |
```

Duration tells us how long an institution exists. Session rules tell us when it can act. Quorum and oath tell us whether those present can legally participate.

### 1. Duration

The Assembly normally continues for five years from its first meeting unless sooner dissolved. During a national emergency, Parliament may extend its term by law for one year at a time, but never beyond six months after the emergency ends.

The Council is permanent. It is not dissolved; approximately one-third of its members retire every second year, and a member normally serves six years.

### 2. Summoning, prorogation and dissolution

Under Article 174, the Governor summons the House or Houses. Six months may not intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. The Governor may prorogue a House and dissolve the Assembly.

| Term | Effect |
|---|---|
| adjournment | presiding officer pauses a sitting or sets the next meeting |
| adjournment sine die | presiding officer ends the sitting without naming a return date |
| prorogation | Governor ends the session |
| dissolution | ends the life of the Assembly |

Prorogation is not dissolution. It does not automatically lapse pending Bills.

### 3. Address, messages and participation

Article 175 permits the Governor to address either House or both assembled together and to send messages. Article 176 requires a special address at the first session after each general election and at the first session of each year.

A minister, including the Chief Minister, may belong to either House. Ministers and the State Advocate-General may speak and otherwise participate in either House and its committees where entitled, but may vote only if they are members of that House. This participation rule does not alter collective responsibility: the ministry survives only while it commands Assembly confidence.

### 4. Oath, quorum and voting

A member must take the prescribed oath or affirmation before taking a seat. Under Article 189, questions are ordinarily decided by a majority of members present and voting. The presiding officer does not vote initially but has a casting vote in a tie.

The constitutional default quorum, until the State legislature otherwise provides by law, is **ten members or one-tenth of the total membership, whichever is greater**. If quorum is absent, the presiding officer must adjourn or suspend the meeting.

### 5. Objection and reply

**Objection:** The six-month constitutional ceiling ensures adequate legislative scrutiny.

**Reply:** It prevents complete dormancy, not inadequate sittings. Two brief sessions can satisfy the text while leaving little time for questions, committees, opposition business or Bill scrutiny. Constitutional compliance is a floor; democratic deliberation requires a higher convention.

### UPSC application

- Separate adjournment, prorogation and dissolution.
- The Council is permanent, but individual members have six-year terms.
- Quorum is a default capable of alteration by State law.
- **2019 Prelims GS-I Q53** tests Governor address/message and Assembly procedure distinctions; keep the displayed PYQ answer-neutral.

### Revision notes

2. Emergency extension is one year at a time.

4. Council is permanent; one-third retire every two years.

### Concept check

**Question:** Distinguish adjournment, prorogation and dissolution by identifying what each one terminates.

**Model answer:** Adjournment interrupts a sitting or meeting, normally through the presiding officer. Prorogation ends a session through the Governor but preserves the House and ordinarily preserves Bills. Dissolution ends the Assembly itself, triggers elections and activates the differentiated Bill-lapse rules; the continuing Council is not dissolved.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use prorogation and dissolution interchangeably: only dissolution terminates the Assembly.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Constitutional session requirements prevent legislative dormancy but do not guarantee legislative deliberation. Discuss.

**Mains model answer:** Article 174 prevents more than six months from intervening between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. This ensures that a State government cannot indefinitely avoid the legislature. Quorum under Article 189, the Governor's special address under Article 176 and voting rules similarly preserve minimum institutional functionality.

Yet these are threshold safeguards. A State may formally satisfy the six-month rule through short sessions. Limited sitting time compresses questions, opposition motions, committee reference and clause-by-clause debate. Frequent disruptions and hurried passage can further reduce scrutiny even when quorum exists.

The remedy is convention and institutional design beyond the minimum text: an annual sitting calendar, protected opposition time, regular committee referral and adequate notice for Bills. Thus constitutional rules keep the legislative door open, but democratic practice determines whether meaningful deliberation occurs inside it.

Qualification permits entry. Disqualification can prevent or terminate membership. Vacancy is the legal consequence only after the relevant constitutional process.

### 1. Qualifications

Article 173 requires Indian citizenship, the prescribed oath or affirmation and the statutory qualifications made by Parliament. Minimum age is **25 for the Assembly** and **30 for the Council**. The Representation of the People Act supplies further electoral qualifications.

Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951:

- an Assembly candidate must be an elector for an Assembly constituency in the concerned State;
- an elected Council candidate must likewise be an elector for an Assembly constituency in that State;
- a person considered for Governor nomination to the Council must be ordinarily resident in the State; and
- a candidate for an SC/ST-reserved Assembly seat must belong to the relevant Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe, although an SC/ST member may also contest a general seat.

The elector requirement displaced the older assumption that a candidate must reside in the particular constituency being contested. State linkage and constituency residence are therefore not identical tests.

### 2. Constitutional and statutory disqualifications

Article 191 covers:

- holding an office of profit under the Union or State government, unless exempted by law;
- being of unsound mind as declared by a competent court;
- being an undischarged insolvent;
- not being an Indian citizen, voluntarily acquiring foreign citizenship, or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign State;
- disqualification under parliamentary law.

Statutory grounds include specified convictions, corrupt practices, dismissal for corruption or disloyalty, certain government contracts or offices, and failure to lodge election-expense accounts.

For Article 191(1) questions, the Governor decides after obtaining the Election Commission's opinion and acts according to that opinion. Anti-defection is different: the presiding officer decides under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.

### 3. Vacancy rules

A seat may become vacant through several distinct routes:

- **double membership within the State legislature:** if elected to both Houses, one seat falls vacant under State law;
- **disqualification:** a later constitutional or statutory disqualification vacates the seat through the competent process;
- **resignation:** addressed to the Speaker or Chairman and effective only upon acceptance; the presiding officer must reject an involuntary or non-genuine resignation;
- **absence:** the House may declare the seat vacant after sixty days' absence without permission, excluding constitutionally disregarded periods;
- **election adjudication or institutional incompatibility:** the seat may also be lost if the election is declared void, the member is expelled, the member is elected President or Vice-President, or is appointed Governor.

If a person sits or votes before taking the oath, or while knowingly unqualified, disqualified or prohibited from doing so, Article 193 imposes a penalty of **₹500 for each day**, recoverable as a debt due to the State.

Article 195 leaves members' salaries and allowances to State legislation. Payment does not create privilege or tenure; it supports the practical capacity to perform representative work.

### 4. Anti-defection mechanism

| Member type | Principal trigger |
|---|---|
| party member | voluntarily gives up party membership or votes/abstains contrary to direction without permitted condonation |
| independent member | joins a political party after election |
| nominated member | joins a party after six months from taking the seat |

The merger exception requires at least **two-thirds of the legislature party**. The old one-third split exception was removed by the 91st Amendment.

*Kihoto Hollohan* treated the presiding officer as a tribunal for Tenth Schedule adjudication and permitted judicial review after decision. *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020) stated that petitions should ordinarily be decided within three months absent exceptional circumstances. In *Padi Kaushik Reddy* (31 July 2025), the Supreme Court required the Telangana Speaker to conclude specified pending petitions within three months and clarified that legislative-procedure immunity does not convert Tenth Schedule adjudication into an unreviewable enclave.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Anti-defection is needed for stable government and voter fidelity.

**Reply:** Stability is legitimate, but a whip on every vote reduces legislators to numbers and makes the presiding officer's delay politically decisive. A narrower whip limited to confidence and core financial survival, combined with an independent or time-bound adjudicatory mechanism, would protect stability while restoring deliberation.

### UPSC application

- Keep Article 191 disqualification and Tenth Schedule adjudication separate.
- Age: Assembly 25, Council 30.
- Resignation is not automatically effective if involuntary or non-genuine.
- The merger threshold is two-thirds.
- Judicial review concerns legality; courts do not take over routine party discipline.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Anti-defection adjudication by State presiding officers creates a conflict between legislative stability and institutional neutrality. Analyse.

**Mains model answer:** The Tenth Schedule protects elected governments from opportunistic floor-crossing. The conflict arises because the Speaker or Chairman remains politically connected while deciding petitions capable of preserving or toppling a government. Delay can be as consequential as a decision. *Kihoto Hollohan* therefore characterised the presiding officer as a tribunal subject to judicial review. *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* stated an ordinarily three-month decision norm and suggested an independent tribunal. *Padi Kaushik Reddy* (2025) required specified Telangana petitions to be concluded within three months, rejecting an absolute procedural-immunity shield.

Yet immediate judicial substitution could intrude into legislative functioning, and stability remains a legitimate constitutional concern. Reform should narrow binding whips to confidence, appropriation and clearly identified manifesto commitments; prescribe a short statutory timetable; require reasoned, published orders; and consider transfer of adjudication to an independent body.

The objective is not to abolish party cohesion but to prevent partisan control over the timing and forum of constitutional adjudication.

### Visual first: the presiding officer wears three constitutional hats

```text

                            |

          |                 |                 |
     PROCEDURE HAT      DECISION HAT     TRIBUNAL HAT
 order, recognition,   casting vote,     Tenth Schedule
 admissibility, rules  Money Bill         adjudication
                       certification
          |                 |                 |

```

The office cannot be understood as a ceremonial chair. It controls who speaks, which motion is admitted, whether disorder interrupts business and, in some contexts, who remains a member.

### 1. Offices and election

The Assembly chooses a Speaker and Deputy Speaker under Article 178. The Council chooses a Chairman and Deputy Chairman under Article 182. The House elects these officers from among its members.

The Deputy performs the principal officer's duties when that office is vacant and presides when the principal officer is absent. Beyond the constitutional deputies, the Speaker nominates a panel of chairpersons for the Assembly and the Chairman a panel of vice-chairpersons for the Council. A panel member may preside when both principal officers are unavailable and exercises the chair's procedural powers while doing so; the panel does not become a second constitutional deputy.

### 2. Vacation, resignation and removal

An Assembly Speaker or Deputy Speaker vacates office on ceasing to be a member, may resign to the counterpart specified by the Constitution, and may be removed by a resolution passed by a majority of **all the then members** of the Assembly after at least fourteen days' notice. The Council Chairman and Deputy Chairman follow the corresponding Article 183 rule.

The Speaker continues after dissolution until immediately before the first meeting of the new Assembly. This prevents a procedural vacuum.

Articles 181 and 185 provide the exact removal-motion control. When a resolution for removal of the Speaker/Deputy Speaker or Chairman/Deputy Chairman is under consideration, the officer concerned does not preside. If the **Speaker's** own removal is under consideration, the Speaker may speak, participate and vote **in the first instance**, but has no casting vote in an equality. Article 185 gives that same special first-instance/no-casting-vote rule to the **Chairman** during consideration of the Chairman's removal. The constitutional text does not extend this exceptional voting rule to a Deputy Speaker or Deputy Chairman merely because the deputy's removal is being considered.

### 3. Institutional support: pay and an independent secretariat

Article 186 provides that the salaries and allowances of the Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman and Deputy Chairman are determined by State law; until then, the Second Schedule governs. These charges are placed on the Consolidated Fund rather than submitted to annual vote.

Article 187 requires separate secretarial staff for the House or Houses. The State legislature may regulate recruitment and service conditions by law. Until such law exists, the Governor may make rules after consulting the Speaker or Chairman. A capable legislative secretariat matters because neutrality requires independent research, procedure, records and committee support, not only a well-intentioned chair.

### 4. Principal powers

- maintains order and interprets procedural rules;
- admits questions, motions and points of order;
- allocates speaking opportunity and recognises party groupings;
- exercises a casting vote in a tie;
- certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 199;
- decides Tenth Schedule petitions;
- leads or influences important House committees and secretariat functioning.

Certification and defection power create special neutrality concerns because they can alter bicameral power or government survival.

### 5. 2018 and 2023 examination anchors

The **2018 Prelims GS-I Q39** demand concerns the conditions under which an Assembly Speaker vacates office. It must remain answer-neutral where displayed.

The **2023 GS-II Q5** asks about presiding officers of State legislatures and impartial conduct of business. A complete answer must move from office and powers to partisan incentives, cases and institutional reform.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Neutrality cannot be guaranteed because the presiding officer is elected by a party majority.

**Reply:** Origin in party politics does not make impartial performance impossible. Continuity across dissolution, removal by an effective majority and judicial review of tribunal-like decisions supply some insulation. Yet conventions remain weaker than in systems where the Speaker resigns party membership. Reform should therefore combine convention, transparent reasons, fixed adjudicatory time and possibly an external defection forum.

### UPSC application

- Removal uses a majority of all then members, not merely present and voting.
- Fourteen days' notice is required.
- The Speaker does not vacate merely because the Assembly is dissolved.
- During the Speaker's or Chairman's own removal motion, first-instance voting survives but the casting vote does not.
- Deputies and nominated panels ensure continuity of the chair, but the special Articles 181(2)/185(2) voting rule belongs only to the Speaker or Chairman in the specified proceeding.
- Articles 186-187 protect office support through charged remuneration and separate legislative staff.
- Distinguish procedural rulings, Money Bill certification and Tenth Schedule adjudication.

**Model answer:** Under Articles 181 and 185, the officer whose removal is under consideration cannot preside. During consideration of the Speaker's or Chairman's own removal, that principal officer may speak, participate and vote in the first instance but has no casting vote in an equality. This exceptional voting rule is stated for the Speaker and Chairman, not generally for deputies.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say that the Chairman cannot vote, or extend the Speaker/Chairman special voting rule automatically to Deputy presiding officers.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Money Bill certification and anti-defection adjudication make the Assembly Speaker a constitutional gatekeeper, not merely a debate manager. Analyse.

**Mains model answer:** The Speaker organises deliberation by recognising members, admitting motions, interpreting rules and maintaining order. Two powers are constitutional gatekeeping. Privilege exists so the House can legislate without intimidation. It is not a licence for corruption, violence or violation of constitutional limits.

### 1. Freedom of speech and vote

Article 194 protects freedom of speech in the legislature subject to the Constitution and House rules. No member is liable in court for anything said or any vote given in the legislature or its committees. Authorised publication also receives protection.

The immunity attaches to the legislative function. It should not be described as a general exemption from criminal law outside the House.

The same constitutional privileges extend to persons entitled to speak and participate in House or committee proceedings, notably State ministers and the Advocate-General. They do not extend to the Governor merely because Article 168 makes the Governor a component of the legislature.

Principal individual privileges conventionally include:

- freedom from arrest in **civil** cases during a session and for forty days before and after it, but not immunity from criminal process or preventive detention;
- freedom of speech and vote within the constitutional and procedural limits of the House; and
- exemption from jury service and an ability, while the House is in session, to decline attendance as a court witness where legislative duty requires presence.

These are functional protections. They cannot be converted into immunity for conduct unconnected with legislative participation.

### 2. Collective privileges

Each House may:

- publish its reports, debates and proceedings and regulate or prohibit unauthorised publication;
- exclude strangers and hold a secret sitting;
- make rules for its procedure and business;
- punish members or outsiders for contempt or breach of privilege, including reprimand, admonition or imprisonment and, for members, suspension or expulsion;
- receive immediate information concerning a member's arrest, detention, conviction, imprisonment or release;
- institute inquiries, compel attendance of witnesses and call for papers or records; and
- protect its precincts from arrest or service of legal process without the presiding officer's permission.

Courts do not inquire into House or committee proceedings merely on procedural grounds, but privilege remains bounded by constitutional legality. Uncodified privilege must be used for institutional necessity, not status.

### 3. Procedure, language and judicial boundary

Article 208 permits each House to make rules. Article 209 allows State law to regulate procedure for timely completion of financial business. Under Article 210, business may be transacted in the State's official language or languages, Hindi or English; the presiding officer may permit a member to use the member's mother tongue. The legislature may regulate continuation of English subject to the Constitution's differentiated transition rules. Article 211 bars discussion of a Supreme Court or High Court judge's conduct in discharge of duties.

Article 212 prevents courts from questioning proceedings merely for **procedural irregularity** and protects officers exercising procedural powers. It does not immunise substantive unconstitutionality, mala fides outside protected functions or tribunal-like decisions from all review.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Judicial review of House action destroys legislative autonomy.

**Reply:** Review of every procedural choice would indeed invite judicial management of the House. But constitutional supremacy requires review where the complaint is illegality, violation of fundamental rights or excess of constitutional power. The workable line is procedural irregularity versus substantive illegality.

### UPSC application

- Privilege belongs to the institution and function, not personal rank.
- Speech/vote immunity is tied to proceedings.
- Article 212 is not a blanket ouster of judicial review.
- Compare Article 194 with Parliament's Article 105, but retain the State context.

### Revision notes

### Concept check

**Question:** How can legislative privilege protect robust debate without becoming a general personal immunity?

**Model answer:** Article 194 protects speech, votes, authorised publication and institutional functions so the House can deliberate and inquire independently. The protection extends to members and participating ministers or the Advocate-General, but not to the Governor merely through Article 168. It does not immunise unrelated criminal conduct, and Article 212 shields procedural irregularity rather than substantive constitutional illegality.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not describe privilege as status-based immunity covering everything a legislator does inside or outside the House.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Legislative privilege and judicial review are complementary safeguards, not necessarily competing powers. Explain.

**Mains model answer:** Article 194 protects speech, voting and authorised publication so members can deliberate without fear of ordinary court proceedings. Collective privileges similarly enable each House to preserve order, punish obstruction and control its internal functioning. Article 212 reinforces autonomy by excluding judicial inquiry based merely on procedural irregularity.

These protections are functional, not personal impunity. Privilege does not place legislators generally above criminal law, and Article 212 does not shield substantive unconstitutionality, jurisdictional excess or violation of constitutional rights. Judicial review therefore polices the outer constitutional boundary without supervising every adjournment, ruling or internal procedural choice.

The two safeguards serve the same constitutional order from different directions: privilege protects legislative independence, while review protects constitutional supremacy. Courts should intervene only where a genuine legality question exists, and Houses should invoke privilege only where institutional functioning requires it.

## Lesson 8 - Ordinary Bills, disagreement and lapse

### Visual first: the Assembly always has the last ordinary-Bill move

```text
ASSEMBLY passes ordinary Bill
             |
             v
COUNCIL acts within first round
  pass / reject / amend / hold > 3 months
             |
             v
ASSEMBLY passes again, with or without accepted amendments
             |
             v
COUNCIL pass / reject / amend / hold > 1 month
             |
             v

```

The Council receives two opportunities to advise and delay, but not a final veto over an Assembly-backed ordinary Bill.

### 1. Introduction and passage

An ordinary Bill may generally originate in either House of a bicameral State. It must be agreed to by the legislative process before presentation to the Governor. But Article 197 creates an asymmetric route where the Assembly originates and insists on a Bill.

In each House the Bill ordinarily passes through three readings:

1. **first reading:** introduction and publication;
2. **second reading:** general consideration, possible committee scrutiny and clause-by-clause consideration; and
3. **third reading:** final debate and vote on passage.

The readings describe internal scrutiny; Article 197 describes what happens when the two Houses disagree after the Assembly has acted.

First delay: up to three months. After the Assembly passes the Bill again, second delay: up to one month. The maximum effective Council delay is therefore roughly four months.

**Worked timeline:** If the Assembly sends a Bill on 1 January and the Council rejects it or leaves it unresolved for three months, the Assembly may pass it again. If it returns the Bill on 10 April, the Council's second opportunity lasts no more than one month. Rejection, unacceptable amendment or inaction at that stage cannot force a joint sitting; the Assembly's second-passage form prevails. Calendar dates merely illustrate the two constitutional stages and do not replace exact computation in a real case.

### 2. Why there is no deadlock in the parliamentary sense

Parliament may use a joint sitting under Article 108 for specified ordinary-Bill deadlocks. A State legislature has no corresponding joint sitting. Instead, Article 197 constitutionally privileges the Assembly's second passage.

This reflects direct democratic primacy. It also makes the Council a **dilatory or advisory chamber**, not a co-equal revising chamber.

### 3. The Council-originated limit

If a Council-originated Bill is rejected by the Assembly, it ends. Article 197 does not provide a reverse mechanism enabling the Council to override the Assembly. The asymmetry runs only in favour of the popular House.

### 4. Lapse on dissolution

| Position when Assembly is dissolved | Result |
|---|---|
| Bill pending only in Council and not passed by Assembly | does not lapse |
| Bill pending in Assembly | lapses |
| Bill passed by Assembly and pending in Council | lapses |
| prorogation occurs without dissolution | Bill does not lapse merely for that reason |
| Bill passed by the unicameral Assembly, or by both Houses, and pending Governor/President assent | does not lapse |
| Bill passed by the House or Houses and returned by the President for reconsideration | does not lapse |

The first row does not mean the Council can enact the Bill alone. It means the proposal remains procedurally alive for a future Assembly to consider.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A chamber that can be overridden after four months is pointless.

**Reply:** Delay can expose defects, mobilise public debate and allow a new compromise. Yet this value depends on genuine scrutiny and political difference. Where the Council merely duplicates party control, the delay adds little; where it supplies expertise or an alternative majority, the second look can matter.

### UPSC application

- There is no State joint sitting.
- Four months means three months plus one month across two passages.
- A Council-originated Bill cannot use Article 197 against the Assembly.
- Prorogation and dissolution have different lapse effects.
- A fully passed Bill awaiting assent, and a Bill returned by the President, survive dissolution.

### Revision notes

1. Ordinary Bills may generally originate in either House.
2. Article 197 concerns Assembly primacy.
3. Each House ordinarily uses first, second and third readings.
4. Council first-stage delay is up to three months.
5. Council second-stage delay is up to one month.
6. Assembly's second-passage form ultimately prevails.
7. No State joint sitting exists.
8. Council-originated rejection by Assembly ends the Bill.
9. Prorogation alone does not lapse a Bill.
10. Assembly-pending Bills lapse on dissolution.
11. Assembly-passed, Council-pending Bills also lapse.
12. Council-only pending Bills survive dissolution procedurally.
13. Fully passed Bills awaiting assent do not lapse.

### Concept check

**Question:** Trace what happens when the Council rejects an Assembly-originated ordinary Bill twice, and explain why no deadlock mechanism is needed.

**Model answer:** The Council may first delay the Bill for up to three months. After Assembly repassage, it has at most one month; if it rejects, delays or proposes unacceptable amendments, the Bill is deemed passed in the Assembly's second-passage form. Assembly primacy therefore resolves the disagreement without a joint sitting.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not import Parliament's joint-sitting mechanism into State bicameralism or give the Council a final veto.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Why is the Legislative Council described as a dilatory rather than a co-equal revising chamber?

**Mains model answer:** The Council may debate, amend and originate ordinary Bills, but Article 197 denies it a final veto over an Assembly-originated proposal. It may reject, amend or retain the Bill for up to three months. If the Assembly passes it again, the Council receives only one further month; thereafter the Bill is deemed passed in the Assembly's second-passage form.

Unlike Parliament, there is no joint sitting. Nor is the asymmetry reversible: a Council-originated Bill rejected by the Assembly ends. Financial and confidence powers are even weaker because the Council cannot vote grants or remove the ministry.

The label "dilatory" does not mean useless. Delay may expose drafting defects, enable public debate and bring expertise into review. But constitutional power remains advisory: the Council can improve or postpone an ordinary Bill, not defeat the directly elected House.


The common trap is to think that any Bill mentioning money is a Money Bill. Constitutional classification is narrower.

### 1. Money Bill

Article 199 defines a Money Bill through specified matters such as taxation, State borrowing, custody or withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund, appropriation, charged expenditure and receipt or audit of public money. Incidental provisions are allowed, but a Bill does not become a Money Bill merely because it imposes fines, fees or local taxes.

A Money Bill:

- may be introduced only in the Assembly;
- requires the Governor's recommendation;
- is certified by the Speaker;
- goes to the Council only for recommendations;
- must be returned within fourteen days;
- may be accepted or rejected by the Assembly regarding recommendations;
- is deemed passed if not returned in fourteen days.

### 2. Financial Bill under Article 207(1)

A Bill or amendment containing one or more Article 199(1)(a)-(f) matters but also containing other matters requires the Governor's recommendation and cannot be introduced in the Council. Because it contains non-Money provisions, it follows the ordinary-Bill bicameral route after valid Assembly introduction.

The Council therefore has more power over this Financial Bill than over a Money Bill, though it cannot introduce it.

### 3. Expenditure Bill under Article 207(3)

A Bill that would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of the State cannot be passed by either House unless the Governor has recommended its consideration. It may originate in either House and is not automatically a Money Bill.

### 4. Worked classification

Suppose a Bill creates a State health authority and also imposes a new tax to fund it. Taxation under Article 199(1)(a) appears alongside non-Money institutional provisions: it is an Article 207(1) Financial Bill, introduced only in the Assembly on recommendation and thereafter processed through the ordinary bicameral route.

If a different Bill merely creates the authority and its implementation will involve Consolidated Fund expenditure, without containing the exclusive Article 199 matters, Article 207(3) applies: either-House origin remains possible, but recommendation is required before passage. The example distinguishes constitutional content from fiscal consequence; the exact drafting of a real Bill still controls classification.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Speaker certification makes the Assembly judge of its own financial dominance.

**Reply:** A conclusive internal certificate prevents paralysing disputes during budgeting, but classification must remain faithful to Article 199. Constitutional finality cannot legitimise colourable labelling that destroys bicameral scrutiny; the precise scope of review depends on the governing constitutional doctrine.

### UPSC application

- "Financial Bill" is not synonymous with "Money Bill."
- Article 207(1) Bills start only in the Assembly but then use the ordinary route.
- Article 207(3) recommendation is required before passage, not necessarily introduction.
- The Council's Money Bill period is fourteen days.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not classify every Bill containing taxation or expenditure language as a Money Bill; exclusive content is decisive.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Explain how the classification of Money and Financial Bills redistributes power between the two Houses of a bicameral State legislature.

**Mains model answer:** Classification determines whether the Legislative Council can merely advise or can participate through the ordinary-Bill route. A Money Bill under Article 199 contains only the specified taxation, borrowing, custody, appropriation, charged-expenditure, receipt or audit matters, with permitted incidentals. It originates only in the Assembly on the Governor's recommendation. The Speaker certifies it, and the Council may only recommend within fourteen days; the Assembly may reject every recommendation.

An Article 207(1) Financial Bill contains one or more Article 199(1)(a)-(f) matters together with other provisions. It also requires recommendation and Assembly introduction, but thereafter follows ordinary bicameral procedure. The Council can therefore amend or delay it under Article 197 rather than merely recommend.

Article 207(3) is wider in fiscal effect but different in route. A Bill involving Consolidated Fund expenditure cannot be passed without the Governor's recommendation for consideration. It may originate in either House and does not become a Money Bill merely because expenditure is involved.

The scheme preserves the directly elected Assembly's control over the core power of the purse while retaining Council scrutiny for mixed or expenditure-bearing legislation. Accurate classification is therefore a constitutional allocation of bicameral power, not a drafting label.

Article 202 requires the Governor to cause the annual financial statement to be laid before the House or Houses. It distinguishes expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund from other expenditure.

No demand for a grant may be made except on the Governor's recommendation. The Assembly may assent, refuse or reduce a demand; it cannot increase it.

Questions, budget debate, cut motions, audit reports and the Public Accounts Committee connect ex ante authorisation with ex post accountability. The formal superiority of the Assembly matters only if members receive time, information and committee support.

**Reply:** Majority discipline limits rejection but does not eliminate accountability. Questions, cut motions and outcome information connect financial choices to political responsibility.

## Lesson 11 - Governor's assent and reservation for the President

Progress: 11/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Articles 200-201, aid and advice, delay and date-controlled doctrine

The difficult issue is not listing options. It is identifying when discretion exists, whether delay may replace decision, and what remedy a court may provide.

### 1. Article 200's three options

Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, paragraph 165.1, reads Article 200 as providing **three**, not four, constitutional options:

1. assent to the Bill;
2. reserve the Bill for the President's consideration; or
3. withhold assent **and return the Bill with comments** to the legislature.

The first proviso does not create a fourth free-standing "return" option. It regulates the third option by requiring return with a message and confines that route to a Bill other than a Money Bill. A bare withholding unaccompanied by return is therefore not a separate fourth choice under the controlling 20 November 2025 formulation.

If the non-Money Bill is passed again and presented, Article 200 says the Governor shall not withhold assent. Reservation remains mandatory where, in the Governor's opinion, the Bill would so derogate from High Court powers as to endanger its constitutional position.

Reservation also serves distinct constitutional purposes that should not be collapsed into one discretionary category:

| Constitutional setting | Purpose and control |
|---|---|
| Article 200, second proviso | reservation is mandatory where serious derogation from High Court powers endangers the Court's constitutional position |
| Article 288 | a Bill imposing specified taxation concerning water or electricity in constitutionally covered Union arrangements must be reserved and receive presidential assent before taking effect |
| Article 254(2) | reservation and presidential assent may preserve a State Concurrent List law within that State despite repugnancy with an earlier Union or existing law, subject to Parliament's later power |
| Article 304(b) | a Bill imposing reasonable public-interest restrictions on inter-State trade requires the President's previous sanction before introduction; reservation has been identified as a route for presidential consideration where that control is engaged, but should not be presented as automatically curing every procedural defect |

Thus the High Court and Article 288 routes carry constitutionally mandatory controls, while Article 254(2) protection is deliberately sought and Article 304(b) must be analysed through its separate prior-sanction requirement. The Governor's general Article 200 discretion does not erase these specific provisions.

### 2. Article 201

For a reserved Bill, the President may assent or withhold assent. If it is not a Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it to the State legislature. The Houses then reconsider within six months; if passed again, the Bill returns for presidential consideration. Repassage does not create automatic assent by the President. Unlike the Article 200 consequence for a Governor-returned Bill, Article 201 does not say that presidential withholding becomes unavailable after State repassage. The President also cannot return a reserved Money Bill.

### 3. Aid, advice and limited discretion

The general parliamentary rule is ministerial aid and advice. Article 200 is a specific current-doctrine exception. Paragraph 165.2 of the 20 November 2025 advisory opinion states that the Governor enjoys discretion in choosing among the three Article 200 options and **is not bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers while exercising that function**. This does not authorise indefinite silence, a merits-free partisan veto or conduct beyond constitutional limits.

The 2026 GS-II Q11 is primarily owned by the Governor topic, but State Legislature Lesson 11 necessarily supports its Article 200-201 limb. Its exact demand asks the Governor's federal position, the nature of assent power and whether aid and advice bind every function.

### 4. Date-controlled current doctrine

The April 2025 litigation concerning Tamil Nadu Bills attempted to cure prolonged inaction through specific timelines and deemed-assent consequences. The later **Special Reference No. 1 of 2025**, advisory opinion dated **20 November 2025 (2025 INSC 1333)**, rejected court-created rigid timelines and automatic "deemed assent" under Articles 200-201 where the Constitution provides none.

The later position must be stated precisely:

- no judicially invented fixed universal deadline;
- no automatic deemed assent merely because time passes;
- the merits of a completed Article 200 choice are not justiciable at the pre-law stage;
- in glaring circumstances of prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction, a court may issue a limited mandamus requiring discharge of the function within a reasonable period;
- that mandamus cannot dictate which of the three options must be selected.

The advisory opinion did not operate as a conventional appellate overruling of the earlier judgment. In an answer dated after November 2025, use the advisory position as the controlling current clarification on timelines and deemed assent.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Without a fixed deadline or deemed assent, the Governor can defeat an elected legislature through silence.

**Reply:** The advisory opinion rejects judicial addition of a textual deadline, not constitutional accountability. Unexplained inaction can still attract limited review and a mandamus to decide. The unresolved institutional risk supports political and legal reforms: prompt reasoned action, transparent communication and a legislatively considered timetable that respects constitutional text.

### UPSC application

- Never call assent a free-standing personal veto.
- State the three options, not a four-option list.
- Withholding and return with comments form one option, available only for a non-Money Bill.
- Under current doctrine, the Governor is not bound by ministerial aid and advice when choosing among Article 200 options.
- Under Article 201, repassage does not bind the President to assent.
- Distinguish mandatory High Court/Article 288 reservation controls from Article 254(2)'s sought repugnancy protection and Article 304(b)'s prior-sanction rule.
- Map **2026 GS-II Q11** here as a cross-owned assent demand.
- Avoid the stale claim that fixed judicial timelines or deemed assent remain the unqualified current rule.

### Revision notes

2. The three routes are assent; reserve; or withhold and return with comments.
3. The first proviso restricts the third route rather than creating a fourth.
4. A Money Bill cannot be returned.
5. A returned non-Money Bill may be reconsidered and repassed.
6. High Court-endangering derogation triggers mandatory reservation.
7. Article 288 supplies a specific mandatory reservation-and-assent control.
8. Article 254(2) presidential assent can protect a repugnant Concurrent List State law within that State.
9. Article 304(b) separately requires prior presidential sanction for specified trade restrictions.
10. Article 201 governs presidential consideration.
11. The President may assent or withhold and may direct return only for a non-Money Bill.
12. State repassage does not compel presidential assent.
13. Paragraph 165.2 says the Governor is not bound by ministerial advice in exercising Article 200.

### Concept check

**Question:** State the current Article 200 decision structure and the limited judicial response to prolonged gubernatorial inaction.

**Model answer:** Special Reference No. 1 of 2025 identifies three routes: assent; reservation for the President; or withholding together with return and comments for a non-Money Bill. Paragraph 165.2 says the Governor is not bound by ministerial advice in choosing among them. Courts cannot invent universal timelines or deemed assent, but prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction may attract limited mandamus to decide without dictating the outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not split withholding and return into two choices, treat silence as a fourth option, or transfer Article 200's repassage consequence to the President under Article 201.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Article 200 creates a constitutional scrutiny power, not a pocket veto. Examine in light of the current judicial position.

**Mains model answer:** Article 200 gives the Governor three routes when a State Bill is presented: assent; reservation for the President; or withholding assent together with return and comments for a non-Money Bill. Special Reference No. 1 of 2025 treats the first proviso as restricting the third option, not creating a fourth. Reservation is mandatory where a Bill seriously endangers the High Court's constitutional position.

The difficulty is remedial. The April 2025 Tamil Nadu Bills decision used timelines and deemed-assent consequences to cure delay. The later advisory opinion, delivered on 20 November, rejected rigid court-created timelines and automatic deemed assent under Articles 200-201. It also states that a completed Article 200 choice is not open to pre-law merits review. Yet prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction can attract a limited mandamus requiring a decision within a reasonable period without dictating the result.

Paragraph 165.2 specifically holds that the Governor is not bound by ministerial aid and advice in choosing among the Article 200 options. This discretion remains a constitutional responsibility, not a partisan licence. Prompt, reasoned and transparent action can reduce conflict without judicially amending the text.

Under Article 201, a reserved Bill moves to the President, who may assent or withhold and may direct return of a non-Money Bill. The six-month rule governs State reconsideration, not the President's decision, and repassage does not compel presidential assent.

Article 200 is consequently a bounded decisional gate: scrutiny is legitimate, silence as an indefinite substitute for decision is not.

```text

```

The Council is not useless merely because it is weak. The correct evaluation asks whether its distinctive representation and scrutiny justify its cost and delay.

### 1. Case for the Council

- provides a second look at hurried Bills;
- offers continuity despite Assembly dissolution;
- includes local-government voices;
- can bring teachers, graduates, experts and experienced public figures into law-making;
- may question ministers and examine policy;
- participates with the Assembly in considering gubernatorial ordinances and reports of constitutional bodies; and
- can expose defects where the Assembly majority is dominant.

### 2. Case against

- cannot defeat an Assembly-backed ordinary Bill;
- has almost no financial power;
- cannot remove the government;
- does not participate in electing the President or the State's Rajya Sabha representatives;
- has no effective final say where a State legislature ratifies a constitutional amendment;
- may become a route for defeated politicians or patronage;
- graduate and teacher constituencies have a narrow, historically dated social base;
- duplicates cost where party composition mirrors the Assembly;
- its existence can be altered through Article 169.

### 3. Why it is weaker than Rajya Sabha

Rajya Sabha participates equally in ordinary and constitutional amendment Bills, represents States in the Union and possesses Articles 249 and 312 powers. Its elected members belong to the presidential electoral college, and a State's Rajya Sabha representatives are elected by that State's elected MLAs; a Legislative Council has no role in either election. The Council has no federal role, no comparable exclusive competence and no joint-sitting leverage. It can only delay an Assembly Bill through Article 197, and the Assembly's will prevails for the State's constitutional-amendment ratification.

At the same time, both Houses are permanent in the sense of staggered membership and both have only recommendatory power over Money Bills. Comparison must therefore be granular rather than absolute.

### 4. Reform choices

1. modernise or replace narrow graduate and teacher electorates;
2. make nominations transparent and merit-based;
3. strengthen committee referral and research support;
4. publish attendance, reports and action taken;
5. evaluate State-specific performance before retention or abolition;
6. prevent the Council from becoming a post-election rehabilitation chamber.

### 5. Balanced verdict

The strongest abolitionist point is not merely expense; it is weak power combined with weak representativeness. The strongest reply is that review need not equal veto to be valuable. The proper verdict is conditional: retain and reform where the Council supplies independent scrutiny; abolish where it remains duplicative patronage.

### UPSC application

- **2021 GS-II Q14** belongs centrally to this evaluative synthesis as well as Lesson 3.
- Do not describe the Council as co-equal with the Assembly.
- Do not dismiss it without explaining representation and revision benefits.
- Comparison with Rajya Sabha should cover purpose, power, composition and entrenchment.

### Revision notes

1. Council utility is advisory, representative and delaying.
2. Continuity may support institutional memory.
3. Local-body representation is a distinctive claim.
4. Graduate and teacher electorates invite reform.
5. Financial and confidence weakness is structural.
6. The Council still participates in ordinance and constitutional-body-report consideration.
7. Article 197 gives delay, not veto.
8. It has no presidential or Rajya Sabha electoral role and no effective final say in State ratification of constitutional amendments.
9. Article 169 makes institutional existence fragile.
10. Rajya Sabha has a federal role; Council does not.
11. Rajya Sabha is equal on amendment Bills; Articles 249 and 312 have no Council equivalent.
12. Both upper Houses continue, but Council retention should depend on genuine independent scrutiny.

### Concept check

**Question:** What constitutional and institutional test should a State apply before retaining a Legislative Council?

**Model answer:** A State should ask whether the Council adds genuinely different representation, informed revision, continuity and committee capacity beyond the Assembly. These gains must be weighed against narrow electorates, patronage, cost and merely partisan delay. Retention is defensible where composition and nomination are reformed and scrutiny is demonstrably useful; otherwise Article 169 permits abolition.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not argue from the Council's existence alone: constitutional permissibility neither proves usefulness nor requires abolition.

### Original Mains practice - 20 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** "A Legislative Council is a revising convenience, not a revising power." Critically evaluate whether States should retain this institution.

**Mains model answer:** The phrase captures constitutional asymmetry. A Council may originate and debate ordinary Bills, question ministers and provide continuity, but Article 197 permits only a three-month delay followed by one month after Assembly repassage. It cannot compel a joint sitting or override the Assembly. On money it only recommends within fourteen days; it cannot vote grants or determine ministerial confidence.

Its composition nevertheless offers a different representative logic. Local bodies elect about one-third, graduates and teachers one-twelfth each, MLAs one-third and the Governor nominates one-sixth for specified expertise. This can bring local experience, specialists and institutional memory into law-making.

Critics answer that the functional electorates are socially narrow, nominations invite patronage, and a weak chamber may simply rehabilitate defeated politicians at public cost. The Council is also far weaker than Rajya Sabha, which represents the federal element, acts equally on ordinary and amendment Bills and has Articles 249 and 312 powers.

A uniform verdict is therefore inappropriate. Councils should be retained where a distinct composition, opposition presence and committee system produce measurable scrutiny. Reform should modernise electorates, publish nomination criteria, strengthen research and normalise Bill referral. Where a Council merely duplicates the Assembly and delays without analysis, Article 169 legitimately permits abolition.

The institution's defence rests not on a veto it lacks, but on the quality of the second look it actually provides.

2. women may be included within vertical SC/ST reservation;

The 106th Amendment inserted Article 332A for one-third reservation of seats filled by direct election in every State Legislative Assembly. The one-third includes one-third of Assembly seats already reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Article 334A ties operation to delimitation undertaken after publication of the relevant figures of the first census taken after commencement. Rotation follows each subsequent delimitation. The constitutional text also contains a fifteen-year duration formulation, subject to continuation by Parliament; quote that formulation rather than calculating a simplistic end-year, because commencement, operation and the first reserved election are distinct.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains model answer:** Constitutional commencement answers when an amendment's legal provisions enter into force; operational implementation answers when the new electoral arrangement can actually govern an election. Gazette S.O. 1922(E) commenced the 106th Amendment on 16 April 2026, so Article 332A is part of the operative constitutional text.

Relevant figures of the first census taken after commencement must be published, and delimitation must then be undertaken for the reservation purpose. The distinction prevents two opposite errors. Treating enactment or commencement as immediate seat allocation invents constituencies before delimitation. Treating delayed operation as if the amendment has no legal existence ignores its commenced constitutional status. As of 1 October 2026, no completed publication-delimitation sequence or first reserved Assembly election is established.

Implementation must also be assessed substantively. Candidate selection, campaign resources, safety, training and committee opportunity determine whether descriptive presence becomes legislative influence. Article 332A creates the entitlement; Article 334A controls its electoral activation.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Cumulative check 1 - Bill route under bicameralism

**Question:** An Assembly-originated ordinary Bill is delayed by the Council, passed again by the Assembly and then presented to the Governor. Identify the three constitutional asymmetries operating across this route.

**Model answer:** First, Article 197 permits Council delay but gives the Assembly the final legislative position after the three-month and one-month stages. Second, no joint sitting is required because the Council lacks a final veto. Third, passage does not itself create law: Article 200 still requires a gubernatorial decision through assent, reservation, or withholding together with return for a non-Money Bill.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assembly primacy over the Council does not eliminate the separate constitutional assent stage.

## Cumulative check 2 - Upper-House comparison

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to call a Legislative Council the State-level Rajya Sabha?

**Model answer:** Rajya Sabha is an entrenched federal chamber, broadly co-equal on ordinary and constitutional amendment Bills and specially empowered by Articles 249 and 312. A Council is optional under Article 169, represents mixed functional constituencies, can only delay Assembly-backed ordinary Bills, recommends on Money Bills and has no confidence or federal role.

**Misconception to avoid:** Shared features such as continuity and staggered retirement do not erase differences in purpose and power.

## Cumulative check 3 - Membership and institutional control

**Question:** Connect member qualification, disqualification adjudication, presiding-officer power and judicial review in one constitutional chain.

**Model answer:** Articles 173 and the RPA define entry qualifications; Articles 190-193 regulate vacancy and disqualification consequences. Article 192 assigns specified post-election disqualification questions to the Governor acting on the Election Commission's opinion, while the Tenth Schedule assigns defection to the presiding officer. Because that adjudication is tribunal-like, judicial review restrains illegality and disabling delay without converting courts into managers of ordinary House procedure.

**Misconception to avoid:** Institutional autonomy does not make every presiding-officer decision immune from constitutional review.

## Cumulative check 4 - Financial accountability chain

**Question:** Show how Assembly primacy, appropriation, audit and committees form one financial-accountability cycle.

**Model answer:** The Assembly votes demands for grants and an Appropriation Act authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund. Executive spending is then audited by the CAG. PAC examines legality and conformity with legislative authority, while Estimates and COPU test economy, design and public-enterprise performance. Action-taken reporting connects post-spending scrutiny back to political responsibility.

**Misconception to avoid:** Passing the budget is the beginning of legislative financial control, not its completion.

## Cumulative check 5 - Representation sequence

**Question:** How do delimitation and Article 334A jointly prevent immediate operation of Article 332A reservation?

**Model answer:** Article 332A creates the constitutional entitlement, but constituencies cannot be reserved operationally without a defined territorial allocation. Article 334A therefore links operation to publication of relevant census figures and a subsequent delimitation undertaken for the reservation purpose. This separates legal commencement from electoral implementation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Neither the amendment's enactment, its commencement nor announcement of a census reference date alone creates reserved Assembly constituencies.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-marker

**Question:** Distinguish the constitutional position of a Legislative Council from that of the Rajya Sabha. (150 words)

**Directive decode:** "Distinguish" requires paired points organised on common dimensions, not two separate descriptions.

**Model answer:** Both are continuing upper Houses with staggered retirement and recommendatory Money Bill roles. The resemblance ends there.

Rajya Sabha is entrenched under Article 79 and represents States in Union law-making. It is broadly co-equal with Lok Sabha on ordinary and constitutional amendment Bills and possesses special federal powers under Articles 249 and 312. A joint sitting may resolve ordinary-Bill deadlocks.

A Legislative Council is optional. Article 169 allows creation or abolition after an Assembly special-majority resolution and an ordinary parliamentary law. Its mixed Article 171 electorates do not embody a federal principle. Under Article 197 it can delay an Assembly Bill for only three months and then one month; there is no State joint sitting. It cannot vote grants or determine confidence.

Therefore Rajya Sabha is a federal revising chamber with constitutionally distinctive powers, while the Council is an advisory and dilatory chamber whose value depends on representation and scrutiny.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares entrenchment, composition, legislative power, finance, confidence and special functions, then states a precise qualified verdict.

## Final 15-marker

**Question:** Examine how presiding-officer powers, privileges and anti-defection adjudication interact with judicial review in State legislatures. (250 words)

**Directive decode:** "Examine" requires the institutional logic, the constitutional limits and a reasoned assessment.

**Model answer:** State presiding officers maintain order, interpret rules, admit motions, exercise a casting vote and, in the Assembly, certify Money Bills. Under the Tenth Schedule they also decide defection petitions. Article 194 protects legislative speech, votes and authorised publication, while Article 212 prevents courts from questioning proceedings merely for procedural irregularity.

These rules protect autonomy but create a boundary problem. A Speaker with party links may delay defection petitions or use classification power in a manner affecting government survival and bicameral scrutiny. Treating every such act as unreviewable "procedure" would convert functional privilege into partisan immunity.

The Supreme Court's solution is differentiated review. *Kihoto Hollohan* treats the presiding officer as a tribunal for Tenth Schedule purposes and permits judicial review. *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* states an ordinarily three-month norm absent exceptional circumstances. *Padi Kaushik Reddy* required specified Telangana petitions to be concluded and rejected an absolute Article 212 shield for Paragraph 6 adjudication. At the same time, courts should not supervise ordinary speaking time, adjournment or internal sequencing merely because another procedure seems preferable.

Reform should require reasoned published rulings, a short adjudicatory timetable, transparent business allocation and consideration of an independent defection tribunal. Legislative privilege and judicial review are thus complementary: one protects functional autonomy; the other protects constitutional legality.

_Model word count: 209._

**Why this earns marks:** It integrates three doctrines, uses named cases for the precise boundary and avoids both judicial supremacy and legislative immunity.

**Unique scoring rubric (15):** officer powers 3 | privilege/Article 212 3 | conflict 2 | three cases 4 | reforms and balanced conclusion 3.

## Final 20-marker

**Question:** State legislative effectiveness depends less on formal law-making power than on the quality of scrutiny across Bills, finance, committees and assent. Critically analyse. (250 words)

**Directive decode:** "Critically analyse" requires a causal account of formal power, operational weaknesses, countervailing institutions and a graded verdict.

**Model answer:** The Assembly has formidable formal authority. It ultimately prevails over a Council on ordinary Bills, monopolises Money Bill introduction and grants, and sustains the ministry. Yet authority does not guarantee scrutiny.

Ordinary Bills may pass rapidly where party majorities dominate; a Council can delay for three months and then one, but cannot veto. Misclassification as a Money Bill can narrow upper-House review. Budget voting may become ritual under party discipline, while supplementary and excess grants weaken ex ante control. Short sessions, late Bills, disruption and limited research support reduce deliberation.

Countervailing institutions therefore matter. Public Accounts, Estimates, Public Undertakings and subject committees enable evidence-based review. Questions and motions create public responsibility. Article 194 protects uninhibited legislative work, while judicial review remains available against substantive illegality. The Governor's Article 200 role can provide constitutional scrutiny, but prolonged inaction risks becoming an extra-textual veto; after the 20 November 2025 advisory opinion, courts cannot invent universal deadlines or deemed assent but may address disabling inaction through limited review.

Reform should adopt predictable sitting calendars, normal committee referral with urgency exceptions, accessible fiscal-risk documents, independent research staff, reasoned Money Bill certification and prompt transparent assent decisions.

Formal Assembly primacy is necessary for democratic responsibility. Effective State legislation, however, arises only when procedure, committees, audit, opposition space and bounded constitutional review convert majority power into reasoned law.

**Why this earns marks:** It links four institutional stages through a single scrutiny thesis, uses exact provisions and current assent doctrine, and offers reforms matched to each diagnosed weakness.

# REMEDIATION

## Error family 1 - treating every upper House alike

**Wrong intuition:** Rajya Sabha and Legislative Council are interchangeable because both are permanent.

**Repair:** Permanence is only one dimension. Ask four questions: Who is represented? Is the chamber entrenched? Can it defeat an ordinary Bill? Does it have exclusive powers? These yield sharply different answers.

## Error family 2 - collapsing all majorities

```text
Article 169 Assembly resolution
= majority of total membership
  + at least 2/3 present and voting

Parliamentary law afterward
= ordinary simple majority

Speaker removal
= majority of all then members
```

The nouns before "majority" matter as much as the fraction.

## Error family 3 - calling every fiscal Bill a Money Bill

Use the three-box test:

1. only Article 199 matters plus incidentals -> Money Bill;
2. Article 199(1)(a)-(f) plus other matters -> Article 207(1) Financial Bill;
3. expenditure from Consolidated Fund without exclusive Money content -> Article 207(3) Bill.

## Error family 4 - inventing a State joint sitting

Parliamentary deadlock invites Article 108 analysis. State bicameral disagreement invokes Article 197: Assembly repassage and limited Council delay.

## Error family 5 - treating Article 212 as total immunity

Procedure protected from review for mere irregularity does not equal substantive lawlessness. Ask whether the complaint concerns a better procedure or a constitutional violation.

## Error family 6 - confusing office disqualification forums

```text
Article 191(1) membership question
       -> Governor after Election Commission opinion

Tenth Schedule defection
       -> Speaker/Chairman as tribunal
       -> judicial review

Election validity under RPA
       -> election petition before High Court
```

## Error family 7 - converting silence into a constitutional option

Article 200 lists decisional routes. Current doctrine rejects automatic deemed assent and universal court-made timelines, but it also rejects an unlimited practical pocket veto. State the narrow review-and-direction position.

## Error family 8 - equating amendment enactment with reservation operation

Write the chain in full: legally effective commencement -> first relevant census -> publication of figures -> delimitation for the purpose -> operation. If one link is unverified, preserve uncertainty.

## Error family 9 - confusing representation with immediate empowerment

A reserved seat secures descriptive presence; it does not by itself guarantee agenda control, committee influence or independent candidacy. Link Article 332A to transparent party selection, resources, safety, training and anti-proxy safeguards without treating those supporting reforms as substitutes for the constitutional reservation.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional spine: Articles 168-212

- **Article 168:** Governor plus one or two Houses; Assembly universal, Council optional.
- **Current bicameral States:** Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Bihar and Uttar Pradesh.
- **Core asymmetry:** Assembly is directly elected and controls confidence, grants and final ordinary-Bill outcomes.
- **Council identity:** continuing, indirectly elected/partly nominated, advisory and dilatory rather than federal.

## Assembly: representation and duration

- Article 170 normal strength: **60-500**, subject to special exceptions.
- Direct election from territorial constituencies.
- Article 332: SC/ST Assembly reservation broadly by population.
- Anglo-Indian nomination ended under the 104th Amendment.
- Normal term: five years unless sooner dissolved.
- National-emergency extension: one year at a time, never beyond six months after emergency ends.
- Readjustment is tied to publication of relevant figures of the first census taken after 2026, not the calendar alone.

## Council: creation, composition and continuity

- **Article 169:** Assembly majority of total membership + two-thirds present and voting; Parliament then uses ordinary simple-majority law.
- Such parliamentary law is not an Article 368 amendment.
- Strength: maximum one-third of Assembly; ordinary minimum forty.
- Parliament fixes the Council's actual strength within that constitutional range.
- Composition: local bodies 1/3; graduates 1/12; teachers 1/12; MLAs 1/3; Governor nomination 1/6.
- Five-sixths indirectly elected through STV proportional representation; one-sixth nominated.
- Nominated fields: literature, science, art, cooperative movement and social service.
- Article 171(2): Parliament may modify or replace the constitutional composition scheme by law.
- Council permanent; one-third retire every two years; member term six years.

## Sessions and participation

- Article 174: Governor summons, prorogues and may dissolve Assembly.
- No more than six months between the constitutionally specified sittings.
- Adjournment is by presiding officer; prorogation ends session; dissolution ends Assembly.
- Articles 175-176: general address/messages and special address.
- A minister, including the Chief Minister, may belong to either House; ministers and the Advocate-General may participate in either House but vote only if members of that House.
- Article 186: presiding-officer salaries/allowances are determined by State law and charged on the Consolidated Fund.
- Article 187: each House or the Houses receive separate legislative secretarial staff, regulated by law or interim gubernatorial rules after consultation.
- Oath precedes taking the seat.
- Default quorum: ten or one-tenth, whichever is greater, until altered by State law.
- Presiding officer has casting vote in a tie.

## Membership and vacancy

- Article 173: citizenship, oath and parliamentary-law qualification.
- Minimum age: Assembly 25; Council 30.
- RPA qualification: an Assembly or elected-Council candidate must be an elector for an Assembly constituency in the State; a Governor nominee to the Council must satisfy the State-residence condition.
- SC/ST membership is required to contest the corresponding reserved Assembly seat, without barring contest from a general seat.
- Article 191 grounds: office of profit, court-declared unsound mind, undischarged insolvency, foreign citizenship/allegiance and parliamentary-law grounds.
- Article 191(1) question: Governor after Election Commission opinion.
- Resignation must be voluntary and genuine.
- Vacancy routes include double membership, disqualification, accepted resignation, sixty-day unauthorised absence, void election, expulsion, election as President/Vice-President and appointment as Governor.
- Article 193 imposes ₹500 per day for knowingly unauthorised sitting/voting, recoverable as a debt due to the State.
- Article 195 leaves members' salaries and allowances to State law.
- Tenth Schedule: party-member defection/whip, independent joining, nominated joining after six months.
- Merger threshold: two-thirds; one-third split exception abolished.
- Cases: *Kihoto Hollohan*; *Keisham Meghachandra Singh*; *Padi Kaushik Reddy*.

## Presiding officers

- Assembly: Articles 178-181; Council: Articles 182-185.
- Removal: majority of all then members + fourteen days' notice.
- Speaker continues after dissolution until immediately before successor Assembly's first meeting.
- Officer does not preside over own removal motion.
- During the Speaker's or Chairman's own removal motion, that principal officer may vote in the first instance but has no casting vote; this special rule is not generalised to deputies.
- The Deputy Speaker/Deputy Chairman and nominated panels provide presiding continuity when principal officers are unavailable.
- Powers: order, rules, admissibility, recognition, casting vote, committees.
- Speaker-specific high-impact powers: Money Bill certification and Tenth Schedule adjudication.
- Neutrality reforms: reasoned orders, time limits, transparent business, party-distance convention or independent defection forum.

## Privilege and procedure

- Article 194: speech/vote immunity and authorised-publication protection.
- Privilege coverage extends to participating ministers and the Advocate-General, but not to the Governor merely as a legislative component.
- Individual privileges include limited civil-arrest protection around sessions, legislative speech/vote immunity and specified jury/witness exemptions; criminal process and preventive detention are not covered by the civil-arrest rule.
- Collective privileges include publication and exclusion controls, internal rule-making, contempt power, arrest information, inquiries, witness/document powers and precinct protection.
- Privilege is functional, not personal impunity.
- Article 208: House rules.
- Article 209: law for timely financial procedure.
- Article 210: State official language(s), Hindi or English may be used; the presiding officer may permit a mother tongue.
- Article 211: no discussion of Supreme Court/High Court judge's official conduct.
- Article 212: no court challenge for mere procedural irregularity; substantive illegality remains reviewable.

## Ordinary-Bill route and lapse

- Ordinary Bill may generally originate in either House.
- It ordinarily passes first, second and third readings in each considering House.
- Assembly-originated Bill: Council delay up to three months, then one month after repassage.
- No State joint sitting.
- Assembly's second-passage form prevails.
- Council-originated Bill rejected by Assembly ends.
- Prorogation does not itself lapse a Bill.
- Council-only pending Bill survives Assembly dissolution.
- Assembly-pending and Assembly-passed/Council-pending Bills lapse on dissolution.
- Fully passed Bills pending Governor or President assent do not lapse.
- Fully passed Bills returned by the President for reconsideration do not lapse.

## Money and Financial Bills

- Article 199 defines Money Bill; fees, fines or local taxes alone do not suffice.
- Assembly-only introduction on Governor recommendation.
- Speaker certification.
- Council: recommendations only, fourteen days.
- Article 207(1): specified Money matters plus other content; Assembly-only introduction, then ordinary route.
- Article 207(3): expenditure from Consolidated Fund; recommendation before passage; either-House origin possible; not automatically Money Bill.

## Budget and grants

- Article 202 annual financial statement.
- Charged expenditure: discussable, not votable.
- Other expenditure: demands for grants voted only by Assembly.
- Assembly may accept, reject or reduce; it may not increase a demand.
- Appropriation Act authorises Consolidated Fund withdrawal.
- Supplementary grant: an authorised amount for an existing service proves insufficient.
- Additional grant: expenditure is required for a new service not contemplated in the budget.
- Excess grant: retrospective regularisation.
- Vote on account: temporary bridge.
- Vote of credit: unexpected indefinite demand.
- Exceptional grant: special non-current-service purpose.

## Assent and reservation

- Article 200 has three options under Special Reference No. 1 of 2025: assent; reserve; or withhold and return with comments for a non-Money Bill.
- The first proviso restricts the third option and does not create a fourth.
- Mandatory reservation where High Court position is endangered by serious derogation.
- Article 288 creates a specific mandatory reservation-and-presidential-assent control; Article 254(2) assent may protect a repugnant Concurrent List State law, while Article 304(b) separately requires prior presidential sanction for specified trade restrictions.
- Article 201: President may assent, withhold or direct return of a non-Money Bill; a reserved Money Bill cannot be returned.
- State repassage does not compel President's assent.
- Paragraph 165.2: the Governor is not bound by ministerial aid and advice when choosing among Article 200 options.
- Current doctrine: Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, 20 November 2025.
- No rigid universal court-made timeline; no automatic deemed assent.
- A completed choice is not open to pre-law merits review, but prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction remains amenable to limited mandamus directing performance without dictating the result.

## Committees and accountability

- Floor: questions, debates, motions and confidence.
- PAC: audit and legislative-authority compliance.
- Estimates: economy and efficiency.
- COPU: public-enterprise accounts and performance.
- Other key committees: Business Advisory, Rules, Privileges, Ethics, Petitions, Assurances and Subordinate Legislation.
- Reform needs: sitting calendar, routine Bill referral, research staff, opposition space, evidence publication and action-taken tracking.

## Council evaluation and Parliament comparison

- Utility: second look, continuity, local-body voice, expertise and alternative majority.
- Criticism: weak power, cost, dated electorates, patronage and duplication.
- Council participates in ordinance and constitutional-body-report consideration, but not in presidential or Rajya Sabha elections and has no effective final say in State ratification of constitutional amendments.
- Rajya Sabha differs through federal role, entrenchment, ordinary/amendment equality and Articles 249/312.
- Qualified verdict: retain-and-reform where scrutiny is real; Article 169 abolition remains valid where duplication dominates.

## Women's reservation and delimitation

- Articles 243D/243T: local-body constitutional foundation.
- 106th Amendment enacted in 2023.
- Article 330A: Lok Sabha; Article 332A: State Assemblies; Delhi separately covered.
- One-third includes one-third within SC/ST-reserved seats.
- Councils and Rajya Sabha excluded.
- Article 334A links operation to the first post-commencement census's relevant published figures and delimitation for the purpose.
- Rotation follows subsequent delimitation.
- Do not equate census reference date, publication, delimitation and election.
- As of 1 October 2026, operation is not established merely from enactment or commencement.
- Gazette S.O. 1922(E) verifies commencement on 16 April 2026.
