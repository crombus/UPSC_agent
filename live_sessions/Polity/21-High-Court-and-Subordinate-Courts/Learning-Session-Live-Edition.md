## Lesson 1 — Constitutional position and territorial design

### Visual first

```text
SUPREME COURT
      ↓ precedent and appeal
HIGH COURT — Arts 214–231
      ↓ appeal · Article 227 · Article 235
DISTRICT/SESSIONS COURTS
      ↓
CIVIL JUDGES / JUDICIAL MAGISTRATES

India has a **single integrated judiciary**, but integration is not administrative absorption. A High Court derives its powers directly from the Constitution. It is bound by Supreme Court law and subject to appeal, yet Articles 215, 226, 227 and 235 give it original constitutional functions.

The first High Courts arose at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in **1862**, followed by Allahabad in **1866**. Their colonial jurisdiction was constitutionalised and enlarged after 1950. Historical inheritance explains why specified chartered High Courts, and Delhi under statute, possess original civil jurisdiction not shared by every High Court.

### Constitutional map

| Article | Core function |
|---:|---|
| 214 | High Court for each State |
| 215 | Court of record |
| 216 | Composition |
| 217–224A | Judges and continuity |
| 225–228 | Jurisdiction, writs, supervision, withdrawal |
| 229 | Staff and expenses |
| 230 | Jurisdiction over Union Territories |
| 231 | Common High Court |

Article 214 is the general rule; Article 231 permits Parliament to establish a **common High Court** for two or more States, or States and a Union Territory. The Seventh Amendment, 1956 enabled this design. Thus “a High Court for each State” does not mean a separate institution for every State. India has **25 High Courts**.

Article 230 permits Parliament to extend or exclude a High Court’s jurisdiction over a Union Territory. Article 225 preserves inherited jurisdiction subject to the Constitution and legislation. Territorial flexibility does not weaken constitutional status.

**Objection:** Because the Supreme Court is the apex, a High Court is only a regional appeal bench.

**Reply:** A mere appeal bench would not possess independent writ, review, contempt, superintendence and subordinate-service control. The High Court is subordinate in appellate hierarchy but autonomous within its constitutional field.

### UPSC use and traps

- Integrated judiciary means coherent hierarchy, not a unitary administrative department.
- Twenty-five High Courts do not equal one court per State.
- Common High Courts are constitutional, not temporary exceptions.
- Article 225 does not exhaust later constitutional jurisdiction.
- The High Court is the territorial constitutional court closest to most litigants.

### Revision notes

1. Articles 214–231 govern High Courts.
2. High Courts sit below the Supreme Court and above subordinate courts.
3. Calcutta, Bombay and Madras began in 1862; Allahabad in 1866.
4. Article 214 supplies the general rule.
5. Article 231 permits common High Courts.
6. The Seventh Amendment enabled the common-court design.
7. India has 25 High Courts.
8. Article 230 concerns Union-Territory jurisdiction.
9. Article 225 preserves existing jurisdiction subject to law.
10. High Court constitutional power is not delegated by the Supreme Court.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** “A High Court is not merely a court below the Supreme Court; it is the constitutional centre of a State’s judicial system.” Explain.

**Mains model answer:** India’s integrated judiciary places the Supreme Court at the apex, but Articles 214–231 give High Courts an independent constitutional field. A High Court is a court of record under Article 215, protects fundamental and ordinary legal rights through Article 226, supervises courts and tribunals under Article 227 and controls the subordinate judiciary under Article 235. It also hears civil and criminal appeals and may withdraw constitutional-interpretation questions under Article 228. Article 231 permits a common High Court, showing territorial flexibility without loss of authority. Supreme Court precedent and appeal preserve national coherence; they do not convert a High Court into a delegated bench. The High Court is therefore a constitutional court, appellate court and administrative guardian of the State judiciary.

**Quantified rubric (10):** position 2 · four powers/articles 4 · integrated-yet-autonomous distinction 2 · conclusion 2.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Article 231 not contradict Article 214?

**Model answer:** Article 214 states the general institutional rule, while Article 231 constitutionally allows that rule to be satisfied through one common High Court serving several territories.

**Misconception to avoid:** “For each State” does not require one physically separate court for every State.

**Bridge to Lesson 2:** Once the High Court's constitutional place is clear, the next question is institutional: who may enter its Bench, and how can appointments protect both independence and accountability?

### Visual first

                    ↓
State constitutional inputs
                    ↓
CJI + two senior-most Supreme Court judges
                    ↓

Article 216 provides a Chief Justice and as many other judges as the President deems necessary. Strength is not constitutionally fixed; flexibility responds to workload but permits harmful delay when sanctioned vacancies remain unfilled.

Under Article 217, the **President** appoints. For a Chief Justice, the CJI and Governor are consulted; for another judge, the High Court Chief Justice is additionally consulted. In a common High Court, concerned States participate through the constitutional process.

The Second Judges Case (1993) established judicial primacy. The Third Judges Case (1998) required the CJI to consult the two senior-most Supreme Court judges for High Court appointments. At the originating High Court, the Chief Justice consults two senior-most colleagues. The NJAC/Fourth Judges Case (2015) invalidated the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act, restoring the collegium.

**Critical distinction:** Article 217 contains appointment and consultation; the collegium’s precise composition is judicial doctrine, not printed constitutional text.

Article 218 applies specified Supreme Court safeguards concerning removal to High Court judges. Article 221 places salaries and related allowances within parliamentary determination, subject to the constitutional protection against disadvantageous variation after appointment.

### Qualifications and oath

A candidate must be an Indian citizen and have either ten years’ judicial office or ten years as a High Court advocate, including High Courts in succession. No minimum age is prescribed. The “distinguished jurist” route belongs to the Supreme Court, not High Courts.

Article 219 requires oath before the Governor or an authorised person. President appoints; Governor administers oath. Parliament determines salaries and conditions, which cannot ordinarily be varied to a judge’s disadvantage after appointment except during Financial Emergency.

Article 229 gives the Chief Justice control over officers and servants, subject to limited constitutional roles concerning rules with financial implications. Administrative expenses are charged on the State Consolidated Fund. Adjudicatory independence would be fragile without staff and registry autonomy.

### Independence and accountability debate

Judicial primacy protects courts reviewing executive action. The strongest objection is that primacy may become opaque self-selection, with delay and weak diversity. The reply is not executive control, but a permanent secretariat, published criteria, vacancy calendars, reasoned disclosure consistent with privacy, and diversity data. Independence and accountability must be designed together.

### UPSC traps

- President appoints; Governor does not.
- Governor administers oath.
- High Court and Supreme Court collegium stages are distinct.
- The two ten-year routes are alternatives.
- No distinguished-jurist eligibility exists.
- Collegium rules are doctrine, not verbatim Article 217.

### Revision notes

1. Article 216 governs composition.
2. Strength varies; the Constitution fixes no number.
3. Article 217 governs appointment and conditions.
4. President appoints after consultation.
5. The High Court Chief Justice is additionally consulted for puisne judges.
6. The originating Chief Justice consults two senior-most High Court colleagues.
7. Supreme Court collegium: CJI plus two senior-most judges.
8. Collegium primacy comes from Judges Cases.
9. Eligibility is citizenship plus ten years’ judicial office or advocacy.
10. No minimum age and no distinguished-jurist route.
11. Oath is under Article 219 before Governor/authorised person.
12. Article 229 supports administrative independence.

### Lesson Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Examine whether appointment of High Court judges adequately reconciles independence, accountability and diversity.

**Mains model answer:** Article 217 places appointment with the President after consultation with the CJI, Governor and, for puisne judges, the High Court Chief Justice. The Second and Third Judges Cases transformed consultation into judicial primacy through High Court and Supreme Court collegiums. This protects a court that frequently reviews executive action. The NJAC judgment of 2015 reaffirmed independence as the central concern.

The process nevertheless suffers from inconsistent transparency, delays and uneven professional and social representation. These deficits weaken legitimacy and aggravate vacancies. Reform should publish eligibility and assessment norms, establish a permanent appointments secretariat, complete recommendations before vacancies arise, disclose reasons to the extent compatible with privacy, and track diversity. Executive input on antecedents is legitimate, but a controlling veto would allow a frequent litigant to shape its reviewer. Collegium primacy is therefore defensible only when joined to transparent, timely and inclusive procedure.

**Quantified rubric (15):** Article 217 3 · Judges Cases/NJAC 3 · independence 3 · critique 3 · reforms/verdict 3.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to say Article 217 itself creates the present collegium?

**Model answer:** Article 217 provides presidential appointment after consultation; the detailed collegium and judicial-primacy rules were developed by the Judges Cases.

**Misconception to avoid:** Constitutional doctrine is binding but should not be misquoted as literal article text.

**Bridge to Lesson 3:** Appointment rules explain entry to the Bench; judicial independence also depends on what happens after entry—tenure, removal, transfer and temporary continuity.

### Visual first

Permanent security                    Temporary continuity
retirement 62                         Art 223 acting CJ
resignation to President              Art 224 additional judge

A High Court judge retires at **62**; a Supreme Court judge at 65. Resignation is addressed to the President. A judge also vacates office on elevation or transfer. Judges do not hold office during executive pleasure.

Removal requires **proved misbehaviour or incapacity** and is deliberately multi-stage. A motion must be signed by at least 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members. The Speaker or Chairman may admit or refuse it. If admitted, a three-member committee investigates: a Supreme Court judge or the Chief Justice of India, a Chief Justice of a High Court, and a distinguished jurist. Only an adverse committee finding permits each House to consider the motion. Each House must pass it in the same session by both a majority of its total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting; the President then orders removal. The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 regulates this process. The threshold protects against retaliation, while its severity leaves an accountability gap for conduct below the removal standard.

Article 222 permits presidential transfer after CJI consultation; the judge’s consent is not constitutionally required. *Sankalchand Sheth* (1977) linked transfer to public interest, not punishment. The Third Judges Case requires broader consultation including four senior-most Supreme Court judges and connected Chief Justices. Legitimate administrative use must be distinguished from perceived punitive transfer.

Article 223 supplies an acting Chief Justice. Article 224 permits an **additional judge** for temporary business or arrears, for up to two years, and an **acting judge** when a permanent judge is temporarily unavailable. Article 224A permits a Chief Justice, with prior presidential and personal consent, to request a retired High Court judge to sit temporarily. The retiree exercises judicial powers while sitting but is not otherwise deemed a serving judge.

Article 220 restricts a retired permanent High Court judge from pleading or acting except in the Supreme Court and other High Courts. The independence package also includes protected service conditions, charged expenditure, limits on legislative discussion of judicial conduct, contempt power, staff autonomy and Article 50 separation.

**Strong objection:** Security of tenure and a nearly unusable removal process may protect weak performance or misconduct as well as independence.

**Reply:** Easier political removal would expose judges to retaliation by governments and litigants. The sound response is credible in-house ethics, transparent administration and reasoned disciplinary processes below the constitutional removal threshold.

**Residual:** India protects decisional independence more clearly than it structures public accountability for non-removal misconduct. Temporary arrangements are similarly necessary safety valves, but routine reliance on them can hide failure to fill permanent posts.

2. Resignation goes to the President.

4. Both Houses require special majority.
5. Article 222 governs transfer.
6. Consent is not constitutionally mandatory.
7. Article 223 concerns acting Chief Justice.
8. Article 224 concerns additional and acting judges.
9. Additional judges address temporary business/arrears.
10. Article 224A recalls a retired judge with two consents.
11. Article 220 restricts post-retirement practice.
12. Independence combines personal, financial and administrative safeguards.

### Lesson Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** “Transfer and temporary-judge provisions are administrative necessities carrying constitutional risks.” Discuss.

**Mains model answer:** Article 222 permits transfer after CJI consultation; Articles 223, 224 and 224A provide an acting Chief Justice, additional or acting judges, and temporary sitting of retired judges. They preserve leadership, meet absence and address short-term arrears. The same devices can threaten confidence. A non-consensual transfer may appear punitive; repeated additional appointments may create insecurity; overuse of retired judges may conceal regular vacancies. *Sankalchand Sheth* requires public interest, while the Judges Cases add collective consultation. Temporary appointments must remain tied to their constitutional triggers, reasons should be disclosed where compatible with institutional needs, and permanent posts should follow a predictable calendar. These provisions are safety valves, not substitutes for a stable Bench.

**Quantified rubric (15):** distinctions 4 · rationale 3 · risks 3 · safeguards 3 · verdict 2.

### Concept check

**Question:** What distinguishes an Article 224 additional judge from an Article 224A retired judge?

**Model answer:** The former is temporarily appointed for increased business or arrears; the latter is a former judge invited to sit with prior presidential and personal consent.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not merge all temporary judicial arrangements into one status or trigger.

**Bridge to Lesson 4:** Once the Bench is securely constituted, the next question is functional: what distinct kinds of case may that Bench hear, correct or constitutionally review?

### Visual first

HIGH COURT
├─ original: first instance
├─ appellate: civil and criminal
├─ writ: Article 226
├─ supervisory: Article 227
├─ control: Article 235
├─ record/contempt: Article 215
└─ judicial review: constitutional legality
```

Original jurisdiction includes writs, contempt, election disputes under law, admiralty, specified revenue matters and Article 228 withdrawals. Calcutta, Bombay and Madras, and Delhi under its statutory design, possess specified original civil jurisdiction; this is not universal.

Civil and criminal appeals reach High Courts under procedural and special statutes. First and second civil appeals have different scopes; second appeal ordinarily centres on substantial questions of law. A Sessions Court may impose death, but execution requires High Court confirmation whether or not the convicted person appeals.

Article 215 makes every High Court a **court of record**: authoritative records and precedents are preserved, and the Court can punish contempt. Civil contempt concerns wilful disobedience; criminal contempt concerns serious interference with justice. Fair reporting and fair criticism are not automatically contempt. Authority must protect proceedings, not suppress reasoned scrutiny.

Judicial review tests legislation and executive action for Fundamental-Rights violation, lack of competence and other constitutional breach. High Courts can review Union as well as State action. The 42nd Amendment attempted to restrict review of Central laws; the 43rd restored the position. A High Court may review its own judgment under governing law and court authority even though Article 137 expressly names only Supreme Court review.

Article 228 allows withdrawal of a subordinate case involving a substantial constitutional-interpretation question necessary for disposal. The High Court may decide the whole case or answer the question and return it. It is not a general transfer provision.

**Concrete example with limit:** If a Sessions Court convicts and awards death, High Court confirmation is mandatory even without an appeal; this demonstrates superior scrutiny of the gravest sentence. It does not mean the High Court conducts every criminal trial afresh—confirmation and appeal operate within criminal procedure and the evidentiary record.

**Strong objection:** Contempt, precedent and judicial review concentrate excessive authority in an unelected court.

**Reply:** These powers secure obedience to lawful orders and constitutional supremacy, but review tests legality rather than policy wisdom, and fair criticism is protected from contempt.

**Residual:** The legitimacy of strong review depends on reasoned jurisdictional restraint and procedural fairness, not merely on the importance of the judicial institution.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain how appellate, court-of-record and judicial-review powers sustain the rule of law.

**Mains model answer:** Appellate jurisdiction lets High Courts correct civil and criminal error; mandatory confirmation of a Sessions Court death sentence adds superior scrutiny. Article 215 preserves authoritative proceedings, creates precedent for subordinate courts and enables contempt action to secure compliance. Judicial review invalidates Union or State action that violates rights, exceeds competence or breaches constitutional limits. Article 228 keeps necessary substantial constitutional interpretation under superior control. These powers reinforce one another: appeals generate coherent law, precedent guides lower courts, review preserves constitutional supremacy and contempt protects enforceability. Their limit is equally important—review is not general policy administration, and contempt must not silence fair criticism.

**Quantified rubric (10):** appellate 2 · Article 215 2 · review 2 · interaction 2 · limits/conclusion 2.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is judicial review different from an ordinary appeal?

**Model answer:** Review tests constitutional legality, competence, rights and jurisdiction; an appeal re-examines a lower decision within a statutorily defined merits scope.

**Misconception to avoid:** Not every High Court correction uses the same jurisdictional power.

## Lesson 5 — Article 226 and the five writs

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual first

```text
illegal detention → HABEAS CORPUS
public duty refused → MANDAMUS
forum about to exceed power → PROHIBITION
illegal order completed → CERTIORARI
public office unlawfully occupied → QUO WARRANTO
```

Article 226 authorises directions, orders and writs for Fundamental Rights **and any other purpose**, meaning ordinary legal rights too. Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and protects Fundamental Rights. Article 226 is therefore **wider in purpose**, not in every dimension.

| Axis | Article 32 | Article 226 |
|---|---|---|
| Court | Supreme Court | High Court |
| Purpose | Fundamental Rights | Fundamental and other legal rights |
| Character | guaranteed right to move Court | constitutional power, equitable discretion |
| Reach | national apex | territorial/cause-of-action based |

Article 226(2), added through the Fifteenth Amendment, permits action against an outside authority when the cause of action arises wholly or partly within the High Court’s territory. Petitioner residence alone is not enough.

### Habeas corpus — liberty first

A petition may be brought by the detained person or, because detention may prevent access, by a relative, friend or another bona fide person. It can run against the State or a private detainer. The respondent must justify present custody. It is unavailable where custody is under a facially valid order of a competent court, unless the order is wholly without jurisdiction or the detention is otherwise constitutionally void; it also becomes infructuous when no custody remains.

**India-centric example:** A family alleges that police have kept an adult at a station without recording arrest or producing the person before a magistrate. The High Court can demand production and legal justification. **Limit:** it cannot use habeas corpus as a routine appeal against a lawful conviction.

> **UPSC trap:** Habeas corpus is not confined to public authorities, but it always requires actual unlawful restraint, not merely an unpleasant restriction.

### Mandamus — enforce a public duty

The petitioner ordinarily shows a legal right and a corresponding public duty that has been refused, neglected or unlawfully withheld. It may issue to government, a public authority, tribunal, lower court or a private body performing a public duty. It does not ordinarily issue against a purely private obligation, the President or Governor for constitutional functions, a legislature to enact law, or to compel an authority to exercise lawful discretion in one predetermined manner. It cannot command an illegal act.

**India-centric example:** A statutory university refuses to consider an eligible student’s application despite a mandatory rule. Mandamus may require lawful consideration. **Limit:** the Court cannot order admission if selection lawfully depends on comparative merit not yet assessed.

> **UPSC trap:** Mandamus can require exercise of discretion; it does not normally dictate the lawful outcome of that discretion.

### Prohibition and certiorari — jurisdiction before and after decision

Both ordinarily control judicial or quasi-judicial action for absence/excess of jurisdiction, breach of natural justice or a legally recognised error. **Prohibition** is preventive: it stops a pending proceeding before the impugned final act. **Certiorari** is corrective: it quashes an order already made. Neither is a general device to reweigh evidence, and neither ordinarily lies against legislative action or a purely administrative act lacking the relevant adjudicatory character, though Article 226 directions may still address administrative illegality through an appropriate form.

**India-centric example:** A tax tribunal begins deciding a dispute the statute assigns exclusively elsewhere; prohibition may stop it. If it has already passed the jurisdictionally void order, certiorari may quash it. **Limit:** an ordinary factual error within jurisdiction normally belongs to appeal.

> **UPSC trap:** The decisive distinction is procedural stage, not that one writ is “stronger.”

### Quo warranto — protect public office

Any bona fide member of the public may seek it; personal injury is unnecessary. The office must be substantive, public, created by the Constitution or law, and held contrary to a mandatory eligibility or appointment rule. The writ tests legal authority to occupy office, not the holder’s general performance, and is unavailable for a private post or where the alleged defect concerns a merely directory condition.

**India-centric example:** A statutory commission requires a qualification that the appointee indisputably lacks. The High Court may ask by what authority the office is held. **Limit:** it does not become a service appeal over every debatable selection assessment.

> **UPSC trap:** Liberal standing does not dilute the strict public-office and legal-ineligibility requirements.

A High Court ordinarily declines a writ where an adequate statutory remedy exists. This is self-restraint, not extinction of power. *Whirlpool* (1998) recognises intervention for Fundamental Rights, natural-justice failure, want of jurisdiction and a vires challenge within doctrine. *Radhey Shyam* (2015) holds that civil-court judicial orders are not ordinary Article 226 targets; Article 227 and statutory remedies have their own field.

*L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) makes Articles 226/227 and Article 32 review part of the basic structure. Tribunal decisions remain reviewable, but the case did not abolish tribunals or turn review into merits appeal.

**Objection:** Discretion makes Article 226 a weaker rights remedy.

**Reply:** Discretion disciplines forum and remedy. Territorial proximity and coverage of ordinary rights make Article 226 the working constitutional remedy for many citizens.

### Lesson Mains practice — 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Analyse why Article 226 is wider than Article 32 but not an unrestricted substitute for ordinary remedies.

**Mains model answer:** Article 32 guarantees access to the Supreme Court for Fundamental Rights. Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue directions, orders and writs for Fundamental Rights and “any other purpose,” including ordinary legal rights; it is wider in subject matter. Article 226(2) also recognises jurisdiction where cause of action arises wholly or partly within the territory.

Breadth is disciplined by remedy-specific limits. Habeas corpus tests detention, mandamus enforces public duty, prohibition prevents jurisdictional excess, certiorari quashes an unlawful order and quo warranto tests title to public office. High Courts ordinarily require use of adequate statutory remedies. Under *Whirlpool*, Fundamental Rights, natural-justice failure, want of jurisdiction and vires challenge can justify intervention. *Radhey Shyam* prevents Article 226 from becoming a general appeal against civil-court orders; *L. Chandra Kumar* keeps tribunals within constitutional review. Thus discretion preserves constitutional correction without collapsing ordinary adjudication.

**Quantified rubric (15):** comparison 4 · writ functions 3 · territory 2 · cases/limits 4 · conclusion 2.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can Article 226 be wider although the Supreme Court is higher?

**Model answer:** Width concerns rights enforceable: Article 226 includes ordinary legal rights, while Article 32 is confined to Fundamental Rights; hierarchy is a different axis.

**Misconception to avoid:** Never say “wider” without identifying the dimension.

**Bridge to Lesson 6:** Writ jurisdiction corrects public illegality in individual disputes, but a High Court must also preserve the lawful functioning of the wider judicial system through supervision, withdrawal and administration.

## Lesson 6 — Superintendence, withdrawal and administration

Article 227 gives administrative and judicial superintendence over courts and tribunals in the territory, excluding Armed-Forces-law forums. The High Court may call returns, frame rules/forms, regulate records and act suo motu. Judicial intervention addresses absent/excess jurisdiction, natural-justice violation, disregard of binding law, perversity or manifest injustice.

The power exists broadly but is exercised sparingly. Reweighing evidence whenever the High Court disagrees would destroy appeal limits. *Radhey Shyam* locates civil-court judicial orders in Article 227 rather than Article 226 without creating a second appeal. In *K. Valarmathi v. Kumaresan* (2025 INSC 606), the Supreme Court held that Article 227 could not be used to reject a plaint where the trial court had that function and a statutory appeal existed.

Article 228 permits withdrawal only where a substantial constitutional-interpretation question is necessary to dispose of a subordinate case. Article 229 protects High Court staffing and charged administration.

Article 235 is related but distinct. Its text vests “control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto” in the High Court and expressly includes posting, promotion and leave of persons in the judicial service holding posts inferior to district judge, while preserving statutory appeal rights and service conditions. Judicial interpretation—not the parenthetical text alone—has read “control” broadly to include administrative and disciplinary control across the district judiciary. Initial appointment and promotion of district judges remain governed by Article 233.

**Concrete example with limit:** If credible misconduct is alleged against a Civil Judge, the High Court may institute disciplinary control rather than leaving the officer’s career to the State executive. **Limit:** control does not authorise a superior judge to dictate how the Civil Judge should decide a pending case.

**Strong objection:** Concentrating service control in the High Court can replace executive pressure with opaque internal hierarchy.

**Reply:** Article 235 removes the more dangerous leverage of a frequent litigant—the executive—but High Court administration must use notice, evidence, recorded reasons and available appeal/review safeguards.

**Residual:** External independence is constitutionally clear; internal judicial due process must be continuously earned through fair administration. Article 227 supervises adjudicatory legality, while Article 235 protects career administration.

Most evidence, bail, property, family and criminal adjudication begins below the High Court. “Subordinate” means hierarchical position, not executive subordination.

Article 233 places appointment, posting and promotion of district judges with the Governor in consultation with the High Court. Article 233(2) also governs direct recruitment and requires High Court recommendation.

In ***Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa***, the Constitution Bench judgment of **9 October 2025** overruled contrary precedent, quashed inconsistent recruitment rules and required the concerned rule-making authorities to amend them. Eligibility is not unconditional: on the application date, the candidate must satisfy the applicable route through **continuous advocacy, continuous judicial service, or qualifying combined advocacy and judicial-service experience**, amounting to at least **seven years**, and must have attained the prescribed **minimum age of 35 years**. The judgment must not be reduced either to the old *Dheeraj Mor* exclusion or to an unrestricted permission for every serving officer.

A separate Supreme Court order dated **11 March 2026** dealt with category-specific transitional relief for recruitments initiated under the earlier legal position. It directed consequential treatment, including appointments for identified eligible candidates, under the order’s recruitment-specific conditions. That transitional relief does not rewrite the general eligibility test for future advertisements.

Article 233A is a narrow validating provision protecting specified pre-1966 district-judge appointments, postings, promotions and judgments from invalidity on the historical ground that Articles 233 or 235 had not been followed. It is not a current general recruitment route.

Article 234 governs recruitment below district judge through Governor-made rules after consultation with the State Public Service Commission and High Court. *All India Judges Association v. Union of India* (20 May 2025; 2025 INSC 735) prospectively restored a minimum three-year Bar-practice condition for Civil Judge (Junior Division) examinations and revised recruitment/promotion directions. This is case law, not literal Article 234 text.

Article 235 vests posting, promotion, leave and disciplinary control in the High Court. It reduces executive career leverage over judges hearing government cases. Article 236 defines “district judge” inclusively—covering additional/joint/assistant district and sessions judges, city civil and small-cause leadership—and defines judicial service. Article 237 permits application of the chapter to classes of magistrates.

The District Judge is Sessions Judge on the criminal side, with original, appellate and administrative authority. A death sentence requires High Court confirmation. Below are State-varied civil and criminal tiers; metropolitan areas use City Civil Courts and Metropolitan Magistrates. Small Causes Courts and panchayat-level courts are statutory/local variations, not replacements for Articles 233–237.

Article 50 directs separation of judiciary from executive. It works with Articles 233–235: the State participates in appointment and funding, but adjudication and judicial careers cannot remain under ordinary executive command.

**Concrete example with limit:** A District Judge hearing a land-acquisition dispute against the State should not depend on the same State department for promotion or discipline; High Court control reduces that leverage. **Limit:** independence does not free the judge from binding precedent, procedure or disciplinary accountability.

**Strong objection:** Consultation-heavy recruitment causes delay and diffuses responsibility.

**Reply:** Shared appointment prevents unilateral patronage; the cure is a published calendar, interoperable vacancy data and defined timelines, not executive dominance.

**Residual:** Federal participation and judicial control remain in tension because States fund and recruit while High Courts protect decisional independence.

“Higher” and “wider” answer different questions. Article 32 is guaranteed and nationally apex; Article 226 covers more categories of right. Both courts are courts of record and review constitutionality, but only High Courts supervise and control the State judicial hierarchy under Articles 227 and 235.

Tribunals under Articles 323A/323B offer specialisation, but *L. Chandra Kumar* invalidated exclusion of High Court/Supreme Court review. Tribunal decisions remain subject to Articles 226/227, ordinarily through a High Court Division Bench before Supreme Court access. Review should focus on legality, rights and jurisdiction rather than automatically repeating facts.

High Courts also serve federalism: they review State and Union action connected to their territory and protect locally rooted judicial administration. Centralised recruitment or tribunal systems must not sideline Articles 233–235.

**Concrete example with limit:** A service tribunal may decide a Central employee’s technical service dispute first, while the territorial High Court later reviews jurisdiction or natural justice. **Limit:** the High Court should not routinely rehear the entire service record merely because another conclusion is possible.

**Strong objection:** High Court review recreates delay and defeats specialised tribunals.

**Reply:** Focused review is the constitutional price of legality; tribunal quality, reasoned decisions and restrained review can preserve speed.

**Residual:** Specialisation solves expertise gaps, not the legitimacy problem of making executive-created adjudication finally unreviewable.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Institution and judges

**Question:** Separate Article 217 text from collegium doctrine.

**Model answer:** The article provides presidential appointment after consultation; the initiating High Court process, judicial primacy and CJI-plus-two collegium arise from Judges Cases.

**Question:** Why are Articles 222, 224 and 224A not interchangeable?

**Model answer:** They govern transfer of a serving judge, temporary additional/acting appointments, and temporary sitting of a former judge respectively.

## Jurisdiction

**Question:** A civil-court order is allegedly wrong and an appeal exists. What routes must be distinguished?

**Model answer:** Statutory appeal is the normal merits route; Article 226 is ordinarily unavailable against the judicial order, while Article 227 remains exceptional supervision.

**Question:** State exactly how Article 226 is wider than Article 32.

**Model answer:** It enforces Fundamental Rights and ordinary legal rights; Article 32 is confined to Fundamental Rights.

## Subordinate justice and reform

**Question:** Divide authority under Articles 233–235.

**Model answer:** Article 233 uses Governor plus High Court consultation for district judges; Article 234 uses Governor-made rules plus SPSC and High Court consultation; Article 235 vests control in the High Court.

**Question:** Why is AIJS possible but not automatically compatible with the current structure?

**Model answer:** Article 312 permits it at district-judge level, but design must preserve High Court control, State participation and language/local-law competence.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks / 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish Article 226 from Article 227.

**Model answer:** Article 226 provides directions, orders and writs for Fundamental Rights and other legal rights. Article 227 gives superintendence over courts and tribunals, excluding Armed-Forces-law forums, and may operate suo motu. The first is a constitutional remedy; the second maintains institutional and jurisdictional discipline. *Radhey Shyam* keeps civil-court judicial orders outside ordinary Article 226 challenge, while *K. Valarmathi* prevents Article 227 from replacing trial and appeal. Neither is a routine factual rehearing. Article 226 is broader in rights-purpose; Article 227 is supervisory and sparingly used.

**Rubric (10):** definitions 3 · functional contrast 3 · cases 2 · limit/conclusion 2.

## 15 marks / 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate High Court independence safeguards against contemporary concerns.

**Model answer:** Article 217 appointment consultation, tenure until 62, difficult removal, protected service conditions, charged expenditure, Article 229 staff autonomy, Article 215 record/contempt authority and Articles 226–227 review protect High Courts from direct executive control. Article 220 limits post-retirement practice; Articles 50 and 235 extend independence to the district judiciary.

Operational deficits remain. Collegium opacity affects accountability and diversity; unexplained transfer can create apprehension; vacancies weaken practical remedies—the 1 July 2026 snapshot recorded 341 High Court vacancies. Internal administrative fairness and post-retirement appointments also shape public confidence. Reform should establish vacancy calendars and a permanent secretariat, publish criteria and reasons within privacy limits, track diversity, ensure fair transfer procedure and provide adequate staff/infrastructure. Accountability should improve process without giving the executive a controlling role over its reviewer. The constitutional framework is strong, but legitimacy depends on transparent, timely and inclusive implementation.

**Rubric (15):** safeguards 6 · critique 4 · evidence 1 · reforms 3 · verdict 1.

## 20 marks / 250 words

**Question:** Discuss the High Court as guardian, supervisor and administrator of the State judiciary, and suggest reforms preserving independence and efficiency.

**Model answer:** The High Court performs three connected roles. As guardian, Article 226 protects Fundamental Rights and other legal rights, and judicial review controls unconstitutional legislative or executive action. As supervisor, Article 227 keeps courts and tribunals within jurisdiction, while Article 228 permits withdrawal of cases involving a substantial constitutional-interpretation question. As administrator, Article 229 protects its establishment and Article 235 places subordinate-court control in the High Court. Civil and criminal appeals, including death-sentence confirmation, connect constitutional authority to trial justice; Article 215 secures record, precedent and contempt power.

These roles need independence. Article 217 consultation, secure tenure, difficult removal, protected service conditions, charged expenditure and bounded transfer power reduce external pressure. At the subordinate level, Articles 233–234 require structured consultation, while Article 235 limits executive career leverage. Yet the system faces vacancies, uneven recruitment, opaque procedure, government litigation, infrastructure deficits, weak legal aid and digital exclusion. Tribunalisation cannot exclude review after *L. Chandra Kumar*; supervision cannot become an appeal after *K. Valarmathi*.

Reform should combine vacancy calendars and timely appointments with transparent criteria and reasons within privacy limits. Article 235-compatible court management, age-based tracking, restrained government appeals, e-Courts with e-Sewa assistance and competent legal aid can improve delivery. Any AIJS must respect Article 312(3), language, State law and High Court control. Efficiency means timely, reasoned and accessible justice—not disposal obtained by weakening hearing quality or judicial independence.

**Rubric (20):** roles 8 · independence 3 · cases/constraints 4 · reforms 4 · conclusion 1.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional spine

- Articles 214–231: High Courts; Articles 233–237: subordinate courts.
- Integrated hierarchy does not erase High Court constitutional autonomy.
- Article 231 permits common High Courts; India has 25.
- Article 230 covers Union-Territory jurisdiction.

## Judges and independence

- Article 216: variable strength.
- Article 217: President appoints after consultation; collegium detail comes from Judges Cases.
- Eligibility: citizen + ten years’ judicial office or advocacy; no distinguished-jurist route.
- Article 219 oath before Governor/authorised person.
- Retirement 62; removal for proved misbehaviour/incapacity by special process.
- Article 222 transfer; Articles 223, 224, 224A continuity arrangements.
- Article 220 post-retirement practice restriction; Article 229 staff autonomy.
- Independence also rests on charged expenditure, protected conditions, contempt and Article 50.

## Jurisdiction

- Original, appellate, writ, supervisory, Article 235 control, record and review.
- Death sentence requires High Court confirmation.
- Article 215: record and contempt.
- Article 226: FR and ordinary rights; cause-of-action territory under clause (2).
- Writs: detention, public duty, preventive control, quashing, public office.
- *Whirlpool*: alternative-remedy exceptions.
- *Radhey Shyam*: civil-court orders not ordinary Article 226 targets.
- Article 227: broad superintendence, sparing exercise; *K. Valarmathi* protects trial/appeal.
- Article 228: necessary substantial constitutional question.
- *L. Chandra Kumar*: review over tribunals is basic structure.

## Subordinate judiciary

- Article 233: Governor plus High Court consultation for district judges.
- *Rejanish K.V.* judgment, 9 October 2025: contrary precedent/rules displaced; seven years of qualifying continuous advocacy, judicial service or combined experience on application date and minimum age 35.
- Supreme Court order, 11 March 2026: category-specific transitional relief and consequential appointments for the earlier recruitment.
- Article 234: Governor-made rules after SPSC and High Court consultation.
- 2025 *All India Judges Association*: prospective three-year Bar practice for Civil Judge entry.
- Article 235: High Court control; Article 236 definitions; Article 237 magistrates.
- District Judge = Sessions Judge; lower nomenclature varies by State.
- Article 50 separates judiciary from executive.

## Reform and access

- 1 July 2026: 1,122 sanctioned, 781 working, 341 vacancies.
- Article 312 permits AIJS; Article 312(3) excludes below-district-judge posts; no operational service verified.
- e-Courts Phase III: 2023–2027, about ₹7,210 crore; assisted access remains necessary.
- Article 39A and the 1987 Act create NALSA/SLSA/HCLSC/DLSA/Taluk support.
- Lok Adalat settles civil and compoundable criminal disputes, not non-compoundable offences.
