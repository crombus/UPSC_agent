*Caption: An ombudsman is the answer to a structural gap — not a general complaint box, but a dedicated channel for allegations against the powerful.*

## Plain-language intuition

Imagine you suspect a minister or a senior officer has taken a bribe. Normally, the
police who would investigate that minister report, through several layers, to the same
government the minister belongs to. That creates an obvious conflict: the people with
the power to investigate may also answer to the people being investigated.

The **ombudsman** idea solves this by creating a separate office — independent of the
day-to-day executive hierarchy — whose entire job is to receive and process complaints
about abuse of public power. India's version of this office, for corruption
specifically, is the **Lokpal** (at the Union level) and the **Lokayukta** (at the
State level).

## Technical doctrine: what exactly is the Lokpal?

✅ **Fact:** The Lokpal is a **statutory body** — created by an ordinary Act of
Parliament, the **Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013**. It is **not a constitutional
body**: it does not appear in the Constitution, and it is not a court.

✅ **Fact:** Section 2 of the Act defines a "complaint" narrowly — it is a complaint,
made in the prescribed form, alleging that a covered public servant has committed an
offence that is punishable under the **Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988**. This
single definitional choice makes the Lokpal **corruption-specific**, not a general
public-grievance body.

⚠️ **Inference:** Because the complaint is tied to a named criminal statute (the PC
Act), the Lokpal's entire machinery — inquiry, investigation, sanction, prosecution —
exists to move a corruption allegation toward a courtroom, not to resolve ordinary
service complaints (delayed pension, poor road repair, and so on).

## The historical argument: why did it take 45 years?

✅ **Fact:** The modern ombudsman office is conventionally traced to **Sweden, 1809**.

✅ **Fact:** India's **First Administrative Reforms Commission (1966-70)** recommended
two institutions for redress of citizens' grievances: a **Lokpal** at the Union level
and **Lokayuktas** at the State level.

✅ **Fact:** Beginning in **1968**, a long series of Lokpal Bills were introduced in
Parliament. Each one lapsed, was withdrawn, or failed to complete enactment — **ten
Bills** across more than four decades.

✅ **Fact:** The **2011 Anna Hazare / India Against Corruption** movement created
intense political pressure that formed part of the context in which the eventual Bill
was finally debated and passed.

✅ **Fact:** The **Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013** received Presidential assent on
**1 January 2014** and commenced on **16 January 2014** through Gazette notification.

```text
1809 Sweden        1966-70 First ARC       1968-2011         2011            2013/2014
ombudsman    -->    recommends Lokpal  --> Ten Bills    -->  Anna Hazare --> Act passed,
born                 + Lokayukta             lapse             movement       assented,
                                                                              commenced
```

⚠️ **Inference:** The forty-five-year delay is not accidental. Every Bill had to solve
the same hard design problem — how to create an institution powerful enough to
scrutinise the Prime Minister, Ministers and senior officials, without turning it into
an unaccountable super-agency that merges investigator, prosecutor and judge into one
body. That design tension explains almost every feature you will study in this topic.

⚠️ **Inference:** Civil-society mobilisation explains why the Bill finally passed when
it did; it is not itself the *legal* source of the institution. The Act, its Rules and
judicial interpretation control the Lokpal's actual powers — not the political movement
that preceded it.

## Comparison: Lokpal is not the only accountability device

| Model | What it mainly does | Typical output | Is this the Lokpal? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical ombudsman | Investigates maladministration/unfairness | Recommendation or report | Historical inspiration only |
| Public-grievance portal | Handles service-delivery complaints | Administrative redress | No — outside Lokpal's statutory core |
| Vigilance institution (CVC) | Departmental integrity and vigilance advice | Advisory/disciplinary route | No — a distinct, CVC-linked layer |
| Police/CBI investigation | Collects evidence for a criminal offence | Charge-sheet | No — an assigned investigating agency |
| **Lokpal** | Screens a *prescribed corruption complaint*, routes it through inquiry/investigation, can sanction and direct prosecution | Routing, supervision, sanction, prosecution direction | **Yes** |
| Special Court | Criminal adjudication | Conviction/acquittal | No — the final judicial stage |

*Caption: Calling every accountability mechanism an "ombudsman" hides the sharp legal differences between them. The Lokpal is a hybrid: more powerful than a classical ombudsman, but still short of a court.*

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "If corruption was already a crime under the Prevention of Corruption
Act, and the police/CBI could already investigate it, why was a whole new institution
needed?"

**Reply:** Ordinary criminal investigation of a minister or senior official runs through
agencies that ultimately answer to the same executive. Without a statutorily
independent body to receive the complaint, decide whether to proceed, and supervise the
investigating agency for that specific case, there was no credible guarantee that a
serious allegation against the powerful would even reach the investigation stage. The
Lokpal does not replace the Prevention of Corruption Act or the CBI; it adds an
independent screening and supervisory layer in front of them.

**Residual:** This reply does not mean the Lokpal has solved the problem completely —
you will see in later lessons that its own effectiveness still depends heavily on the
same investigating agencies (principally the CBI) whose capture problem it was designed
to counter-balance.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** Exact dates (1809 Sweden; 1966-70 First ARC; 2013/2014 Act), the number of
Bills before 2013, and especially the correct characterisation of Lokpal as **statutory,
not constitutional** — this single trap recurs across years.

**Mains:** A frequent framing is "trace the evolution of the Lokpal" or "the long delay
in enacting the 2013 Act reflects a deeper institutional design problem — comment."
Structure such an answer as: origin idea → First ARC recommendation → repeated Bills →
2011 catalyst → 2013 Act → the underlying design tension (power vs. accountability) that
explains the delay.

**Common trap:** Treating the Anna Hazare movement as the *legal source* of the Lokpal.
It is political context; the Act itself is the legal source.

## Revision notes

1. The ombudsman idea originates in **Sweden, 1809**.
2. India's **First ARC (1966-70)** first recommended a Lokpal (Centre) and Lokayuktas (States).
3. **Ten Bills** were introduced between 1968 and 2011 before the law finally passed.
4. The **2011 Anna Hazare movement** supplied political context, not legal authority.
5. The **Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013** received assent on **1 January 2014**.
6. It **commenced on 16 January 2014** by Gazette notification.
7. The Lokpal is a **statutory body** — not constitutional, not a court.
8. Section 2 defines "complaint" as an allegation of a **Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988** offence by a covered public servant.
9. This definition makes the Lokpal **corruption-specific**, not a general grievance body.
10. The forty-five-year delay reflects a genuine design tension: strong enough to scrutinise high office, not so strong as to merge investigator, prosecutor and judge.
11. The Lokpal supplements, but does not replace, the CBI and the Prevention of Corruption Act framework.

### Concept check

**Question:** A classmate says, "The Lokpal was created by a constitutional amendment, just like the Finance Commission." What is wrong with this statement, and what is the correct position?

**Model answer:** The Lokpal is **not** a constitutional body — it was created by an ordinary Act of Parliament, the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, which commenced in January 2014. It does not appear in the Constitution's text, unlike the Finance Commission (Article 280). The correct position is that the Lokpal is a **statutory anti-corruption ombudsman**, created and defined entirely by this 2013 Act and its subordinate Rules.

**Misconception to avoid:** Students often assume every major accountability institution with "constitutional-sounding" powers (binding directions, judicial-style procedure) must itself be a constitutional body. Legal source (statute vs Constitution) and functional importance are independent questions — the Lokpal is functionally significant but constitutionally silent.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** "The forty-five-year delay between the First ARC's recommendation and the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 reflects a genuine institutional design problem, not mere political inertia." Examine.

**Mains model answer:** The First ARC recommended a Lokpal in 1966-70, yet ten Bills lapsed between 1968 and 2013. This was not simple inertia: every draft had to resolve how an institution could scrutinise the Prime Minister, Ministers and senior officials without itself becoming an unaccountable body combining investigation, prosecution and adjudication. The 2011 Anna Hazare movement supplied the political momentum that finally forced resolution, but the resulting 2013 Act still reflects the underlying caution — a capped, diverse membership, PM safeguards, no suo motu power and continued dependence on the CBI for investigation. The delay therefore mirrors a real constitutional-design anxiety about concentrating anti-corruption power, which the final statute only partially resolved rather than eliminated.

**Scoring rubric:** Evolution timeline accurately stated (3 marks) + design-tension argument, not mere "political will" narrative (4 marks) + link to at least one enacted safeguard as evidence of the caution (2 marks) + qualified conclusion (1 mark).

*Caption: Every lesson that follows is a close-up of one box in this diagram. Memorise this map first — the rest of the topic is detail, not a new structure.*

## Plain-language intuition

Why not give the Lokpal one single unified power to investigate, decide and punish —
the way a strong police commissioner might? Because concentrating accusation,
investigation, prosecution and adjudication in one hand is exactly the danger the
institution's designers were trying to avoid (recall Lesson 1). Instead, the Act
**deliberately splits** the process into distinct legal stages, each with a different
actor and a different legal task.

## Technical doctrine: the five legally distinct stages

✅ **Fact:** The Act creates a multi-stage system:

1. **Lokpal bench** decides how a complaint proceeds (screening/scrutiny).
2. The **Inquiry Wing** (or CVC, or an agency) conducts **preliminary inquiry**.
3. An **investigating agency** (commonly the CBI/DSPE) conducts **investigation**.
4. The **Prosecution Wing** or agency **prosecutes**.
5. The **Special Court** **adjudicates** — decides guilt or innocence.

| Stage | Principal actor | Legal task | What it does **not** decide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Filing/scrutiny | Registry and Lokpal bench | Form, jurisdiction, maintainability | Criminal guilt |
| Preliminary inquiry | Inquiry Wing/CVC/agency | Whether a prima facie case exists | Final culpability |
| Investigation | CBI/DSPE or other agency | Collect admissible evidence | Conviction |
| Post-report decision | At least three Lokpal Members | Sanction, prosecution/closure, departmental route | Trial verdict |
| Prosecution | Prosecution Wing/agency | Present the case before the Special Court | Judicial outcome |
| Trial | Special Court | Decide charges on the evidence | Institutional policy |

*Caption: "Lokpal investigated and convicted the official" is an invalid compression of at least four separate legal stages.*

## Example: following one complaint through the pipeline

A complaint alleges a Group A officer demanded a bribe. The Registry checks the
prescribed form; the bench decides a **preliminary inquiry** is warranted and refers it
to the CVC (because the officer is Group A). The CVC reports back finding a prima facie
case. A three-Member bench then orders **investigation** by the CBI. The CBI's report
goes to the court with a copy to Lokpal; a three-Member bench considers it and grants
**sanction** to prosecute. The **Prosecution Wing** files the case. The **Special
Court** tries it and eventually convicts or acquits.

⚠️ **Limit of the example:** Not every complaint travels the full chain — many are
closed after preliminary inquiry for want of a prima facie case, and the example above
assumes no procedural defect at any stage.

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "This dispersal of power across so many actors must make the process
painfully slow — surely a single empowered body would deliver justice faster?"

**Reply:** Speed is not the only value at stake. A single empowered body able to accuse,
investigate, prosecute and convict in one motion would be far more vulnerable to error,
political misuse, or simple mistake without any internal check. The staged design
trades some speed for institutional safety: each stage's output is reviewed by a
different actor before the process can move forward.

**Residual:** The trade-off is genuine, not merely theoretical — multi-stage routing
does create real delay and coordination risk, which later lessons on timelines and
institutional capacity will examine honestly rather than explain away.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** Identifying which actor performs which stage (a frequent close-option
trap: confusing the Inquiry Wing's preliminary-inquiry role with the CBI's
investigation role).

**Mains:** This big-picture map is the natural **answer spine** for almost any Lokpal
question: "explain" questions should walk through this pipeline; "critically examine"
questions should use it to locate exactly where the institution's strengths and
weaknesses sit.

## Revision notes

1. The Act creates **five legally distinct stages**: scrutiny, preliminary inquiry, investigation, post-report decision, prosecution/trial.
2. Different actors perform each stage — no actor performs all of them.
3. A **three-Member bench** considers both the preliminary-inquiry report and the investigation report.
4. "Investigated and convicted" improperly compresses at least four separate stages.
5. The dispersal of power is a deliberate design choice, not accidental complexity.
6. The trade-off is safety against concentration of power, at the cost of speed and coordination simplicity.
7. This pipeline diagram is the master map for every later lesson in this topic.
8. Preliminary inquiry and investigation are not the same thing (explored fully in Lesson 12).
9. Sanction and prosecution are not the same as conviction (explored fully in Lesson 13).

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the Act require a *three-Member bench*, rather than a single Member, to decide what happens after a preliminary inquiry or investigation report?

**Model answer:** Requiring at least three Members to jointly consider the report before deciding on investigation, departmental action, closure, or sanction builds a collective check into the Lokpal's own internal decision-making — preventing a single Member from unilaterally controlling the fate of a case, consistent with the Act's broader strategy of never concentrating power in one hand at any single stage.

**Misconception to avoid:** Students sometimes think "the Lokpal decided" means a single Chairperson-level decision. In fact, most substantive post-report decisions require a multi-Member bench, mirroring the broader multi-actor design of the whole pipeline.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain why the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 distributes complaint-processing power across several distinct actors rather than vesting it in a single body.

**Mains model answer:** The Act splits the process into scrutiny, preliminary inquiry, investigation, post-report decision and prosecution/trial, with a different actor responsible for each — the Registry and bench, the Inquiry Wing or CVC, the CBI/DSPE, a three-Member bench, and the Prosecution Wing and Special Court respectively. This dispersal directly answers the historical anxiety that produced the 2013 Act: a single all-powerful anti-corruption body could itself become unaccountable, merging accusation, investigation, prosecution and judgment. By contrast, the staged design builds in review at every transition, at the cost of coordination delay and inter-agency dependence, which remain genuine operational weaknesses rather than purely theoretical risks.

**Scoring rubric:** Names at least four distinct stages/actors correctly (4 marks) + explicit design rationale (not concentrating power) (3 marks) + honest cost/trade-off noted (2 marks) + clear structure (1 mark).

## 🖼️ Visual: Two separate percentage rules

```text
                         LOKPAL
                           |
          +----------------+----------------+
          |                                 |
     CHAIRPERSON                      MEMBERS: <= 8
    (one person)                            |
                         +-------------------+-------------------+
                         |                                       |
                50% of MEMBERS                           remaining Members
                must be JUDICIAL                          (non-judicial route)

ACROSS ALL MEMBERS (a separate, overlapping calculation):
not less than 50% must come from SC / ST / OBC / minorities / women
```

*Caption: Two different 50% rules apply to the same pool of Members — one about judicial background, one about social representation. They can overlap in the same person, but they are not the same rule.*

## Plain-language intuition

If you were designing a body to judge serious corruption allegations against powerful
people, you would want two things simultaneously: enough legal/judicial rigor to make
its findings credible, and enough social breadth that it does not look or feel like an
exclusive club. Section 3 tries to engineer both at once, inside a small, capped body.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** Section 3 provides a **Chairperson plus not more than eight Members** — a
ceiling of nine people total, not a fixed or guaranteed number.

✅ **Fact:** **Fifty per cent of the Members shall be Judicial Members.** This
percentage is calculated over the **Members**, not over a denominator that
automatically includes the Chairperson.

✅ **Fact:** **Not less than fifty per cent of the Members** must be drawn from persons
belonging to **Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, minorities
and women.**

⚠️ **Inference:** These are two **different** calculations that happen to share the
same pool (the Members). A judicial Member who is also a woman, for instance, can
satisfy part of both quotas simultaneously — but satisfying one rule never
automatically satisfies the other.

⚠️ **Limit:** "Maximum eight" is a ceiling, not a permanent guarantee that all eight
seats are always filled. This topic does not freeze the current occupancy of any seat —
vacancy is a live administrative fact, not a legal rule.

| Rule | Denominator | Exact language | Wrong shortcut to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Judicial membership | Members | 50% shall be Judicial Members | "At least half of the whole Lokpal, including the Chairperson" |
| Social representation | Members | Not less than 50% from the listed categories | "Exactly half, always including the Chairperson" |
| Total size | Members | Not exceeding eight | "Always exactly eight Members" |

## Example

If the Lokpal currently has eight Members, four must be Judicial Members, and at least
four Members (who may or may not be the same four) must satisfy the SC/ST/OBC/minority/
women representation condition. If only six Member seats happen to be filled at a given
time, the 50% calculations apply to that filled number of six, not to the statutory
ceiling of eight.

## Comparison: a capped-and-quota body versus an open-ended one

| Feature | Lokpal (Section 3 model) | A hypothetical open-ended anti-corruption body |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Capped at Chairperson + 8 | No ceiling |
| Judicial share | Fixed 50% of Members | Unregulated |
| Social representation | Statutory floor (50%) | Unregulated |
| Predictability | High — composition rules are fixed in advance | Low — depends entirely on executive discretion each time |

*Caption: A capped, quota-based composition trades flexibility for predictability and legitimacy.*

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "A judicial-majority-leaning, quota-constrained body of at most nine
people seems too small and too constrained to handle serious national corruption work."

**Reply:** The small, carefully quota-bound bench is itself a safeguard — it prevents
an unwieldy body whose internal composition could be manipulated piecemeal, and ensures
that every important decision is made by individuals meeting a demonstrated threshold of
judicial rigor and representative diversity, rather than by an unlimited executive
appointee pool.

**Residual:** A large bench of this kind has, in practice, also proved slow to
constitute and staff fully — a real operational cost that the statutory design does not
by itself solve.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The sharpest trap is converting "50% of Members" into "50% of the whole
institution including the Chairperson," or converting "not exceeding eight" into
"exactly eight." The verified 2025 Prelims PYQ (see the Final PYQ Index after Lesson
19) directly tests a closely related composition statement.

**Mains:** Composition questions are typically paired with eligibility (Lesson 4) and
appointment (Lesson 5) to build a complete "who sits on the Lokpal and how" answer.

## Revision notes

1. Lokpal = **one Chairperson + not more than eight Members** (ceiling of nine total).
2. **50% of Members** must be **Judicial Members** — denominator is Members, not the whole institution.
3. **Not less than 50% of Members** must satisfy the **SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women** representation floor.
4. The two 50% rules are separate and can overlap but are not identical.
5. "Not exceeding eight" is a ceiling, not a guaranteed fixed number.
6. This topic does not freeze current seat occupancy or vacancies.
7. A large judicial-social-diversity-bound bench is slow to constitute but resistant to manipulation.
8. Composition questions connect directly to eligibility (Lesson 4) and appointment (Lesson 5).

### Concept check

**Question:** If the Lokpal currently has only six filled Member seats (out of a ceiling of eight), how many must be Judicial Members under the statutory rule?

**Model answer:** The 50% judicial-membership rule applies to the **actual filled Member strength**, not the statutory ceiling of eight. With six filled seats, at least three must be Judicial Members (50% of six), since the rule's denominator is "Members," which in ordinary statutory reading means the Members currently constituting the body, not a hypothetical maximum.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating "50% of Members" as always meaning "four out of eight" regardless of actual strength is a common and incorrect shortcut; the correct anchor is the actual composition at any given time, subject to the ceiling.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish between the "judicial membership" rule and the "social representation" rule in the composition of the Lokpal under Section 3.

**Mains model answer:** Section 3 imposes two distinct, overlapping requirements on the same pool of Members. First, fifty per cent of the Members must be Judicial Members — former Supreme Court Judges or former High Court Chief Justices — ensuring adjudicatory rigor in a body that screens serious corruption allegations. Second, not less than fifty per cent of the Members must belong to Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, minorities or women — a social-representation floor rare among statutory oversight bodies. Both percentages use "Members," not the whole institution including the Chairperson, as their denominator. A single individual may satisfy both conditions simultaneously, but meeting one never automatically satisfies the other; they protect different values — professional rigor and representative legitimacy.

**Scoring rubric:** Both rules stated with correct percentage and denominator (5 marks) + explicit statement that they are distinct, overlappable rules (3 marks) + value each rule protects (2 marks).

## 🖼️ Visual: Two gateways — eligibility route, then a disqualification screen

```text

  current/former CJI  --OR--  current/former SC Judge  --OR--  eminent person
```

## Plain-language intuition

Two separate questions determine whether someone can sit on the Lokpal. First: do they
have the right background (judicial office or long specialised expertise)? Second,
independently: is there any disqualifying fact about them (sitting legislator, criminal
conviction for moral turpitude, dismissal from service, too young)? A person can be
perfectly qualified on the first question and still be disqualified on the second.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** A **Chairperson** may be a person who **is or has been** Chief Justice of
India, **is or has been** a Supreme Court Judge, or an **eminent person** satisfying the
non-judicial expertise condition.

✅ **Fact:** A **Judicial Member** must be or have been a **Supreme Court Judge** or a
**Chief Justice of a High Court**.

✅ **Fact:** A **non-judicial Member** must be a person of **impeccable integrity and
outstanding ability** with at least **twenty-five years** of special knowledge and
expertise in anti-corruption policy, public administration, vigilance, finance
(including insurance and banking), law and management.

✅ **Fact:** No appointee — Chairperson or Member — may be below **forty-five years**
on assuming office.

✅ **Fact:** Section 3 also **excludes**: an MP; a State/UT legislator; a Panchayat or
Municipality member; a person convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude; and a
person removed or dismissed from Union or State service.

✅ **Fact:** An appointee cannot hold **another office of trust or profit**, be
affiliated with a **political party**, carry on **business**, or practise a
**profession**; any existing conflicting engagement must be **severed before entry
into office**.

| Office | Eligible background | Experience threshold |
|---|---|---:|
| Chairperson — judicial route | Current/former CJI or current/former SC Judge | Office-based |
| Chairperson — eminent-person route | Integrity and outstanding ability in listed fields | At least 25 years |
| Judicial Member | Current/former SC Judge or current/former HC Chief Justice | Office-based |
| Non-judicial Member | Listed anti-corruption/governance/finance/law/management expertise | At least 25 years |
| Every appointee | Must be at least 45 on assuming office | Age floor |

## Example

A retired Chief Secretary with twenty-eight years of public administration experience,
impeccable integrity, and no disqualifying history can be appointed a non-judicial
Member — this person was never a judge, yet is fully eligible through the
"eminent/expert" non-judicial route.

⚠️ **Limit of the example:** The same person, if still a sitting MLA or holding any
other office of profit, would need to resign that office before entering Lokpal
office — eligibility credentials alone do not override the disqualification screen.

## Comparison: Chairperson eligibility is wider than many students assume

| Common assumption | Correct position |
|---|---|
| Only a serving/retired CJI can chair the Lokpal | A current/former CJI, a current/former SC Judge, **or** a qualifying eminent person may chair it |
| A Judicial Member must be a sitting judge | Judicial Members are drawn from **current or former** SC Judges/HC Chief Justices |
| Any retired bureaucrat qualifies as a non-judicial Member | Only those meeting the **25-year expertise + impeccable integrity** threshold qualify |

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "Allowing a non-judicial 'eminent person' to chair an institution with
such serious anti-corruption powers dilutes judicial rigor at the very top."

**Reply:** The eminent-person route is not a dilution — it is bounded by an explicit,
demanding statutory standard (integrity, outstanding ability, twenty-five years of
named expertise), and it widens the talent pool beyond the judiciary to include deeply
experienced administrators, vigilance specialists and finance experts whose domain
knowledge may be directly relevant to complex corruption cases.

**Residual:** Whether this widened pool is used well in practice depends on the
Selection Committee's actual choices (studied in Lesson 5) — the eligibility rule only
sets the outer boundary of who *can* be chosen, not who *will* be chosen.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** "Only the Chief Justice of India can be Chairperson" is a classic false
statement in objective questions generally — recheck both eligibility routes (judicial
and eminent-person) in the table above before evaluating any such claim.

**Mains:** Eligibility criteria pair naturally with composition (Lesson 3) to answer
"evaluate the design of the Lokpal's membership" questions.

## Revision notes

1. Chairperson eligibility has **two routes**: judicial (current/former CJI or SC Judge) or eminent-person (25 years expertise).
2. Judicial Members: current/former SC Judge or current/former HC Chief Justice.
3. Non-judicial Members: impeccable integrity, outstanding ability, 25+ years in named fields.
4. Minimum age for every appointee is **45** on assuming office.
5. Disqualifications: sitting MP/State-UT legislator, Panchayat/Municipality member, moral-turpitude conviction, removal/dismissal from service.
6. Political affiliation, business, profession, or other office of profit must be severed before entry.
7. Eligibility credentials never cure an express disqualification — both tests apply independently.
8. The Chairperson is **not** restricted to only a serving or retired CJI — this is a classic exam trap.
9. The eligibility rule sets the *outer boundary* of eligible persons; actual selection is a separate, later step.

### Concept check

**Question:** A sitting Municipal Councillor who is also a retired High Court Chief Justice with impeccable integrity is proposed as a Judicial Member. Can this person be appointed as-is?

**Model answer:** No. Although the person clearly satisfies the **eligibility route** for a Judicial Member (retired HC Chief Justice), Section 3's **disqualification screen** separately bars a sitting Panchayat or Municipality member from appointment. The person would first have to resign the Municipal Councillor position before being eligible for appointment — meeting the eligibility route does not cure an active disqualification.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that strong eligibility credentials automatically override a disqualifying fact. The two tests are independent and both must be satisfied.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** "Eligibility and disqualification operate as two independent gates for appointment to the Lokpal." Explain with reference to Section 3.

**Mains model answer:** Section 3 first defines who is eligible: a Chairperson may be a current/former Chief Justice of India, a current/former Supreme Court Judge, or an eminent person with twenty-five years of expertise in named fields; Judicial Members are current/former Supreme Court Judges or High Court Chief Justices; non-judicial Members need the same twenty-five-year expertise threshold with impeccable integrity. Independently, Section 3 disqualifies sitting legislators at any level, local-body members, persons convicted of moral turpitude, and those removed or dismissed from government service, while requiring severance of political affiliation, business or other profitable office before entry. A person may be fully eligible on credentials yet barred by a live disqualification, so both gates must be checked separately before appointment, not merged into a single eligibility test.

## 🖼️ Visual: Three separate legal acts, not one

```text
SEARCH COMMITTEE
at least 7 experts, >= 50% diversity floor
prepares a PANEL (not exclusive)
        |
        v
SELECTION COMMITTEE
PM (chair) + LS Speaker + LS LoP + CJI/SC-Judge nominee + eminent jurist

        v

```

*Caption: Search identifies candidates. Selection recommends. The President appoints. Collapsing these into one act is the most common error on this topic.*

## Plain-language intuition

Appointing someone to a body that can investigate the Prime Minister is sensitive by
design. The Act tries to avoid both "the executive just picks whoever it wants" and
"no appointment is ever possible because some seat is vacant." It does this through a
layered process with a built-in fail-safe for vacancies.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** The **President** appoints the Chairperson and Members, **on the
recommendation of the Selection Committee**.

✅ **Fact:** Section 4 names **five** Selection Committee positions:

1. **Prime Minister** — Chairperson of the Committee;
2. **Speaker of the Lok Sabha**;
3. **Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha**;
4. **Chief Justice of India**, or a Supreme Court Judge nominated by the CJI; and
5. **one eminent jurist**, nominated by the **President** on the recommendation of the
   first four.

✅ **Fact:** The current statutory text names the **Leader of Opposition**. It does
**not** insert "the leader of the single largest opposition party" as a substitute seat.

✅ **Fact:** Section 4(2) provides that **no appointment is invalid merely because of a
vacancy** in the Selection Committee.

✅ **Fact:** The Selection Committee constitutes a **Search Committee of at least seven
persons** of standing and relevant expertise, of whom not less than fifty per cent must
come from the same diversity categories (SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women). The Selection
Committee **may consider a person not recommended** by the Search Committee.

| Seat | Status under Section 4 | Frequent confusion |
|---|---|---|
| Prime Minister | Chairperson of Selection Committee | Not the President |
| Lok Sabha Speaker | Member | Not the Rajya Sabha Chairman |
| Lok Sabha Leader of Opposition | Member | No largest-party substitute in the text |
| CJI or CJI-nominated SC Judge | Member | Not any judge chosen by the executive |
| Eminent jurist | President nominates, on recommendation of first four | Not independently chosen by the President |

## The *Common Cause* (2017) argument

```text
NO RECOGNISED LoP IN THE LOK SABHA
              |
Section 4(2): appointment not invalid merely because of a Selection
              Committee vacancy
              |
Common Cause v Union of India (2017):
truncated Selection Committee can appoint the eminent jurist,
constitute the Search Committee and recommend appointments
              |
RESULT: the Act remains workable; a vacancy cannot suspend enforcement
```

✅ **Fact:** The Supreme Court held the Act **"eminently workable"** and rejected the
claim that enforcement had to wait for proposed amendments; it read Section 4(2) to
permit a truncated Selection Committee, absent a recognised Leader of Opposition, to
appoint the eminent jurist, constitute the Search Committee and recommend appointments.

⚠️ **Limit:** *Common Cause* did **not** rewrite Section 4 to create a substitute seat
for the leader of the largest opposition party. The judicial solution was
**workability through the vacancy clause**, not a new statutory seat.

## Comparison: Search Committee versus Selection Committee

| Feature | Search Committee | Selection Committee |
|---|---|---|
| Size | At least seven | Five statutory positions |
| Main task | Prepare a panel | Select and recommend |
| Diversity rule | Not less than 50% from listed categories | No identical quota on committee seats |
| Expertise | Anti-corruption, administration, vigilance, finance, law, management | Institutional office-holders + eminent jurist |
| Binding effect | Panel is **not exclusive** | May consider a person outside the Search panel |

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "With the Prime Minister chairing the Selection Committee, how
independent can Lokpal appointments really be?"

**Reply:** The Committee is deliberately plural — alongside the PM sit the Speaker, the
Leader of Opposition, the CJI (or nominee) and an independently nominated eminent
jurist — diluting pure executive control and requiring cross-institutional consensus
for every appointment.

**Residual:** The executive-political component (PM plus Speaker, both typically from
the ruling coalition) remains substantial, and the Search Committee's panel is not
binding — so the degree of real independence ultimately depends on how the Committee
exercises its discretion, not merely on its formal composition.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The exact five Selection Committee seats, and the correct sequence
(Search → Selection → President), are frequent objective-question material.

**Mains:** "Evaluate whether the Lokpal's appointment process secures genuine
institutional independence" is a natural essay/GS-II question building directly on this
lesson plus Lesson 19's capstone synthesis.

## Revision notes

1. **President appoints**, on recommendation of the **Selection Committee**.
2. Selection Committee: **PM (chair) + LS Speaker + LS LoP + CJI/SC-Judge nominee + eminent jurist**.
3. The eminent jurist is nominated by the **President**, on the recommendation of the other four.
4. Current text names the **Leader of Opposition** — no largest-opposition-party substitute exists in the statute.
5. Section 4(2): a **vacancy does not invalidate** an appointment.
6. **Search Committee**: at least **seven** experts, with a 50% diversity floor.
7. The Search Committee's panel is **not exclusive** — the Selection Committee may look beyond it.
8. ***Common Cause* (2017)**: the Act is workable despite an LoP vacancy; a truncated Committee can still appoint the jurist, form the Search Committee, and recommend.
9. *Common Cause* did **not** create a new substitute seat.
10. Appointment is a three-step chain: Search → Selection (recommendation) → President (appointment) — never collapse these into one act.

### Concept check

**Question:** Suppose the Lok Sabha currently has no recognised Leader of Opposition. Does this prevent any new Lokpal appointment from being made?

**Model answer:** No. Section 4(2) expressly states that a vacancy in the Selection Committee does not invalidate an appointment, and the Supreme Court in *Common Cause v Union of India* (2017) confirmed that a truncated Selection Committee can still appoint the eminent jurist, constitute the Search Committee and recommend appointments. The Act remains workable despite the LoP vacancy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing that *Common Cause* inserted the leader of the single largest opposition party as a formal substitute for the LoP seat — the Court relied on the existing vacancy-saving clause, not a new statutory seat.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** How did the Supreme Court in *Common Cause v Union of India* (2017) address the absence of a recognised Leader of Opposition in Lokpal appointments?

**Mains model answer:** Facing the absence of a recognised Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, the Supreme Court held the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 "eminently workable" and rejected arguments that its enforcement must await legislative amendment. It read Section 4(2) — which states that a vacancy in the Selection Committee does not invalidate an appointment — to permit a truncated Selection Committee to nominate the eminent jurist, constitute the Search Committee, and recommend appointments despite the vacancy. ## Lesson 6: Tenure and Post-Tenure Restrictions

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Sections 6 and 8, Topic 38 package

📰 CA Found: None — tenure rules are fixed statutory text
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🖼️ Visual: The tenure clock and the post-tenure firewall

```text
ENTRY INTO OFFICE
       |
       +--> five-year period ends
       |
       +--> age 70 reached
       |
EARLIER EVENT ENDS TENURE

EXCEPTIONAL CAREER MOVE:
Member -> Chairperson permitted
only if aggregate Lokpal tenure <= 5 years

AFTER LEAVING OFFICE:
no reappointment (subject to the Member-to-Chair exception)
no diplomatic/UT administrator/other presidential-warrant post
no Union/State office of profit
no contesting President/VP/MP/State legislature/Municipality/Panchayat
for FIVE YEARS after relinquishing office
```

*Caption: "Five years" is subject to the age ceiling, and tenure is paired with a firm post-tenure cooling-off period.*

## Plain-language intuition

Two different risks threaten independence at the *exit* end of the tenure, not just at
appointment. First, a Member might make decisions designed to please a future
employer. Second, a Member might be effectively entrenched if they can expect automatic
reappointment. The Act's answer is a fixed exit point plus a hard post-tenure ban on the
most tempting future rewards.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** Section 6 fixes the term at **five years from entering office, or until age
seventy, whichever is earlier**.

✅ **Fact:** The Chairperson or a Member may **resign** to the President, or be
**removed under Section 37** (studied fully in Lesson 7).

✅ **Fact:** On leaving office, the Chairperson and every Member are **ineligible** for:
reappointment as Chairperson or Member; a diplomatic assignment; Union Territory
administrator; or another presidential-warrant assignment; and **further office of
profit** under the Union or State government.

✅ **Fact:** They cannot contest election as **President, Vice-President, MP, State
legislator, Municipality or Panchayat member for five years** after relinquishing
office.

✅ **Fact:** A **Member may be appointed Chairperson** if the **aggregate tenure** as
Member and Chairperson does not exceed **five years**.

| Restriction | Duration/form |
|---|---|
| Reappointment to Lokpal | Ineligible, subject to the Member-to-Chair aggregate exception |
| Diplomatic/UT administrator/specified presidential appointment | Ineligible |
| Government office of profit | Ineligible |
| Election to named public offices | Five-year cooling-off period |

## Example

A Member appointed at age 50 who serves exactly five years must leave office at 55 —
unless age 70 would be reached earlier, in which case that earlier age governs. If this
Member is later elevated to Chairperson after three years as a Member, the Chairperson
tenure can run for only two more years (keeping the aggregate at five), not a fresh full
five-year term.

## Comparison: tenure design versus a hypothetical "renewable" model

| Feature | Lokpal (fixed, non-renewable, cooling-off) | Hypothetical renewable model |
|---|---|---|
| Incentive to please future appointer | Reduced — no reappointment prospect | Present — reappointment possible |
| Incentive to seek post-tenure political reward | Reduced — five-year electoral cooling-off | Present — immediate political career possible |
| Institutional memory | Lower — no continuity through reappointment | Higher — continuity through renewal |

*Caption: The fixed-term, no-reappointment, cooling-off design sacrifices institutional memory for independence.*

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "A rigid five-year/age-70 rule with no reappointment wastes experienced
talent exactly when it has become most valuable."

**Reply:** That loss of continuity is a deliberate price for removing any incentive to
decide cases with an eye on reappointment — an irreversible, one-shot tenure is a
stronger independence guarantee than a renewable one.

**Residual:** The design does not fully address the separate risk that officeholders
might angle for **other** future appointments or rewards unrelated to Lokpal
reappointment itself; the post-tenure restrictions (diplomatic posts, offices of
profit, electoral cooling-off) exist precisely to close that residual gap, though they
cannot eliminate every informal incentive.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The exact figures — **five years**, **age 70**, **five-year** cooling-off —
are tightly tested, often by substituting a wrong number (six years, age 65, three-year
cooling-off).

**Mains:** Tenure and post-tenure restrictions together form the backbone of any
"evaluate Lokpal's independence safeguards" answer (completed in Lesson 19).

## Revision notes

1. Tenure = **five years from entry, or age 70**, whichever is earlier.
2. Resignation is to the **President**; removal follows **Section 37**.
3. **No reappointment** as Chairperson/Member after leaving office (subject to one exception).
4. No diplomatic assignment, UT administrator role, or other presidential-warrant post after leaving office.
5. No further **office of profit** under Union or State government.
6. **Five-year** electoral cooling-off: cannot contest President, VP, MP, State legislature, Municipality or Panchayat elections.
7. A Member may become Chairperson only if **aggregate tenure stays within five years**.
8. The fixed, non-renewable design trades institutional memory for independence.
9. Post-tenure restrictions close the gap left by removing the reappointment incentive.

### Concept check

**Question:** A Member completes three years in office and is then appointed Chairperson. For how long can this person serve as Chairperson?

**Model answer:** For a maximum of two more years. Section 6 permits a Member to become Chairperson only if the **aggregate** tenure as Member and Chairperson together does not exceed five years. Having already served three years as Member, only two years remain within the statutory aggregate ceiling, regardless of the ordinary five-year term that would otherwise apply to a fresh Chairperson appointment.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that moving from Member to Chairperson resets the five-year clock to a fresh full term — the aggregate-tenure cap explicitly prevents this.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain how the Lokpal's tenure and post-tenure rules together protect institutional independence.

**Mains model answer:** Tenure is fixed at five years from entry into office, or age seventy, whichever is earlier, with no reappointment as Chairperson or Member except through a narrow aggregate-tenure route when a Member is later appointed Chairperson. This removes any incentive to decide cases hoping for renewal. Post-tenure restrictions reinforce this: former Chairpersons and Members cannot take diplomatic assignments, become Union Territory administrators, accept other presidential-warrant posts, or hold any further office of profit under the Union or State government, and they face a five-year bar on contesting elections to the Presidency, Vice-Presidency, Parliament, State legislatures, Municipalities or Panchayats. Together, these rules reduce both the incentive to please a future reappointing authority and the temptation to convert Lokpal service into an immediate political or executive reward.

**Scoring rubric:** Correct tenure figures (3 marks) + correct post-tenure restrictions (4 marks) + explicit independence-rationale linking the two (3 marks).

## 🖼️ Visual: Two separate removal routes

```text
ROUTE 1: MISBEHAVIOUR (needs Supreme Court inquiry)

PETITION SIGNED BY >= 100 MPs
              |
        PRESIDENTIAL REFERENCE
              |
       SUPREME COURT INQUIRY
              |
SC reports that removal is warranted
              |
       PRESIDENTIAL ORDER

PENDING REFERENCE:
suspension only on SC recommendation/interim order

ROUTE 2: DIRECT GROUNDS (no Supreme Court inquiry under this route)

adjudged insolvent             -->  President may remove directly
paid outside employment        -->  President may remove directly
during tenure
infirmity of mind/body         -->  President may remove directly
rendering person unfit

DEEMED MISBEHAVIOUR (routes back into Route 1):
interest in a Union/State government contract or its profit/benefit
```

*Caption: Not every removal ground uses the same procedure — mixing the two routes is the most common error.*

## Plain-language intuition

Removing a Lokpal Chairperson or Member must be hard enough that the executive cannot
casually dismiss an inconvenient investigator, but not so rigid that genuinely
disqualifying situations (insolvency, incapacity) require a cumbersome judicial inquiry
every time. Section 37 therefore splits removal into a high-threshold route for
contested "misbehaviour" and a more direct route for objectively verifiable facts.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** Lokpal **cannot inquire into a complaint against its own** Chairperson or
Member — a structurally necessary exclusion to avoid self-judging conflicts.

✅ **Fact:** For removal on **misbehaviour**, the route is: a petition signed by at
least **one hundred Members of Parliament**; **Presidential reference** to the Supreme
Court; **Supreme Court inquiry** and a report that removal is warranted; **Presidential
removal order**.

✅ **Fact:** Once a reference is made, the **President may suspend** the Chairperson or
Member on the Supreme Court's recommendation or interim order, until the final report
is acted upon.

✅ **Fact:** The President may **directly remove** a Chairperson or Member who is
adjudged **insolvent**, engages in **paid employment outside official duties** during
the term, or is considered **unfit by reason of infirmity of mind or body**.

✅ **Fact:** **Interest in a Union or State government contract**, or participation in
its profit/benefit beyond ordinary incorporated-company membership, is **deemed
misbehaviour** — so it routes back into the Supreme Court inquiry process, not the
direct-removal route.

| Route | Ground | Supreme Court inquiry? |
|---|---|---|
| Section 37(2) | Misbehaviour | Yes |
| Section 37(4)(a) | Insolvency | No, under this direct route |
| Section 37(4)(b) | Paid outside employment during term | No, under this direct route |
| Section 37(4)(c) | Mental or physical infirmity rendering person unfit | No, under this direct route |
| Section 37(5) | Government-contract interest | Deemed misbehaviour — Supreme Court route |

## Example

If a Member is found to have taken paid outside employment while in office, the
President may remove that Member directly under the specified ground — no hundred-MP
petition or Supreme Court inquiry is required. But if the same Member is instead accused
of holding a financial interest in a government contract, that allegation is **deemed
misbehaviour**, and the full Supreme Court inquiry route applies.

## Comparison: do not import another institution's removal formula

| Institution | Removal mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Lokpal (Section 37)** | Own statutory route: hundred-MP petition + Presidential reference + SC inquiry (misbehaviour); direct grounds for insolvency/infirmity/outside employment |
| CVC | Its own distinct statutory removal provisions |
| NHRC | Its own distinct constitutional/statutory removal provisions |
| Election Commission | Judge-like removal under Article 324 for the CEC |

*Caption: Each institution has its own removal architecture — Section 37 cannot be assumed to mirror any of these.*

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "Requiring one hundred MPs' signatures before even referring a
misbehaviour allegation sets the bar so high that genuine misconduct may never reach
the Supreme Court."

**Reply:** A high threshold for *initiating* a misbehaviour reference protects the
Lokpal from being destabilised by a small, politically motivated faction; the actual
merits determination still belongs to the Supreme Court, not to Parliament itself,
preserving judicial rigor at the point of final decision.

**Residual:** The hundred-MP threshold does make it harder to even start a legitimate
misbehaviour case, which is a genuine (not merely theoretical) trade-off against swift
accountability.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The number **"at least one hundred MPs"** and the distinction between the
misbehaviour route (SC inquiry) and the direct-grounds route (no SC inquiry) are
frequently tested together as a close-option pair.

**Mains:** Removal architecture is a central pillar of any "independence versus
accountability" essay on the Lokpal (synthesised fully in Lesson 19).

## Revision notes

1. Lokpal **cannot inquire into complaints against its own** Chairperson or Members.
2. **Misbehaviour route:** petition by **≥100 MPs** → Presidential reference → **Supreme Court inquiry** → Presidential order.
3. **Suspension** during the pending reference requires SC recommendation or interim order.
4. **Direct removal grounds** (no SC inquiry needed): insolvency; paid outside employment during term; infirmity rendering the person unfit.
5. **Government-contract interest** is **deemed misbehaviour** — it uses the SC-inquiry route, not the direct route.
6. Do not import CVC, NHRC or Election Commission removal formulas into Section 37 answers.
7. The dual-route design balances protection from arbitrary removal against efficient handling of objectively verifiable disqualifying facts.

### Concept check

**Question:** A Member is alleged to hold a financial interest in a government contract. Which removal route applies, and why?

**Model answer:** The **misbehaviour route** applies, even though "government-contract interest" might look like an objectively verifiable fact similar to insolvency. Section 37(5) expressly **deems** such an interest to be misbehaviour, which means the full process — hundred-MP petition, Presidential reference, and Supreme Court inquiry — governs, not the shorter direct-removal route reserved for insolvency, outside paid employment, or infirmity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming every "factual" disqualifying ground (like a contract interest) automatically falls under the direct-removal route. The statute specifically routes contract-interest allegations back into the misbehaviour/Supreme Court process.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish the two removal routes available under Section 37 of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013.

**Mains model answer:** Section 37 provides two structurally distinct removal routes. The first, for "misbehaviour," requires a petition signed by at least one hundred Members of Parliament, a Presidential reference to the Supreme Court, a Supreme Court inquiry concluding that removal is warranted, and a final Presidential removal order; suspension during this process needs Supreme Court recommendation or interim order. The second route permits direct Presidential removal, without a Supreme Court inquiry, for specified objectively verifiable grounds: adjudged insolvency, paid outside employment during the term, or infirmity of mind or body rendering the person unfit. A financial interest in a government contract is separately deemed misbehaviour, routing it into the first, judicially-scrutinised process rather than the direct route, to prevent conflict-of-interest allegations from bypassing judicial oversight.

**Scoring rubric:** Both routes correctly described (6 marks) + correct placement of government-contract interest as deemed misbehaviour (3 marks) + clear structure (1 mark).

## 🖼️ Visual: Concentric jurisdiction map

## Plain-language intuition

"Who can the Lokpal investigate?" sounds like a simple question, but the Act answers it
with concentric rings rather than one flat list: core political office, the ordinary
civil service, entities connected to government funding or control, and specified
private actors connected to a corruption allegation. Registration as an NGO or society,
by itself, is never enough to attract jurisdiction — a specific statutory condition
must also be met.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** Section 14 covers current and former **Prime Ministers**, **Union
Ministers**, **Members of Parliament**, **Group A-D Union officials**, and specified
persons in statutory, financed, or controlled bodies.

✅ **Fact:** **Former public servants remain covered** for conduct during the period in
which they held or served in the relevant capacity — former status does not erase
jurisdiction over past conduct, and present fame or a later office cannot create
retrospective coverage for conduct before the person ever held a covered capacity.

✅ **Fact:** Officers of entities **established by an Act of Parliament**, or **wholly
or partly financed or controlled by the Central Government**, fall within clause (f).

✅ **Fact:** Clause (g) reaches officers of other societies, associations or trusts
**wholly or partly financed by Government** where annual income exceeds a
Centrally-notified amount — because the threshold is notification-dependent, it is not
frozen as a fixed figure here.

✅ **Fact:** Clause (h) reaches officers of societies, associations or trusts receiving
**foreign-source donations exceeding ten lakh rupees in a year**, or a higher notified
amount.

✅ **Fact:** The public-servant definition **excludes** persons for whom jurisdiction or
procedure lies under the **Army, Air Force, Navy or Coast Guard Acts**.

✅ **Fact:** Section 1(3) extends the Act's application to public servants **in and
outside India**.

|---|---:|---|
| Sitting/former Prime Minister | Yes | Special exclusions and procedure (Lesson 9) |

| Group A-D Union official | Yes | CVC routing differs by category (Lesson 12) |
| Former Union official | Yes | Conduct must relate to period of covered service |
| State official | Not automatically | State-consent provisions may apply (Lesson 10) |
| Armed-forces personnel under named service Acts | Excluded from this Act's public-servant definition | Governed by special legal systems |
| Any NGO merely because registered | No | Funding/control/foreign-contribution conditions must also be met |

## Current-status spotlight: does jurisdiction reach a High Court judge?

✅ **Fact:** In January 2025, the Lokpal asserted, in a specific complaint, that it had
jurisdiction over a sitting High Court judge under the "entity established by an Act
of Parliament" framing of Section 14(1)(f), since High Courts can be read that way for
some purposes.

✅ **Fact:** On **20 February 2025**, a Special Bench of the Supreme Court **stayed**
that Lokpal order, describing it as "very disturbing," and stressed that High Court
judges are **constitutional authorities**, not statutory functionaries, whose
independence is a core constitutional principle. The Court also directed that the
judge's identity and complaint details not be revealed.

✅ **Fact (freshly verified):** As of **October 2026**, that stay remains in force and
the matter remains **pending** before the Supreme Court — no final merits ruling has
been delivered either way.

⚠️ **Limit:** A stay is an **interim order**, not a final holding. Do not present the
Lokpal's original order as settled law, and do not present the Supreme Court's stay as
a final exclusion ruling either — both characterisations overstate what has actually
been decided.

```text
ALLEGED ACT DATE
      |

      |
  yes + complaint within limitation -> former status does not erase jurisdiction
  no  -> present fame or later office cannot create retrospective coverage
```

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "If jurisdiction depends on such intricate clause-by-clause tests
(government financing thresholds, foreign-contribution amounts, former-service
periods), ordinary citizens cannot reliably know whether the Lokpal can even take up
their complaint."

**Reply:** The intricacy exists precisely because jurisdiction over private or
quasi-private entities needs careful boundaries — an unconditional sweep into every
NGO or trust would raise serious overreach concerns; the conditions (government
financing, foreign-contribution thresholds) are the price of principled, bounded
coverage rather than arbitrary reach.

**Residual:** The genuine cost is reduced predictability for complainants at the
margins (for example, where a notified financial threshold is unclear) — a real,
not merely theoretical, access problem that the reform discussion in Lesson 19 returns
to.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The ten-lakh-rupee foreign-contribution threshold, the armed-forces
exclusion, and the current/former-status test are all directly testable factual
anchors.

**Mains:** The unresolved High Court-judge jurisdiction dispute is a strong, currently
live example for any "judicial independence versus statutory accountability" essay —
use it as evidence of an unsettled boundary question, not a settled precedent either
way.

## Revision notes

1. Section 14 covers PM, Union Ministers, MPs, Groups A-D officials, and specified entities.
2. **Former public servants remain covered** for conduct during their period of covered service.
3. Clause (f): officers of Parliament-created or Centrally financed/controlled entities.
4. Clause (g): notification-dependent government-finance threshold for societies/trusts.
5. Clause (h): foreign-source donations **exceeding ten lakh rupees/year** (or higher notified amount).
6. Armed-forces personnel under named service Acts are **excluded** from this Act's public-servant definition.
7. Section 1(3): the Act applies to public servants **in and outside India**.
8. Registration as an NGO alone never creates jurisdiction — a specific statutory condition must also be met.
9. **As of October 2026**, the Supreme Court's 20 February 2025 stay of the Lokpal's High Court-judge jurisdiction order remains in force; the dispute is unresolved.
10. A stay is interim, not final — neither the Lokpal's original order nor the Supreme Court's stay should be presented as settled law on the merits.

### Concept check

**Question:** A trust receives a government grant below the notified clause (g) threshold but also receives foreign-source donations of twelve lakh rupees in a year. Is it covered by Lokpal jurisdiction?

**Model answer:** Yes, through clause (h), not clause (g). Clause (h) independently covers entities receiving foreign-source donations **exceeding ten lakh rupees in a year** (or a higher notified amount), regardless of whether the clause (g) government-financing threshold is separately met. The two clauses are independent jurisdictional gateways — failing one does not prevent coverage through the other.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating clauses (g) and (h) as a single combined test. They are separate, independently sufficient conditions for jurisdiction over a society, association or trust.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** "Lokpal jurisdiction over private entities is conditional, not automatic." Explain with reference to Section 14(1)(f)-(h).

**Mains model answer:** The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act does not treat registration as a society, trust or association as sufficient for jurisdiction. Clause (f) covers officers of entities established by an Act of Parliament, or wholly or partly financed or controlled by the Central Government. Clause (g) reaches other government-financed societies, associations or trusts only where annual income exceeds a Centrally-notified amount. Clause (h) separately covers entities receiving foreign-source donations exceeding ten lakh rupees a year, or a higher notified sum. Each clause sets an independent, verifiable condition, so a purely private, unfunded, domestically-financed organisation falls outside Lokpal's reach altogether, however prominent it may be — jurisdiction tracks statutory conditions, not public visibility or private wrongdoing in the abstract.

**Scoring rubric:** All three clauses correctly distinguished (6 marks) + explicit "conditional, not automatic" framing (2 marks) + example or counter-example (2 marks).

## Lesson 9: The Prime Minister — Included but Ring-Fenced

Progress: 9/19 | Stage: Core | Lesson: The Prime Minister — Included but Ring-Fenced

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Section 14 PM provisos, Complaint Rules 2020
🔍 CA Search: "Lokpal Prime Minister jurisdiction safeguards 2026"
📰 CA Found: None altering the statutory PM-safeguard framework
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🖼️ Visual: The PM safeguard screen

```text
COMPLAINT AGAINST PM
        |
Does the allegation relate to an excluded domain?
I-S-P-A-S: International relations | external/internal Security |
           Public order | Atomic energy | Space
        |
yes -> no Lokpal inquiry into that specific matter
no  -> full bench considers initiation
        |
at least two-thirds of Members approve?
        |
no  -> inquiry not initiated
yes -> in-camera inquiry
        |
if the complaint is DISMISSED:
inquiry records shall not be published or made available to anyone
```

*Caption: Subject-matter exclusion and supermajority approval are cumulative safeguards — both must be cleared, not either/or.*

## Plain-language intuition

Including the head of government within an anti-corruption ombudsman's jurisdiction was
the headline reform of 2013 — but doing so without any safeguard risks destabilising
government through politically motivated complaints touching sensitive sovereign
functions. The Act's answer is neither blanket immunity nor ordinary identical
treatment: it is targeted exclusion plus a high internal threshold plus confidentiality.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** Section 14(1)(a) includes a person who **is or has been Prime Minister**
within Lokpal jurisdiction.

✅ **Fact:** Lokpal **cannot inquire** into a corruption allegation against the Prime
Minister insofar as it relates to: **international relations; external and internal
security; public order; atomic energy; and space.**

> 🔑 **Mnemonic: "I-S-P-A-S"** — **I**nternational relations, external/internal
> **S**ecurity, **P**ublic order, **A**tomic energy, **S**pace.

✅ **Fact:** Initiation requires a **full bench** — the Chairperson and all Members —
and at least **two-thirds of its Members** must approve.

✅ **Fact:** The inquiry must be held **in camera**. If Lokpal concludes the complaint
deserves dismissal, the **inquiry records shall not be published or made available to
anyone**.

✅ **Fact:** The **Complaint Rules, 2020** require a complaint against the Prime
Minister to be decided by the **full bench** at the admission stage itself — not merely
at some later discretionary point.

⚠️ **Inference:** The model is neither total immunity nor ordinary parity with any
other covered official. It layers four distinct safeguards — subject exclusion,
collective full-bench decision, supermajority, and confidentiality — that are
**cumulative**, not alternative.

| Stage/outcome | Publicity rule |
|---|---|
| Inquiry itself | In camera |
| Complaint dismissed | Records not published or made available to anyone |
| Matter proceeds | Governed by the Act, Rules and applicable confidentiality orders |

## Example

A complaint alleges the Prime Minister accepted a bribe connected to a purely domestic
infrastructure contract (not touching any excluded domain). This complaint is
jurisdictionally eligible for consideration, but it must still go to the **full bench**
and clear the **two-thirds** threshold before any in-camera inquiry begins. By
contrast, a complaint alleging wrongdoing in an atomic-energy decision falls entirely
outside Lokpal's inquiry power, regardless of how the full bench might vote.

⚠️ **Limit of the example:** The exclusion is **subject-matter specific**, not a
blanket shield for the PM in general — a domestically framed allegation is not
automatically protected merely because it touches a sensitive-sounding ministry in
passing.

## Comparison: PM safeguards versus ordinary-official procedure

| Feature | Ordinary covered official | Prime Minister |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-matter exclusions | None | Five named domains excluded |
| Bench requirement for initiation | Ordinary bench procedures apply | Full bench required |
| Approval threshold | Ordinary majority practice | At least two-thirds of Members |
| Confidentiality on dismissal | Standard process | Records not published/made available to anyone |

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "A two-thirds supermajority plus full-bench requirement, layered on top
of subject-matter exclusions, makes a PM inquiry nearly impossible to initiate in
practice — this is accountability in name only."

**Reply:** The threshold is demanding precisely because the consequences of a wrongly
initiated inquiry against a sitting head of government — reputational, diplomatic, and
governmental-stability consequences — are far larger than for an ordinary official;
a high but not impossible bar is a proportionate response, not a disguised immunity.

**Residual:** The honest cost remains that very few complaints are likely to clear
every layer of this safeguard, meaning the practical accountability value of including
the PM may be more symbolic-institutional than frequently operative — a genuine,
unresolved tension this topic does not paper over.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The exact five excluded domains (I-S-P-A-S) and the "two-thirds of
Members" (not a simple majority) threshold are classic close-option traps — the
verified 2025 Prelims PYQ (see the Final PYQ Index after Lesson 19) tests a directly
adjacent statement about PM coverage.

**Mains:** "The Prime Minister is included, but the inclusion is heavily filtered" is
the correct balanced thesis for any PM-jurisdiction essay — avoid both "total immunity"
and "equal treatment" extremes.

## Revision notes

1. Section 14(1)(a): PM, present or former, is within Lokpal jurisdiction.
2. **Five excluded domains (I-S-P-A-S):** international relations, external/internal security, public order, atomic energy, space.
3. Initiation requires the **full bench** (Chairperson + all Members).
4. At least **two-thirds of Members** must approve initiation.
5. The inquiry is **in camera**.
6. If dismissed, inquiry records **cannot be published or made available to anyone**.
7. Complaint Rules, 2020 route a PM complaint to the **full bench at the admission stage**.
8. The exclusions are **subject-matter specific**, not a blanket personal shield.
9. These four safeguards are **cumulative** — all apply together, not as alternatives.
10. The practical effect is a high but not impossible bar, not a hidden immunity.

### Concept check

**Question:** A complaint against the Prime Minister alleges corruption in a domestic highway-contracting decision. Does the subject-matter exclusion automatically block this complaint?

**Model answer:** No. The five excluded domains are international relations, external/internal security, public order, atomic energy and space — a purely domestic highway-contracting allegation falls outside all five. The complaint is therefore not blocked by subject-matter exclusion, but it must still pass through the full-bench, two-thirds-approval and in-camera-inquiry safeguards before any substantive inquiry proceeds.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating the PM's safeguards as a general-purpose shield against any inconvenient allegation. The exclusion is narrowly limited to five named sovereign-function domains; everything else is jurisdictionally open, though still procedurally filtered.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** "The Lokpal Act neither grants the Prime Minister total immunity nor treats the Prime Minister identically to an ordinary covered official." Discuss.

**Mains model answer:** The Act includes a serving or former Prime Minister within Lokpal jurisdiction, rejecting total immunity. However, it excludes allegations touching international relations, external or internal security, public order, atomic energy, and space — narrow, subject-specific carve-outs, not a general shield. Where a complaint falls outside these domains, it still cannot proceed like an ordinary case: initiation requires the full bench of the Chairperson and all Members, approval by at least two-thirds of them, and an in-camera inquiry; if dismissed, the inquiry records cannot be published or made available to anyone. This layered design — inclusion, subject exclusion, supermajority, confidentiality — reconciles accountability of the highest office with protection of sensitive governmental functions and institutional stability, occupying a deliberate middle ground between immunity and ordinary parity.

**Scoring rubric:** States inclusion (2 marks) + correctly lists the five exclusions (3 marks) + full-bench/two-thirds/in-camera safeguards (3 marks) + balanced "neither/nor" conclusion (2 marks).

## Lesson 10: Jurisdictional Boundaries — Privilege and Consent

Progress: 10/19 | Stage: Core | Lesson: Jurisdictional Boundaries — Privilege and Consent

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Section 14(2)-(3); *Sita Soren v Union of India* (2024 INSC 161); DSPE Act consent provisions
🔍 CA Search: "Sita Soren legislative privilege bribery 2026 current status"
📰 CA Found: No further Supreme Court modification of the *Sita Soren* holding reported since the 2024 judgment — the precedent remains current and unmodified
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🖼️ Visual: Two separate boundary questions, never to be merged

```text
QUESTION 1: IS AN MP'S CONDUCT PROTECTED BY PARLIAMENTARY PRIVILEGE?
            -> apply Article 105(2) / Section 14(2) / Sita Soren

BONA FIDE SPEECH OR VOTE IN PARLIAMENT
                |
          Article 105(2) shield applies

SEPARATE ACCEPTANCE OF A BRIBE FOR THAT SPEECH/VOTE
                |
       Sita Soren (2024): NO privilege immunity for bribery

QUESTION 2: DOES LOKPAL HAVE JURISDICTION OVER A STATE-CONNECTED PERSON?
            -> apply Lokpal Act Section 14(3) consent proviso

            (THIS IS NOT THE SAME QUESTION AS:)

CAN DSPE/CBI EXERCISE POLICE POWERS IN A STATE'S TERRITORY?
            -> apply DSPE Act Sections 5-6 territorial consent

NEVER MERGE THESE TWO CONSENT TESTS
```

*Caption: Privilege protects a legislative function, not its corrupt sale. Person-based Lokpal consent and territory-based DSPE consent solve two entirely different federal problems.*

## Plain-language intuition

Two very different "boundary" questions recur across this topic, and students routinely
confuse them. The first asks: *is this particular MP conduct protected speech, or is it
really bribery wearing a parliamentary costume?* The second asks: *can the Union's
Lokpal reach a person connected with State affairs, and separately, can the Union's
investigating agency (CBI/DSPE) physically operate inside a State at all?* Keeping
these four moving parts cleanly separated is essential.

## Technical doctrine, Part A: MPs and parliamentary privilege

✅ **Fact:** Section 14(2) bars Lokpal inquiry into a corruption allegation against an
MP in respect of **anything said or a vote given** in Parliament or a committee, where
**Article 105(2)** applies.

✅ **Fact:** In ***Sita Soren v Union of India* (2024 INSC 161)**, a **unanimous
seven-judge** Supreme Court bench **overruled** the relevant majority position in
*P.V. Narasimha Rao*. It held that a legislator who **accepts a bribe** for a speech or
vote receives **no Article 105/194 immunity**; bribery is complete on acceptance.

⚠️ **Inference:** The provisions can be harmonised through a functional boundary:
**bona fide** speech and vote remain protected, but a **separate criminal bargain to
sell** that function is not converted into protected legislative activity merely
because it relates to a vote.

⚠️ **Limit:** *Sita Soren* did **not abolish** parliamentary freedom of speech or
ordinary vote immunity. It removed only the claimed shield for **bribery**.

```text
BONA FIDE SPEECH OR VOTE IN PARLIAMENT
                |
          Article 105(2) shield

SEPARATE ACCEPTANCE OF BRIBE FOR SPEECH/VOTE
                |
       Sita Soren: no privilege immunity
```

| Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Is the allegation merely about protected speech/vote? | Section 14(2) exclusion applies | Continue |
| Is there an independent allegation of bribery/bargain? | *Sita Soren* denies privilege immunity | Ordinary jurisdiction test |
| Is the person otherwise covered by Section 14? | Lokpal process may apply | No Lokpal jurisdiction |
| Is the complaint timely and prescribed? | Proceed to scrutiny | Defect/limitation route |

## Technical doctrine, Part B: State-connected persons and consent

✅ **Fact:** Section 14 contains **consent protections** where a person connected with
Union service or a Union-linked entity is working in connection with State affairs, or a
State-established/financed/controlled entity.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 14(3)** also requires **State consent** before action against a
connected person serving in State affairs — recognising that Lokpal is a **Union**
institution and cannot automatically convert every State-linked corruption allegation
into central jurisdiction.

⚠️ **Limit:** This **person-based, Lokpal Act consent question** is entirely distinct
from the **DSPE Act Section 5-6 territory-based consent** that governs whether CBI/DSPE
may physically exercise police powers within a State's territory. One asks "does
Lokpal have jurisdiction over this person?"; the other asks "can this Union agency
operate in this State at all?" — an answer to one never resolves the other.

```text
QUESTION 1: DOES LOKPAL HAVE SECTION 14(3) JURISDICTION
            OVER A STATE-CONNECTED PERSON?
            -> apply Lokpal Act consent provisos

QUESTION 2: CAN DSPE/CBI EXERCISE POLICE POWERS
            IN A STATE'S TERRITORY?
            -> apply DSPE Act Sections 5-6 / court-ordered route

NEVER MERGE THE TWO TESTS
```

⚠️ **Boundary note:** The DSPE Act's own territorial-consent architecture, and the CBI's
institutional structure generally, belong to a separate topic and are not re-taught
here — this lesson teaches only the Lokpal Act's own person-based consent question and
its precise point of distinction from the DSPE's territorial question.

## Example

A State government officer, deputed to a Centrally-controlled body, is accused of
corruption connected with that Central assignment — Lokpal's ordinary Section 14
jurisdiction may apply because the allegation concerns the Union-linked role. If the
same officer were instead accused purely in connection with ordinary State-government
duties, Section 14(3)'s **State-consent** requirement would need to be satisfied before
Lokpal could act — a separate question from whether the CBI could even enter that
State to investigate, which is governed by the DSPE Act's own territorial-consent rule.

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "If *Sita Soren* already shows bribery is never immunised, why does the
Act still need Section 14(2) at all?"

**Reply:** Section 14(2) is not undermined by *Sita Soren* — it continues to protect
**genuine** parliamentary speech and voting function, which remains constitutionally
essential for free legislative debate; *Sita Soren* narrows only the **misuse** of that
protection as a bribery shield, leaving the core privilege intact.

**Residual:** Distinguishing "genuine legislative function" from "bribery dressed as
legislative function" is not always a clean factual exercise, and contested cases will
still require careful evidentiary analysis rather than a mechanical rule.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** A very common trap pairs "MPs are wholly outside Lokpal" (false) against
"every MP speech is now open to corruption scrutiny" (also false) — the correct middle
position must be stated precisely.

**Mains:** Any question combining privilege and federalism should explicitly flag that
these are two separate boundary tests, never conflating Section 14(3) consent with
DSPE territorial consent.

## Revision notes

1. Section 14(2) protects genuine parliamentary **speech or vote** under Article 105(2).
2. ***Sita Soren* (2024, 7-judge, unanimous)** overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* on bribery immunity.
3. Bribery for a speech/vote is **complete on acceptance** and receives **no privilege immunity**.
4. *Sita Soren* does **not abolish** ordinary privilege — only the bribery shield.
5. Section 14(3): **State consent** is required before action against a person connected with **State affairs**.
6. This is a **person-based** Lokpal Act consent question.
7. It is **entirely distinct** from the DSPE Act's **territory-based** consent (Sections 5-6) for CBI/DSPE operations within a State.
8. Never merge the Lokpal-Act consent question with the DSPE-Act territorial-consent question.
9. This lesson does not re-teach the CBI/DSPE's own institutional architecture — that belongs to a separate topic.

### Concept check

**Question:** An MP is alleged to have voted a particular way in Parliament *after* accepting money for that vote. Does Article 105(2) protect this MP from Lokpal's jurisdiction?

**Model answer:** No. While Article 105(2) ordinarily protects what an MP says or how an MP votes in Parliament, *Sita Soren v Union of India* (2024) holds that accepting a bribe connected to that speech or vote is not protected — the offence of bribery is complete upon acceptance, independent of whether the vote was later cast as agreed. The privilege shields genuine legislative function; it does not immunise the separate criminal act of selling that function.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that because the bribery relates to "a vote in Parliament," Article 105(2) automatically applies. *Sita Soren* specifically separates the legislative act from the corrupt bargain surrounding it.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish the Lokpal Act's Section 14(3) State-consent requirement from the DSPE Act's territorial-consent requirement.

**Mains model answer:** Section 14(3) of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act addresses a person-based question: before Lokpal can act against a person connected with State affairs or a State-established, financed or controlled entity, State consent is required, since Lokpal is constitutionally and statutorily a Union institution without automatic reach into purely State-linked corruption matters. This is entirely different from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act's Sections 5-6, which address a territory-based question — whether the CBI, a Union agency, may exercise police powers of investigation within a particular State's geographical jurisdiction at all. One test asks whether Lokpal has jurisdiction over a person; the other asks whether a Union investigating agency may operate in a State's territory. Treating them as interchangeable produces a serious federalism-related error in any Lokpal-CBI coordination answer.

**Scoring rubric:** Correct statement of Section 14(3) (4 marks) + correct statement of DSPE territorial consent, without re-teaching its full architecture (3 marks) + explicit distinction stated (3 marks).

## Lesson 11: Complaint Entry, Limitation and False Complaints

Progress: 11/19 | Stage: Core | Lesson: Complaint Entry, Limitation and False Complaints

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Lokpal (Complaint) Rules, 2020; Sections 46 and 53
🔍 CA Search: "Lokpal complaint rules limitation false complaint 2026"
📰 CA Found: None affecting the Rules or these statutory provisions
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🖼️ Visual: From filing to registration, with two statutory gates

```text
PRESCRIBED FORM + IDENTITY PROOF + AFFIDAVIT +
SIGNED DETAILED ALLEGATION + (where applicable) ORGANISATIONAL
AUTHORITY DOCUMENTS + FILING-MODE REQUIREMENTS
                |
       RECEIPT / DIARY NUMBER
                |
     FORM AND ANNEXURE SCRUTINY
                |
       +--------+--------------------+
       |                             |
     defect                      no defect
       |                             |
  call for cure /              registration and
  bench may condone            bench allocation
       |
  not yet a merits finding

GATE 1 — IN-LIMINE DISPOSAL (Rule 4): illegible | vague/ambiguous |
         trivial/frivolous | no allegation against a public servant |
         beyond the 7-year limitation | pending elsewhere

GATE 2 — LIMITATION (Section 53): complaint after 7 years from the
         DATE OF THE ALLEGED OFFENCE -> Lokpal cannot inquire/investigate
```

*Caption: Registry scrutiny filters filing compliance; it is not a merits finding. Limitation and in-limine disposal are separate statutory gates, both upstream of any inquiry.*

## Plain-language intuition

Before the Lokpal can even begin screening whether a corruption allegation has merit, a
complaint has to clear basic procedural gates: is it in the right form, with the right
supporting documents, filed within the time the law allows, and not an abuse of
process? None of these gates judge guilt — they judge whether the complaint is
**workable** enough to proceed to the merits stage at all.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** The **Lokpal (Complaint) Rules, 2020** require the **prescribed form**.
Filing may be **electronic, by post, or in person**.

✅ **Fact:** Where filed electronically, a **hard copy** must be submitted within
**fifteen days**; a complete electronic complaint should **not** be kept pending merely
for that reason.

✅ **Fact:** A complaint is ordinarily in **English**, but Lokpal may entertain one in
an **Eighth Schedule** language.

✅ **Fact:** Required annexures include **identity proof**, organisational
registration/incorporation and authorisation where relevant, the specified
**affidavit**, and a duly signed **detailed statement of allegation**.

✅ **Fact:** **Rule 4** permits **in-limine disposal** where content is illegible,
vague or ambiguous, trivial or frivolous, lacks an allegation against a public servant,
is time-barred, or concerns a cause pending before another court, tribunal or
authority.

⚠️ **Limit:** A formal defect is **not** a finding that the allegation is false. Defect
cure, maintainability, prima facie assessment, and final adjudication are **different**
questions, each resolved at a different stage.

| Ground under Rule 4 | Why it matters | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Illegible | No workable allegation | A curable form issue may differ |
| Vague/ambiguous | No determinate case | Must not substitute speculation |
| Trivial/frivolous | Statutory filter | "Unproved" is not automatically frivolous |
| No allegation against a public servant | Outside the complaint definition | Connected-person rule may still require analysis |
| Beyond Section 53 | Seven-year bar | Date of alleged offence controls |
| Cause pending elsewhere | Avoid parallel process | Apply exact pendency facts |

## Limitation: Section 53

✅ **Fact:** Section 53 bars Lokpal inquiry or investigation where the complaint is
made after **seven years from the date of the alleged offence**.

```text
DATE OF ALLEGED OFFENCE
          |
      7 YEARS
          |
complaint after expiry -> Lokpal cannot inquire/investigate

NOT THE SAME AS:
date of discovery | retirement date | date complaint is publicised
```

⚠️ **Inference:** Anchoring limitation to the **offence date** (not discovery, not
retirement) creates a predictable, fixed deadline, but it can also bar genuinely
late-discovered corruption — a real cost of legal certainty.

## False and frivolous complaints: Section 46

✅ **Fact:** Section 46 punishes, on conviction, a **false and frivolous or vexatious**
complaint with imprisonment up to **one year** and fine up to **one lakh rupees**; a
convicted complainant may also be ordered to pay **compensation and legal expenses**.

✅ **Fact:** Section 46 does **not apply to complaints made in good faith** — the Act
defines good faith through due care, caution and responsibility, or mistake of fact
under the stated criminal-law provision.

⚠️ **Limit:** Closure, acquittal, or an unproved allegation does **not** automatically
establish a false, frivolous or vexatious complaint.

| Situation | Section 46 automatically applies? |
|---|---:|
| Complaint dismissed for lack of evidence | No |
| Public servant acquitted | No |
| Filing defect | No |
| Complaint judicially found false and frivolous/vexatious through statutory process | Potentially |
| Good-faith complaint | Expressly protected |

## Comparison: screening versus merits

| Stage | Tests | Does it decide guilt or falsity? |
|---|---|---|
| Form/annexure scrutiny | Completeness of filing | No |
| In-limine disposal (Rule 4) | Workability, timeliness, non-duplication | No |
| Section 53 limitation | Timeliness only | No |
| Section 46 false-complaint process | Deliberate bad faith | Only after its own separate statutory process |
| Preliminary inquiry/investigation (Lesson 12) | Prima facie case/evidence | No — this is still pre-conviction |
| Special Court trial | Guilt | **Yes** |

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "Heavy penalties for false complaints, combined with demanding form
requirements, will discourage genuine whistle-blowers from coming forward at all."

**Reply:** Section 46 is deliberately narrowed to deliberately false, frivolous, or
vexatious complaints, with an express good-faith shield — an honest complainant whose
allegation simply fails for want of evidence is not automatically exposed to
prosecution.

**Residual:** The chilling-effect concern is not fully eliminated by the good-faith
shield alone — a genuinely risk-averse potential complainant may still be deterred by
the mere existence of a penal provision, which is a real institutional-design cost
examined further in Lesson 19's reform discussion.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The **seven-year** limitation anchor (offence date, not discovery date)
and the precise **Section 46** penalty figures are frequently tested close options.

**Mains:** "Does the Lokpal's complaint-entry design protect against abuse without
chilling genuine complainants?" is a natural "critically examine" framing building on
this lesson.

## Revision notes

1. Complaint Rules, 2020 prescribe form, mode (electronic/post/person), and required annexures.
2. Electronic filing needs a hard copy within **15 days**, but should not be kept pending for that reason alone.
3. Complaints are ordinarily in **English**, but an Eighth Schedule language complaint may be entertained.
4. **Rule 4** permits in-limine disposal for illegibility, vagueness, triviality/frivolity, non-allegation against a public servant, time-bar, or pendency elsewhere.
5. Registry scrutiny is **not** a merits finding.
6. **Section 53**: complaint barred after **seven years from the date of the alleged offence** (not discovery, not retirement).
7. **Section 46**: false/frivolous/vexatious complaint punishable with up to **1 year** imprisonment and up to **₹1 lakh** fine, plus possible compensation/costs.
8. Section 46 does **not** apply to **good-faith** complaints.
9. Dismissal or acquittal alone never automatically proves a complaint was false.
10. The Act creates **no anonymous-complaint channel**.

### Concept check

**Question:** A complaint is dismissed after preliminary inquiry because the evidence available was simply insufficient to establish a prima facie case. Can the complainant now be prosecuted under Section 46?

**Model answer:** Not automatically. Section 46 requires that a complaint be judicially found **false and frivolous or vexatious** through its own statutory process; mere dismissal for insufficient evidence does not, by itself, establish bad faith. The Act's good-faith protection further shields complainants who acted with due care and caution, even if their complaint ultimately failed on the merits.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating "complaint dismissed" or "public servant acquitted" with "complaint was false." These are different legal findings, requiring a separate statutory determination before Section 46 can apply.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Examine whether the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act balances protection against false complaints with the risk of discouraging genuine whistle-blowers.

**Mains model answer:** Section 46 penalises false, frivolous or vexatious complaints with up to one year's imprisonment and one lakh rupees' fine, plus possible compensation, reflecting a genuine concern about weaponised complaints against public servants. The Act tempers this with an explicit good-faith protection, and dismissal or acquittal alone does not establish bad faith — a separate statutory finding of falsity is required before Section 46 applies. This design tries to deter deliberate abuse while sparing honest complainants whose allegations simply fail for want of evidence. However, the mere existence of a penal provision can still have a chilling effect on risk-averse complainants regardless of the good-faith shield, meaning the balance, while reasoned, is not cost-free for genuine whistle-blowers.

**Scoring rubric:** Section 46 penalty correctly stated (3 marks) + good-faith protection explained (3 marks) + honest residual chilling-effect point (3 marks) + structure (1 mark).

## 🖼️ Visual: Two different roads from the same junction

```text
LOKPAL DECIDES HOW TO PROCEED
         |
   +-----+------------------------+
   |                              |
PRELIMINARY INQUIRY          DIRECT INVESTIGATION
(tests prima facie case)     (where prima facie case
   |                          already exists)
Inquiry Wing / CVC /              |

   |
 +-+----------+
 |            |

 |            |

```

*Caption: Group category changes where the preliminary-inquiry report returns and which statutory route continues — this is a precise, testable branching rule, not a loose generalisation.*

## Plain-language intuition

"Preliminary inquiry" and "investigation" sound almost interchangeable in everyday
speech, but in the Act they are different legal stages with different purposes, actors,
and outputs. Confusing them is one of the most common errors on this topic.

## Technical doctrine: inquiry versus investigation

✅ **Fact:** On receiving a complaint and deciding to proceed, Lokpal may order either a
**preliminary inquiry** (by its Inquiry Wing or an agency, including DSPE, to ascertain
whether a prima facie case exists) or an **investigation** (by an agency, including
DSPE, where a prima facie case already exists).

✅ **Fact:** Before ordering investigation, Lokpal must call for the public servant's
**explanation** to help determine whether a prima facie case exists — this does not
interfere with necessary search and seizure.

| Dimension | Preliminary inquiry | Investigation |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Determine prima facie case | Collect evidence for a criminal report |
| Actors | Inquiry Wing, CVC, or agency | CBI/DSPE or other investigating agency |
| Comments/hearing | Comments from the public servant and competent authority; hearing before decision | Explanation before direct investigation; further comments at the post-report stage |
| Output | Investigate, departmental action, or closure | Court report + Lokpal copy; prosecution/closure/departmental route |
| Legal character | Screening/inquiry | Criminal investigation |

## CVC routing for Groups A-D

✅ **Fact:** If Lokpal decides to proceed through **preliminary inquiry**, Section 20
requires reference of complaints or categories involving **Groups A, B, C or D to the
CVC** by general or special order.

✅ **Fact:** For **Groups A and B**, the CVC conducts preliminary inquiry and submits
its report **to Lokpal**.

✅ **Fact:** For **Groups C and D**, the CVC proceeds under the **CVC Act, 2003**.

✅ **Fact:** Section 25(2) requires the CVC to send **statements at intervals**
directed by Lokpal about action on referred complaints; Lokpal may issue guidelines for
effective and expeditious disposal.

⚠️ **Inference:** This is neither a simple hierarchy nor duplication. Lokpal remains the
high-level complaint-routing institution, while the CVC has **category-specific**
preliminary-inquiry and vigilance functions — the Group letter (A/B versus C/D) is the
precise switch that determines what happens next, and students should not generalise a
single "CVC reports to Lokpal" rule across all four Groups.

## Statutory timelines

✅ **Fact:** During preliminary inquiry, the Inquiry Wing or agency collects material,
seeks comments, and submits a report within **sixty days from receipt of the
reference**.

✅ **Fact:** Every preliminary inquiry should **ordinarily be completed within ninety
days**, extendable for recorded reasons by a further **ninety days**.

✅ **Fact:** A bench of **at least three Members** considers the report, gives the
public servant an opportunity of hearing, and chooses investigation, departmental/other
action, or closure (with possible Section 46 action against the complainant where
appropriate).

✅ **Fact:** Investigation should be completed within **six months** from Lokpal's
order, extendable by a further period **not exceeding six months at a time**, with
recorded reasons.

```text
REFERENCE FOR PRELIMINARY INQUIRY
        |
report target: 60 days from reference
        |
ordinary preliminary-inquiry completion: 90 days
        |
recorded-reason extension: further 90 days
        |
INVESTIGATION ORDER
        |
ordinary completion: 6 months
        |
recorded-reason extension: <= 6 months at a time
```

*Caption: The 60-day report requirement, the 90+90 inquiry framework, and the six-month investigation period are three distinct timelines, not interchangeable figures.*

## Example

A complaint against a Group C official is referred for preliminary inquiry. Because the
official belongs to Groups C/D, the CVC proceeds under its own **CVC Act, 2003**
framework rather than reporting findings directly to Lokpal in the Groups A/B manner.
If the official had instead been Group A, the CVC would have conducted the preliminary
inquiry and reported its findings back to Lokpal for a three-Member bench decision.

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "Routing preliminary inquiries through the CVC, which has its own
distinct statutory existence and procedures, risks diluting Lokpal's intended role as a
unified single-window anti-corruption body."

**Reply:** The CVC routing leverages an already-existing, experienced vigilance
institution for category-specific preliminary screening rather than duplicating that
capacity inside Lokpal — an efficient division of labour rather than a dilution,
provided Lokpal retains final decision-making for Groups A/B and overall guideline
powers under Section 25(2).

**Residual:** Coordination between two statutorily distinct institutions (Lokpal and
CVC) genuinely does create friction and delay risk, which is examined fully as part of
the Lokpal-CVC-CBI functional-chain lesson (Lesson 17).

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The precise 60-day, 90(+90)-day, and six-month figures, and the Groups A/B
versus C/D CVC-routing split, are tightly tested.

**Mains:** "Preliminary inquiry is not investigation" is a frequently required
distinguishing statement in any process-description answer.

## Revision notes

1. **Preliminary inquiry** tests whether a prima facie case exists; **investigation** gathers evidence for a criminal report.
2. Before ordering investigation, Lokpal must call for the public servant's **explanation**.
3. For Groups A-D, preliminary inquiry references route to the **CVC**.
4. **Groups A/B**: CVC inquires and **reports to Lokpal**.
5. **Groups C/D**: CVC proceeds under the **CVC Act, 2003** (a different route).
6. Preliminary-inquiry report target: **60 days** from reference.
7. Ordinary preliminary-inquiry completion: **90 days**, extendable by a further **90 days** for recorded reasons.
8. Investigation: ordinarily **six months**, extendable by **up to six months at a time** with recorded reasons.
9. A **three-Member bench** decides the post-report course: investigate, departmental action, or closure.
10. These are three distinct timelines — do not interchange them.

### Concept check

**Question:** A preliminary-inquiry reference concerns a Group B official. What happens to the CVC's findings once its inquiry is complete?

**Model answer:** Because the official belongs to Group B, the CVC conducts the preliminary inquiry and **reports its findings to Lokpal**, which then considers the report through a three-Member bench and decides whether to order investigation, direct departmental action, or close the matter. This differs from the Groups C/D route, where the CVC instead proceeds under its own CVC Act, 2003 framework rather than reporting back to Lokpal in this manner.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the CVC always simply "reports to Lokpal" regardless of the official's Group. The reporting-back rule applies specifically to Groups A and B; Groups C and D follow a separate CVC Act-based route.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain the statutory distinction between preliminary inquiry and investigation under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013.

**Mains model answer:** Preliminary inquiry is a threshold screening stage, conducted by Lokpal's Inquiry Wing, the CVC (for Groups A-D Union officials, with routing that differs by Group), or another agency, aimed solely at determining whether a prima facie case exists; its typical outputs are a recommendation to investigate, initiate departmental action, or close the matter. Investigation, by contrast, is the evidence-gathering criminal-process stage, undertaken by the CBI, DSPE, or another agency once Lokpal is satisfied a prima facie case exists, and it produces a formal investigation report placed before the jurisdictional court with a copy to Lokpal. Preliminary inquiry answers "should this proceed further?"; investigation answers "what evidence supports prosecution?" Neither stage determines guilt, which remains exclusively for the Special Court.

**Scoring rubric:** Correct purpose of each stage (4 marks) + correct actors (3 marks) + explicit statement that neither determines guilt (3 marks).

## Lesson 13: Post-Report Decision, Sanction and the Special Court

## 🖼️ Visual: From investigation report to a courtroom verdict

```text
AGENCY INVESTIGATION REPORT
      |
filed before jurisdictional court
+ copy forwarded to Lokpal
      |
>= 3-MEMBER LOKPAL BENCH
+ comments of competent authority/public servant
      |
 +-----------+--------------+----------------+
 |           |              |                |
sanction +  closure       departmental     other
charge-sheet report       action           appropriate action
 |
Prosecution Wing / agency
 |

      |

(1-year target -> 3-month recorded extensions -> total <= 2 years)
```

*Caption: The post-report stage combines criminal and departmental choices without treating either as established guilt — only the Special Court decides guilt.*

## Plain-language intuition

Once an agency finishes investigating, someone still has to decide: do we prosecute, do
we instead pursue a departmental route, or do we close the matter? And even once
prosecution is sanctioned, someone independent of the accusing machinery must actually
judge the case. This lesson covers that final decision chain, ending in the Special
Court.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** The investigating agency submits its statutory investigation report to the
**court having jurisdiction** and forwards a **copy to Lokpal**.

✅ **Fact:** A bench of **at least three Members** considers the report and, after
comments from the competent authority and public servant, may **grant sanction** to the
Prosecution Wing or agency to file a charge-sheet, direct a **closure report**, or
direct **departmental/other action**.

✅ **Fact:** Lokpal may then direct its **Prosecution Wing** or investigating agency to
**initiate prosecution** in the Special Court.

✅ **Fact:** Section 23 gives Lokpal **prosecution-sanction power** for the Section
20(7)(a) route, subject to the constitutional-office qualification in **Section
23(3)**.

✅ **Fact:** Where the investigation concerns the **Prime Minister, Union Minister or
MP** and discloses a Prevention of Corruption Act offence, **Section 24** permits
Lokpal to **file a case** in the Special Court directly and requires the
report/findings to be sent to the competent authority.

⚠️ **Inference:** "Sanction" is procedural authorisation to prosecute; it is **not** a
merits verdict. Likewise, a charge-sheet is an accusation supported by investigative
material, **not** a conviction.

| Question | Lokpal | Special Court |
|---|---:|---:|
| Prima facie routing | Yes | No |
| Prosecution sanction under the Act | Yes | No |
| Charge framed/trial conducted | No | Yes |
| Attachment confirmed | Applies/seeks | Decides |
| Conviction/acquittal | No | Yes |
| Loss assessment after conviction | No | Yes |

## The Special Court

✅ **Fact:** Section 35 targets trial completion within **one year**, with **recorded
three-month extensions**, and a **total ceiling of two years**.

⚠️ **Limit:** These are statutory **targets**, not guaranteed outcomes — real trials
can and do extend beyond statutory expectations for various procedural reasons; stating
the figure as an automatic completion time overstates what the law actually guarantees.

## Example

An investigation against a Union Minister discloses a Prevention of Corruption Act
offence. Because the person is a Union Minister, **Section 24** allows Lokpal to file
the case in the Special Court directly (after sending findings to the competent
authority), rather than relying solely on the ordinary Section 20(7)(a)/Section 23
sanction route used for most other officials.

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "If Lokpal can grant sanction, direct prosecution, and even file cases
directly for high officials under Section 24, isn't the Special Court stage a mere
formality?"

**Reply:** Sanction and direct filing determine only whether a prosecution **may
proceed**, not whether the accused is guilty — the Special Court independently
examines evidence, applies the criminal standard of proof, and can acquit even where
Lokpal sanctioned prosecution; institutional separation between the sanctioning and
adjudicating bodies is preserved precisely to prevent the formality concern from
materialising.

**Residual:** Separation of function does not eliminate every practical influence a
well-resourced sanctioning/prosecuting apparatus might have on how a case is ultimately
tried — courts remain the formal safeguard, but resourcing asymmetries between
prosecution and defence are a genuine, broader criminal-justice concern beyond this
Act's specific design.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The Section 24 **direct-filing route for PM/Minister/MP cases**, as
distinct from the ordinary Section 20(7)(a)/23 sanction route for other officials, is a
precise, testable distinction.

**Mains:** "Sanction is not conviction" is an essential qualifying sentence in any
answer describing Lokpal's prosecution-enabling powers.

## Revision notes

1. Investigation report goes to the **jurisdictional court**, with a **copy to Lokpal**.
2. A **three-Member bench** considers the report and chooses sanction, closure, or departmental action.
3. **Section 23**: prosecution-sanction power for the Section 20(7)(a) route, subject to Section 23(3)'s constitutional-office qualification.
4. **Section 24**: for PM/Union Minister/MP cases disclosing a PC Act offence, Lokpal may **file the case directly** in the Special Court.
5. "Sanction" and "charge-sheet" are **not** conviction.
6. The **Special Court** alone decides guilt, acquittal and sentence.
7. Section 35 targets **one-year** trial completion, with **three-month recorded extensions**, capped at **two years total**.
8. These are statutory **targets**, not guaranteed outcomes.

### Concept check

**Question:** An investigation against an MP discloses a Prevention of Corruption Act offence. Which Lokpal provision allows the case to be filed in the Special Court, and how does this differ from the ordinary route used for most officials?

**Model answer:** Section 24 permits Lokpal to file the case directly in the Special Court where the investigation concerns the Prime Minister, a Union Minister, or an MP and discloses a Prevention of Corruption Act offence, after sending its findings to the competent authority. This differs from the ordinary route for most other officials, which proceeds through a three-Member bench granting prosecution sanction under Section 23, tied to the Section 20(7)(a) process.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the same single sanction-and-prosecute route applies uniformly to every covered person. Section 24 creates a distinct, more direct filing pathway specifically for the PM, Union Ministers and MPs.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** "Sanction to prosecute under the Lokpal Act is procedural authorisation, not a merits verdict." Explain.

**Mains model answer:** After an investigation report is filed before the jurisdictional court with a copy to Lokpal, a bench of at least three Members considers it, along with comments from the competent authority and the public servant, before deciding whether to grant sanction for prosecution, direct closure, or pursue departmental action. Granting sanction authorises the Prosecution Wing or investigating agency to proceed; it does not itself establish guilt. The Special Court, applying ordinary criminal procedure and the standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt, independently examines the evidence and may convict or acquit regardless of Lokpal's sanction decision. Collapsing "sanctioned" into "guilty" is therefore a serious legal error — adjudication remains exclusively with the Special Court, within its statutory one-year trial target.

**Scoring rubric:** Correct description of the sanction decision (4 marks) + explicit sanction-is-not-guilt distinction (4 marks) + correct reference to Special Court's independent role (2 marks).

*Caption: Institutional labels must follow the statutory function actually performed, and formal constitution must not be mistaken for proof of complete operational capacity.*

## Plain-language intuition

An Act can create a wing on paper years before that wing is actually staffed,
organised, and functioning. This lesson teaches both the Act's statutory mandate for
two specific wings, and the verified, dated evidence of when each wing was actually,
formally brought into existence — while being careful not to overstate what that formal
constitution proves.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** **Section 11** requires an **Inquiry Wing**, headed by the **Director of
Inquiry**, to conduct preliminary inquiries into alleged Prevention of Corruption Act
offences by public servants. Officers not below **Under Secretary rank** have specified
**civil-court powers** for this purpose.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 12** requires Lokpal, by notification, to constitute a
**Prosecution Wing**, headed by the **Director of Prosecution**. When directed, it
files the case before the Special Court in accordance with the investigation report and
takes necessary prosecution steps.

✅ **Fact (verified current status):** The official Lokpal circulars/notifications
register records the **"Constitution of Inquiry Wing of Lokpal of India"** dated **5
September 2024**. The official revised organogram, approved by the Full Bench on **13
September 2024**, depicts a Director of Inquiry and an Inquiry structure within the
Judicial Wing.

✅ **Fact (verified current status):** The Chairperson formally constituted the
**Prosecution Wing** by order dated **6 June 2025**; an official Gazette notification
dated **13 June 2025** records that constitution under Section 12.

⚠️ **Limit:** Formal constitution and a published organogram prove **legal/
organisational creation**. They do **not**, by themselves, prove that every sanctioned
post is filled, that the wings possess complete independent operational capacity, or
any particular level of case output. This topic does not freeze current staffing
numbers or case-disposal figures.

| Wing | Statutory mandate | Verified implementation evidence | Controlled conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inquiry Wing | Preliminary inquiry, Section 11 | Constitution entry dated 5 Sep 2024; revised organogram dated 13 Sep 2024 | Formally constituted and structurally depicted |
| Prosecution Wing | File/prosecute cases when directed, Section 12 | Formal order 6 Jun 2025; Gazette notification 13 Jun 2025 | Formally constituted and notified |
| Investigation function | Criminal investigation | Assigned to CBI/DSPE or other agency under Section 20 | Wings do not create a general Lokpal police force |

## Example

If an exam question asks whether the Lokpal has "fully operationalised" its prosecution
machinery, the defensible answer notes the verified 2025 formal-constitution and
Gazette-notification dates, while explicitly flagging that formal constitution is not
equivalent to proof of complete staffing or measured case output — an answer that
simply asserts "fully operational since 2014" would be factually wrong, and one that
denies any institutional progress at all would also be wrong.

## Comparison: three different claims that must not be collapsed into one

| Claim | What it actually proves |
|---|---|
| "Created in the Act" | A statutory mandate exists (2013/2014) |
| "Formally constituted" | An actual order/notification brought the wing into being (2024/2025) |
| "Fully staffed and effective" | Requires separate staffing/output evidence, not frozen here |

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "If the Inquiry Wing and Prosecution Wing were only formally constituted
roughly a decade after the Act commenced, the Lokpal effectively lacked functioning
machinery for most of its existence."

**Reply:** The verified dates do show a long gap between commencement (2014) and formal
wing constitution (2024-2025), which is a legitimate critique of implementation delay;
however, this is a capacity-building timeline problem, not evidence that the statutory
design itself was flawed — the same two-wing structure could have been constituted
earlier under the same legal provisions.

**Residual:** This lesson does not resolve whether the delay reflects resourcing
constraints, administrative caution, or institutional under-prioritisation — the
available verified evidence establishes the dates, not the causes, of the delay.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The exact pairing — **Inquiry Wing: 2024**, **Prosecution Wing: 2025** —
and the distinction between "constituted" and "fully staffed" are precise, testable
anchors.

**Mains:** This is strong, genuinely current evidence for any "has the Lokpal
delivered since 2019?" or "implementation gaps in India's anti-corruption
architecture" essay — use the dates honestly, without overclaiming performance data
that is not available.

## Revision notes

1. **Section 11**: Inquiry Wing, headed by the **Director of Inquiry**, conducts preliminary inquiries; Under-Secretary-rank-or-above officers have specified civil-court powers.
2. **Section 12**: Prosecution Wing, headed by the **Director of Prosecution**, files and conducts prosecution when directed.
3. **Verified:** Inquiry Wing formally constituted **5 September 2024**; organogram revised **13 September 2024**.
4. **Verified:** Prosecution Wing formally constituted by order **6 June 2025**; Gazette-notified **13 June 2025**.
5. Formal constitution proves legal/organisational creation, **not** full staffing or case-output volume.
6. Neither wing is a general police force — investigation remains with the CBI/DSPE or another assigned agency.
7. "Created in the Act," "formally constituted," and "fully staffed/effective" are **three separate claims**.
8. These dated constitution and Gazette-notification facts are static docket verification,
   not the topic's one genuine file-level current linkage (that is the unresolved Supreme

### Concept check

**Question:** A statement claims "the Lokpal's Prosecution Wing has existed and functioned since the Act commenced in 2014." Evaluate this claim.

**Model answer:** This claim is inaccurate. While Section 12 of the 2013 Act mandated the creation of a Prosecution Wing from commencement, the wing was only **formally constituted** by an order dated 6 June 2025, with a Gazette notification following on 13 June 2025 — roughly a decade after the Act's 2014 commencement. The statutory mandate existed from 2014, but actual formal constitution is a verified 2025 event, not a 2014 one.

**Misconception to avoid:** Conflating "the Act required this wing from 2014" with "the wing has existed and operated since 2014." Legal mandate and actual institutional constitution are distinct, separately verifiable facts.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

## Lesson 15: Search, Civil-Court Powers, Attachment and Confiscation

Progress: 15/19 | Stage: Advanced | Lesson: Search, Civil-Court Powers, Attachment and Confiscation

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Sections 26-32, Topic 38 package
🔍 CA Search: "Lokpal search seizure attachment confiscation 2026"
📰 CA Found: None affecting the statutory search/attachment architecture
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🖼️ Visual: From a secreted document to a confiscated asset

```text
RELEVANT DOCUMENTS SECRETED?
      |
Lokpal authorises the investigating agency -> search/seizure (Section 26)

INQUIRY WING CIVIL-COURT POWERS (Section 27):
summons | attendance | oath examination | document discovery/
production | affidavit evidence | public-record requisition | commissions

PROCEEDS OF CORRUPTION AT RISK?
      |
recorded reasons + supporting material
      |
PROVISIONAL ATTACHMENT <= 90 DAYS (Section 29)
      |
order/material sent to the Special Court
      |
Prosecution Wing applies for confirmation within 30 days (Section 30)
      |
Special Court confirms or refuses
      |
acquittal -> ordinarily restored
conviction -> confiscation of proceeds relatable to the offence

SEPARATE ROUTE (Section 31): Special Court confiscation in special
circumstances, on prima facie evidence, until acquittal — with
restoration/value plus statutory interest if annulled or acquitted
```

*Caption: Search is agency-executed; attachment is court-controlled after the provisional stage — three distinct legal acts (search, attachment, confiscation), not one undifferentiated "seizure power."*

## Plain-language intuition

If Lokpal suspects someone is hiding documents or might dissipate corruptly acquired
assets before a case concludes, it needs tools that act quickly — but quick action
creates real risk of harm to an eventually-innocent person. This lesson studies how the
Act balances speed (search, provisional attachment) against safeguards (reasoned
orders, time limits, and ultimate court control).

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** **Section 26** allows Lokpal, where it has reason to believe relevant
documents are secreted, to **authorise the investigating agency** to search for and
seize them.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 27** gives the **Inquiry Wing** civil-court powers during
preliminary inquiry: summoning and attendance, examination on oath, document discovery/
production, affidavit evidence, public-record requisition, and commissions.

✅ **Fact:** Proceedings before Lokpal are **deemed judicial proceedings** for the
false-evidence provision referenced by the Act. This does **not** transform Lokpal into
a criminal court generally.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 29** permits **provisional attachment for up to ninety days**
where recorded reasons and supporting material show a belief about **proceeds of
corruption** and a risk of concealment/transfer. The order and material go to the
**Special Court**.

✅ **Fact:** Under **Section 30**, the Prosecution Wing must apply within **thirty
days** for confirmation of the provisional attachment. **Acquittal** ordinarily leads
to **restoration**; **conviction** permits **confiscation** of proceeds relatable to the
offence.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 31** separately permits **Special Court confiscation in special
circumstances** on prima facie evidence **until acquittal**, with restoration or value
plus statutory interest if the order is annulled or the public servant is acquitted.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 32** permits Lokpal to **recommend transfer or suspension**
where continuation threatens inquiry, evidence, or witnesses. The Central Government
ordinarily accepts, unless written administrative reasons make it infeasible.

| Proposition | Correct? | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Inquiry Wing has specified civil-court powers | Yes | Section 27 |
| Lokpal proceedings are deemed judicial for false-evidence law | Yes | Section 27(2) |
| Lokpal is a civil court for **all** purposes | No | Powers are purpose-specific |
| Lokpal is a criminal court | No | The Special Court adjudicates |
| Lokpal has a general contempt jurisdiction | No general power located in the Act | Do not infer this from "judicial proceeding" |

## Example

Lokpal has reason to believe an official is hiding ledgers evidencing bribe payments.
It authorises the investigating agency to search and seize those ledgers under Section
26. Separately, concerned that the official might sell a property bought with corrupt
proceeds, Lokpal records its reasons and orders provisional attachment of that property
for up to ninety days under Section 29; the Prosecution Wing must then apply to the
Special Court within thirty days to confirm that attachment.

⚠️ **Limit of the example:** Search and attachment are **separate legal acts** serving
different purposes — one preserves evidence, the other preserves assets — and neither
automatically triggers the other.

## Comparison: Sections 29 and 31 are not the same attachment power

| Feature | Section 29 (provisional attachment) | Section 31 (special-circumstance confiscation) |
|---|---|---|
| Stage | Pre-conviction, time-limited (90 days) | Special-circumstance route, until acquittal |
| Trigger | Recorded reasons + risk of concealment/transfer | Prima facie evidence in special circumstances |
| Confirmation | Prosecution Wing applies within 30 days | Court-controlled under its own special route |
| Reversal consequence | Restoration on acquittal | Restoration/value + statutory interest if annulled/acquitted |

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "Allowing attachment of property before conviction, even provisionally,
risks serious harm to someone who may ultimately be acquitted."

**Reply:** The Act builds in multiple safeguards against that risk: attachment requires
**recorded reasons** and supporting material, is capped at **ninety days** in the first
instance, requires **Special Court confirmation** within a further thirty days, and
guarantees **restoration** (with statutory interest under Section 31's route) if the
person is acquitted or the order is annulled.

**Residual:** Restoration after the fact cannot fully undo the practical harm (loss of
use, reputational damage) a person may suffer during the attachment period even if
ultimately vindicated — a real, not merely theoretical, due-process cost of the
speed-versus-safeguard trade-off.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The **ninety-day** provisional-attachment period and the **thirty-day**
confirmation-application window are precise, frequently tested figures — do not
interchange them.

**Mains:** "Lokpal's coercive powers are real but court-controlled" is a defensible,
balanced thesis line for any "does Lokpal have teeth?" essay, directly supported by
this lesson's Section 29-31 material.

## Revision notes

1. **Section 26**: Lokpal authorises the investigating agency to search/seize secreted documents.
2. **Section 27**: Inquiry Wing has specified **civil-court powers** during preliminary inquiry.
3. Lokpal proceedings are **deemed judicial** for the false-evidence provision only — not a general civil-court or contempt jurisdiction.
4. **Section 29**: provisional attachment, up to **90 days**, on recorded reasons; order/material go to the Special Court.
5. **Section 30**: Prosecution Wing must apply for confirmation within **30 days**.
6. **Acquittal** ordinarily restores attached property; **conviction** permits confiscation of proceeds relatable to the offence.
7. **Section 31**: a **separate** special-circumstance confiscation route, with restoration/interest if annulled or acquitted.
8. **Section 32**: Lokpal may recommend transfer/suspension where continuation threatens inquiry, evidence or witnesses.
9. Search, attachment and confiscation are **three distinct legal acts**, not one undifferentiated power.

### Concept check

**Question:** Lokpal provisionally attaches a property under Section 29. Sixty days later, the accused is acquitted at trial. What happens to the attached property?

**Model answer:** On acquittal, the attached property is **ordinarily restored** to the person from whom it was attached. Section 29's provisional-attachment mechanism is designed to preserve suspected proceeds of corruption only until the matter is judicially resolved; an acquittal removes the legal basis for continued attachment, and restoration — not permanent confiscation — follows as the default consequence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that attachment, once ordered, automatically converts into confiscation regardless of trial outcome. Confiscation of proceeds relatable to the offence follows **conviction**, not attachment alone; restoration follows acquittal.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain the safeguards built into the Lokpal Act's provisional-attachment power under Section 29.

**Mains model answer:** Section 29 permits Lokpal to provisionally attach suspected proceeds of corruption for up to ninety days, but only where recorded reasons and supporting material establish both the corrupt character of the proceeds and a genuine risk of concealment or transfer. The order and supporting material must be sent to the Special Court, and under Section 30 the Prosecution Wing must apply for confirmation within thirty days, placing judicial oversight promptly over an initially administrative action. If the accused is ultimately acquitted, the property is ordinarily restored; the separate Section 31 route for special-circumstance confiscation similarly guarantees restoration or value plus statutory interest where an order is annulled or acquittal follows. These time limits, reasoned-order requirements and restoration guarantees collectively prevent attachment from operating as a disguised, irreversible penalty before conviction.

**Scoring rubric:** Correct 90-day/30-day figures (3 marks) + recorded-reasons/risk requirement (3 marks) + restoration/interest safeguard on acquittal (3 marks) + structure (1 mark).

## Plain-language intuition

Asset declaration serves two different purposes: making corruption harder to hide (if
your lifestyle outpaces your declared income, that is a red flag), and giving the law a
tool to presume wrongdoing when someone is caught with assets they cannot explain. The
2016 amendment changed *how* the first purpose is operationalised, while the second
purpose (the presumption rule) remained essentially distinct and unaffected.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** The **original Section 44** (2013 text) contained a more detailed
declaration structure. The **Lokpal and Lokayuktas (Amendment) Act, 2016** substituted
it **retrospectively from commencement**.

✅ **Fact:** The **current Section 44** states that every public servant shall declare
assets and liabilities **in such form and manner as may be prescribed**.

✅ **Fact:** **Section 45** provides a **rebuttable presumption** where a public servant
**wilfully or without justifiable reason fails to declare assets**, or supplies
**misleading information** and is found possessing **undisclosed or misdescribed
assets**.

✅ **Fact (verified status):** DoPT's official 2016 memoranda state that the earlier
2014 asset-return rules became **redundant** after substitution, and that fresh rules
would be prescribed. In the official DoPT/Lokpal material checked, **no
later fresh Section 44 form-and-manner rules were located**.

⚠️ **Limit:** This is a controlled search result, not proof that no such document could
exist anywhere. Do not describe the current statutory text as if the original 2014
public-disclosure design remains in force unchanged.

⚠️ **Limit:** ***Lok Prahari v Union of India* (2018)** concerned **election-candidate/
legislator disclosure**, sources of income, voter information, and a permanent
monitoring mechanism. It did **not** judicially restore the original Section 44 format
— this is a frequently confused cross-reference.

| Dimension | Original framework (2013) | Current statutory framework (post-2016) |
|---|---|---|
| Detail in the Act | Extensive declaration/publication architecture | General duty |
| Operative wording | Specified assets/liabilities and related disclosure design | Form and manner to be prescribed |
| Rules status | 2014 rules existed | DoPT stated old rules redundant after amendment |
| Safe exam statement | Historical design | Current Section 44 + rule-status qualification |

```text
WILFUL / UNJUSTIFIABLE FAILURE TO DECLARE
                 OR
MISLEADING INFORMATION + UNDISCLOSED ASSETS FOUND
                 |
REBUTTABLE PRESUMPTION
assets belong to the public servant, acquired by corrupt means
                 |
public servant may prove otherwise
```

## Example

A public servant is found possessing property far beyond declared income, and an
inquiry establishes the declaration itself was misleading. Section 45's rebuttable
presumption then shifts the practical burden: the assets are presumed to belong to the
public servant and to have been corruptly acquired, unless that person can satisfactorily
explain the discrepancy.

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "Replacing a detailed, specific asset-disclosure design with a vague
'form and manner to be prescribed' duty, and then apparently never issuing replacement
rules, effectively guts the transparency purpose of asset declaration."

**Reply:** The underlying **legal duty** to declare assets was retained, not abolished
— the amendment changed only where the operative detail would live (delegated rules
rather than the Act itself), which is a common legislative drafting technique for
provisions needing administrative flexibility.

**Residual:** The honest, verified problem is that the practical transparency value of
a declaration duty is weakened if no clear, current form-and-manner rule is actually in
force to operationalise it — this is a genuine implementation gap, not merely a
theoretical drafting nuance, and critics are right to flag it as a dilution concern
(discussed further in Lesson 19's reform map).

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** The precise chain — **2016 amendment substituted Section 44** → **duty
retained, form/manner to be prescribed** → **old rules redundant** → **no later fresh
rule verified** — is a layered factual sequence examiners like to test with scrambled
or incomplete versions.

**Mains:** Never cite *Lok Prahari* as having restored the original Section 44 format —
this is one of the most consequential misattribution traps on this entire topic.

## Revision notes

1. **Original Section 44** (2013) had a detailed declaration/publication design.
2. The **2016 Amendment Act** substituted Section 44, **retrospectively from commencement**.
3. **Current Section 44**: general duty to declare, "in such form and manner as may be prescribed."
4. DoPT (2016): old 2014 rules described as **redundant**; fresh rules to be prescribed.
5. **No later fresh form-and-manner rules were located** in the official repositories checked.
6. **Section 45**: rebuttable presumption on wilful/unjustifiable non-declaration or misleading declaration plus undisclosed assets found.
7. The presumption can be **rebutted** by the public servant.
8. ***Lok Prahari* (2018)** concerns **electoral** disclosure/monitoring — it did **not** restore the original Section 44 format.
9. The 2016 amendment retained the duty but changed where its operative detail lives — a genuine implementation-gap concern, not merely a drafting nuance.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student writes: "*Lok Prahari v Union of India* (2018) restored the Lokpal Act's original detailed asset-declaration format." Is this correct?

**Model answer:** No, this is incorrect. *Lok Prahari* concerned **electoral** disclosure — directing amendment of election Form 26/rules so candidates and associates declare sources of income, and requiring a permanent mechanism to monitor disproportionate asset growth of legislators and associates. It did not interpret or restore Section 44 of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, which remains governed by its 2016-amended "form and manner to be prescribed" text, independent of the *Lok Prahari* holding.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating any Supreme Court case about "asset disclosure" as interchangeable regardless of which statute or context it actually addresses. *Lok Prahari* is an electoral-law case, not a Lokpal Act Section 44 case.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Trace how Section 44 of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act has changed since 2013, and assess the current status of asset-declaration rules.

**Mains model answer:** The original 2013 Section 44 contained a detailed declaration and publication architecture for public servants' assets and liabilities. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas (Amendment) Act, 2016 substituted this provision retrospectively from commencement, replacing the detailed design with a general duty to declare assets "in such form and manner as may be prescribed." The Department of Personnel and Training's 2016 memoranda described the earlier 2014 rules as redundant following this substitution and indicated fresh rules would follow; however, no later fresh form-and-manner rules were located in the official repositories checked. The current, honest position is therefore a retained legal duty operating without a clearly verified current operative rule, which is a genuine transparency gap distinct from, and not cured by, the unrelated electoral-disclosure holding in *Lok Prahari v Union of India* (2018).

## Lesson 17: Lokpal, CVC and CBI — A Functional Chain, Not a Hierarchy

Progress: 17/19 | Stage: Advanced | Lesson: Lokpal, CVC and CBI — A Functional Chain, Not a Hierarchy

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — Sections 20, 23 and 25 only (the Lokpal Act's own cross-references); CVC/CBI's own architecture is owned by a separate topic and is not re-taught here
🔍 CA Search: "Lokpal CVC CBI coordination anti-corruption 2026"
📰 CA Found: None altering the statutory functional-chain architecture
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🖼️ Visual: Differentiated roles, not a single command pyramid

```text
COMPLAINT ACCOUNTABILITY        VIGILANCE             INVESTIGATION
      Lokpal                    CVC / CVOs            CBI / DSPE
          \                         |                     /
           \                        |                    /
            +----- departmental / prosecution choices --+
                              |
                        SPECIAL COURT
                              |
                      judicial determination
```

*Caption: Coordination is necessary because no single body lawfully performs every function — this is a functional network, not a chain of command.*

## Plain-language intuition

It is tempting to picture Lokpal as "the boss" of CVC and CBI in anti-corruption
matters. That picture is wrong. Each institution has its own statutory source, its own
domain, and its own output — the relationship between them is one of **referral and
limited supervision**, defined precisely by Sections 20, 23 and 25 of the Lokpal Act,
not a general command relationship. (CVC and CBI's own internal architecture —
composition, appointment, independent statutory powers — belongs to a separate topic
and is not re-taught here; this lesson teaches only how the Lokpal Act itself connects
to them.)

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** **Lokpal** is the complaint-routing and decision institution under its own
Act.

✅ **Fact:** Under **Section 20**, where Lokpal proceeds by preliminary inquiry for
Groups A-D Union officials, the matter is referred to the **CVC** — Groups A/B
reporting back to Lokpal, Groups C/D proceeding under the CVC's own Act (as studied in
Lesson 12).

✅ **Fact:** Under **Section 23**, Lokpal holds **prosecution-sanction power** for the
Section 20(7)(a) route, subject to the Section 23(3) constitutional-office
qualification (as studied in Lesson 13).

✅ **Fact:** Under **Section 25**, Lokpal has **superintendence and direction** over
**DSPE** in matters it refers for preliminary inquiry or investigation — but it
**cannot** require the agency to investigate or dispose of a case in a particular
manner. A DSPE officer investigating a Lokpal-referred case **cannot be transferred
without Lokpal's approval**, and DSPE may, with Lokpal's consent, appoint a panel of
advocates other than government advocates for referred cases.

```text
LOKPAL-REFERRED MATTER
       |
superintendence + lawful directions
       |
NO DICTATION OF PARTICULAR MANNER OR DISPOSAL
       |
investigating officer transfer requires Lokpal approval
       |
advocate panel requires Lokpal consent
```

| Power | Lokpal position | Key limit |
|---|---|---|
| Direct DSPE in a referred matter | Yes | Cannot dictate particular investigation/disposal |
| Transfer approval for a referred-case investigator | Yes | Only for the Lokpal-referred case |
| Conduct general police investigation | No | Agency function |
| Authorise search/seizure | Yes | Through the agency entrusted with investigation |
| Require information/documents | Yes | For inquiry/investigation under the Act |
| Recommend suspension/transfer | Yes | Government ordinarily accepts; may record administrative reasons |
| Convict or sentence | No | Court function |

## Grievance redress versus corruption — a necessary boundary

⚠️ **Inference:** A classical public-grievance mechanism (delayed pension, poor service
delivery) and the Lokpal's corruption-specific jurisdiction are **not the same thing**.
The Act's Section 2 complaint definition is tied to an alleged **Prevention of
Corruption Act offence** — an ordinary administrative grievance, however frustrating,
does not by itself become a Lokpal complaint merely because it concerns a public
servant.

| Function | Lokpal | CVC | CBI/DSPE | Special Court |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Receive a prescribed Lokpal complaint | Yes | Referred route | No primary Lokpal intake | No |
| Preliminary inquiry | Inquiry Wing/agency | Yes, for referred categories | May be assigned | No |
| Criminal investigation | Directs agency | No general police role | Yes | No |
| Referred-case superintendence | Yes | Statutory role in its own field | Subject to lawful oversight | Judicial review/trial |
| Prosecution sanction under the Lokpal route | Yes | No | Uses sanction/process | No |
| Trial and guilt | No | No | No | Yes |

## Example

A Lokpal-referred investigation is underway with a particular CBI officer assigned. The
government wishes to transfer that officer for unrelated administrative reasons. Under
Section 25, this transfer **cannot occur without Lokpal's approval** — a direct,
case-specific safeguard. But Lokpal cannot simultaneously instruct the CBI, in that same
case, to "find evidence of X" or "close the file" — such direction-on-the-merits would
exceed the superintendence power's statutory limit.

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "If Lokpal cannot dictate how a referred investigation is conducted or
disposed of, its 'superintendence' power is mostly symbolic."

**Reply:** The power is narrower than general command, but not symbolic — the
transfer-approval safeguard and the ability to require periodic statements/guidelines
under Section 25(2) give Lokpal real, continuous visibility and protection against the
single most common method of quietly derailing a sensitive investigation (removing the
officer handling it).

**Residual:** Genuine operational control of the investigation's pace, thoroughness and
resourcing still rests with the CBI/DSPE itself — Lokpal's functional role is
coordination and narrow protection, not substitute command, and this is a real
institutional limitation, not an oversight.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** "CVC is subordinate to Lokpal" and "Lokpal controls all CBI work" are both
classic false options — the correct position is category-specific and case-specific
functional coordination.

**Mains:** "Analyse the Lokpal-CVC-CBI relationship; why is calling it a hierarchy
misleading?" is a frequently framed GS-II question directly answered by this lesson's
material.

## Revision notes

1. Lokpal, CVC and CBI each have a **distinct legal source, domain and output**.
2. **Section 20**: preliminary-inquiry referral to CVC for Groups A-D, with Groups A/B versus C/D routing differences.
3. **Section 23**: Lokpal's prosecution-sanction power for the Section 20(7)(a) route.
4. **Section 25**: Lokpal superintendence/direction over DSPE for **referred matters only**; cannot dictate manner/disposal; transfer of a referred-case investigator needs Lokpal approval.
5. The relationship is a **functional network**, not a single command pyramid.
6. A classical public-grievance complaint is **not** automatically a Lokpal complaint — the Section 2 corruption-specific definition governs.
7. Lokpal's referred-case safeguards are real but narrow — they do not amount to operational command over CBI's day-to-day investigation.
8. This lesson teaches only the Lokpal Act's own cross-references (Sections 20, 23, 25) — CVC/CBI's own full institutional architecture belongs to a separate topic.

### Concept check

**Question:** Is it accurate to say "the CVC is a subordinate office of the Lokpal"?

**Model answer:** No. The CVC is a distinct statutory body with its own legal source and functions; under Section 20 of the Lokpal Act, it performs a category-specific preliminary-inquiry role for referred Groups A-D complaints (reporting to Lokpal for Groups A/B, proceeding under its own CVC Act for Groups C/D), but this referral relationship is functional and case-specific, not a general subordination of the CVC to the Lokpal.

**Misconception to avoid:** Inferring institutional subordination from the existence of a statutory referral relationship. Referral and reporting obligations in specific categories of cases do not create a general command hierarchy between two independently constituted statutory bodies.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Why is it misleading to describe the Lokpal-CVC-CBI relationship as a simple hierarchy?

**Mains model answer:** Each institution retains a distinct legal source, domain and output. Under Section 20, Lokpal refers preliminary inquiries concerning Groups A-D Union officials to the CVC, which reports back to Lokpal for Groups A/B but proceeds under its own CVC Act for Groups C/D — a category-specific, not general, referral. Under Section 25, Lokpal exercises superintendence and direction over the CBI/DSPE only for matters it has referred, expressly barred from dictating investigation or disposal in a particular manner, though it must approve any transfer of the investigating officer in such cases. Describing this as a hierarchy wrongly implies general command; the accurate description is a functional, case-specific coordination network in which authority shifts with the stage, category and referral status of each matter.

**Scoring rubric:** Correct Section 20 routing (3 marks) + correct Section 25 superintendence limit (4 marks) + explicit "network, not hierarchy" conclusion (3 marks).

## Plain-language intuition

Why does India not simply have one uniform Lokayukta model copied into every State?
Because anti-corruption institution-building for State governments is itself a matter
of State legislative choice; Parliament's 2013 Act could require that States
**establish** such a body, but could not — without displacing ordinary federal
legislative competence — dictate every State's exact institutional design.

## Technical doctrine

✅ **Fact:** **Section 63** requires every State, if it had not already done so, to
establish a Lokayukta by a **law of the State Legislature** within **one year** from
commencement of the Union Act.

✅ **Fact:** Section 63 does **not** prescribe a uniform national composition,
selection panel, jurisdiction, tenure, removal process, investigation wing, binding
force, or Upalokayukta model.

✅ **Fact:** Local sources record **Maharashtra** as the **first State** to establish a
Lokayukta, in **1971** (noting earlier Odisha legislation with later enforcement).

⚠️ **Inference:** The Union Act creates a **minimum institutional obligation**, while
State legislation determines **design quality**. Federal flexibility permits
contextual adaptation and also produces genuinely uneven accountability capacity across
States.

⚠️ **Limit:** This topic does **not** freeze a current State count or classify named
States by today's vacancies or performance — "strong" and "weak" below describe legal
**design features**, not a permanent ranking of actual States.

| What Section 63 does | What it does **not** do |
|---|---|
| Requires every State to establish a Lokayukta | Prescribe identical composition |
| Requires establishment through State law | Fix one appointment process |
| Sets a Union statutory expectation | Uniformly define CM/minister/bureaucracy jurisdiction |
| Leaves operative design to States | Equalise powers, budgets, or removal safeguards |

## Comparing strong and weak statutory design (not a ranking of actual States)

| Design axis | Stronger statutory design | Weaker statutory design |
|---|---|---|
| Jurisdiction | Broad high-office and official coverage with narrow exclusions | Wide exclusions |
| Appointment | Multi-institutional, transparent panel | Executive-dominant process |
| Investigation | Dedicated/insulated capacity | Dependence without safeguards |
| Recommendations | Reasoned response and follow-up duties | Easily ignored advice |
| Tenure/removal | Fixed tenure and protected removal | Easy executive displacement |
| Reporting | Timely legislative/public reporting | Opaque disposal |

*Caption: Federal comparison should evaluate statutory features, not rely on a changing vacancy list that this topic deliberately does not freeze.*

## Comparison: Lokpal versus Lokayukta

| Head | Lokpal | Lokayukta |
|---|---|---|
| Level | Union | State |
| Governing law | 2013 parliamentary Act | Relevant State Act |
| Uniform composition | Yes, under Section 3 | No national uniformity |
| PM/Union officials | Lokpal jurisdiction | Not State Lokayukta's core |
| CM/State officials | Not automatically Lokpal's domain | Depends on State law |
| CVC/CBI routing | Express Union statutory links (Sections 20, 23, 25) | State-specific machinery |

*Caption: Never transplant Lokpal's composition or five-member Selection Committee into every State statute — this is one of the most common and serious errors on this topic.*

## Example

Two States may differ sharply on whether their Chief Minister is covered by their
Lokayukta, on whether the Lokayukta has its own investigating wing, and on whether its
recommendations bind the government or are merely advisory — none of which is dictated
by Section 63 itself. An answer that assumes a uniform national Lokayukta design is
factually wrong regardless of which specific State is cited.

## Strongest objection, and the honest reply

**Objection:** "Leaving Lokayukta design entirely to the States produces unequal
anti-corruption protection by geography — citizens in a State with a weak Lokayukta
law are effectively less protected than citizens elsewhere."

**Reply:** This is a genuine cost of the federal design, but the alternative — Parliament
unilaterally imposing one detailed Lokayukta model on every State — would displace a
significant area of State legislative autonomy over their own public-service
accountability architecture, which is itself a serious federalism concern.

**Residual:** A **model State Lokayukta law**, offered as guidance rather than
imposed uniformity, is the most defensible reform direction — it preserves State
legislative choice while raising the floor of minimum institutional quality, a proposal
developed further in Lesson 19.

## UPSC application

**Prelims:** **Maharashtra, 1971** as the first State to establish a Lokayukta is a
precise, frequently tested fact; do not universalise one State's selection panel, age
limit or tenure to all Lokayuktas.

**Mains:** "Why does Section 63 not create a uniform national model of Lokayuktas?" is
directly answerable from this lesson's design-variable table.

## Revision notes

1. **Section 63**: every State must establish a Lokayukta **by State law**, within **one year** of the Union Act's commencement (if not already done).
2. Section 63 does **not** prescribe uniform composition, appointment, jurisdiction, tenure, removal, or investigative capacity.
3. **Maharashtra (1971)** was the first State to establish a Lokayukta.
4. The Union Act creates a **minimum obligation**; States determine **design quality**.
5. This topic does not freeze a current State-by-State vacancy or performance count.
6. **Never transplant** Lokpal's Section 3 composition or five-member Selection Committee onto State Lokayuktas.
7. A **model State Lokayukta law** (guidance, not imposition) is the most defensible reform direction.
8. Federal flexibility is a genuine value, but it comes at the cost of uneven accountability capacity across States.

### Concept check

**Question:** A student claims, "Every Lokayukta in India follows the same five-member Selection Committee used for the Union Lokpal." Evaluate this claim.

**Model answer:** This claim is false. Section 63 of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act requires every State to establish a Lokayukta through its own State legislation, but it does not prescribe the Lokpal's Section 3/Section 4 composition or Selection Committee model as a mandatory template. Each State's Lokayukta statute independently defines its own composition, appointment process, jurisdiction and tenure, producing genuinely diverse institutional designs across India rather than one uniform national model.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming the Union Lokpal's detailed, uniform statutory design under Sections 3-8 automatically extends to every State Lokayukta. Section 63 mandates establishment only; design is a State legislative choice.

### Lesson Mains practice — 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** Why does Section 63 of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 produce a non-uniform national model of Lokayuktas?

**Mains model answer:** Section 63 requires every State, if it had not already done so, to establish a Lokayukta through a law of its own State Legislature within one year of the Union Act's commencement. It does not transplant the Union Lokpal's composition, selection committee, jurisdiction or powers into State law. Consequently, States differ on whether the Chief Minister is covered, which officials and local bodies fall within jurisdiction, whether the institution has its own investigative wing, and how its recommendations are enforced. This diversity respects federal legislative autonomy over State-level accountability architecture, but it also produces genuinely unequal anti-corruption protection across States, which a carefully designed model State Lokayukta law — offered as guidance rather than binding uniformity — could help address without displacing legislative choice.

## Technical doctrine: the independence scorecard

✅ **Fact:** Independence-supporting features include **multi-institutional

### Concept check

**Question:** Is it fair to call the Lokpal "toothless" simply because it has no suo motu power?

**Model answer:** Not entirely fair as a standalone claim. The absence of suo motu power is a genuine structural limitation, but the Lokpal still holds real coercive capacities once a complaint is filed — ordering preliminary inquiry or investigation, directing search and provisional attachment, granting prosecution sanction, and controlling transfer of investigating officers in referred cases. "No suo motu power" is one accurate limitation among several real powers; calling the institution wholly "toothless" overstates the limitation and understates its actual process capacities.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a single named weakness (no suo motu power) as proof of total institutional ineffectiveness, rather than weighing it against the institution's demonstrated process powers.

**Mains model answer:** The Lokpal's design treats independence and accountability as mutually reinforcing, not competing, values. Independence safeguards — plural appointment, fixed tenure, protected removal, Consolidated Fund-charged expenditure, and referred-case control over investigating-officer transfers — protect the institution from improper executive pressure. Accountability safeguards — reasoned bench decisions, Special Court adjudication, hearing rights for the accused, confidentiality rules calibrated to protect fairness rather than conceal wrongdoing, and the federal duty to create State Lokayuktas — ensure the institution itself remains answerable and does not become an unchecked power centre. Genuine institutional credibility requires both simultaneously: independence without accountability risks arbitrariness, while accountability without independence risks capture by the very power it is meant to scrutinise.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These three capstone questions are distinct from every lesson-local Mains question
above — they synthesise across multiple lessons rather than testing any single stage.

## Capstone 10 marks | 150 words

**Mains question:** "The Lokpal is deliberately built to reach high political office while filtering how that reach is exercised." Explain.

**Mains model answer:** The Lokpal Act extends jurisdiction to a serving or former Prime Minister, Union Ministers, MPs and Groups A-D officials, rejecting the idea that anti-corruption oversight should stop below the political executive. Yet this reach is immediately filtered: a Prime Minister inquiry excludes five sovereign-sensitive domains and requires full-bench, two-thirds approval and in-camera procedure; MPs retain genuine speech and vote privilege, though not bribery immunity after *Sita Soren*; and State-connected persons require separate consent before Lokpal can act. These filters are not loopholes but deliberate due-process safeguards, reconciling the headline inclusion of high office with institutional stability, confidentiality and federal propriety — breadth of jurisdiction and operational restraint were designed together, not as an afterthought.

**Scoring rubric:** Correct breadth stated (3 marks) + at least two correctly described filters (5 marks) + explicit "designed together" conclusion (2 marks).

## Capstone 15 marks | 250 words

**Mains question:** Evaluate whether the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 achieves a workable balance between institutional independence and procedural accountability.

**Mains model answer:** The Act pursues independence through plural appointment (a Selection Committee spanning the executive, legislature and judiciary, assisted by an expert Search Committee), fixed non-renewable tenure with a protected, dual-route removal process, Consolidated Fund-charged finance, and case-specific control over the transfer of DSPE officers handling referred matters. These features insulate the institution from casual executive pressure.

Accountability is pursued through a staged process architecture: preliminary inquiry and investigation remain analytically and institutionally distinct from adjudication, a three-Member bench reviews reports before any sanction decision, hearings are guaranteed to accused public servants, and the Special Court — not Lokpal itself — decides guilt within a statutory trial-timeline target. Confidentiality rules, particularly for Prime Ministerial complaints, are calibrated to protect fairness rather than conceal wrongdoing.

The balance is genuinely workable on paper: no single actor controls accusation, investigation, prosecution and judgment simultaneously. However, real limitations persist — an executive-weighted Selection Committee, absence of suo motu power, continued dependence on the CBI for investigation, a long historical gap between statutory mandate and actual wing constitution (2014 to 2024-2025), and uneven State-level Lokayukta design. The Act's architecture is defensible in principle; its practical delivery remains contingent on timely appointments, adequate staffing and genuine inter-agency coordination that the statute cannot itself guarantee.

**Scoring rubric:** At least three independence features (5 marks) + at least three accountability features (5 marks) + at least three honestly stated limitations (3 marks) + qualified, non-absolute verdict (2 marks).

## Capstone 20 marks | 250 words

**Mains question:** "A statutory ombudsman is only as strong as the agencies and federal structures it must depend upon." Critically examine this claim with reference to the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013.

**Mains model answer:** The claim is substantially correct, though not completely. The Lokpal possesses genuine independent capacities: it can order preliminary inquiry, authorise search and provisional attachment, grant prosecution sanction, and — for the Prime Minister, Ministers and MPs under Section 24 — file cases directly before the Special Court. Its own statutory Inquiry and Prosecution Wings, though only formally constituted in 2024-2025, add institutional capacity beyond mere complaint-routing.

Yet dependence remains structurally real. Criminal investigation itself is performed by the CBI/DSPE or another agency, not by Lokpal; its superintendence over DSPE is confined to referred matters and expressly cannot dictate investigative manner or disposal, leaving actual evidence-gathering quality outside Lokpal's direct control. For Groups C and D officials, preliminary inquiry proceeds under the CVC's own Act rather than reporting back to Lokpal. At the federal layer, Section 63 leaves Lokayukta design entirely to State legislatures, producing uneven State-level capacity that no Union-level reform can directly cure without displacing legislative autonomy.

The accurate verdict is therefore not "powerless dependency" but "bounded agency": the Lokpal exercises real, law-backed coordinating and sanctioning authority, while its ultimate effectiveness is genuinely conditioned by the capacity and independence of the CBI, the CVC's category-specific role, and each State's own Lokayukta design — a capable coordinator, not an omnipotent super-agency.

**Scoring rubric:** Independent Lokpal powers correctly named (5 marks) + genuine dependence correctly identified across agency and federal dimensions (7 marks) + explicit "bounded agency" qualified verdict rejecting both extremes (3 marks).

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Identity and Core Status

> **One-line definition:** The Lokpal is a **statutory Union anti-corruption
> ombudsman** created by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013; it is neither a
> constitutional body nor a criminal court.

| Field | Register entry |
|---|---|
| Source | Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 |
| Nature | Statutory, multi-member |
| Level | Union |
| Core role | Complaint scrutiny, preliminary inquiry/investigation routing, prosecution-enabling decisions |
| Adjudication | Special Court |
| State counterpart | Lokayukta, under State law |

## 2. Evolution

```text
Swedish ombudsman idea (1809)
        ->
First ARC recommends Lokpal/Lokayukta (1966-70)
        ->
Ten Bills lapse (1968-2011)
        ->
Anna Hazare movement as bounded political context (2011)
        ->
Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 (assent 1 Jan 2014)
        ->
Commencement, 16 Jan 2014
        ->
2016 asset-declaration amendment (Section 44 substituted)
        ->
Complaint Rules, 2020; Inquiry Wing, 2024; Prosecution Wing, 2025
```

## 3. Composition (Section 3)

| Element | Exact rule |
|---|---|
| Chairperson | One |
| Members | Not more than eight |
| Judicial Members | 50% of Members |
| Representation floor | Not less than 50% of Members from SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women |
| Minimum age | 45 |
| Non-judicial expertise | At least 25 years in named fields |
| Tenure | Five years or age 70, whichever earlier |

> **Trap:** The Chairperson is not included in the denominator when the Act says "Members."

## 4. Eligibility and Disqualifications

```text
Chairperson: current/former CJI OR current/former SC Judge OR
             eminent person (25 yrs expertise)
Judicial Member: current/former SC Judge OR current/former HC Chief Justice
Non-judicial Member: impeccable integrity + 25 yrs expertise

DISQUALIFIED: sitting MP/legislator, Panchayat/Municipality member,
moral-turpitude conviction, removed/dismissed from service, under 45
MUST SEVER: political affiliation, business, profession, other office of profit
```

## 5. Appointment (Section 4)

```text
Search Committee (>=7 experts, 50% diversity floor)
             |
Panel/recommendations -- not exclusive
             |
Selection Committee: PM | LS Speaker | LS LoP | CJI/nominee | eminent jurist
             |
Recommendation
             |
President appoints
```

- The eminent jurist is nominated by the President on recommendation of the other four Selection Committee members.
- Section 4(2): a Selection Committee vacancy does not invalidate an appointment.
- *Common Cause v Union of India* (2017): the Act remains workable despite an LoP vacancy.
- The current text names the Leader of Opposition — no largest-opposition-party substitute exists.

## 6. Tenure and Post-Tenure (Sections 6, 8)

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Tenure | 5 years from entry, or age 70, whichever earlier |
| Member-to-Chairperson | Permitted only if aggregate tenure stays within 5 years |
| Reappointment | Barred, subject to the above exception |
| Post-tenure bars | Diplomatic/UT-administrator/presidential-warrant posts; office of profit |
| Electoral cooling-off | 5 years for President, VP, MP, State legislature, Municipality, Panchayat |

## 7. Removal and Suspension (Section 37)

| Route | Trigger/process |
|---|---|
| Misbehaviour | Petition by >=100 MPs -> Presidential reference -> Supreme Court inquiry -> Presidential removal |
| Suspension | President may suspend on SC recommendation/interim order during the pending reference |
| Direct grounds | Insolvency; paid outside employment during tenure; infirmity rendering person unfit |
| Deemed misbehaviour | Government-contract interest (routes into the Supreme Court inquiry process) |

> **Trap:** Do not copy the CVC/NHRC/Election Commission removal formula — Section 37 is self-contained.

## 8. Jurisdiction (Section 14)

| Covered category | Qualification |
|---|---|
| PM/former PM | Special exclusions and procedure (Lesson 9) |
| Union Minister/former Minister | Covered |
| MP/former MP | Article 105(2) speech/vote protection (Lesson 10) |
| Groups A-D Union public servants | Category-specific CVC routing may apply |
| Former public servants | Covered for conduct during the covered period |
| Government-created/controlled/financed bodies | Statutory and notification conditions (clause f/g) |
| Specified societies/trusts | Foreign-contribution threshold >Rs 10 lakh/yr (clause h) |

- Section 1(3): the Act extends to public servants in and outside India.
- Armed-forces personnel under named service Acts are excluded from this Act's public-servant definition.
- As of October 2026, the Supreme Court's 20 February 2025 stay of the Lokpal's High Court-judge jurisdiction order remains in force and unresolved on the merits.

## 10. MPs and Privilege

- Section 14(2) respects Article 105(2) for genuine speech/vote in Parliament or committees.
- *Sita Soren v Union of India* (2024, 7-judge, unanimous): bribery is not immunised merely because it relates to a vote.
- The judgment does not abolish privilege; it separates the legislative act from the corrupt bargain.

## 20. Prelims Rapid Revision — 20 Facts

1. Lokpal is statutory, not constitutional.
2. One Chairperson plus not more than eight Members.
3. 50% of Members are Judicial Members.
4. At least 50% of Members satisfy the representation rule.
5. Minimum entry age is 45.
6. Non-judicial expertise requirement is at least 25 years.
7. Tenure is five years or age 70, whichever earlier.
8. President appoints on Selection Committee recommendation.
9. Search Committee has at least seven experts.
10. Search Committee's panel is not exclusive.
11. Selection Committee vacancy does not invalidate appointment.

13. MPs retain Article 105(2) protection, but bribery is not privileged after *Sita Soren*.
14. Groups A-D Union officials are covered; CVC routing differs by Group.
15. Preliminary inquiry and investigation are legally different stages.
16. Lokpal superintendence over DSPE is referred-matter specific.
17. Section 53 uses a seven-year bar from the alleged-offence date.
18. Section 44 was substituted in 2016; no later fresh rule has been verified.
19. Section 63 leaves Lokayukta design to State legislation.
20. The Special Court, not Lokpal, decides criminal guilt.
