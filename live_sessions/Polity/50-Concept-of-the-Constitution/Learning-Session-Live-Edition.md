# Concept of the Constitution — Live Session Edition

A constitution answers two different questions: **who may exercise public power, through which institutions and procedures?** And **what may even a lawful government never do arbitrarily?** The first concerns constituting authority; the second concerns constitutionalism. Keeping them separate clarifies rule of law, limited government, separation, checks, constitutional morality and amendment limits.

**Current-affairs control date: 3 October 2026.** The single live anchor is the Supreme Court's official judgment dated **23 September 2026** in *Dr. Jaya Thakur and Others v. Union of India and Another*, 2026 INSC 1040. The Court directed that papers concerning the constitutional challenge to the 2023 law on appointment of the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners be placed before the Chief Justice for considering a Constitution Bench. The judges delivered differing opinions on whether a Constitution Bench was required; one opinion used Ambedkar's account of constitutional morality as conventions and voluntary self-restraint that give substance to a written constitution's legal skeleton. ✅ **Fact:** their difference led to placement of the papers before the Chief Justice, and this was not a final merits ruling on the Act's validity. ⚠️ **Inference:** it illustrates the gap between possessing constitutional forms and sustaining constitutionalism through institutional restraint.

### A community before constitutional rules

Imagine a new political community with people, territory and shared problems, but no settled answer to who makes law, executes it, decides disputes, shares power with regions or protects dissenters. Elections alone cannot answer these questions because elections themselves need prior rules. The community needs a higher arrangement that creates offices, allocates competence and binds winners and losers.

### Visual: five jobs of a constitution

| Job | Question | Indian illustration | Limit of illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Define community | Who and what territory form the order? | Article 1 | Does not settle every citizenship issue |
| Create institutions | Who legislates and adjudicates? | Parts V-VI; Articles 79, 124 | Creation does not make an organ unlimited |
| Allocate power | Which organ or level may act? | Articles 73/162, 245-255; Seventh Schedule | Allocation still generates disputes |
| Limit government | What binds public power? | Part III; Articles 13, 32, 226 | Rights need access and compliance |
| Express purposes | What should public action serve? | Preamble; Parts IV and IVA | Goals do not bypass competence or resources |

*Caption: a constitution is blueprint, boundary map, rights charter and statement of purpose.*

A **constitution** is the fundamental arrangement that identifies the political community, creates public institutions, allocates and limits their powers, regulates relations between government and people, protects rights and states governing purposes.

Its **constitutive function** creates authority. Parliament and courts do not possess public power naturally; the constitutional order gives them legal existence and competence. Its **restraining function** prevents conferred authority from becoming arbitrary. The same Constitution that creates Parliament subjects legislation to constitutional limits.

```text
Collective action requires institutions
        -> institutions require competence
        -> competence creates domination risk
        -> rights, procedures and review restrain it
        -> social change requires lawful adaptation
        -> legitimacy requires public justification
```

Articles 79 and 124 illustrate creation; Articles 13, 32 and 226 restraint; the Preamble orientation; Article 368 change. But quotation of text does not prove successful operation. Remedies may be inaccessible and conventions may deteriorate.

**Objection:** current democratic choice should not be blocked by an old text.

**Reply:** democracy needs stable rules defining the electorate, competition, opposition and lawful change. A temporary majority cannot be allowed to cancel future competition.

**Residual:** excessive entrenchment can obstruct reform. The design must protect democratic foundations while retaining lawful change.

### UPSC application

The 2023 Prelims demand on the chief purpose of a constitution tests both creation and limitation. A Mains thesis should say: *a constitution constitutes power and places that power under higher law*.

### Revision notes

1. A constitution is the fundamental arrangement of the political community.
2. It creates institutions and competence.
3. It allocates power horizontally and territorially.
4. Its constitutive function explains lawful authority.
5. Its restraining function checks arbitrariness.
6. Rights, procedures, review and remedies are distinct restraints.
7. Preamble and DPSPs orient public power.
8. Article 368 permits managed change.
9. Elections presuppose constitutional rules.
10. Textual existence does not prove constitutional government.
11. Strong answers link authority, restraint, adaptation and legitimacy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it incomplete to define a constitution only as a document creating government offices?

**Model answer:** Creation is only its constitutive function. A constitution also allocates competence, limits arbitrary action, protects rights, manages change and states public purposes. A document that creates rulers without effective restraint is constitutionally thin.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating welfare goals alone as the chief purpose; welfare must be pursued through lawfully created and limited institutions.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how a constitution performs both constitutive and restraining functions. Use Indian illustrations.

**Model solution:** A constitution first **constitutes** public power. Articles 79 and 124 establish Parliament and the Supreme Court, while Articles 73, 162 and 245-255 distribute executive and legislative authority. It then **restrains** the power it creates. Fundamental Rights, Articles 13, 32 and 226, parliamentary responsibility, federal limits and judicial review prevent authority from becoming arbitrary. The Preamble and Directive Principles orient power toward justice and welfare, while Article 368 permits lawful adaptation. Constitutional design therefore joins authority with accountability. The qualification is that text cannot guarantee restraint by itself; access to remedies, institutional independence, conventions and public vigilance convert legal limits into constitutionalism.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** dual definition — 2; two constitutive anchors — 3; two restraining anchors — 3; text-practice qualification — 1; verdict — 1.

### One word, four layers

Learners often say “the Constitution says” when a proposition comes from a judgment, statute, rule or convention. Accuracy requires three questions: what is the source, what is its rank, and how is it enforced?

### Visual: rule-source map

| Layer | Meaning | Example | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitution | supreme foundational text and amendments | Article 75(3), Part III | constituted authority must conform |
| Constitutional law | wider rules on institutions, powers, rights and remedies | text plus binding doctrine and constitutional statutes | operationalises the framework |
| Ordinary law | legislation under conferred power | Representation of the People Act, 1951 | invalid if constitutionally inconsistent |
| Convention | political practice guiding lawful discretion | appointing a PM able to command confidence | politically important, not automatically justiciable |

*Caption: constitutional law is wider than text; ordinary law and convention have different sources and remedies.*

The Constitution is foundational. **Constitutional law** includes the text, valid amendments, authoritative interpretation and legal rules governing public institutions. Not every repeated practice becomes constitutional law: it may be convention, habit or abuse.

Ordinary legislation derives competence from the Constitution. Indian Parliament is therefore not sovereign in the classic British sense. Federal competence, rights and structural limits remain reviewable.

A convention guides discretion where several legal choices exist. Confidence, cabinet solidarity and restraint by formal heads substantially depend on conventions.

```text
LAW: legal source -> breach may produce invalidity -> legal remedy
CONVENTION: accepted political practice -> breach triggers political sanction -> not automatically a court command
```

A convention cannot override express text. Nor should courts turn every desirable practice into a judicial direction. Text supplies boundaries; convention helps offices exercise choices responsibly.

**Objection:** non-justiciable conventions are too weak to matter.

**Reply:** parliamentary government cannot litigate every confidence, resignation or courtesy question. Political enforcement is a real constitutional mechanism.

**Residual:** erosion may justify legal clarification, but over-legalisation can shift political responsibility to courts.

### UPSC application

Use source-accurate verbs: the Constitution provides, the Act prescribes, the Court held, convention guides.

### Revision notes

1. Constitution is the supreme foundational text.
2. Constitutional law is wider than text alone.
3. Judicial doctrine may bind without becoming a printed Article.
4. Ordinary law derives competence from the Constitution.
5. Indian Parliament lacks classic British legal sovereignty.
6. Convention guides the responsible use of discretion.
7. Importance does not automatically create justiciability.
8. Repetition cannot legalise unconstitutional practice.
9. Courts may use structure without judicialising every convention.
10. Over-legalisation may weaken political responsibility.
11. Precise source verbs improve accuracy.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a convention be important without being judicially enforceable?

**Model answer:** It coordinates responsible use of lawful discretion, especially in parliamentary government. Its force is political and institutional; breach may cause criticism, confidence loss or resignation even when no court remedy follows.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every settled practice as either binding law or irrelevant habit.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Distinguish the Constitution, constitutional law, ordinary law and constitutional convention. Why does the distinction matter?

**Model solution:** The **Constitution** is India's supreme foundational text. **Constitutional law** is wider: it includes the text, amendments, binding interpretation and rules governing institutions, rights and remedies. **Ordinary law** is enacted under constitutionally conferred competence and remains reviewable. A **constitutional convention** is a politically accepted practice guiding lawful discretion, such as selecting a Prime Minister able to command Lok Sabha confidence.

Source determines authority and remedy. Parliament is not sovereign in the British sense because legislation remains subordinate to the Constitution. Yet not every difficulty should become litigation: confidence, cabinet solidarity and political responsibility often depend on conventions. Courts may draw on structure when interpreting legal power but should not convert every good practice into an enforceable command. India thus combines supreme law with political responsibility. A precise answer identifies whether a proposition is textual, judicial, statutory or conventional before claiming breach and remedy.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** four definitions — 4; hierarchy/enforceability — 3; two illustrations — 2; sovereignty distinction — 2; convention trade-off — 2; conclusion — 2.

### The suitcase mistake

Calling India “written” does not tell us whether it is federal or rigid. Each label measures a different feature, like checking a suitcase's origin, material, lock and contents.

### Visual: five independent axes

| Axis | Poles | India |
|---|---|---|
| formation | evolved / enacted | enacted, while later operation evolves |
| incorporation | codified / uncodified | codified authoritative text |
| amendment | rigid / flexible | mixed, graded routes |
| territory | federal / unitary | federal distribution with centralising features |
| purpose | procedural / prescriptive | both institutional and transformative |

*Caption: labels accumulate because they answer separate questions.*

An **evolved** constitution develops through statutes, conventions, judgments and practice; the UK is the standard example. An **enacted** constitution is deliberately framed through an authorised process; India is enacted. Enacted does not mean frozen.

A **codified** constitution consolidates core rules. An **uncodified** one disperses them across statutes, cases, conventions and authoritative works.

> **Trap:** the UK is uncodified, not wholly unwritten.

Rigidity concerns amendment. India combines specified simple-majority changes outside Article 368, special-majority amendments and special-majority plus half-State ratification for federal provisions. Basic structure is a separate substantive ceiling.

Federal/unitary classification concerns territorial power. India has divided competence, supremacy and review, alongside Union residuary power, single citizenship, integrated judiciary, emergency centralisation and boundary-altering power. Use **federal with a centralising tilt**.

A procedural constitution structures institutions and limits. A prescriptive constitution also states substantive goals. India combines both through institutions, rights, Preamble, DPSPs and reform commitments.

**Objection:** mixed classifications are evasive.

**Reply:** forced binaries erase differentiated procedures and composite design.

**Residual:** “mixed” must identify the precise feature and consequence.

### UPSC application

Define the axis, give both poles, locate India, qualify. Do not answer an amendment question with a federal label.

### Revision notes

1. Classification axes are independent.
2. India is enacted but evolves through lawful practice.
3. Codified is more precise than merely written.
4. Uncodified does not mean oral.
5. Rigidity concerns amendment procedure.
6. India has differentiated amendment routes.
7. Federal/unitary concerns territorial authority.
8. India combines federal division and strong Union features.
9. Procedural design structures institutions.
10. Prescriptive design states substantive goals.
11. India combines procedure and transformation.
12. “Mixed” requires exact evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can India be enacted, codified, mixed in rigidity, federal with a centralising tilt and prescriptive simultaneously?

**Model answer:** Each label measures a different dimension: origin, incorporation, amendment, territorial distribution and purpose. They therefore accumulate rather than compete.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating evolved/enacted as exact synonyms for uncodified/codified.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why should constitutional classifications be treated as separate axes? Explain with reference to India.

**Model solution:** Evolved/enacted concerns historical formation; codified/uncodified concerns consolidation; rigid/flexible concerns amendment; federal/unitary concerns territorial power; procedural/prescriptive concerns purpose. India is enacted and codified because an authorised Constituent Assembly adopted a consolidated text. It is mixed in rigidity because changes use simple majority, special majority or State ratification. It distributes power federally but has centralising features. It is procedural in structuring institutions and prescriptive through rights, the Preamble and Directive Principles. Composite classification is therefore analytical, not indecisive. The qualification is that “mixed” must name precise provisions and consequences.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** five axes — 3; four correct Indian locations — 4; one trap — 1; qualification — 2.

### Two opposite failures

If constitutional rules change like ordinary policy, they cannot protect settled expectations. If they cannot respond to social change, pressure moves outside lawful channels. Workability lies in managed tension.

### Visual: virtue and excess

| Quality | Benefit | Excess-risk | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| clarity | predictability | false precision | open principles plus reasons |
| comprehensiveness | fewer gaps | complexity and rigidity | ordinary law for operational detail |
| stability | continuity | immobility | entrenched core plus amendment |
| adaptability | peaceful reform | opportunistic alteration | graded procedure and limits |
| suitability | social legitimacy | copied or parochial design | contextual adaptation |
| enforceability | real restraint | over-judicialisation | courts plus political accountability |

*Caption: every virtue can become a vice when maximised without a counterweight.*

Brevity aids access, but a diverse federation may require detail. Clarity limits discretion, yet equality, liberty and reasonableness must govern unforeseen facts. Comprehensiveness fills gaps, but excessive detail constitutionalises administration.

```text
TOO FLEXIBLE -> ordinary majority rewrites competition -> insecurity
BALANCED -> entrenchment + amendment + interpretation -> continuity with reform
TOO RIGID -> peaceful demand lacks a channel -> extra-constitutional pressure
```

India adapts through graded amendment, judicial interpretation and conventions, while basic structure protects identity. Borrowing also requires adaptation: parliamentary government did not import British parliamentary sovereignty.

Enforceability needs remedies, independent institutions, compliance and civic knowledge. Yet courts cannot administer every relationship.

**Objection:** living interpretation lets judges amend informally.

**Reply:** interpretation is unavoidable when general language meets new facts; text, structure, precedent, reasons and competence discipline it.

**Residual:** the interpretation/amendment boundary remains contestable and requires institutional humility.

### UPSC application

Do not list qualities. Present tensions: clarity/openness, detail/adaptability, stability/reform and enforceability/judicialisation.

### Revision notes

1. Constitutional quality is relational.
2. Clarity cannot remove all open texture.
3. Diversity may justify detail.
4. Excess detail can obscure principle.
5. Stability protects expectations and competition.
6. Adaptability channels peaceful change.
7. India uses amendment, interpretation and convention.
8. Suitability requires adapted borrowing.
9. Enforceability needs remedies and compliance.
10. Courts are necessary but insufficient.
11. Living interpretation needs a limiting method.
12. The ideal is continuity through disciplined change.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why are stability and adaptability complements rather than simple opposites?

**Model answer:** Stability protects predictable rules and democratic competition; adaptability allows peaceful response to new conditions. Graded amendment and disciplined interpretation preserve the framework by making reform possible.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating adaptability with easy amendment; several bounded mechanisms can adapt the order.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** A workable constitution must be stable without becoming static and adaptable without becoming politically disposable. Analyse.

**Model solution:** Stability protects settled expectations, institutional continuity and democratic competition. If an ordinary majority could redesign elections or rights at will, the Constitution would cease to be higher law. Yet excessive rigidity can block peaceful reform.

India pursues a middle path. Specified matters change by simple majority outside Article 368; most entrenched provisions require special majority; federal provisions also require State ratification. Judicial interpretation applies open guarantees to new conditions, while conventions adapt parliamentary practice. Basic structure protects identity against destructive amendment.

Clarity must coexist with open texture; comprehensiveness must not constitutionalise every administrative detail; enforceability needs remedies without judicial management of all politics. The risk is that living interpretation becomes unbounded updating or entrenchment becomes judicial veto. Text, structure, precedent, reasons and competence should discipline change. A workable constitution preserves continuity precisely by permitting principled reform.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** stability rationale — 2; adaptability rationale — 2; three Indian mechanisms — 4; two trade-offs — 3; judicial boundary — 2; conclusion — 2.

### The locked-room test

A document may create a legislature while rulers control candidates, silence opposition, ignore rights and block review. There is a constitution in an organisational sense, but no genuine constitutionalism.

### Visual: exact distinctions

| Concept | Decisive question | Necessary content | Not the same as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitution | What is the framework? | institutions, competence, rights, procedures | proof that limits work |
| Constitutionalism | Is power effectively limited? | checks, accountability, review, remedies | possession of a document |
| Limited government | Is every organ bounded? | competence, rights, procedure | small or weak government |
| Constitutional government | Is government under effective higher rules? | democratic, limited, responsible rule | constitutional vocabulary alone |

*Caption: the constitution supplies the framework; constitutionalism tests effective restraint.*

Constitutionalism asks whether power has a source, bounded scope, lawful procedure and review. It is the antithesis of arbitrary rule. Its elements include popular sovereignty, rule of law, responsible government, separation with checks, independent courts, rights, lawful civilian authority and remedies.

**Limited** does not mean **minimal**. A welfare state can regulate and redistribute while remaining bounded. A state with few functions can act arbitrarily.

```text
LIMITED = bounded by competence, rights, procedure and review
MINIMAL = narrow range of state functions
```

Constitutionalism is also not judicial supremacy. Courts enforce limits but are themselves constituted institutions requiring jurisdiction, reasons and restraint.

Indian mechanisms include the Preamble, elections, Article 75(3) responsibility, federal division, rights, Articles 32/226, independent bodies, judicial review, amendment limits, conventions and public debate. No single mechanism suffices.

**Objection:** limits frustrate the people's will.

**Reply:** a mandate authorises government under existing rules; it does not authorise cancellation of equal citizenship, opposition or future choice.

**Residual:** limits must not immunise elite institutions from accountability.

### UPSC application

Prelims defines constitutional government through limitation. Mains should use the formula: necessary document, insufficient without institutions, remedies and restraint.

### Revision notes

1. Constitutionalism is an effective-restraint test.
2. A State may have a constitution without constitutionalism.
3. Constitutional government opposes arbitrary government.
4. Limited means bounded, not minimal.
5. Popular sovereignty operates through constitutional forms.
6. Elections are necessary but insufficient.
7. Rights without remedies may be nominal.
8. Judicial review is not judicial sovereignty.
9. Courts themselves are limited.
10. Mandates do not authorise destruction of competition.
11. Political and legal accountability complement each other.
12. The synthesis is democratic energy plus restraint.

### Concept check

**Question:** Can an expansive welfare state still be a limited government?

**Model answer:** Yes. Limited government concerns legal boundaries, rights, procedures, competence and review, not the number of functions. Broad action remains limited when authorised, non-arbitrary, accountable and reviewable.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating limited government with laissez-faire or a weak state.

### Original Mains question

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** “A constitution may organise power without constitutionalising it.” Explain.

**Model solution:** A document can create offices and prescribe procedures while leaving rulers effectively unrestrained. **Constitutionalism** additionally requires that power remain limited, accountable and governed by law. Its mechanisms include responsible government, rights, separation with checks, independent review, federal limits and remedies. India illustrates this distinction: Parliament and courts derive authority from the Constitution; ordinary law is reviewable, the executive is responsible to Lok Sabha, and Article 368 power is limited by basic structure. Yet written provisions do not guarantee compliance. Weak scrutiny, inaccessible remedies or abandoned conventions can preserve form while enabling arbitrariness. A constitution is therefore necessary but insufficient: constitutionalism converts organisation into genuinely limited government.

**Scoring rubric (10 marks):** distinction — 3; four mechanisms — 3; Indian evidence — 2; form-practice gap — 1; verdict — 1.

### Four brakes, not four names

All these ideas resist arbitrariness, but each attacks a different risk.

### Visual: restraint architecture

```text
RULE OF LAW
lawful authority + equality + non-arbitrariness + review
        -> FUNCTIONAL SEPARATION
        differentiated institutional roles
        -> CHECKS AND BALANCES
        authorised reciprocal controls
        -> ACCOUNTABILITY + REMEDIES
        reasons + consequences + correction
        -> LIMITED GOVERNMENT
```

*Caption: shared purpose does not erase distinct mechanisms.*

**Rule of law** is not rule by any enacted law. Formally enacted law can violate rights, competence or basic structure. Rule of law requires authorised, equal, non-arbitrary and reviewable action.

**Separation of powers** differentiates functions. India rejects watertight isolation: parliamentary government fuses executive leadership with legislative confidence; ordinance powers, delegated legislation and judicial review create overlaps. *Ram Jawaya Kapur* supports functional rather than rigid separation.

**Checks and balances** are authorised controls: legislative confidence, scrutiny, review, removal processes, bicameralism and federal controls. A check is not permission to absorb another organ's whole function.

| Accountability | Mechanism | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| political | questions, confidence, elections | resignation or replacement |
| legal | review, appeal, remedy | invalidation or correction |
| administrative | audit, reasons, vigilance | institutional correction |
| public | transparency, media, civil society | reputational and political pressure |

Remedies make norms effective, but must be lawful and proportionate.

**Objection:** overlap defeats separation.

**Reply:** authorised overlap often is the check. The question is whether each organ's essential role survives.

**Residual:** poor overlap can create delay, blame shifting or overreach.

### UPSC application

For the 2019 question, begin with model choice, then use selected Articles 50, 75, 121/211, 122/212, 123/213, 13, 32, 226 and 368.

### Revision notes

1. Rule of law requires lawful and non-arbitrary power.
2. Rule by statute is not automatically rule of law.
3. Constitutionalism is wider than rule of law.
4. Separation differentiates functions.
5. India rejects watertight separation.
6. Parliamentary responsibility creates intentional fusion.
7. Checks permit controlled interaction.
8. A check does not authorise takeover.
9. Accountability requires reasons and consequences.
10. Remedies make restraint effective.
11. Courts are checks but remain limited.
12. India's formula is functional separation plus checks.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does executive ordinance power not by itself disprove separation of powers?

**Model answer:** India follows functional separation, not watertight isolation. The Constitution permits limited executive law-making while legislative control and judicial review check it. The overlap is designed and bounded.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming any overlap abolishes separation.

### Original Mains question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Distinguish rule of law, separation of powers, checks and balances and limited government. How do they sustain Indian constitutionalism?

**Model solution:** **Rule of law** requires lawful authority, equality, non-arbitrariness and review. **Separation** differentiates legislative, executive and judicial functions. **Checks and balances** permit authorised inter-organ controls, while **limited government** is the condition in which no organ is unlimited.

India combines them rather than pursuing rigid separation. Parliamentary government fuses executive leadership with legislative confidence under Article 75, while Articles 121/211 and 122/212 protect institutional spheres. Ordinance powers show authorised overlap. Judicial review under Articles 13, 32 and 226 controls unconstitutional action; Parliament scrutinises and can remove a ministry; federal distribution limits competence; Article 368 remains subject to basic structure.

Rule of law is not mere rule by statute, judicial review is not judicial supremacy, and a check cannot become takeover. India's constitutionalism therefore rests on functional separation, reciprocal control, reasons, accountability and remedies.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** four distinctions — 4; Indian synthesis — 4; five anchors — 3; two qualifications — 2; conclusion — 2.

### 1. Constituent and constituted power

Original constituent power creates or fundamentally reconstitutes an order. Legislatures, executives and courts are constituted powers. Parliament's Article 368 authority is constituent in function but constituted in source; this explains why amendment can be wide without becoming unlimited and why judicial enforcement of a ceiling does not make courts original sovereigns.

### 2. Legal and political constitutions

A legal constitution relies on enforceable text, rights and review; a political constitution relies on elections, legislatures, conventions and public accountability. India combines them. Legalism risks over-judicialisation; political control risks majoritarian or executive dominance. The solution is dual control, not selection of one pure model.

### 3. Constitutional conventions

Conventions guide legally available choices: selection of a Prime Minister able to command confidence, cabinet solidarity, ministerial resignation, restraint by formal heads and federal courtesy. Importance does not automatically create justiciability. Courts may interpret legal powers through constitutional structure without converting every practice into a judicial command.

### 4. Constitutional morality

Constitutional morality is fidelity to forms, procedures, roles, dissent and equal citizenship. It is stricter than literal compliance but narrower than personal morality. Its safeguards are identifiable text and structure, transparent reasons, institutional role and proportionate remedy.

### 5. Transformative constitutionalism

Transformation uses dignity, liberty, equality, fraternity, rights, Directive Principles, affirmative action and remedies to challenge entrenched hierarchy. It remains a constitutional project: competence, evidence, budget, federal distribution and lawful institutions still matter.

### 6. Living interpretation and original meaning

| Approach | Contribution | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| original public meaning | textual discipline and democratic legitimacy | understating open language or new conditions |
| living application | responsiveness and rights protection | judicial updating without limits |
| structural interpretation | coherence across institutions | abstraction from text |
| precedent | continuity and predictability | hardening error |

An Indian answer should combine text, history, structure, precedent and consequences rather than declare one imported school exclusively controlling.

### 7. Sovereignty distinctions

Popular sovereignty locates legitimacy in the people; parliamentary sovereignty denotes classic legal unlimitedness; constitutional sovereignty bounds every constituted authority. India combines popular authorship with constitutional supremacy. Parliament is democratically central but not British-style sovereign; courts are authoritative but not politically sovereign.

### 8. Constitutional identity and plural citizenship

Identity is the integrated character produced by republicanism, dignity, liberty, equality, fraternity, secularism, social justice, parliamentary responsibility, federalism, review, limited amendment, minority protection and transformation. **Plural citizenship here means equal Indian citizenship lived amid multiple linguistic, religious, regional and cultural identities; it does not mean separate Union and State citizenship.** India constitutionally has single citizenship. Identity is not a closed list or immunity from amendment.

### 9. Constitutional failure and resilience

Failure may occur through emergency excess, rights hollowing, capture, vacancies, deliberate non-performance, convention breakdown or mandate absolutism without formal repeal. Resilience is distributed across legislatures, opposition, federal institutions, constitutional bodies, audit, professional administration, courts, elections, free debate, civil society and civic education.

### 10. Advanced normative framework

Evaluate a practice through authority, purpose, rights, structure, procedure, institutional competence, remedy and long-term precedent. The advanced step is to identify conflicts among factors, justify priority with evidence and choose a remedy that corrects the breach without producing a second institutional overreach.

### Optional synthesis check

A sophisticated answer should show that constitutional endurance depends on interaction: text authorises, rights restrain, politics legitimises, conventions coordinate, morality sustains good faith, interpretation adapts, identity preserves continuity and remedies correct failure.

## Original 15-mark question

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Constitutional classifications illuminate design only when their axes and limits are explicit. Analyse with reference to India and the United Kingdom.

**Model answer:** Constitutional labels answer distinct questions. Codified/uncodified concerns consolidation; evolved/enacted historical formation; rigid/flexible amendment; federal/unitary territorial authority. The United Kingdom is better called **uncodified**, not wholly unwritten: statutes, judgments, conventions and authoritative works form its constitution. Its order is evolutionary and legally flexible because Westminster Parliament can generally alter constitutional rules through ordinary legislation.

India is enacted and codified, but neither wholly rigid nor flexible. Specified matters change by simple majority, most entrenched provisions by special majority, and federal provisions also need State ratification. Basic structure adds a substantive ceiling. India is federally organised with centralising features, an issue not answered by calling it written.

The comparison's limit is context. India's parliamentary government borrows Westminster mechanisms but rejects classic parliamentary sovereignty through constitutional supremacy and review. Labels aid analysis only when the measured feature, legal consequence and qualification are stated.

**Scoring rubric (15 marks):** four axes — 4; UK and uncodified trap — 3; Indian amendment synthesis — 3; sovereignty consequence — 2; context qualification — 2; conclusion — 1.

## Original 20-mark question

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Constitutional success depends as much on self-restraint and political practice as on enforceable law. Critically examine.

**Model answer:** Enforceable law supplies the constitutional floor. Competence rules, Fundamental Rights, federal distribution and judicial review prevent ordinary majorities acting outside the supreme framework. Yet law cannot specify every responsible use of discretion. Conventions guide confidence, cabinet solidarity, ministerial responsibility and restraint by formal heads. Constitutional morality asks office-holders to respect forms, roles, dissent, equal citizenship and democratic rules even where literal text leaves room for opportunism.

The differing opinions in *Dr. Jaya Thakur* (2026) illustrate disciplined disagreement. One judge treated governing principles as settled; the other found substantial questions requiring larger-Bench consideration. The papers went to the Chief Justice, without a final merits ruling. One opinion invoked Ambedkar's account of conventions and self-restraint in electoral institutional design.

Political practice also matters: legislative scrutiny, opposition, federal bargaining, free elections and public reasons can correct conduct before litigation. However, convention cannot override text, morality cannot become personal preference, and review cannot become general governance. Each needs identifiable constitutional anchors, transparent reasons, accountability and proportionate remedies. Constitutional success therefore rests on interaction: law restrains, conventions coordinate, morality sustains good faith, democratic practice legitimises and remedies correct breakdown.

**Scoring rubric (20 marks):** law — 3; convention — 3; morality — 3; current anchor/status — 2; three risks — 3; safeguards — 3; integrated verdict — 3.

# REMEDIATION

1. **Constitution equals constitutionalism:** test effective rights, accountability, review and remedies.
2. **Limited means small:** separate range of functions from legal restraint.
3. **Rule of law means enacted law:** add authority, equality, non-arbitrariness and review.
4. **India has no separation:** use functional separation with checks.
5. **UK is wholly unwritten:** use uncodified.
6. **Article 368 is unlimited original power:** it is constitutionally conferred and basic-structure limited.
7. **Constitutional morality means progressive preference:** require text, structure, role and reasons.
8. **Living Constitution means judicial updating at will:** require text, history, structure, precedent and competence.
9. **Every convention is enforceable:** begin with political sanction.
10. **Formal legality settles validity:** continue through purpose, rights, structure, procedure and remedy.

## Core proposition

- Constitution constitutes, distributes, restrains and orients power.
- Constitutionalism tests effective limitation.
- Constitutional government means democratic government under higher rules.

## Legal layers

- Constitution: supreme foundational text.
- Constitutional law: text plus binding public-law doctrine and rules.
- Ordinary law: subordinate exercise of conferred competence.
- Convention: politically enforced guidance for lawful discretion.
- Source verbs: Constitution provides; Act prescribes; Court held; convention guides.

- clarity ↔ open texture;
- comprehensiveness ↔ excessive detail;
- stability ↔ adaptability;
- enforceability ↔ over-judicialisation;
- borrowing ↔ contextual adaptation.

## Exact restraint vocabulary

- Rule of law: authorised, equal, non-arbitrary and reviewable power.
- Limited government: every organ bounded; not necessarily minimal government.
- Separation: functional differentiation.
- Checks: authorised reciprocal control.
- Accountability: reasons plus consequences.
- Remedy: lawful correction making norms effective.
- Constitutionalism: the wider system joining these restraints.

## Indian sovereignty formula

```text
popular authorship + constitutional supremacy
+ parliamentary democracy + judicial review without judicial sovereignty
= Indian constitutional democracy
```

## Optional advanced enrichment

### Amendment and identity

- Original constituent power creates/replaces the order.
- Article 368 supplies constituted amending power.
- *Kesavananda*: amendment without destruction.
- *Minerva Mills*: limited amending power and review are basic features.
- Identity integrates republicanism, dignity, equality, fraternity, secularism, federalism, responsibility, review and transformation.
- Plural citizenship means equal single Indian citizenship across plural identities and minority protections; it does not mean dual Union-State citizenship.
- Basic structure is illustrative, not a closed printed list.

## Morality and adaptation

- Constitutional morality = fidelity to forms, roles, procedures and equal citizenship.
- Not social morality; not personal judicial morality.
- Conventions guide discretion through political sanction.
- Living interpretation uses text, history, structure, precedent and consequences.
- Transformative constitutionalism attacks hierarchy through lawful institutions.
- *Dr. Jaya Thakur* (23 September 2026) contains differing opinions on referral; it is a current illustration, not a final merits ruling.

## Failure and resilience

- Failure: emergency excess, rights hollowing, capture, non-performance, convention breakdown, mandate absolutism.
- Resilience: Parliament, opposition, federal voice, bodies, audit, civil services, courts, elections, media, civil society, civic education.
- Stress test: authority → purpose → rights → structure → procedure → competence → remedy → precedent.
