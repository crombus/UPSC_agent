# Parliament - Live Session Edition

Parliament is where representation, law-making, taxation and executive accountability meet. The central puzzle is not whether Parliament is powerful in text, but how two differently designed Houses convert public consent into law while remaining bounded by the Constitution.

The President is not a member of either House, yet Parliament is constitutionally incomplete without the President. This is because a bill ordinarily becomes an Act only after assent, and the President summons and prorogues the Houses.

### 1. Why not a single elected House?

A single chamber may reflect the latest popular majority quickly. That is also its danger. A second chamber creates delay for reconsideration, represents States through a different electoral route and supplies continuity when the Lok Sabha is dissolved.

The Rajya Sabha is therefore not a duplicate Lok Sabha. Its design performs three different tasks:

1. **revision** - a second examination of ordinary legislation;
2. **federal participation** - representation of States in Union law-making;
3. **continuity** - a permanent House remains available during Lok Sabha dissolution.

The Lok Sabha has the stronger democratic claim on finance and government survival because it is directly elected. The Rajya Sabha has special federal powers under Articles 249 and 312.

| Field | Relationship between Houses |
|---|---|
| Ordinary legislation | broadly equal, subject to joint-sitting mechanism |
| Constitution amendment | equal; each must pass separately |
| President/Vice-President related electoral functions | constitutionally assigned roles; neither is a general subordinate |
| Money Bill and demands for grants | Lok Sabha dominant |
| Confidence in Council of Ministers | Lok Sabha alone |
| State List legislation in national interest, new All-India Service | Rajya Sabha has initiating gatekeeping powers |

### 2. The two exclusive Rajya Sabha gates

Under **Article 249**, a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting may authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List matter in the national interest. The resolution lasts one year and may be renewed.

Under **Article 312**, a similar special-majority resolution can authorise Parliament to create one or more All-India Services.

These powers do not make the Rajya Sabha generally superior. They show why a federal chamber is necessary precisely when the Union seeks to enter a normally State-controlled field.

### 3. Example - a safety lock, not a permanent brake

Think of bicameralism as a door with two differently placed locks. The Lok Sabha lock tests current popular approval; the Rajya Sabha lock tests continuity and federal acceptability. The analogy stops there: a Money Bill deliberately uses a different route, and a joint sitting can resolve some ordinary-bill deadlocks.

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the Rajya Sabha represents States unequally by population and may be overcome numerically in a joint sitting, it is merely a delaying chamber.

**Reply:** Equal State representation is not the only form of federal representation. The Rajya Sabha's permanence, equal vote on ordinary and amendment Bills, and exclusive Articles 249 and 312 powers make it constitutionally consequential. The criticism remains partly valid on money, confidence and joint-sitting arithmetic.

### UPSC application

- Do not say that the President is a member of Parliament; say the President is an **integral component** under Article 79.
- A balanced answer on Rajya Sabha should use the formula: **secondary on finance and confidence, co-equal on ordinary law and amendment, distinctive on federal gates**.
- **2020 GS-II Q14** can be answered by separating areas of subordination from areas of equality and exclusivity.

### Revision notes

1. Article 79 creates Parliament as President plus two Houses.
2. The President is integral but not a member of either House.
3. Bicameralism combines popular representation, federal voice, revision and continuity.
4. Lok Sabha controls confidence and dominates financial business.
5. Rajya Sabha is permanent and cannot be dissolved.
6. Ordinary legislation generally requires both Houses.
7. Constitutional amendment Bills require separate passage by both Houses; there is no joint sitting.
8. Article 249 is the Rajya Sabha's national-interest State List gate.
9. Article 312 is its All-India Services gate.
10. India's Rajya Sabha is population-weighted, not an equal-State chamber like the US Senate.
11. "Supporting chamber" is accurate only with qualifications.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the Rajya Sabha constitutionally important even though the Lok Sabha dominates money and confidence?

**Model answer:** It supplies continuity, revises ordinary legislation, represents States and has equal power over ordinary and constitutional amendment Bills. Articles 249 and 312 also give it exclusive federal gatekeeping roles. Its inferiority is confined mainly to finance, confidence and joint-sitting arithmetic.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer that unequal State representation or weaker financial power makes the Rajya Sabha constitutionally dispensable.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** "The Rajya Sabha is a supporting chamber, not a subordinate chamber." Examine.

**Model response:** The Rajya Sabha revises legislation, preserves continuity and voices State interests. It is weaker where democratic responsibility requires Lok Sabha primacy: a Money Bill originates only in the Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha only recommends within fourteen days, grants are voted only by Lok Sabha, and the Council of Ministers survives on Lok Sabha confidence.

Yet subordination is not general. Both Houses possess equal power over ordinary Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills, which must pass separately. The Rajya Sabha also elects and removes its own Deputy Chairman and participates in constitutional elections. More importantly, Articles 249 and 312 make it the initiating federal gate for Union legislation on State List matters in the national interest and for creation of All-India Services.

Thus, the Rajya Sabha is secondary on finance and confidence but co-equal or distinctive on law and federation. Its constitutional role is supportive without being subordinate.

The two Houses represent democratic consent through different mechanisms. The Lok Sabha asks, "Who won each territorial constituency?" The Rajya Sabha asks, "How should State legislative strength be translated proportionally into a continuing Union chamber?"

### 1. Composition and election

| Feature | Rajya Sabha | Lok Sabha |
|---|---|---|
| Constitutional maximum | 250: up to 238 elected plus 12 nominated | 550: up to 530 States plus 20 Union Territories |
| Stable present institutional figure | 245 sanctioned; working strength varies | 543 elected seats; working strength varies |
| Election | indirect, proportional representation by single transferable vote; Union Territory representation is provided only as law and the Fourth Schedule prescribe | direct, first-past-the-post |
| Voting age in popular election | mediated through elected MLAs | 18 years after the 61st Amendment |
| Nominated membership | 12 | Anglo-Indian nomination ceased under the 104th Amendment |
| Duration | permanent; member term six years, roughly one-third retire every second year | normally five years unless sooner dissolved |

Rajya Sabha elections use an **open ballot** under the statutory 2003 change. The State-domicile requirement for a candidate was also removed by statute, upheld in *Kuldip Nayar v Union of India* (2006). Neither change was a constitutional amendment.

Article 80 also permits representation of Union Territories in the Rajya Sabha in the manner Parliament prescribes by law. A Union Territory does not automatically receive equal or any fixed representation merely because it is a Union Territory; allocation follows the Fourth Schedule and the statutory electoral arrangement. Where seats are provided, representatives are chosen indirectly through the electoral body specified by law.

### 2. Why tenure differs

The Lok Sabha's complete renewal permits voters to replace a government. The Rajya Sabha's staggered retirement prevents the entire parliamentary institution from disappearing at once. During a national emergency, Parliament may extend the Lok Sabha's term by law for one year at a time, but not beyond six months after the emergency ends.

### 3. Representation and the delimitation bridge

Article 81 links Lok Sabha seat allocation broadly to population. **Article 82** requires readjustment after each census by the authority and in the manner Parliament determines by law. **Article 329(a)** bars courts from questioning the validity of a law relating to delimitation or allotment of seats, insulating the completed statutory exercise from ordinary judicial challenge.

India has constituted four nationwide Delimitation Commissions under the **Delimitation Commission Acts of 1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002**. Their orders have the force of law. The **42nd Amendment (1976)** froze inter-State allocation on the 1971 census; the **84th Amendment (2001)** continued the freeze until the relevant figures of the first census taken after 2026 are published; the **87th Amendment (2003)** enabled constituency-boundary readjustment using the 2001 census without changing State totals.

The constitutional concern is Parliament's representative design: demographic equality versus the federal bargain that States should not be penalised for population control.

### Deliberate current linkage - Census 2027 and the representation trigger

Official PIB material states that Census 2027 has a general reference date of **00:00 hours on 1 March 2027** and an earlier **1 October 2026** reference date for specified snow-bound areas. Population enumeration is scheduled for February 2027 generally, with the special areas enumerated earlier. This is the first census taken after 2026 for the freeze formula.

The **106th Amendment Act, 2023**, which inserted Articles 330A, 332A and 334A, was brought into force on **16 April 2026** by Gazette notification **S.O. 1922(E)**. That commencement does **not** operationalise women's reservation. Article 334A still requires publication of the relevant figures of the first post-commencement census and a delimitation exercise for that purpose. Enumeration date, publication date, delimitation date and first reserved election are four different registers.

The reservation covers **one-third of the total seats** filled by direct election in the **Lok Sabha**, every **State Legislative Assembly**, and the **Legislative Assembly of the National Capital Territory of Delhi**. It includes one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in those legislatures.

### 4. Objection and balanced reply

**Objection:** Population-proportional reallocation may penalise States that reduced fertility and transfer national power toward more populous States.

**Reply:** Permanent malapportionment also weakens "one person, one vote" equality. A legitimate settlement must reconcile democratic equality with federal fairness; the Constitution sets the trigger but not the final political bargain. No State-wise post-freeze seat projection is law.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the notification commencing the 106th Amendment not itself reserve Lok Sabha seats for women?

**Model answer:** Article 334A makes operation conditional on a later sequence: relevant figures of the first census after commencement must be published and delimitation must then be undertaken for this purpose. Commencement activates the legal framework; it does not complete those conditions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat enactment, commencement, census enumeration, publication, delimitation and operational reservation as interchangeable dates.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Analyse the democratic and federal tensions embedded in post-2026 delimitation.

**Model response:** Delimitation reconnects representation with population. Article 81 embodies population-based Lok Sabha allocation, while Article 82 supplies post-census readjustment machinery. Persistent differences in constituency size dilute citizens' votes.

The federal difficulty arises from the freeze. The 42nd Amendment froze State-wise allocation on the 1971 Census; the 84th continued it until publication of figures from the first census after 2026. This protected States that reduced population growth from losing relative political weight. Ending it may shift seats, confidence arithmetic and influence in presidential elections toward more populous States. Because Rajya Sabha representation is also population-weighted, smaller States lack a US-Senate-style counterweight.

Indefinite freezing, however, entrenches malapportionment. Article 329(a) insulates delimitation law from ordinary judicial challenge, making transparent statutory design crucial. Four nationwide commissions have operated under the 1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002 Acts.

Census 2027 supplies a trigger, but enumeration is not publication and no State-wise allocation is law. The April 2026 package proposed a larger House and use of the 2011 Census, but the official 17 April Government Bills record labels the combined proceeding "not passed". It does not prove a separate mode of disposal or exact division figures for each Bill. A settlement must reconcile equal citizenship, federal fairness and demographic performance.

### 1. Qualifications

Article 84 and the Representation of the People Act supply the basic entry rules. A member must be an Indian citizen, make the prescribed oath or affirmation, satisfy the age threshold - **30 for Rajya Sabha, 25 for Lok Sabha** - and meet statutory qualifications.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 adds material operational qualifications. A candidate for either House must be registered as an elector for a parliamentary constituency. A candidate for a seat reserved for Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes must belong to the relevant protected category as the Act requires; an SC/ST candidate may also contest an unreserved seat. The 2003 amendment removed the requirement that a Rajya Sabha candidate be an elector in the particular State represented.

The Constitution sets the framework; election law supplies operational detail. That division matters because not every candidature rule is written in Article 84.

### 2. Constitutional disqualifications

Article 102 includes:

- holding an office of profit under Union or State government, unless Parliament exempts it;
- unsound mind declared by a competent court;
- undischarged insolvency;
- loss or non-possession of Indian citizenship or voluntary allegiance to a foreign State;
- disqualification under a law made by Parliament.

The **Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act, 1959** exempts specified offices. Whether a sitting member incurs an Article 102(1) disqualification is decided by the President after obtaining the Election Commission's opinion under Article 103.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 further disqualifies, subject to its precise periods and conditions, persons found guilty of specified election offences or corrupt practices; convicted of listed offences; failing to lodge election-expense accounts; having a disqualifying interest in government contracts, works or services; holding specified management or profit positions in a government-controlled corporation; or dismissed from government service for corruption or disloyalty. Preventive detention by itself is not a conviction-based disqualification. These grounds must be applied through the statute, not converted into a vague moral test.

The Tenth Schedule adds defection disqualification through a distinct presiding-officer decision. Its immediate parliamentary consequence is that adjudication affects House composition and voting.

### 3. Vacancy rules

| Event | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Membership of both Houses | one seat must be vacated under statutory rules |
| Parliament and State legislature membership | under Article 101(2), the Parliament seat becomes vacant if the State-legislature seat is not resigned within the prescribed period |
| Resignation | accepted by presiding officer only if voluntary and genuine |
| Absence for 60 days without permission | House may declare the seat vacant; specified periods are excluded in computation |
| Disqualification | vacancy follows constitutional/statutory decision |
| Death | vacancy |

The presiding officer's inquiry into whether a resignation is voluntary and genuine prevents coerced resignations from automatically changing House arithmetic.

### 4. Election petitions: how a disputed mandate is tested

Article 329(b) channels a challenge to a completed parliamentary election into an **election petition**, not an ordinary suit or an interruption of the ongoing election process. Under sections 80, 80A and 81 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, a candidate or elector presents the petition to the jurisdictional High Court, ordinarily within **45 days** from the returned candidate's election.

Section 83 requires a concise statement of the **material facts** relied on, full particulars of any alleged corrupt practice - including the names of the parties said to have committed it and the date and place - signature and verification in the prescribed manner, and the prescribed affidavit where corrupt practice is alleged. Pleading evidence is unnecessary, but omitting a material fact can leave no complete cause of action.

Section 86 creates a threshold scrutiny. The High Court must dismiss a petition that does not comply with section 81, section 82's necessary-party rule or section 117's security-for-costs requirement. Once the petition survives that gate, the High Court tries it, as nearly as practicable, under the Code of Civil Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act through section 87. Trial should continue from day to day as far as practicable, and the Act directs an endeavour to conclude it within six months.

Section 100 allows the High Court to declare the election void where:

- the returned candidate was not qualified or was disqualified on the election date;
- the returned candidate, election agent, or another person with their consent committed a corrupt practice;
- a nomination was improperly rejected; or
- the result was materially affected by improper acceptance of a nomination, specified corrupt practice without such consent, improper reception/refusal/rejection of votes or receipt of a void vote, or non-compliance with the Constitution, the Act, rules or orders.

At conclusion, section 98 authorises dismissal, a declaration that the returned candidate's election is void, or that declaration together with a section 101 declaration that the petitioner or another candidate was duly elected. Section 101 requires either a majority of valid votes or proof that, but for corrupt-practice votes obtained by the returned candidate, the other candidate would have obtained the majority.

Where corrupt practice is proved, section 99 requires the High Court to record its nature and name the persons proved guilty. A person who is not already a party must first receive notice and an opportunity to be heard and to cross-examine relevant witnesses. Section 116A permits an appeal to the Supreme Court on law and fact, ordinarily within **30 days**. Filing does not itself stay the High Court's order; section 116B separately empowers the High Court or Supreme Court to grant a stay on conditions.

*N.P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer* explains why electoral challenge waits for the statutory petition after the election process; *Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner* reinforces the Article 329(b) channel; *Jyoti Basu v Debi Ghosal* stresses that election rights and remedies are statutory. *F.A. Sapa v Singora* distinguishes material facts from fuller particulars and explains procedural fairness before a non-party is named under section 99.

### 5. Example and limit

Suppose an MP accepts a government-linked position. The label "office" is insufficient. The inquiry considers appointment, removal, remuneration and governmental control, and Parliament may have exempted the office. The example teaches why "office of profit" is a constitutional conflict-of-interest test, not a ban on every public association.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Giving the President the formal decision while requiring the Election Commission's opinion obscures accountability.

**Reply:** The design separates political form from expert electoral assessment. Yet delay can still distort representation, so timely decision remains essential.

### UPSC application

- **2019 Prelims Q49** tests the Parliament Disqualification Act and office of profit.
- **2022 GS-II Q11** requires sections 81/83 filing and contents, section 86 threshold and trial, sections 98/99 orders, section 100 void grounds, section 101 relief, section 116A appeal and case-law control.
- Do not merge pre-election disqualification, post-election membership disputes and Tenth Schedule adjudication.
- A resignation is not automatically effective merely on submission; voluntariness and genuineness matter.

### Revision notes

1. Article 84 governs qualifications for Parliament.
2. Minimum age is 30 for Rajya Sabha and 25 for Lok Sabha.
3. RPA 1951 requires registration as an elector for a parliamentary constituency.
4. Reserved-seat candidature carries the Act's SC/ST qualification; protected-category candidates may contest general seats.
5. Article 102 lists constitutional disqualifications.
6. RPA disqualifications include specified corrupt practices, convictions, election-expense default, government-contract interests and specified government-corporation or service misconduct.
7. Office of profit is subject to parliamentary exemptions.
8. The 1959 Act exempts specified offices; it does not abolish the doctrine.
9. Article 103 gives the President the decision after the Election Commission's opinion.
10. Tenth Schedule adjudication is constitutionally distinct.
11. Under Article 101(2), failure to resign the State-legislature seat in time vacates the Parliament seat.
12. A resignation must be voluntary and genuine.
13. Sixty-day absence may permit the House to declare a vacancy.
14. Election-petition architecture runs through filing under section 81, contents under section 83, threshold dismissal and trial under sections 86-87, and final orders under sections 98-99.
15. Section 100 void grounds, section 101 declaration relief and section 116A Supreme Court appeal on law and fact must be kept distinct.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is every government-associated position not automatically an office of profit disqualification?

**Model answer:** The constitutional inquiry turns on the legal characteristics of the office, including governmental control and benefit, and Parliament may exempt specified offices by law. Association with government alone is not the complete test.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the phrase "office of profit" as a purely factual salary test or confuse Article 103 with Tenth Schedule adjudication.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Explain how the Constitution balances legislative independence with representative continuity through its membership-disqualification framework.

**Model response:** Parliament's membership framework protects integrity without making an electoral mandate casually terminable. Article 84 prescribes citizenship, oath, age and statutory qualifications. The RPA 1951 requires electoral registration, regulates reserved-seat candidature and adds specified disqualifications for corrupt practices, convictions, election-expense default and conflicts involving government contracts, corporations or service misconduct. Article 102 separately covers an unexempted office of profit, declared unsoundness of mind, undischarged insolvency, citizenship or allegiance, and parliamentary-law grounds.

Safeguards temper exclusion. Parliament may exempt offices under the 1959 Act. Under Article 103, the President decides a sitting member's disqualification after obtaining the Election Commission's opinion. Resignation operates only when found voluntary and genuine; prolonged unauthorised absence may permit vacancy. Article 101(2) makes the Parliament seat vacant if a simultaneous State legislator fails to resign the State seat in time. Tenth Schedule adjudication is distinct and judicially reviewable.

### 1. Speaker and Deputy Speaker

Articles 93-97 establish the Lok Sabha offices. The House chooses both. The Speaker vacates office by ceasing to be a member, resigning to the Deputy Speaker or being removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the House - an **effective majority** - after fourteen days' notice.

The Deputy Speaker may likewise resign to the Speaker or be removed by a Lok Sabha resolution passed by a majority of all the then members after fourteen days' notice. The Speaker continues after dissolution until immediately before the first sitting of the new Lok Sabha. This prevents a constitutional vacuum.

Article 96 prevents either the Speaker or Deputy Speaker from presiding while a resolution for that officer's removal is under consideration. The Speaker has an express right to speak, take part and vote in the first instance on the Speaker-removal resolution, but has no casting vote. The Deputy Speaker, being a member, may participate and vote when not presiding under the ordinary voting rule.

### 2. Chairman and Deputy Chairman

The Vice-President is ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and is not a member of that House. The Chairman is removed through removal of the Vice-President under Article 67(b): a Rajya Sabha resolution passed by a majority of all the then members, agreed to by the Lok Sabha, after at least fourteen days' notice.

The Rajya Sabha elects its Deputy Chairman from among its members. Under Article 90, the Deputy Chairman may resign to the Chairman or be removed by a Rajya Sabha resolution passed by a majority of all the then members after fourteen days' notice.

Article 92 prevents the Chairman from presiding while the Vice-President's removal resolution is considered; the Chairman may speak and take part but cannot vote at all. It also prevents the Deputy Chairman from presiding over a resolution for the Deputy Chairman's removal; as a Rajya Sabha member, the Deputy Chairman may participate and vote under the ordinary rule when not presiding.

Before a newly elected Lok Sabha chooses its Speaker, the President appoints a **Speaker pro tem**, ordinarily a senior member, to administer oaths and conduct the Speaker's election. A panel of chairpersons may preside during the Speaker's and Deputy Speaker's absence, but a panel member cannot perform the full constitutional succession role when both offices are vacant.

### 3. Powers of the Speaker

- maintains order and interprets rules within the House;
- decides admissibility of motions and questions;
- certifies a Money Bill under Article 110;
- presides over a joint sitting under Article 108;
- uses a casting vote when votes are equally divided;
- decides Tenth Schedule petitions, subject to judicial review;
- protects committee functioning and nominates members/chairs where rules so provide.

The text calling Money Bill certification "final" does not create absolute immunity from constitutional judicial review. *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2018) upheld the Aadhaar certification by 4:1, while *Rojer Mathew* (2019) doubted the broader reasoning and referred the issue to a larger bench. Official Supreme Court portals revealed no final resolution as checked on 30 September 2026.

### 4. Participation, secretariats and voting

Article 88 permits ministers and the Attorney-General to speak and take part in either House and its committees, but they may vote only if they are members of that House. Each House has a separate secretarial staff under Article 98, supporting institutional autonomy from the executive.

Article 100 generally uses a majority of members present and voting. The presiding officer normally does not vote initially but exercises a casting vote in a tie. "Present and voting" excludes abstentions; "total membership" in an effective or special majority serves a different function.

### 5. Impartiality problem

India did not adopt the full British convention of "once a Speaker, always a Speaker." The Indian Speaker ordinarily retains party membership and later contests elections. The office nevertheless makes decisions affecting party fortunes.

**Objection:** Personal expectations of neutrality cannot overcome partisan incentives in Money Bill certification and defection cases.

**Reply:** Removal protection, charged expenditure, rule-based procedure and judicial review provide insulation. The residual problem is real because review is often delayed and cannot replace neutral first-instance decision-making.

### UPSC application

- **2020 GS-II Q5** tests objectivity of the Speaker.
- **2026 GS-II Q3** tests vulnerability under anti-defection and institutional reform.
- Objective demands include Deputy Speaker powers, conduct during removal proceedings and continuity through dissolution.

### Revision notes

1. Lok Sabha elects the Speaker and Deputy Speaker.
2. Rajya Sabha Chairman is the Vice-President ex officio.
3. Rajya Sabha elects the Deputy Chairman from among its members.
4. Speaker and Deputy Speaker removal each require an effective majority and fourteen days' notice.
5. Speaker continues through dissolution until the new House's first sitting.
6. Article 96 bars the officer whose removal is being considered from presiding.
7. The Speaker may vote initially on the Speaker-removal resolution but has no casting vote.
8. Vice-President removal begins in Rajya Sabha by effective majority, needs Lok Sabha agreement and fourteen days' notice.
9. Deputy Chairman removal requires a Rajya Sabha effective majority and fourteen days' notice.
10. Under Article 92, the Chairman may participate in the Vice-President-removal debate but cannot vote.
11. Article 100 ordinarily uses present-and-voting majority.
12. A casting vote breaks a tie; it is not a routine vote.
13. Money Bill certification is textually final but not absolutely immune from review.
14. A Speaker pro tem conducts oaths and the Speaker's election.
15. Articles 88 and 98 govern participation and separate secretariats.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the Speaker's continuation after Lok Sabha dissolution not contradict the rule that the Speaker must be a member?

**Model answer:** Article 94 creates a specific continuity rule: the Speaker does not vacate on dissolution and remains until immediately before the first sitting of the new House. It is a constitutional bridge designed to avoid an institutional vacuum.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say the Speaker remains for the whole next Lok Sabha or that the Speaker can preside over the motion for the Speaker's own removal.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Examine the structural sources of partisan vulnerability in the office of the Lok Sabha Speaker and suggest reforms.

**Model response:** The Speaker is expected to be an impartial guardian but is produced by partisan competition. Unlike the British convention of "once a Speaker, always a Speaker," an Indian Speaker generally retains party membership and may seek re-election on a party ticket.

This vulnerability matters because the office controls procedure, admissibility and discipline; certifies Money Bills under Article 110; presides over joint sittings; and decides Tenth Schedule disqualification petitions. Certification can reduce Rajya Sabha scrutiny, while delay in defection decisions can alter a floor test. The official vacancy of the Deputy Speaker since May 2019 also shows that constitutional offices may depend on majority convenience.

Existing safeguards include removal only by effective majority after notice, salary charged on the Consolidated Fund, procedural autonomy and judicial review. *Kihoto Hollohan* treats the Speaker as a reviewable tribunal, but review after delay is an incomplete cure.

Reforms may require resignation from party office, convention-based unopposed re-election, time-bound defection decisions and transfer of Tenth Schedule adjudication to an independent tribunal. Money Bill reasons should be recorded and review remain narrow but real.

Neutrality cannot rest only on personal virtue. Institutional insulation must align the Speaker's incentives with the House rather than the governing party.

### Visual first: four different ways parliamentary time changes

| Event | Actor | Ends | Pending Bills |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adjournment | presiding officer | a sitting for a stated time | survive |
| Adjournment sine die | presiding officer | a sitting without naming next date | survive |

### 1. Summoning and session frequency

Article 85 requires the President to summon each House so that no more than six months intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. It therefore produces at least two sessions in a year, but **does not prescribe a minimum number of sitting days**.

Budget, Monsoon and Winter are conventional labels. The Constitution does not fix those names, dates or durations.

### 2. Quorum and voting capacity

Article 100(3) fixes quorum at one-tenth of total membership: conventionally stated as **55 for a 543-seat Lok Sabha and 25 for a 245-seat Rajya Sabha**. If quorum is absent, the presiding officer must adjourn or suspend the meeting.

### 3. Language

Article 120 permits business in Hindi or English, with the presiding officer able to permit a member unable adequately to express themselves in either to address the House in their mother tongue. This is a procedural inclusion rule, not a declaration of India's national language.

A **lame-duck session** is the final session of an outgoing Lok Sabha after a new House has been elected but before the old House's term formally ends. Members who were not re-elected may still participate during that interval.

### 4. Dissolution and Bill lapse

```text
LOK SABHA DISSOLVES
  |
  +--> Bill pending in Lok Sabha -----------------> lapses
  +--> Bill passed by LS, pending in RS ----------> lapses
  +--> Bill pending in RS, not passed by LS ------> survives
  +--> Bill passed by both, awaiting assent ------> survives
  +--> Bill returned by President ----------------> survives
  +--> joint sitting already notified ------------> survives
```

Prorogation does not lapse Bills. Dissolution affects only the Lok Sabha because the Rajya Sabha is permanent.

### 5. Example and its limit

A school timetable analogy helps: adjournment pauses one class; prorogation closes the term; dissolution closes the institution's elected life and requires a new intake. The analogy is limited because parliamentary Bills have constitution-specific survival rules rather than all work simply disappearing.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** The six-month ceiling is too weak to ensure meaningful deliberation; a government can meet briefly and remain formally compliant.

**Reply:** The criticism is valid as a design gap. Political accountability, House rules and possible statutory or rules-based minimum sitting norms must supply what Article 85 does not. The constitutional minimum is frequency, not deliberative adequacy.

### UPSC application

- **2020 Prelims Q20** tests summoning and minimum sittings.
- **2024 Q94** tests Bill lapse; **2024 Q95** tests prorogation and dissolution.
- Avoid saying pending notices and pending Bills have identical consequences on prorogation.

### Revision notes

1. Article 85 governs summoning, prorogation and dissolution.
2. The six-month rule measures last sitting to first sitting.
3. There is no constitutional minimum number of sitting days.
4. Budget, Monsoon and Winter are conventions.
5. Adjournment is by the presiding officer and pauses a sitting.
6. Prorogation is by the President and ends a session.
7. Dissolution ends only the Lok Sabha's life.
8. Quorum is one-tenth under Article 100(3).
9. Article 120 governs language in Parliament.
10. A Bill pending in Rajya Sabha and not passed by Lok Sabha survives dissolution.
11. A Bill passed by Lok Sabha but pending in Rajya Sabha lapses.
12. A notified joint sitting survives dissolution.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a Bill pending in the Rajya Sabha survive Lok Sabha dissolution while one passed by Lok Sabha and pending in Rajya Sabha lapses?

**Model answer:** The first has not yet acquired the dissolved House's approval and remains alive in the continuing chamber. The second depends on the mandate of the dissolved Lok Sabha that passed it, so a newly elected House must reconsider it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use the blanket statement that all pending Bills lapse when the Lok Sabha dissolves.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Distinguish adjournment, prorogation and dissolution, and explain their significance for parliamentary accountability.

**Model response:** Adjournment suspends a sitting and is ordered by the presiding officer; adjournment sine die ends it without naming the next date. Prorogation, ordered by the President under Article 85, ends a session. Bills survive it, although pending notices generally lapse. Dissolution ends the Lok Sabha's life; the Rajya Sabha is never dissolved.

These distinctions determine whether scrutiny pauses or an electoral mandate ends. Dissolution makes Lok-Sabha-pending Bills, and Bills passed by it but pending in Rajya Sabha, lapse. Rajya-Sabha-pending Bills, Bills awaiting assent, returned Bills and Bills with a notified joint sitting survive.

Article 85's six-month ceiling ensures periodic meetings but no minimum sitting days. Consequently, formal compliance may coexist with weak scrutiny. Parliamentary accountability depends not merely on convening sessions but on adequate time for questions, debate, grants and committee reports.

### 1. Stages in each House

First Reading introduces the Bill. Second Reading does the substantive work: general discussion, possible committee scrutiny and clause-by-clause consideration. Third Reading considers acceptance or rejection of the final text.

An ordinary Bill may originate in either House and ordinarily requires agreement on identical text.

A **government Bill** is introduced by a minister; a **private member's Bill** by any other member. Both are legislative proposals, but government control of time and majority support makes private-member enactment rare. The distinction concerns sponsorship, not constitutional validity.

### 2. Deadlock

Article 108 permits a joint sitting if the second House rejects the Bill, the Houses finally disagree on amendments, or more than six months elapse without passage. The President summons it; the Lok Sabha Speaker normally presides.

Only three joint sittings have occurred: the Dowry Prohibition Bill (1961), Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill (1978) and Prevention of Terrorism Bill (2002).

No joint sitting exists for a Money Bill or Constitution Amendment Bill.

### 3. Why joint sitting favours Lok Sabha

Members vote together, so the Lok Sabha's larger numbers usually dominate. Yet the Rajya Sabha's delay and amendments can change public debate before that point. Joint sitting is therefore a last-resort majoritarian solution, not bicameral equality.

### 4. Presidential choices

For an ordinary Bill, the President may assent, withhold assent or return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration. If both Houses pass it again, with or without amendment, the President must assent. This is a suspensive, not absolute, check.

### 5. Example and limit

Imagine two editorial teams required to approve one final article. They may negotiate wording; if deadlocked, a combined meeting decides. The analogy fails for Money Bills and amendment Bills, where the Constitution deliberately prohibits the combined-meeting solution.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Joint sitting allows the numerically larger Lok Sabha to erase federal revision.

**Reply:** That risk explains why Article 108 is narrow and rare. It resolves ordinary legislative paralysis while preserving equal Rajya Sabha power over constitutional amendments and excluding the special Money Bill route. The residual federal concern remains when ordinary laws substantially affect States.

### UPSC application

- Always separate "six months" in Article 108 from the "fourteen days" applicable to Money Bills.
- A joint sitting is optional and presidentially summoned, not automatic.
- Do not place a Constitution Amendment Bill in the ordinary-bill pipeline.

### Revision notes

1. Ordinary Bills may originate in either House.
2. First Reading is introduction.
3. Second Reading includes general discussion, committee and consideration stages.
4. Third Reading accepts or rejects the final Bill.
5. Both Houses must ordinarily agree on identical text.
6. Article 108 identifies deadlock situations.
7. Joint sitting is summoned by the President.
8. Lok Sabha Speaker normally presides.
9. Lok Sabha numerical strength usually prevails.
10. India has used joint sitting three times.
11. No joint sitting applies to Money or Constitution Amendment Bills.
12. The President may return an ordinary Bill once.
13. Repassage converts the veto into a duty to assent.
14. Government and private-member Bills differ by sponsor, not legal status after enactment.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a joint sitting a deadlock device rather than a normal stage of ordinary legislation?

**Model answer:** Bicameral agreement remains the normal rule. Article 108 is activated only by specified disagreement, rejection or delay and requires presidential summoning. Its rarity preserves the Rajya Sabha's revising role while providing an exceptional way to prevent paralysis.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat six months of delay as automatically convening a joint sitting or apply Article 108 to amendment Bills.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** Assess whether the joint-sitting mechanism adequately reconciles bicameralism with legislative efficiency.

**Model response:** Article 108 reconciles bicameral revision with the need to prevent ordinary legislation from being blocked indefinitely. A joint sitting may be summoned when the second House rejects a Bill, the Houses finally disagree on amendments, or more than six months pass without enactment. The Lok Sabha Speaker presides and the combined membership decides.

Lok Sabha's larger numbers usually overcome deadlock. Yet the mechanism is exceptional: India has used it only for the Dowry Prohibition, Banking Service Commission Repeal and POTA Bills. Its rarity encourages negotiation.

Combined voting may reduce the Rajya Sabha's revising voice to delay. The Constitution partly answers this by excluding Money Bills and amendment Bills, on which each House has a non-overridable veto.

Thus, joint sitting is an adequate safety valve for ordinary law, provided it remains exceptional rather than a routine bypass of bicameral scrutiny.

## Lesson 7 - Money, Financial and Constitution Amendment Bills

Progress: 7/13 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Classification as a map of bicameral power

### PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST

Classify a Bill from its constitutional contents before applying origination, recommendation and Rajya Sabha rules.

### Visual first: classify before applying procedure

| Bill | Origin | President's recommendation | Rajya Sabha power | Joint sitting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary | either House | generally no | full | possible |
| Money, Art. 110 | Lok Sabha only | before introduction | recommendations within 14 days | no |
| Financial Bill-I, Art. 117(1) | Lok Sabha only | before introduction | full power | possible |
| Financial Bill-II, Art. 117(3) | either House | before consideration | full power | possible |
| Constitution Amendment, Art. 368 | either House | no | equal, separate passage | no |

### 1. Money Bill - an exhaustive category

Article 110 covers only matters such as taxation, government borrowing, custody or withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund, appropriation and charged expenditure, plus matters incidental to them. Merely having financial consequences does not make a Bill a Money Bill.

The Speaker certifies it. The Rajya Sabha must return it within fourteen days and may only recommend amendments. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations. If not returned in time, the Bill is deemed passed in the Lok Sabha form.

The President may assent or withhold assent but cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration.

### 2. Financial Bills are not all Money Bills

A Financial Bill-I contains one or more Article 110 matters plus other provisions. It shares Lok Sabha origin and prior recommendation, but the Rajya Sabha has full amending and rejecting power.

A Financial Bill-II involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund but contains no Article 110 matter. It may originate in either House; recommendation is required before consideration, not necessarily introduction.

> All Money Bills are financial Bills, but not all financial Bills are Money Bills.

### 3. Constitution Amendment Bill

Article 368 Bills may originate in either House, require special majority in each House and sometimes ratification by at least half the State legislatures. There is no joint sitting and the President must assent after valid passage.

This lesson distinguishes amendment procedure from the substantive basic-structure limitation.

### 4. Strongest objection - Money Bill route

**Objection:** A broad certification can bypass the Rajya Sabha and convert bicameral law-making into a Lok Sabha act.

**Reply:** Article 110 is textually exhaustive, the Speaker's certificate carries high institutional weight, and courts do not review routine procedural irregularity under Article 122. However, illegality or constitutional misuse is reviewable. The unresolved larger-bench question requires cautious language rather than declaring either total immunity or final invalidity.

### UPSC application

- Objective demands include the Money Bill definition, Finance Bill distinctions and Article 109 procedure.
- Never use "financial Bill" as a synonym for Money Bill.
- A Constitution Amendment Bill needs no prior presidential recommendation and has no joint sitting.

### Revision notes

1. Article 110 is an exhaustive Money Bill definition.
2. Money Bill originates only in Lok Sabha.
3. Prior presidential recommendation is required.
4. Speaker certifies the Bill.
5. Rajya Sabha may recommend within fourteen days.
6. Lok Sabha may reject every recommendation.
7. No joint sitting applies.
8. President cannot return a Money Bill.
9. Financial Bill-I is wider than a Money Bill and gives Rajya Sabha full power.
10. Financial Bill-II may originate in either House.
11. Article 368 Bills need separate special-majority passage.
12. Some amendments also need half-State ratification.
13. Money Bill judicial-review doctrine remains status-sensitive.

### Concept check

**Question:** What decisive feature distinguishes a Financial Bill-I from a Money Bill despite their shared Lok Sabha origin?

**Model answer:** A Financial Bill-I includes non-Article 110 matters in addition to financial provisions. Therefore, the Rajya Sabha retains full power to amend or reject it and an ordinary-bill deadlock may lead to joint sitting, unlike the recommendation-only Money Bill route.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not decide classification from the Bill's title, fiscal importance or requirement of presidential recommendation alone.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** Critically examine how the Money Bill procedure affects bicameralism in India.

**Model response:** The Money Bill procedure constitutionally translates the Lok Sabha's direct electoral mandate into financial primacy. Article 110 exhaustively defines the category. Such a Bill originates only in the Lok Sabha on presidential recommendation; the Speaker certifies it; the Rajya Sabha may only recommend changes within fourteen days; and no joint sitting is available. This enables a government responsible to the Lok Sabha to secure taxation and expenditure authority.

The difficulty begins when certification expands beyond the narrow Article 110 field. A Financial Bill-I may also contain financial provisions, but the Rajya Sabha has full power over it. Misclassification therefore does not merely alter procedure; it removes a federal chamber's legislative veto. The Aadhaar litigation in *Puttaswamy* upheld certification by a 4:1 majority, while *Rojer Mathew* doubted the wider reasoning and referred the issue to a larger bench. No final resolution was located on official Supreme Court portals by 30 September 2026.

Article 110 calls the Speaker's decision final, but constitutional finality cannot protect illegality or colourable bypass. At the same time, routine judicial substitution for the Speaker would weaken legislative autonomy.

The sound balance is narrow textual classification, reasoned certification and exceptional judicial review for constitutional misuse. Financial primacy is legitimate; transforming it into general unicameralism is not.

Separate presentation, discussion, grant voting, appropriation and taxation into their constitutional sequence.

### Visual first: no lawful spending without the parliamentary chain

```text
ANNUAL FINANCIAL STATEMENT (Art. 112)
        |
general discussion
        |
DRSC examination of ministry demands
        |
LOK SABHA votes DEMANDS FOR GRANTS (Art. 113)
 policy cut / economy cut / token cut / guillotine
        |
APPROPRIATION BILL (Art. 114)
 authority to withdraw from Consolidated Fund
        |
FINANCE BILL
 taxation proposals become law
```

### 1. Charged and voted expenditure

The Annual Financial Statement distinguishes expenditure **charged** on the Consolidated Fund from other expenditure. Charged expenditure may be discussed but is not voted. Demands for grants cover votable expenditure and are voted only by the Lok Sabha, on the President's recommendation.

The Rajya Sabha may discuss the budget but does not vote demands for grants.

### 2. Cut motions

| Motion | Reduction | Political meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Policy cut | demand reduced to Re 1 | disapproval of policy |
| Economy cut | specified reduction | economy in expenditure |
| Token cut | reduction by Rs 100 | ventilates a specific grievance |

Cut motions rarely succeed against a majority government, but their deliberative purpose is to force explanation.

### 3. Appropriation and Finance Bills

The Appropriation Bill authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund for voted grants and charged expenditure. The Finance Bill gives legal effect to taxation proposals. They are related but not interchangeable: expenditure authority and revenue authority are different constitutional acts.

### 4. Three constitutional funds

| Fund | Constitutional basis and contents | Withdrawal control |
|---|---|---|
| Consolidated Fund of India | Article 266(1): Union revenues, loans raised and repayment of loans | every withdrawal for expenditure requires authorisation by law through appropriation; charged expenditure is included in appropriation but is not voted as a grant |
| Public Account of India | Article 266(2): other public money received by or on behalf of the Government, such as money held in a trustee or banker capacity | withdrawals do not undergo the annual appropriation process in the same manner because the money is not government revenue available for ordinary expenditure; operation remains governed by law and rules |
| Contingency Fund of India | Article 267: statutory imprest placed at the President's disposal | an advance meets urgent unforeseen expenditure pending parliamentary authorisation; after the relevant grant and appropriation, the amount is **recouped from the Consolidated Fund** |

The funds answer different questions: the Consolidated Fund holds the government's main fiscal resources, the Public Account holds other people's or earmarked money administered by government, and the Contingency Fund temporarily bridges an unforeseen need. A Contingency Fund advance is not a substitute for later parliamentary approval.

### 5. Interim and exceptional devices

Article 116 provides **vote on account** for short-term spending pending full budget enactment, **vote of credit** for an unexpected demand of indefinite character, and **exceptional grant** for a special purpose outside current service.

Article 115 covers supplementary, additional and excess grants. Supplementary or additional grants address inadequacy or a new service during the year. An excess grant regularises expenditure after it has exceeded authorisation and is examined through the PAC-CAG accountability loop.

### 6. Guillotine

At the deadline, undiscussed demands may be put to vote together. This **guillotine** preserves fiscal continuity but reveals the cost of inadequate parliamentary time: enormous expenditure can receive formal approval without ministry-specific debate.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Majority discipline makes the power of the purse fictional because the government commands the votes.

**Reply:** Floor defeat is uncommon, but ex ante DRSC scrutiny, debate, disclosure, cut-motion criticism and ex post CAG-PAC audit create a layered control system. The weakness is not absence of instruments but their underuse and non-binding committee recommendations.

### UPSC application

- Never say Parliament votes charged expenditure.
- Demands for grants belong only to Lok Sabha.
- Do not apply the Consolidated Fund's appropriation rule mechanically to Public Account withdrawals.
- A Contingency Fund advance must later be authorised and recouped from the Consolidated Fund.
- Distinguish vote on account from an interim budget: the former is a grant device; the latter is a political description of a budget presented before transition.
- Link guillotine to executive dominance and debate quality.

### Revision notes

1. Article 112 names the Annual Financial Statement.
2. Charged expenditure is discussed but not voted.
3. Demands for grants are voted only by Lok Sabha.
4. Rajya Sabha may discuss the budget.
5. Policy, Economy and Token cuts test policy, economy and a specific grievance respectively.
6. Appropriation authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund; Finance legislation gives effect to taxation.
7. Article 266(1) places Union revenues, borrowing and loan repayments in the Consolidated Fund.
8. Consolidated Fund expenditure follows parliamentary authorisation and appropriation.
9. Article 266(2) Public Account money is held in a trustee/banker capacity and is not appropriated like ordinary government expenditure.
10. Article 267 Contingency Fund advances meet urgent unforeseen expenditure pending approval.
11. Contingency Fund advances are recouped from the Consolidated Fund after grant and appropriation.
12. Vote on account provides temporary spending authority.
13. Vote of credit addresses an unexpected, indefinite demand.
14. Excess grant is post-facto and connects to PAC scrutiny.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is passage of the Finance Bill insufficient by itself to authorise government expenditure?

**Model answer:** The Finance Bill gives legal effect to taxation and revenue proposals. Withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund requires appropriation under Article 114, covering voted grants and charged expenditure. Revenue authority and spending authority are constitutionally distinct.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use "budget passed" as if presentation, grant voting, appropriation and taxation were a single legal step.

### Original Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Mains question:** "India's parliamentary power of the purse is procedurally elaborate but politically fragile." Evaluate.

**Model response:** The Constitution creates an elaborate financial-control chain. The Annual Financial Statement under Article 112 separates charged from votable expenditure. Department-related Standing Committees examine ministry demands; the Lok Sabha votes grants under Article 113; cut motions enable scrutiny; appropriation under Article 114 authorises Consolidated Fund withdrawal; and the Finance Bill enacts taxation.

Articles 266-267 further distinguish fiscal custody. The Consolidated Fund contains Union revenues and borrowing and is subject to parliamentary expenditure control. Public Account money is held in a trustee or banker capacity and is not withdrawn through annual appropriation in the same manner. The Contingency Fund advances money for unforeseen expenditure pending approval and is later recouped from the Consolidated Fund after grant and appropriation.

Its political fragility comes from majority government. Party discipline makes successful cut motions exceptional. The Rajya Sabha cannot vote grants. The guillotine may pass undiscussed demands at the deadline, while committee recommendations are advisory. Thus, formal authorisation may occur without proportionate deliberation.

Yet the power is not empty. Committee examination supplies technical scrutiny; appropriation makes unauthorised Consolidated Fund withdrawal unlawful; and the CAG-PAC loop detects excess or irregular expenditure.

The central reform is better use: adequate sitting time, timely reports, protected debate and reasoned responses. The power of the purse is legally strong but behaviourally dependent on information, time and majority willingness to scrutinise its executive.

### 1. Questions

A **starred question** receives an oral answer and permits supplementaries. An **unstarred question** receives a written answer and no supplementaries. A **short-notice question** addresses urgent public importance with notice shorter than the ordinary period and is answered orally.

Question Hour creates official, attributable information. Supplementaries are especially important because they permit immediate probing after the minister's first answer.

### 2. Zero Hour and discussions

Zero Hour developed as an Indian innovation from 1962 and is not formally mentioned in the Rules of Procedure. It follows Question Hour in conventional practice and allows urgent matters to be raised.

Calling Attention combines a member's notice with a ministerial statement. Half-an-Hour Discussion clarifies matters arising from a previous answer. Short-Duration Discussion permits debate without a formal vote.

### 3. Motions and resolutions

A motion is a proposal placed before the House; a resolution is a more formal expression of opinion or decision. They may be substantive, substitute or subsidiary depending on function.

An **Adjournment Motion**, admitted only in the Lok Sabha, interrupts normal business to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance and carries censure implications. A **Censure Motion** must state reasons and may target particular policies or ministers. A **No-Confidence Motion** need not state reasons; it tests whether the entire Council of Ministers retains Lok Sabha confidence and requires support of at least fifty members for admission.

Other devices occupy different procedural niches. A **privilege motion** alleges breach of privilege by a member or minister. A **motion of thanks** debates the President's address and an adverse amendment may carry major political weight. **Closure motions** seek to end debate and reach a decision; the guillotine is a specialised financial closure. A **point of order** asks whether House rules are being followed and is decided by the presiding officer. **Special Mention** in the Rajya Sabha and matters under Rule 377 in the Lok Sabha allow issues to be placed on record without becoming confidence tests.

Closure may be simple, by compartments, or selective ("kangaroo"); the guillotine puts remaining financial demands to vote at the fixed time. A **no-day-yet-named motion** has been admitted but awaits scheduling. Resolutions may be private-member, government or statutory resolutions; unlike a broad discussion, each places a definite proposition before the House.

The **Leader of the Opposition** is a statutory office under the Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977. The Act turns on recognition by the Speaker or Chairman; it does not itself prescribe a ten-percent threshold. **MPLADS** is an administrative scheme, not a statutory member entitlement; funds are non-lapsable and implementation is monitored through the designated governmental machinery. No current per-member amount is stated here because that figure requires date-specific verification.

### 4. Why devices differ

The distinction is not ceremonial. A question seeks information; a discussion tests explanation; censure records disapproval; no-confidence determines government survival. Treating all devices as interchangeable hides their escalating constitutional stakes.

### 5. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Disruptions and scripted answers have made accountability devices performative.

**Reply:** Written answers create durable public records, committee and audit work can follow disclosures, and a confidence vote still has binding consequence. The objection identifies degraded use, not doctrinal redundancy.

### UPSC application

- Never describe Zero Hour as a constitutional or Rules-based hour.

A Censure Motion records disapproval with stated reasons. A No-Confidence Motion, admitted with support of fifty members, tests the Council of Ministers' collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha under Article 75 and can remove the government.

### 1. Why privileges exist

Privileges are special rights and immunities necessary for each House, its committees and members to perform legislative functions without external intimidation. They belong partly to individual members and partly to the House collectively.

Individual protections include freedom of speech subject to the Constitution and House rules, and immunity from court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in Parliament. Collective powers include regulating internal proceedings, excluding strangers, publishing debates and punishing breach of privilege or contempt.

### 2. Privilege versus fundamental freedom

Parliamentary speech is wider than ordinary Article 19 speech because Article 105 is function-specific. Yet it is not unregulated: rules, the presiding officer and constitutional prohibitions apply. Article 122 generally prevents courts from questioning proceedings for mere procedural irregularity, but does not immunise substantive illegality or unconstitutionality.

### 3. Bribery boundary

In ***Sita Soren v Union of India* (2024)**, a unanimous seven-judge Bench overruled the contrary bribery immunity in *P.V. Narasimha Rao* (1998). Accepting a bribe for a speech or vote is not protected; the offence is complete upon acceptance. The judgment preserves immunity for genuine legislative speech and voting while refusing to convert privilege into a corruption shield.

### 4. Ethics, privilege and contempt

Ethics concerns standards of conduct. Breach of privilege violates a specific privilege; contempt is broader conduct obstructing the House or its members in performing functions. The Ethics Committee and Committee of Privileges are therefore not interchangeable.

### 5. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Uncodified privileges allow Houses to be judges in their own cause and threaten press or citizen liberty.

**Reply:** Flexibility protects unforeseen legislative needs, and courts preserve constitutional boundaries. The residual uncertainty supports clearer rules, proportional sanctions and a functional-nexus test rather than complete abolition.

### UPSC application

- Use the test: **Does the protection facilitate legislative function?**
- Do not write that privilege places Parliament "above the Constitution."
- The 2024 objective demand tests the Ethics Committee distinction.

### Revision notes

1. Article 105 governs parliamentary powers and privileges.
2. Privilege is functional, not a personal status benefit.
3. Speech and votes in Parliament receive special immunity.
4. House rules still regulate speech.
5. Authorised publication receives protection.
6. Houses control internal proceedings and may punish contempt.
7. Article 122 distinguishes procedural irregularity from illegality.
8. Ethics and privilege are different jurisdictions.
9. Contempt may be wider than a technical breach of privilege.
10. *Sita Soren* (2024) removed bribery immunity.
11. The judgment overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* on that point.
12. Genuine speech-and-vote protection remains.
13. Codification debate balances certainty and institutional flexibility.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can bribery for a vote be punishable while the vote itself remains protected from court proceedings?

**Model answer:** The immunity protects the legislative act of speaking or voting so deliberation remains independent. Bribery is a separate criminal transaction completed on acceptance and lacks a legitimate functional connection to legislative freedom; punishing it does not question the vote's validity in court.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say *Sita Soren* abolished parliamentary privilege or made courts general supervisors of legislative debate.

### Original Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Mains question:** "Parliamentary privilege is a shield for legislative function, not a sword against constitutional accountability." Discuss.

**Model response:** Article 105 protects freedom of speech in Parliament and grants immunity from court proceedings for words spoken or votes given. Collective privileges allow each House to regulate proceedings, publish debates, exclude strangers and punish obstruction. These protections prevent executive, judicial or private intimidation of legislative work.

They are not personal impunity. Speech remains subject to constitutional provisions and House rules. Article 122 protects proceedings from challenge for procedural irregularity, not substantive illegality. In *Sita Soren v Union of India* (2024), a unanimous seven-judge Bench held that accepting a bribe for a speech or vote is not privileged and overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao* on that point.

Uncodified privilege creates uncertainty for citizens and the press, but exhaustive codification may reduce institutional flexibility. The appropriate test is functional nexus and proportionality: protect conduct necessary for deliberation, while permitting review where privilege becomes a cover for corruption or unconstitutional action.

### 1. Accountability architecture

Collective responsibility under Article 75(3) makes the Council of Ministers answerable to the Lok Sabha. Questions disclose information; motions impose political judgment; grant voting controls expenditure; committees investigate detail; no-confidence can remove the government.

### 2. Anti-defection bridge

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment and tightened by the 91st, can disqualify a party member who defies a valid whip. For Parliament, the key consequence is behavioural: the majority's backbenchers may fear losing their seats, so legislative scrutiny becomes party-controlled.

For the Parliament-accountability problem, three propositions are decisive:

1. the Speaker's decision is judicially reviewable under *Kihoto Hollohan*;
2. delay can distort parliamentary majorities;
3. an overbroad whip trades deliberative independence for government stability.

### 3. Delegated legislation

Parliament often enacts a framework and authorises the executive to make detailed rules. Delegation is necessary for technical detail and adaptability, but excessive delegation moves norm-making away from open debate. The Committee on Subordinate Legislation checks whether rules remain within the parent Act and respect procedural requirements.

Parliament cannot delegate its essential legislative function: it must state policy and standards rather than hand the executive an uncontrolled blank cheque.

### 4. Ordinance interface

Article 123 permits the President to promulgate an ordinance whenever the two Houses are **not simultaneously in session** and immediate action is considered necessary. Thus, the power is available if either House is not in session. The ordinance has the force of an Act but must be laid before both Houses. If the Houses reassemble on different dates, it ceases six weeks from the **later** reassembly date. It may cease earlier if **both Houses** pass resolutions disapproving it, and it may be withdrawn by the President.

*D.C. Wadhwa* (1987) and *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017) condemn routine re-promulgation as a fraud on the Constitution. Ordinances are temporary emergency substitutes, not an alternative legislative calendar.

### 5. Law-making quality

Quality depends on pre-legislative consultation, reasoned drafting, adequate debate, committee referral, clause scrutiny and post-legislative review. Fast enactment is not necessarily efficient if ambiguity, litigation or implementation failure follows.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** In a parliamentary majority system, the executive inevitably controls the legislature; reform cannot change arithmetic.

**Reply:** Arithmetic explains passage, not scrutiny quality. Protected committee referral, narrower whips, minimum deliberation periods and better research can improve law without denying the majority its mandate.

**Model response:** Parliament possesses strong formal controls: Article 75(3) collective responsibility, Question Hour, motions, grant voting, financial committees, DRSC scrutiny and no-confidence. Declining scrutiny therefore reflects institutional incentives more than textual absence.

First, the anti-defection whip converts many votes into party commands and weakens the individual legislator. Second, the executive controls most House time and legislative business. Third, the guillotine passes undiscussed demands, while major Bills may avoid committee referral. Fourth, disruptions consume Question Hour and debate. Fifth, broad delegated legislation and Article 123 ordinances shift norm-making toward the executive. Short enactment timelines then increase ambiguity and litigation.

The defence is that disciplined majorities provide stability and allow implementation of an electoral mandate. Delegation is unavoidable in a technical State, and ordinances may meet genuine urgency. The constitutional problem is misuse, not existence.

Reform should preserve confidence discipline but confine the whip mainly to government-survival and core financial votes; make committee referral the default with stated exceptions; guarantee scrutiny time for major Bills and grants; strengthen legislative research; require reasoned responses to committee reports; and enforce judicial limits on re-promulgation. Post-legislative review should test whether enacted law achieved its purpose.

Responsible government needs both governability and answerability. A majority may secure passage, but democratic legitimacy requires that Parliament test evidence, hear dissent and expose executive reasons before it authorises coercive law.

### 1. British sovereignty versus Indian supremacy

The British doctrine means Parliament may make or unmake any law and no court may invalidate an Act as unconstitutional. India instead has a written, federal Constitution, enumerated legislative fields, Fundamental Rights and judicial review.

Indian Parliament is powerful but legally limited. It may legislate within competence, control Union finance, hold the executive responsible and amend the Constitution under Article 368. It cannot exercise these powers contrary to constitutional limitations.

### 2. Four principal limits

1. **Written Constitution:** powers and procedures come from a higher legal text.
2. **Federalism:** legislative competence is distributed; State ratification is needed for specified amendments.
3. **Rights and judicial review:** courts may invalidate laws violating enforceable constitutional provisions.
4. **Basic structure:** Parliament's constituent power cannot destroy constitutional identity.

The decisive conclusion is that amendment power is wide but not legally sovereign.

### 3. Article 122 and internal autonomy

Courts should not question proceedings for mere procedural irregularity. This protects the separation of powers and permits each House to manage business. But allegations of substantive illegality, unconstitutional certification or violation of mandatory constitutional requirements remain reviewable.

### 4. Ordinance-Parliament relationship revisited

An ordinance derives democratic durability only through Parliament. The power exists whenever both Houses are not simultaneously in session. If they reassemble on different dates, the six-week clock runs from the later date. Early cessation through parliamentary disapproval requires both Houses to pass disapproval resolutions; action by one House alone is insufficient. Replacement legislation must pass the proper bicameral route. An ordinance cannot amend the Constitution or permanently escape legislative examination.

### 5. Representation bridge and institutional verdict

Delimitation determines who Parliament represents; procedure determines how it acts; judicial review determines whether action remains constitutional. These are not separate silos. A legislature can claim democratic authority only if representation is fair, process is deliberative and power remains legally bounded.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Judicial review and basic structure allow unelected judges to override Parliament's democratic mandate.

**Reply:** Courts do gain counter-majoritarian power, and doctrine must be reasoned and restrained. Yet Parliament's mandate is constituted, not pre-constitutional. Judicial review enforces the rules that make majority power legitimate. The unresolved residual is how to prevent both legislative majoritarianism and judicial overreach.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate political centrality or control over the executive with British-style power to make any law free from judicial invalidation.

**Mains question:** "Indian Parliament is institutionally central but constitutionally limited." Discuss with reference to legislation, finance, executive accountability and judicial review.

**Model response:** Parliament is institutionally central because it connects representation to coercive public power. It enacts Union law, authorises taxation and withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund, debates policy, sustains or removes the Council of Ministers and may amend the Constitution under Article 368. Bicameralism adds popular and federal-revising legitimacy.

Its power is nevertheless constituted. Ordinary law must fall within distributed legislative competence and comply with Fundamental Rights. Money Bill classification cannot legitimately become a device to bypass the Rajya Sabha. Financial control requires grants and appropriation, while executive accountability operates through questions, motions, committees and confidence. Ordinances under Article 123 remain temporary: if the Houses reassemble on different dates, they cease six weeks from the later date, unless withdrawn earlier or disapproved by both Houses.

The written Constitution, federalism and judicial review distinguish India from British parliamentary sovereignty. Article 122 protects internal proceedings from challenge for mere irregularity, but not substantive constitutional illegality. Article 368 confers constituent power, yet the basic-structure doctrine prevents destruction of constitutional identity.

The counter-majoritarian concern is genuine: judicial review may displace an elected majority. Its justification is that the majority derives authority from constitutional rules and cannot simultaneously claim power to erase them.

Thus, Parliament is supreme neither over the Constitution nor over courts. It is central within a system of separated but interdependent institutions, where democratic mandate, bicameral deliberation and constitutional limitation jointly create legitimate law.

_Model word count: 230._

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 - institutional design

**Question:** A proposal concerns an ordinary Union subject, but the Rajya Sabha refuses it while the Lok Sabha repeatedly passes it. Identify the constitutional route and its limitation.

**Model answer:** Article 108 may permit a presidentially summoned joint sitting after a qualifying deadlock. Combined voting usually favours Lok Sabha numbers. The route is unavailable for Money Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills and should remain exceptional to preserve bicameral revision.

## Check 2 - financial classification

**Question:** A Bill contains a taxation clause and extensive non-financial regulatory provisions. Why is it unsafe to classify it automatically as a Money Bill?

**Model answer:** Article 110 is exhaustive. A Bill containing Article 110 matters plus other substantive provisions is ordinarily a Financial Bill-I, over which the Rajya Sabha has full power. Fiscal content alone does not justify the recommendation-only route.

## Check 3 - accountability

**Question:** Why can Parliament be formally strong yet behaviourally weak in controlling the executive?

**Model answer:** Questions, motions, grants, committees and confidence provide strong formal instruments, but the party whip, executive control of time, disruptions, guillotine and optional committee referral reduce independent scrutiny and deliberation.

## Check 4 - constitutional supremacy

**Question:** Reconcile Article 122 with judicial review.

**Model answer:** Article 122 prevents courts from invalidating proceedings for mere procedural irregularity, protecting legislative autonomy. It does not bar review of substantive illegality, constitutional violations or misuse of constitutionally limited powers.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker - 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the constitutional roles of the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha without describing either as universally superior.

**Model answer:** The directly elected Lok Sabha carries the stronger popular mandate and determines government survival. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible only to it. Money Bills originate there, Rajya Sabha only recommends within fourteen days, and only Lok Sabha votes demands for grants.

The Rajya Sabha is nevertheless not generally subordinate. It is a permanent revising chamber representing States. Both Houses possess equal power over ordinary Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills, which must pass separately. Under Articles 249 and 312, the Rajya Sabha alone may initiate parliamentary entry into a State List field in the national interest and creation of All-India Services.

At a joint sitting, Lok Sabha numbers usually prevail, but that mechanism excludes Money and amendment Bills. Therefore, Lok Sabha is dominant on finance and confidence, while Rajya Sabha is co-equal on ordinary law and amendment and distinctive in federal gatekeeping.

_Model word count: 143._

**Rubric (10):** Lok Sabha primacy 3 | Rajya Sabha equality 3 | exclusive federal roles 2 | precise verdict 2.

## Original 15-marker - 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate whether parliamentary committees have become the principal site of legislative accountability in India.

**Model answer:** Parliamentary committees increasingly perform scrutiny that the crowded and partisan floor cannot. The PAC examines CAG reports and expenditure regularity; the Estimates Committee suggests economies; and CoPU reviews public undertakings. Twenty-four DRSCs examine ministry demands for grants, referred Bills, annual reports and long-term policy. Subordinate Legislation, Assurances, Ethics and Privileges Committees cover specialised control.

Their comparative advantage is depth. Smaller groups can question officials, receive evidence, work across party lines and develop subject expertise. Committee reports create a reasoned public record and allow Parliament to inspect policy before and after expenditure. This makes committees a principal accountability site even though final authorisation remains with the Houses.

Their position is not secure. Recommendations are advisory, one-year membership weakens specialisation, research support is limited and governments need not refer every Bill. Major laws may therefore be passed quickly without the institution best equipped for clause scrutiny. Non-public deliberation lowers grandstanding but may also reduce transparency.

Committees should not replace floor debate, ministerial responsibility or voting. Default referral with reasoned exceptions, adequate time, professional staff and mandatory government responses would connect committee expertise to plenary accountability.

Thus, committees are the principal site of detailed scrutiny, not the sole site of democratic accountability. Their strength lies in preparing Parliament to decide; their weakness is that the executive often controls whether they receive the opportunity.

**Rubric (15):** committee architecture 3 | reasons for primacy 4 | limitations 3 | floor relationship 2 | reforms 2 | evaluated verdict 1.

## Original 20-marker - 250 words

**Question:** Critically examine the proposition that executive dominance has converted Parliament from a deliberative legislature into a ratifying body.

**Model answer:** Executive dominance is structurally plausible in a parliamentary system. The Council of Ministers sits within the legislature, controls most government business and normally commands a disciplined majority. The Tenth Schedule makes dissent costly; the guillotine may pass undiscussed grants; optional committee referral reduces clause scrutiny; delegated legislation transfers detail to ministries; and Article 123 ordinances can precede debate. These forces weaken the individual parliamentarian and support the image of ratification.

The proposition is nevertheless incomplete. Parliament remains the legal source of ordinary legislation, taxation and appropriation. Question Hour and written answers produce disclosures; no-confidence gives collective responsibility a binding edge; DRSCs examine grants and Bills; PAC converts CAG audit into political accountability; and both Houses must separately pass constitutional amendments. Courts also police constitutional limits without replacing political scrutiny.

The real decline is therefore in the quality and frequency of deliberation, not the formal extinction of parliamentary power. A majority government is entitled to enact its programme, but mandate cannot substitute for evidence and reason-giving.

Reform should confine binding whips mainly to confidence and core financial votes, make committee referral the default, protect minimum scrutiny time, strengthen legislative research, require reasoned responses to committee reports and prevent ordinance re-promulgation.

Parliament has not become a mere seal, but its capacity to revise executive proposals is uneven. It remains strong where information, time and institutional independence are available and weak where party control compresses them.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Constitutional identity and bicameral logic

- **Article 79:** Parliament = President + Rajya Sabha + Lok Sabha; President is integral, not a member.
- **Bicameral purposes:** direct popular will, federal participation, revision and continuity.
- **Rajya Sabha:** permanent; one-third retire every second year; normal member term six years.
- **Lok Sabha:** normally five years, sooner dissolvable; emergency extension one year at a time, no more than six months beyond emergency.
- **Power formula:** Lok Sabha dominant on finance/confidence; Houses broadly equal on ordinary law and amendment; Rajya Sabha distinctive under Articles 249 and 312.

## Composition, elections and representation

- RS maximum **250**: up to 238 elected plus 12 nominated; stable sanctioned strength 245.
- LS maximum **550**: up to 530 States plus 20 UTs; 543 elected seats.
- RS elected by elected MLAs through **STV proportional representation**; LS through direct **FPTP**.
- Article 80(5): Union Territory representation in Rajya Sabha exists only in the manner Parliament prescribes; allocation follows the Fourth Schedule and law.
- RS open ballot and removal of State-domicile requirement came by 2003 statutory amendment, upheld in *Kuldip Nayar*.
- Anglo-Indian Lok Sabha nomination ceased under the **104th Amendment**.
- Articles 81/82 connect representation to population and statutory readjustment; Article 329(a) insulates delimitation law from judicial challenge.
- Four nationwide Delimitation Commissions were constituted under the Acts of **1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002**.
- Freeze chain: **42nd (1976) -> 84th (2001) -> 87th (2003)**.
- Census 2027 is the first census after 2026; publication of figures, not enumeration alone, matters.
- 106th Amendment commenced **16 April 2026** under S.O. 1922(E); women's reservation remains non-operational pending the Article 334A census-publication-delimitation chain.
- Scope: one-third in Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly, including within SC/ST-reserved seats.
- Articles 243D/243T form the local-body stage; Article 334A controls parliamentary/Assembly operation.
- Duration is "fifteen years from such commencement", continuance is as Parliament may by law determine, and rotation follows each subsequent delimitation.
- The official Sansad Government Bills record dated 17 April 2026 labels the combined three-Bill proceeding "not passed"; it does not establish a separate disposal mode or exact division figures for each Bill.

## Membership integrity

- Article 84: citizenship, oath, age and statutory qualification.
- Ages: RS **30**, LS **25**.
- RPA 1951: elector registration is required; reserved-seat candidature carries the statutory SC/ST qualification.
- Article 102: office of profit, declared unsound mind, undischarged insolvency, citizenship/allegiance and statutory disqualification.
- RPA grounds include specified corrupt practices/election offences, listed convictions, election-expense default, government-contract interests, specified government-corporation positions and dismissal for corruption/disloyalty.
- 1959 Prevention of Disqualification Act exempts listed offices.
- Article 103: President decides sitting-member disqualification after Election Commission opinion.
- Article 101(2): if a simultaneous State legislator does not resign the State seat in time, the **Parliament seat** becomes vacant.
- Resignation must be voluntary and genuine.
- Unauthorised absence for 60 days may permit vacancy declaration.
- Tenth Schedule adjudication by the presiding officer is subject to judicial review.

## Election petitions and returned-candidate validity

- Article 329(b) channels a completed election dispute into the statutory election-petition process.
- RPA sections 80/80A/81: candidate or elector petitions the jurisdictional High Court, ordinarily within 45 days.
- Section 83 requires material facts, full corrupt-practice particulars, verification and the prescribed affidavit.
- Sections 86-87 govern threshold dismissal, CPC/Evidence Act trial, day-to-day conduct as practicable and an endeavour to finish within six months.
- Section 99 requires the nature of any proved corrupt practice and responsible persons to be recorded, with notice and hearing before a non-party is named.
- Section 100 void grounds cover qualification/disqualification, specified corrupt practices, improper rejection of nomination and result-material nomination/vote/non-compliance defects.
- Sections 98/101 govern dismissal, voiding and declaration of another candidate as elected in the stated conditions.
- Section 116A provides a Supreme Court appeal on law and fact, ordinarily within 30 days; stay is separately governed by section 116B.
- *N.P. Ponnuswami*, *Mohinder Singh Gill*, *Jyoti Basu* and *F.A. Sapa* define the statutory channel, pleading discipline and section 99 fairness.

## Presiding officers and voting

- Articles 93-97: Speaker/Deputy Speaker; Vice-President is RS Chairman; RS elects Deputy Chairman.
- Speaker and Deputy Speaker removal: Lok Sabha effective majority and fourteen days' notice.
- Vice-President/Chairman removal: Rajya Sabha effective-majority resolution, Lok Sabha agreement and fourteen days' notice.
- Deputy Chairman removal: Rajya Sabha effective majority and fourteen days' notice.
- Speaker survives dissolution until immediately before new House's first sitting.
- Articles 96/92 bar the officer concerned from presiding during that officer's removal proceeding.
- Speaker may vote initially but has no casting vote; Chairman may speak during the Vice-President-removal proceeding but cannot vote.
- Article 100: ordinarily majority present and voting; presiding officer uses casting vote in tie.
- Speaker certifies Money Bills, presides over joint sittings and decides defection cases.
- Speaker pro tem administers oaths and conducts the Speaker's election; Article 88 governs minister/Attorney-General participation.
- Article 98 provides separate parliamentary secretariats.
- Official Lok Sabha status: Deputy Speaker office vacant since May 2019.

## Sessions, quorum, language and lapse

- Article 85: no more than six months between last sitting of one session and first sitting of next.
- No constitutional minimum sitting days; Budget/Monsoon/Winter are conventions.
- Adjournment: presiding officer, sitting; prorogation: President, session; dissolution: President/expiry, Lok Sabha life.
- Quorum under Article 100(3): one-tenth.
- Article 120: Hindi or English, with mother-tongue permission.
- Lapse: LS-pending; LS-passed/RS-pending.
- Survive: RS-pending not passed by LS; both-House-passed awaiting assent; returned Bill; notified joint sitting.

## Legislative procedures

- Ordinary Bill: either House -> three readings -> second House -> President.
- Article 108 deadlock; only three joint sittings: 1961, 1978, 2002.
- No joint sitting for Money or amendment Bills.
- Money Bill Article 110: LS only, prior recommendation, Speaker certificate, RS recommendations in 14 days, no return by President.
- Financial Bill-I Article 117(1): LS origin and recommendation, but RS full power.
- Financial Bill-II Article 117(3): either House, recommendation before consideration.
- Amendment Bill: either House, separate special-majority passage, possible half-State ratification, mandatory assent.
- *Puttaswamy* 2018 and *Rojer Mathew* 2019 require status caution on Money Bill review.

## Budget and financial control

- Article 112: Annual Financial Statement.
- Charged expenditure discussed but not voted.
- Article 113: LS-only demands for grants; President's recommendation.
- Cut motions: Policy **Re 1**, Economy specified amount, Token **Rs 100**.
- Article 114: Appropriation authorises Consolidated Fund withdrawal.
- Finance legislation gives effect to taxation.
- Article 266(1): Union revenues, borrowing and loan repayments form the Consolidated Fund; expenditure follows parliamentary authorisation and appropriation.
- Article 266(2): Public Account holds other public money in a trustee/banker capacity and does not use annual appropriation in the same manner.
- Article 267: Contingency Fund advances meet unforeseen expenditure pending approval and are recouped from the Consolidated Fund after grant and appropriation.
- Article 115: supplementary, additional and excess grants.
- Article 116: vote on account, vote of credit, exceptional grant.
- Guillotine passes undiscussed demands at deadline.
- Financial accountability chain: DRSC ex ante -> grant/appropriation -> CAG audit -> PAC ex post.

## Accountability devices

- Starred: oral and supplementaries; unstarred: written, no supplementaries; short-notice: urgent, oral.
- Zero Hour: Indian innovation from 1962, not in Rules.
- Calling Attention: ministerial statement; Half-an-Hour: clarify prior answer; Short-Duration: debate without vote.
- Adjournment Motion: LS, definite urgent matter, censure effect, 50-member support.
- No-Confidence: LS, 50-member admission support, no reasons necessary, tests Article 75 responsibility.
- Censure states reasons and can target policy/ministers.
- Privilege motion, motion of thanks, closure motions, point of order, Special Mention and Rule 377 matters serve distinct procedural functions.
- Leader of Opposition is a statutory office under the 1977 Act; the Act itself contains no ten-percent threshold.
- MPLADS is an administrative, non-lapsable scheme, not a statutory entitlement.

## Committees

- Standing versus ad hoc.
- PAC **22 = 15 LS + 7 RS**, opposition chair convention, CAG-based post-facto scrutiny.
- Estimates **30 LS only**, economies and administrative improvement.
- CoPU **22 = 15 + 7**, public undertakings.
- DRSCs: **17 in 1993 -> 24 from 2004**; each **31 = 21 LS + 10 RS**; 16 LS-serviced and 8 RS-serviced; reports advisory.
- Welfare of SCs/STs: **30 = 20 LS + 10 RS**.
- Subordinate Legislation checks delegated rules; Ethics differs from Privileges.
- Select/Joint Committees on Bills and JPCs are ad hoc; consultative committees are not parliamentary committees.
- Parliamentary forums are issue-learning platforms, not committees or law-making bodies.
- IPG, formed in 1949, is India's National Group in the IPU and main India Branch in the CPA.

## Privilege and discipline

- Article 105 privileges are function-based.
- Freedom of speech/vote immunity protects deliberation subject to Constitution and rules.
- Collective privilege includes internal control and punishment for contempt.
- Article 122 bars review for mere procedural irregularity, not substantive illegality.
- *Sita Soren* (2024): bribery is not privileged; *P.V. Narasimha Rao* overruled on that point.
- Ethics, breach of privilege and contempt are related but distinct.

## Executive dominance and reform

- Formal controls: questions, motions, finance, committees and confidence.
- Weakening mechanisms: broad whip, agenda control, short sittings, disruption, guillotine, optional referral, delegated legislation and ordinances.
- Anti-defection effect: *Kihoto Hollohan* review and adjudicatory delay can alter House arithmetic.
- Article 123 applies whenever both Houses are not simultaneously in session; the ordinance is laid before both Houses.
- It ceases six weeks from the later reassembly date; earlier disapproval requires resolutions of both Houses.
- *D.C. Wadhwa* and *Krishna Kumar Singh*: re-promulgation cannot substitute for legislation.
- Reforms: narrower survival-vote whip, default committee referral, protected scrutiny time, research support, reasoned responses and post-legislative review.

## Parliamentary sovereignty limits

- India has parliamentary government, not British parliamentary sovereignty.
- Limits: written Constitution, federal competence, Fundamental Rights, judicial review and basic structure.
- Parliament is institutionally central but constitutionally limited.
- Answer verdict: **strong in instruments, variable in use, bounded in law**.
