# Parliamentary System — Live Session Edition

India’s parliamentary system answers one basic democratic question: **how can the executive possess enough authority to govern while remaining continuously answerable to elected representatives?** The Constitution answers by joining the executive to the legislature, separating the ceremonial head from the governing head, and making the ministry dependent on the confidence of the popular House.

### Visual: What makes a system parliamentary?

```text
PEOPLE ELECT THE LOK SABHA
           ↓
MAJORITY SUPPORT PRODUCES THE GOVERNMENT
           ↓
MINISTERS SIT IN / PARTICIPATE IN PARLIAMENT
           ↓
THE MINISTRY MUST EXPLAIN AND DEFEND ITS ACTIONS
           ↓
LOK SABHA MAY WITHDRAW CONFIDENCE
           ↓
THE WHOLE MINISTRY MUST GO

Core test: Can the elected lower House remove the political executive?
           YES → parliamentary responsibility
           NO, fixed independent executive → presidential design
```

The diagram identifies the decisive test. Elections alone do not make a government parliamentary. The defining feature is that the **political executive remains in office only while it commands legislative confidence**.

### 1. Begin with the practical problem

Every democracy needs an executive capable of taking decisions. But an executive that receives authority for a fixed period and cannot be removed for ordinary political failure may become insulated from day-to-day legislative accountability.

The parliamentary answer is **responsible government**:

- the ministry emerges from the legislature;
- it must answer questions, defend policy and obtain financial approval;
- it may be removed through loss of confidence;
- the executive and legislature therefore work in a relationship of **fusion with accountability**.

This is why parliamentary government is also called:

| Expression | What it emphasises |
|---|---|
| **Responsible government** | Continuing answerability to the legislature |
| **Cabinet government** | The Cabinet is the governing decision core |
| **Westminster model** | Historical association with the British parliamentary tradition |
| **Parliamentary government** | The executive’s dependence on legislative confidence |

Ivor Jennings called it the **Cabinet system** because the Cabinet is the nucleus of power. The label is descriptive, not a constitutional phrase.

### 2. Constitutional spine in India

| Level | Aid-and-advice provision | Ministerial-responsibility provision | Popular House |
|---|---|---|---|
| Union | **Article 74** | **Article 75** | Lok Sabha |
| State | **Article 163** | **Article 164** | Legislative Assembly |

At the Union:

1. Article 74 requires a Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister to aid and advise the President.
2. Article 75 governs appointment, tenure, responsibility, oaths, ministry size and the non-member rule.
3. Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the **House of the People**, not to both Houses equally.

At the State, Articles 163 and 164 create a parallel structure, subject to constitutionally recognised areas of gubernatorial discretion.

### 3. The eight features as one connected mechanism

```text
1. NOMINAL + REAL EXECUTIVE
          ↓
2. MAJORITY / COALITION FORMS GOVERNMENT
          ↓
3. COLLECTIVE RESPONSIBILITY TO LOWER HOUSE
          ↓
4. POLITICAL HOMOGENEITY OR COALITION CONSENSUS
          ↓
5. MINISTERS LINK EXECUTIVE AND LEGISLATURE
          ↓
6. PRIME MINISTER PROVIDES LEADERSHIP
          ↓
7. LOWER HOUSE MAY BE DISSOLVED
          ↓
8. SECRECY PROTECTS INTERNAL DELIBERATION;
   SOLIDARITY PRESENTS ONE PUBLIC POSITION
```

These are not eight disconnected facts. They form a chain. Majority support creates the ministry; membership creates fusion; collective responsibility produces accountability; Prime Ministerial leadership creates coordination; secrecy permits candid internal debate; solidarity makes responsibility enforceable against one identifiable government.

### 4. The system’s central trade-off

The parliamentary system favours:

- **responsibility** over guaranteed tenure;
- **replaceability** over fixed executive stability;
- **coordination** over rigid institutional separation;
- **representative Cabinet government** over a single independently elected executive.

Its cost is that a ministry may fall before completing the lower House’s normal term. That is not automatically a constitutional failure. It is the price of making executive survival conditional on political confidence.

### 5. Example and its limit

Suppose a government loses support from coalition partners. In a parliamentary system, it must demonstrate majority support or leave office. This shows continuing responsibility.

**Limit of the example:** the head of State cannot treat every disagreement within a party or coalition as proof that confidence has been lost. Majority ordinarily must be tested through the House, not inferred from press statements or private political assessments.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Fusion allows the executive to dominate the legislature, especially when a disciplined party has a large majority.

**Reply:** That danger is real, but the answer lies in strengthening questions, committees, debate, financial control, judicially enforceable constitutional limits and intra-party deliberation. Moving to a fixed executive would increase stability but would also remove the ordinary confidence sanction.

**Qualified assessment:** Parliamentary responsibility is strongest as a constitutional principle and variable as a political practice. A majority may blunt scrutiny, but it does not abolish the ministry’s dependence on Lok Sabha confidence.

### UPSC application

- **Prelims:** identify responsibility and removability as the defining test.
- **Mains:** distinguish parliamentary government from parliamentary sovereignty.
- **Answer line:** *A parliamentary executive is powerful because it leads the legislature, but responsible because its authority lasts only while the popular House sustains it.*

### Verified PYQ linkage

**2020 Prelims GS-I, Question 11**

> A Parliamentary System of Government is one in which
> (a) all political parties in the Parliament are represented in the Government
> (b) the Government is responsible to the Parliament and can be removed by it
> (c) the Government is elected by the people and can be removed by them
> (d) the Government is chosen by the Parliament but cannot be removed by it before completion of a fixed term

**Demand:** isolate the defining institutional test rather than an incidental feature.
**Approach:** compare responsibility, removability, popular election and fixed tenure as separate institutional tests.

### Revision notes

1. The executive–legislature relationship classifies parliamentary and presidential systems.
2. Parliamentary government means a ministry responsible to the legislature.
3. The Union structure rests primarily on Articles 74 and 75.
4. The State structure rests primarily on Articles 163 and 164.
5. Collective responsibility is owed to the Lok Sabha at the Union level.
6. Cabinet, responsible and Westminster government are related descriptions.
7. Fusion of powers does not mean absence of constitutional checks.
8. A flexible political tenure is the price of continuing responsibility.
9. Majority must ordinarily be established on the floor of the House.
10. India has parliamentary government but not British-style parliamentary sovereignty.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the existence of elections insufficient to prove that a country has a parliamentary system?

**Model answer:** Elections determine democratic legitimacy, but the parliamentary classification depends on the executive’s relationship with the legislature. The decisive feature is that the ministry remains collectively responsible to the elected lower House and can be removed through loss of confidence. A separately elected fixed executive may also be democratic, but it is presidential rather than parliamentary.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not define parliamentary government merely as government “chosen by Parliament”; responsibility and removability are the controlling tests.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain why collective responsibility, rather than legislative membership alone, is the defining principle of parliamentary government.

**Model answer — 146 words:**
Legislative membership creates institutional fusion, but **collective responsibility** converts that fusion into democratic accountability. Under Article 75(3), the Union Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha: it must defend a common programme and resign if the House withdraws confidence. This makes executive authority continuously conditional rather than fixed for a guaranteed term.

Membership alone cannot perform this function. A minister may speak in either House under Article 88, and a non-member may temporarily hold ministerial office under Article 75(5). Neither fact by itself determines whether the government survives. The decisive mechanism is the confidence relationship with the popular House.

However, responsibility can weaken in practice when party discipline converts scrutiny into automatic support. Even then, the legal power of the Lok Sabha to remove the whole ministry remains the system’s load-bearing rule. Thus membership links the organs; collective responsibility makes the link accountable.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** definition and thesis 1; Article 75(3) mechanism 3; membership contrast 2; practical limitation 2; reasoned conclusion 2.

### Visual: One State, two executive roles

```text
FORMAL CONSTITUTIONAL HEAD
PRESIDENT / GOVERNOR
        │
        │ executive action expressed in the head's name
        ▼
REAL POLITICAL EXECUTIVE
PM / CM + COUNCIL OF MINISTERS
        │
        │ decides policy, administers and answers to lower House
        ▼
PARLIAMENTARY ACCOUNTABILITY

Union: President ← Article 74 advice ← PM-led Council
State: Governor  ← Article 163 advice ← CM-led Council
```

The division prevents the symbolic unity of the State from being confused with day-to-day political rule.

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Think of the nominal executive as the constitutional signature and the real executive as the political decision-maker. Government action is formally taken in the President’s name, but policy is ordinarily decided by the elected ministry.

This arrangement serves two purposes:

1. it gives the State a continuing constitutional head above routine party contest;
2. it locates governing responsibility in ministers who can be questioned and removed by the elected House.

### 2. Union design: Articles 53, 74 and 75

The President is the head of State. The Prime Minister is the head of government.

Article 74 provides:

- a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at its head;
- aid and advice to the President;
- after the Forty-second Amendment, binding ministerial advice;
- after the Forty-fourth Amendment, one opportunity for reconsideration, after which the reconsidered advice binds.

Article 74(2) bars judicial inquiry into **whether and what advice** was tendered. It does not immunise every resulting executive act from judicial review.

Article 75 then makes the ministry politically responsible. This matters because real power and answerability must remain in the same hands.

### 3. Is the President only a rubber stamp?

| Normal politics | Exceptional political margins |
|---|---|
| Acts on binding advice | May need to identify who can command majority support |
| Formalises Cabinet decisions | May seek a prompt floor test in a hung House |
| May return advice once | May examine dissolution advice where the ministry has lost confidence |

The President has no general personal policy discretion. Situational judgement arises when electoral arithmetic does not produce a clear ministry, not whenever the President disagrees with elected ministers.

### 4. State parallel and its qualification

At the State level:

- Governor = nominal head;
- Chief Minister and Council = real executive;
- Article 164(2) makes the Council collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly.

But Article 163 contains a discretion carve-out where the Constitution requires the Governor to act in discretion. This is narrower than a general licence to override the ministry.

In *Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974), the Supreme Court treated the President and Governor as constitutional heads who normally act on ministerial advice, subject to recognised exceptions.

### 5. Why the dual executive is not duplication

**Objection:** Two executive heads create ambiguity and waste.

**Reply:** They perform different constitutional functions. The head of State supplies continuity, form and a limited reserve role; the head of government supplies democratic direction and accountability.

**Residual risk:** Ambiguity becomes dangerous in hung legislatures, dismissal or dissolution disputes. The safeguard is objective support material and an early floor test, not personal assessment by the constitutional head.

### 6. Example and limit

If a party wins a clear Lok Sabha majority, the President has no meaningful political choice about whom to appoint Prime Minister.

If no party has a majority, the President may identify the claimant most likely to command confidence. But the appointment is provisional in a democratic sense: the House, not the President, supplies the final political verdict.

### UPSC application

- Use **de jure/titular** for the nominal executive and **de facto/real** for the PM-led ministry.
- Distinguish a constitutional head from a powerless head.
- Compare Article 74’s express binding framework with Article 163’s limited discretion.
- **Answer line:** *The head of State embodies constitutional continuity; the ministry exercises democratic power and bears democratic responsibility.*

### Revision notes

1. President is nominal executive; PM-led Council is real executive.
2. Formal Union executive power is vested in the President but exercised through the parliamentary structure.
3. The Forty-second Amendment made Article 74 advice binding.
4. The Forty-fourth Amendment permits one reconsideration.
5. Reconsidered advice binds the President.
6. Article 74(2) protects advice from judicial inquiry, not every executive act.
7. The President’s situational judgement is narrow and episodic.
8. Governor’s discretion is textually wider but constitutionally limited.
9. *Shamsher Singh* makes aid and advice the normal rule.
10. Floor confidence, not head-of-State preference, determines who governs.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the President be a nominal executive without being a constitutional nullity?

**Model answer:** The President ordinarily acts on binding ministerial advice and does not personally govern. Yet the office supplies constitutional continuity, may return advice once, and exercises bounded situational judgement in exceptional circumstances such as a hung Lok Sabha. These functions do not create an alternative political executive because majority support must ultimately be established in the House.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Nominal” does not mean optional, personally sovereign or completely powerless; it means that ordinary governing authority belongs to the responsible ministry.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** “The Indian President is a constitutional head, not an alternative centre of political power.” Examine.

**Model answer — 141 words:**
The Constitution separates the head of State from the head of government. Article 74 requires a Prime-Minister-headed Council to aid and advise the President; the Forty-second Amendment made that advice binding, while the Forty-fourth permits only one reconsideration. Political power therefore rests with ministers who are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3).

The President is nevertheless not a nullity. The office formalises executive action, receives information through Article 78 and may exercise bounded situational judgement when no Prime Ministerial claimant has an evident majority. Even then, discretion ends in an objective floor test rather than presidential preference.

The strongest criticism is that reserve discretion can invite partisan intervention. Constitutional convention and judicial review answer this by confining the President to facilitating, not manufacturing, a government. The office is therefore consequential at constitutional margins but ministerial in ordinary governance.

**Rubric — 10 marks:** constitutional position 2; Articles 74–75 mechanism 3; bounded discretion 2; criticism and safeguard 2; precise verdict 1.

### Visual: From election result to a legitimate ministry

```text
LOK SABHA RESULT
      │
      ├── Clear single-party majority
      │       └── Majority leader appointed PM
      │
      ├── Pre-poll coalition majority
      │       └── Coalition leader appointed PM
      │
      ├── No clear majority
      │       ├── support letters / alliance claim examined
      │       ├── claimant appointed
      │       └── prompt FLOOR TEST
      │
      └── No viable claimant
              ├── explore alternative government
              └── dissolution only after alternatives fail
```

The legitimacy of appointment comes from the Constitution; the legitimacy of continuance comes from the House.

### 1. Majority-party rule

The normal parliamentary pattern is simple:

1. a party wins a majority in the Lok Sabha;
2. its recognised leader becomes Prime Minister;
3. other ministers are appointed on the Prime Minister’s advice;
4. party strength sustains the ministry.

This creates **political homogeneity**: ministers usually share a programme and party discipline. Homogeneity makes collective responsibility workable because ministers can defend a common policy.

### 2. Coalition government

When no single party has a majority, several parties may combine. Coalition government changes the source of political unity:

| Single-party ministry | Coalition ministry |
|---|---|
| Common party manifesto | Negotiated common programme |
| One leadership hierarchy | Shared bargaining among allies |
| Stronger Cabinet discipline | Greater need for consultation |
| Easier policy coherence | Wider representation but possible veto points |

Coalitions do not suspend collective responsibility. The ministry still stands or falls together. What changes is the internal method by which solidarity is produced.

### 3. Minority government

A minority government lacks an independent majority but survives through:

- formal support from parties outside the ministry;
- issue-based support;
- abstentions that prevent an adverse majority;
- confidence-and-supply understandings.

The key distinction is:

```text
Minority in seats ≠ absence of confidence

Government survives if it can defeat a no-confidence motion
or otherwise demonstrate majority support among members voting.
```

Minority government can increase deliberation because the executive must negotiate. It can also create instability if external support is easily withdrawn.

### 4. The head of State’s role in a hung House

Article 75(1) says the Prime Minister is appointed by the President but gives no detailed selection formula. Convention fills the gap.

The President should identify the person most likely to command Lok Sabha confidence. Relevant material may include:

- pre-poll alliance arrangements;
- post-poll coalition agreements;
- written support;
- election of a legislative-party leader.

The President should not decide ideological desirability or long-term political stability. The appointment must be tested promptly on the floor.

### 5. State cases as constitutional guidance

The clearest judicial rules arose from State disputes:

- *S.R. Bommai* (1994): majority is ordinarily tested on the floor, not through the Governor’s subjective assessment.
- *Rameshwar Prasad* (2006): anticipated horse-trading cannot justify destroying a legislature before a floor test.
- *Subhash Desai* (2023): a floor test requires objective material indicating loss of confidence; intra-party dissent by itself is insufficient.

These cases directly govern Governors, but their deeper democratic principle is transferable: **the House determines political majority**.

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Coalition and minority governments permit small parties to exercise influence disproportionate to their seats.

**Reply:** Bargaining may be excessive, but coalition government also represents a fragmented electoral mandate more accurately and forces consultation. The relevant test is not whether bargaining occurs, but whether it remains transparent, programme-based and answerable to the House.

**Balanced residual:** A decisive majority improves coordination; a dispersed majority improves negotiation. Either can become unaccountable—one through domination, the other through opaque bargaining.

### UPSC application

- Distinguish appointment from continuance.
- Treat support letters as evidence for appointment, not a substitute for a floor test.
- Use coalition government to qualify the textbook claim of political homogeneity.
- **Answer line:** *In a hung House, the constitutional head opens the door, but only the elected House decides who may remain inside.*

### Revision notes

1. Clear majority normally eliminates head-of-State discretion.
2. Coalition government replaces single-party homogeneity with negotiated consensus.
3. Minority government can possess confidence without possessing a majority of all seats.
4. Article 75(1) does not prescribe a detailed appointment order.
5. Objective support material guides a hung-House appointment.
6. A prompt floor test supplies democratic validation.
7. *Bommai* privileges the floor over subjective assessment.
8. *Rameshwar Prasad* rejects dissolution based on anticipated misconduct.
9. *Subhash Desai* requires objective material for a floor test.
10. Intra-party dissent is not automatically loss of House confidence.
11. Coalition breadth may enhance representation but reduce policy coherence.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a minority government still be constitutionally legitimate?

**Model answer:** Parliamentary legitimacy depends on the ability to retain the confidence of the Lok Sabha, not on holding more than half of all seats independently. A minority ministry may survive through external support, abstentions or issue-based arrangements. Its legitimacy continues only while the House sustains it, and any serious doubt must be resolved through a floor test.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate “minority government” with an unconstitutional or automatically defeated government.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Analyse the constitutional principles that should guide the President while appointing a Prime Minister in a hung Lok Sabha.

**Model answer — 192 words:**
Article 75(1) empowers the President to appoint the Prime Minister but does not prescribe a mechanical order of preference. The controlling constitutional objective is to establish a ministry capable of commanding Lok Sabha confidence while keeping presidential judgement politically neutral.

First, a clear pre-poll alliance or demonstrable post-poll coalition should be assessed through objective material such as leadership decisions and written support. Second, the President should not decide which coalition is ideologically preferable or likely to last longer. Appointment is a facilitative act, not a personal electoral mandate. Third, where rival claims remain uncertain, the appointee must face a prompt floor test.

State-level jurisprudence clarifies the principle. *S.R. Bommai* made the floor the proper forum for determining majority. *Rameshwar Prasad* rejected dissolution based on anticipated horse-trading, while *Subhash Desai* held that a floor test requires objective material and cannot be triggered merely by intra-party dissent.

The objection that written support may be unstable is valid, but instability must be demonstrated in the House. Presidential discretion is therefore widest before appointment yet narrow in purpose: create a testable government, explore viable alternatives and avoid premature dissolution. The House supplies the final democratic verdict.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** Article 75 framework 2; objective appointment principles 4; floor-test case law 4; alternative-government/dissolution qualification 3; balanced conclusion 2.

## Lesson 4 — Collective Responsibility and Confidence

Book context: Queried — Article 75(3), confidence devices and Cabinet solidarity

CA found: None relevant for this static subtopic
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: The confidence chain

```text
LOK SABHA CONFIDENCE
        ↓
PM REMAINS IN OFFICE
        ↓
COUNCIL OF MINISTERS REMAINS IN OFFICE
        ↓
COMMON POLICY + COMMON DEFENCE
        ↓
ACCOUNTABILITY AS ONE GOVERNMENT

If confidence is lost:
NO-CONFIDENCE / DEFEAT ON AN ACCEPTED CONFIDENCE ISSUE
        ↓
RESIGNATION OF THE WHOLE MINISTRY
        OR
ADVICE FOR DISSOLUTION, SUBJECT TO CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS
```

Collective responsibility prevents ministers from claiming power individually while avoiding responsibility collectively.

### 1. Text and meaning

Article 75(3) states that the Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the House of the People.

This has three dimensions:

1. **Collective survival:** the ministry remains or leaves as one body.
2. **Collective policy ownership:** Cabinet decisions bind all ministers.
3. **Collective parliamentary defence:** every minister must support the government’s position inside and outside Parliament.

The rule applies to ministers from the Rajya Sabha as well. They are members of the same Council even though the Rajya Sabha cannot remove the ministry.

### 2. Confidence and no-confidence

A no-confidence motion asks one decisive question: does the Lok Sabha still support the Council?

It differs from criticism of one policy or one minister. If carried, it affects the whole ministry because the government’s authority is collective.

| Device | Central concern | Government survival directly at issue? |
|---|---|---|
| No-confidence motion | Whether the House supports the Council | Yes |
| Censure motion | Specific policy or conduct | Politically serious, but framed differently |
| Question Hour | Information and accountability | No |
| Committee scrutiny | Detailed examination | No |
| Budget/confidence defeat | Ability to govern and obtain supply | May become a confidence issue |

Detailed procedural rules belong to parliamentary procedure; the system-level lesson is that the confidence sanction makes the executive continuously removable.

### 3. “Swim and sink together”

The phrase captures joint political fate. It does not mean every minister personally participated in every decision. It means that the government presents one accountable position.

Collective responsibility would collapse if ministers could:

- claim credit for popular decisions;
- disclaim unpopular decisions;
- remain in office while publicly rejecting Cabinet policy.

Internal disagreement is permitted. Public repudiation while retaining office is not compatible with Cabinet solidarity.

### 4. Coalition qualification

Coalition partners may criticise one another, bargain over portfolios or negotiate policy in public. This weakens the convention’s political appearance.

But the legal core survives:

- a coalition ministry is still one Council;
- a confidence defeat removes the entire Council;
- a Rajya Sabha minister cannot remain merely because the Rajya Sabha supports them.

Thus coalitions dilute **uniformity of voice**, not the constitutional consequence of lost confidence.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Party whips make confidence automatic, turning responsibility into a fiction.

**Reply:** The whip does protect majority governments, but confidence remains politically meaningful when allies withdraw support, governing-party numbers change or elections alter the mandate. Moreover, responsibility extends beyond removal to questions, debates, finance and committees.

**Residual:** Formal removability is necessary but insufficient. Effective responsibility also requires time, information, opposition space and legislative independence.

### UPSC application

- Never write that the Council is collectively responsible to Parliament “equally”; the constitutional destination is the Lok Sabha.
- Distinguish responsibility from day-to-day liability in court.
- Use coalition practice as a qualification, not as evidence that Article 75(3) has disappeared.
- **Answer line:** *Collective responsibility converts many ministers into one answerable government and many departmental acts into one political fate.*

### Revision notes

1. Article 75(3) is the constitutional bedrock of Union parliamentary government.
2. Responsibility is to the Lok Sabha.
3. Rajya Sabha ministers fall with the entire Council.
4. No-confidence addresses the whole ministry.
5. Cabinet decisions bind dissenting ministers while they remain in office.
6. Internal debate is compatible with solidarity.
7. Public rejection requires resignation.
8. Coalition bargaining may weaken unity in practice.
9. Coalition defeat still removes the ministry collectively.
10. Whips strengthen survival but may weaken deliberative accountability.
11. Confidence is necessary; effective scrutiny requires additional institutions.

### Concept check

**Question:** Does public disagreement among coalition partners abolish collective responsibility?

**Model answer:** No. Public disagreement may weaken Cabinet solidarity as a political convention, but Article 75(3) still treats the Council as one body responsible to the Lok Sabha. If the government loses a confidence vote, every minister must leave, including ministers from coalition parties and the Rajya Sabha.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse imperfect political solidarity with disappearance of the constitutional rule.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Explain how collective responsibility operates both as a rule of government survival and as a rule of Cabinet conduct.

**Model answer — 146 words:**
Article 75(3) makes the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. As a rule of **survival**, it means that a no-confidence defeat removes the entire ministry, including ministers belonging to the Rajya Sabha. Executive tenure is therefore conditional on support in the popular House.

As a rule of **conduct**, collective responsibility requires ministers to defend Cabinet decisions publicly even when they disagreed during confidential deliberation. A minister unwilling to support the common position must resign; otherwise the House could not identify which government it was holding accountable.

Coalition politics qualifies this unity because allies may bargain or dissent openly. Yet the constitutional consequence remains collective: the coalition stands or falls as one Council. Party whips may protect the government and weaken individual scrutiny, but they do not erase the confidence relationship. Collective responsibility thus joins authority, solidarity and political liability in one institutional rule.

## Lesson 5 — Individual Responsibility, Solidarity and Secrecy

Progress: 5 / 12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Ministerial responsibility inside a collectively responsible government

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — responsibility of ministers, oath of secrecy and India–UK legal-responsibility distinction
CA search: Not required — static constitutional and conventional doctrine
CA found: None relevant for this static subtopic
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: Three meanings of ministerial responsibility

| Form | Constitutional or conventional basis | Who enforces it? | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collective political responsibility** | Article 75(3) | Lok Sabha | Whole Council leaves after loss of confidence |
| **Individual political responsibility** | Article 75(2) pleasure, operated through PM advice | Prime Minister/President formally | Particular minister resigns or is removed |
| **Legal responsibility** | Ordinary law and judicial review of action | Courts | Unlawful action may be invalidated; personal liability depends on law |

The table prevents a common error: these three ideas overlap, but they are not synonyms.

### 1. Individual responsibility

Article 75(2) says ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President. Under parliamentary government, that pleasure is not ordinarily personal presidential discretion. A minister is removed on the Prime Minister’s advice.

This gives the Prime Minister a disciplinary power:

```text
Ministerial failure / disagreement / loss of confidence
                    ↓
PM asks minister to resign
                    ↓
If refusal continues
                    ↓
PM advises formal removal
```

The power helps preserve collective responsibility. A government cannot present one position if a minister openly pursues another.

### 2. Cabinet solidarity

Cabinet solidarity has two sides:

- **freedom inside:** ministers may argue in confidential meetings;
- **unity outside:** after a decision, ministers defend the common position.

This is not a demand for intellectual conformity. It is a rule for identifiable public accountability. Parliament must know which policy the government owns.

Historical ministerial resignations illustrate the convention. B.R. Ambedkar resigned after differences surrounding the Hindu Code Bill; other ministers have resigned when they could not support government policy. The lesson is the constitutional convention—**dissent may continue, but office may not be used to oppose the Cabinet’s settled position**.

### 3. Why secrecy exists

The Third Schedule oath requires a Union minister not to communicate or reveal matters that come to them as minister except as required for due discharge of duties.

Secrecy performs legitimate functions:

1. it enables candid internal debate;
2. it protects sensitive security and diplomatic information;
3. it allows the Cabinet to reach one decision before public defence.

But secrecy is not a blanket licence to hide illegality, evade statutory disclosure or mislead Parliament.

### 4. India and the British idea of legal responsibility

The classic British convention required ministerial countersignature for official acts of the Crown, allowing responsibility to be attached to a minister while “the King can do no wrong.”

India does not reproduce that countersignature system. Official presidential action need not bear a minister’s countersignature, and Article 74(2) protects the content of advice from judicial inquiry.

This does **not** mean ministers are above law. Courts may review the resulting governmental action for legality, constitutional competence, mala fides or rights violations where applicable.

### 5. Departmental responsibility and its weakness

Ideally, a minister answers for:

- personal misconduct;
- policy choices;
- departmental administration;
- serious failures occurring under their authority.

In practice, resignations for departmental failure are uncommon. Administrative complexity and bureaucratic chains make personal attribution difficult, while party leadership often determines whether resignation occurs.

**Strongest objection:** individual responsibility has become a discretionary weapon of the Prime Minister rather than an independent accountability principle.

**Reply:** the Prime Minister’s disciplinary role is inherent in a coordinated ministry, but Parliament, courts, audit and public scrutiny must ensure that discipline does not become mere political convenience.

### UPSC application

- Article 75(3) = collective responsibility.
- Article 75(2) = pleasure and individual tenure.
- Oath of secrecy supports deliberation; it does not extinguish parliamentary accountability.
- India lacks the British countersignature model of ministerial legal responsibility.
- **Answer line:** *Secrecy protects deliberation; solidarity protects accountability; resignation protects the credibility of both.*

### Revision notes

1. Individual and collective responsibility must be separated.
2. The President’s pleasure over ministers is normally exercised on PM advice.
3. Individual removal can preserve collective unity.
4. Cabinet solidarity permits disagreement before decision.
5. Public repudiation of Cabinet policy is incompatible with continued office.
6. Ministers take oaths of office and secrecy.
7. Secrecy enables candour and protects sensitive information.
8. Secrecy does not legalise concealment of illegality.
9. India does not require British-style ministerial countersignature.
10. Article 74(2) bars inquiry into advice, not review of every resulting action.
11. Departmental resignation conventions are weakly enforced in practice.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Cabinet secrecy complement rather than contradict collective responsibility?

**Model answer:** Confidentiality permits ministers to disagree candidly before a decision. Once the Cabinet decides, solidarity requires a common public position so Parliament can hold one government accountable. Secrecy is therefore legitimate when it protects deliberation, but not when used to conceal illegality or defeat constitutional and statutory accountability.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat secrecy as freedom from parliamentary, judicial or legal control.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Distinguish collective, individual and legal responsibility of ministers in India. Why is the distinction important?

**Model answer — 199 words:**
Ministerial responsibility operates at three different levels. **Collective responsibility**, expressly stated in Article 75(3), makes the Council of Ministers answerable to the Lok Sabha as one body. Loss of confidence requires the entire ministry, including Rajya Sabha ministers, to leave.

**Individual responsibility** is associated with Article 75(2), under which ministers hold office during the President’s pleasure. In parliamentary practice, the Prime Minister is the effective enforcer: a minister may be asked to resign or be formally removed on Prime Ministerial advice for misconduct, failure or disagreement. This power supports Cabinet solidarity.

**Legal responsibility** is different. India does not follow the British countersignature convention under which a minister formally assumes legal responsibility for acts of the Crown. Article 74(2) also protects ministerial advice from judicial inquiry. However, governmental action remains reviewable for constitutional or statutory illegality, and a minister may incur liability where ordinary law provides.

The distinction matters because political defeat, individual dismissal and judicial invalidation have different institutions and consequences. Coalition dissent may weaken solidarity without ending collective responsibility; secrecy may protect discussion without insulating unlawful action. A precise answer therefore avoids converting every administrative failure into legal liability or every ministerial disagreement into loss of House confidence.

**Rubric — 15 marks:** three-part conceptual distinction 5; Articles 75(2), 75(3) and 74(2) 3; British contrast 2; enforcement and examples 3; analytical significance 2.

### Visual: The membership clock

```text
PERSON APPOINTED MINISTER
          │
          ├── Already MP/MLA/MLC → continues subject to other rules
          │
          └── Not a legislator
                    ↓
             SIX CONSECUTIVE MONTHS
                    ↓
        Becomes member of either relevant House?
             YES ──► may continue
             NO  ──► ceases to be minister

Speaking right: either House
Voting right: only the House of membership
```

The exception permits limited outside entry without allowing an indefinitely unelected political executive.

### 1. What “double membership” really means

In a parliamentary system, ministers belong to both:

- the executive, because they administer government;
- the legislature, because they are members or must become members.

The expression does not mean simultaneous membership of two legislative Houses. It means the same person participates in two organs.

### 2. The six-month exception

Article 75(5) provides that a minister who is not a member of either House of Parliament for six consecutive months ceases to be a minister.

At the State level, Article 164(4) provides the corresponding rule.

The rule balances:

| Need | Safeguard |
|---|---|
| Appoint expertise or a leader not currently in the House | Temporary six-month entry |
| Maintain democratic and parliamentary connection | Mandatory acquisition of membership |
| Permit membership through either House | No requirement that the PM must enter Lok Sabha |
| Prevent indefinite unelected ministership | Automatic cessation after the period |

### 3. Prime Minister from either House

The Indian Prime Minister may be a member of the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha. This differs from the British convention that the Prime Minister belongs to the House of Commons.

The distinction matters:

- collective responsibility remains to the Lok Sabha;
- an individual Prime Minister may nevertheless sit in the Rajya Sabha;
- responsibility is institutional, not dependent on the PM’s personal chamber.

### 4. Judicial clarification

In *S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda* (1996), the Supreme Court accepted that a person who was not then a member of either House could be appointed Prime Minister, subject to Article 75(5).

The case confirms that:

- prior membership is not an absolute appointment condition;
- the six-month rule applies to the Prime Minister as a minister;
- parliamentary legitimacy is restored by acquiring membership within the constitutional period.

### 5. Article 88: participation is wider than voting

A minister may:

- speak in either House;
- take part in proceedings of either House;
- participate in a joint sitting;
- participate in a committee of which the minister is a named member.

But a minister may vote only in the House of which they are a member.

This permits government business to be defended across both Houses while respecting the distinct representative membership of each.

### 6. What the exception does not permit

- It does not allow repeated six-month appointments to evade membership.
- It does not mean any voter is automatically qualified to be a minister.
- It does not override constitutional or statutory disqualifications.
- A member disqualified for defection is separately barred from ministership under Article 75(1B) for the prescribed period.

> (a) 1 only
> (b) 2 only
> (c) Both 1 and 2
> (d) Neither 1 nor 2

**Demand:** distinguish the six-month exception from eligibility and disqualification rules.
**Approach:** test each proposition independently; do not treat “eligible to vote,” “qualified for legislative membership” and “eligible for ministership” as identical categories.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The six-month exception weakens representative government by admitting unelected ministers.

**Reply:** It is time-bound and requires entry into the legislature. It can accommodate expertise, accidental electoral absence or leadership transitions without creating a permanently external executive.

**Residual concern:** use of the Rajya Sabha route for leaders repeatedly rejected in direct elections may be politically criticised, but the constitutional design expressly permits membership through either House.

### UPSC application

- Six months is a maximum temporary period, not an automatic guaranteed tenure.
- “Member of either House” includes Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha at the Union.
- A minister may speak in both Houses but vote only in their own.
- Keep Article 75(5) separate from the Tenth Schedule’s different six-month rule for nominated members.
- **Answer line:** *The six-month rule opens a temporary door to executive talent but closes it unless parliamentary membership follows.*

### Revision notes

1. Double membership means executive–legislative overlap.
2. Article 75(5) governs Union non-member ministers.
3. Article 164(4) governs State non-member ministers.
4. A non-member may be appointed but must acquire membership within six consecutive months.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can a Rajya Sabha Prime Minister remain collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha?

**Model answer:** Collective responsibility attaches to the Council of Ministers as an institution under Article 75(3), not to the Prime Minister’s personal chamber. A Rajya Sabha Prime Minister may speak in the Lok Sabha under Article 88, while the Council’s survival still depends on Lok Sabha confidence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that collective responsibility requires every minister, or even the Prime Minister, to be a Lok Sabha member.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** Does the six-month rule for non-member ministers weaken parliamentary democracy? Give a reasoned answer.

**Model answer — 150 words:**
Article 75(5) allows a person who is not a member of either House to serve as minister for no more than six consecutive months. The provision creates flexibility without abandoning parliamentary control. It permits the appointment of expertise, a leader temporarily outside Parliament or a Prime Minister awaiting election, while requiring legislative membership within a fixed period.

The Supreme Court in *S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda* accepted this arrangement for a non-member Prime Minister. Article 88 also permits ministers to participate in either House, though they may vote only in their own House.

The democratic objection is that an unelected person temporarily exercises executive power. The safeguard is the strict time limit and the ministry’s continuing collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha. The exception would become abusive if recycled to avoid membership or used despite disqualification. Properly applied, it is a transitional flexibility, not a substitute for representative legitimacy.

The Prime Minister is powerful not because one Article grants a complete catalogue of supremacy, but because several coordinating functions converge in one office.

### 1. Council of Ministers and Cabinet

| Council of Ministers | Cabinet |
|---|---|
| Wider constitutional ministry under Articles 74–75 | Smaller decision-making core |
| Includes the appointed ministerial ranks | Primarily Cabinet-rank ministers |
| Carries collective responsibility | Deliberates major policy and coordinates government |
| Actual size varies within Article 75(1A) | No permanently fixed constitutional size |

The word “Cabinet” entered the constitutional text expressly in Article 352(3) through the Forty-fourth Amendment for emergency advice. Cabinet government nevertheless existed through constitutional structure and convention before that textual insertion.

### 2. Why the Prime Minister leads

The Prime Minister:

- heads the Council and Cabinet;
- advises appointment and removal of ministers;
- allocates and reshuffles portfolios;
- chairs central decision processes;
- communicates decisions and information to the President under Article 78;
- leads the government’s parliamentary programme;
- normally leads the majority party or coalition;
- may advise Lok Sabha dissolution.

The Prime Minister is therefore the connecting point among party, Cabinet, Parliament, President and administration.

### 3. From *primus inter pares* to prime-ministerial government

*Primus inter pares* means “first among equals.” It captures collegial Cabinet government but may understate modern Prime Ministerial power.

Crossman and Mackintosh used **prime-ministerial government** to describe the migration of authority from the Cabinet towards the Prime Minister.

This is an analytical characterisation, not a constitutional category.

### 4. What produces concentration?

```text
PARTY LEADERSHIP
      +
PORTFOLIO CONTROL
      +
CONTROL OF CABINET AGENDA
      +
ARTICLE 78 CHANNEL TO PRESIDENT
      +
DISSOLUTION ADVICE
      ↓
PRIME-MINISTERIAL LEVERAGE
```

The degree of concentration varies:

- strongest under a cohesive single-party majority;
- moderated by coalition allies;
- constrained by federal politics, elections, courts and organised parliamentary scrutiny;
- weakened when the governing party itself is factional.

### 5. Cabinet government’s defence

**Objection:** Cabinet government becomes elective dictatorship when the same majority controls both executive and legislature.

**Reply:** The Cabinet remains collectively removable, ministers remain accountable, courts enforce constitutional limits and political authority must be renewed through elections. Committees, questions and financial control retain checking capacity.

**Residual:** These checks may be institutionally strong yet politically weak when party discipline is extreme. The danger is not fusion alone, but fusion without deliberation.

### 6. Cabinet committees, PMO and kitchen cabinet—bounded distinctions

- **Cabinet Committees:** extra-constitutional bodies arising through governmental business practice; composition changes.
- **Prime Minister’s Office:** non-constitutional, non-statutory staff institution supporting the Prime Minister.
- **Kitchen Cabinet:** informal circle of trusted advisers; it has no constitutional decision-making identity.

These bodies explain power concentration but must not be confused with the constitutional Council of Ministers.

### UPSC application

- Explain power concentration as a mechanism, not as a slogan.
- Pair each concentrating factor with a counter-institution.
- Avoid saying the PM is formally “leader of Parliament”; describe functional leadership precisely.
- **Answer line:** *The Constitution creates Cabinet government; political arithmetic determines how far it becomes prime-ministerial government.*

1. The Council is wider than the Cabinet.
2. Collective responsibility belongs to the Council.
3. Cabinet is the principal decision core.
4. Article 352(3) expressly defines Cabinet for emergency advice.
5. The PM controls portfolios and ministerial continuance.
6. Article 78 makes the PM the communication hinge with the President.
7. Dissolution advice increases PM leverage.
8. Prime-ministerial government is an analytical description.
9. Single-party majorities strengthen concentration.
10. Coalitions strengthen bargaining and collegiality.
11. PMO, Cabinet committees and kitchen cabinet are not constitutional substitutes for the Council.
12. Fusion becomes dangerous when legislative scrutiny is weak.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “prime-ministerial government” compatible with the continued legal existence of Cabinet government?

**Model answer:** Cabinet government remains the constitutional form because the Council collectively advises the President and is responsible to the Lok Sabha. “Prime-ministerial government” describes a practical concentration of leadership through party control, portfolios, agenda-setting, Article 78 and dissolution advice. It qualifies how Cabinet government operates; it does not replace its legal structure.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present prime-ministerial government as a separate constitutional system created by an amendment.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Has Cabinet government in India been transformed into Prime-Ministerial government? Critically examine.

**Model answer — 201 words:**
India remains constitutionally a Cabinet government: Articles 74 and 75 create a Prime-Minister-headed Council that advises the President and is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Yet political practice can concentrate authority in the Prime Minister.

The Prime Minister advises ministerial appointments and removals, allocates portfolios, controls Cabinet agenda, leads the governing party and communicates with the President under Article 78. The ability to recommend dissolution further strengthens the office. Under a cohesive majority, party discipline also allows the executive leadership to control legislative business. These mechanisms support Crossman and Mackintosh’s description of “prime-ministerial government.”

The transformation is not complete. Cabinet deliberation remains necessary for major decisions; Article 75(3) makes the ministry a joint body; coalition allies may veto personnel and policy; federal politics, elections and judicial review constrain central leadership. Parliamentary questions, committees and confidence retain an accountability floor.

The criticism that majority government becomes Cabinet dictatorship is therefore partly valid, especially where the whip and weak scrutiny turn Parliament into a ratifying chamber. However, concentration varies with political arithmetic rather than constitutional form. India has strong Prime-Ministerial leadership within Cabinet government, not a presidential executive. The remedy is stronger deliberation and legislative scrutiny, not denial of necessary executive coordination.

Dissolution is not the first response to every political crisis. Parliamentary flexibility includes both replacement without election and return to the electorate.

### 1. Dissolution as a feature

The Lok Sabha normally has a five-year term under Article 83(2), unless sooner dissolved. Under parliamentary practice, the President ordinarily dissolves it on ministerial advice.

The power distinguishes parliamentary from presidential government:

- the lower House’s term is flexible;
- the executive may seek a fresh mandate;
- the President of the United States cannot dissolve Congress.

### 2. Advice after loss of confidence

A ministry that still commands confidence may advise dissolution. A ministry that has clearly lost confidence cannot automatically compel dissolution if a viable alternative majority exists.

The constitutional sequence should be:

1. establish whether confidence is genuinely lost;
2. explore a credible alternative government;
3. dissolve only when no viable ministry can be formed or a fresh mandate is constitutionally appropriate.

This protects Parliament from being dissolved merely to serve the outgoing executive’s partisan convenience.

### 3. Continuity after dissolution

Article 74 requires a Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President. Government cannot disappear merely because the Lok Sabha has been dissolved.

In *U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi* (1971), the Supreme Court held that the Council does not automatically cease to exist after dissolution. The President cannot exercise executive power without constitutionally required ministerial aid and advice.

The case establishes **continuity**, not unlimited caretaker authority.

### 4. Caretaker government

The Constitution does not create a separately named “caretaker government.” It is a situational arrangement and constitutional convention.

A caretaker ministry may arise when:

- the Lok Sabha has been dissolved for a general election;
- a ministry has resigned or lost confidence but continues until a successor is available;
- election results have not yet produced a new ministry.

Its central duty is continuity with restraint.

| Normally appropriate | Normally avoided |
|---|---|
| Day-to-day administration | Major irreversible policy |
| Essential appointments where delay is harmful | Large patronage commitments |
| Urgent national-security action | Measures binding the successor without necessity |
| Election cooperation and neutral administration | Use of State resources for partisan advantage |

These restraints are conventional, not a complete constitutional code. Urgency and national interest may justify necessary action.

### 5. Simultaneous elections: a live constitutional proposal

The **Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill, 2024** was introduced in the Lok Sabha on **17 December 2024**. It proposes Article 82A and related term-alignment changes for simultaneous elections.

The proposal is relevant because it would alter the ordinary flexibility of parliamentary tenure:

- Assemblies constituted after an appointed date would be aligned with the Lok Sabha’s term;
- a House elected after premature dissolution would serve only the remaining or “unexpired” portion contemplated by the Bill’s associated amendments;
- confidence could still be lost, but the electoral consequence would be fitted into a synchronised cycle.

**Status verified on 30 September 2026:** the Bill remains with the Joint Committee chaired by P.P. Chaudhary. On 29 July 2026, the Lok Sabha extended the Committee’s reporting time to the first day of the last week of the Winter Session 2026. No report was located in the official reports listings during this verification.

> **Status caution:** The Bill is a proposal. Article 82A is **not** operative constitutional text, and no implementation year should be asserted.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection to flexible dissolution:** repeated elections reduce continuity and encourage opportunistic collapse.

**Reply:** flexibility permits an unworkable ministry to be replaced or the electorate to resolve a deadlock. Stability can also be improved through conventions, coalition agreements or a constructive vote of no confidence without eliminating responsibility.

**Objection to synchronisation:** fixed alignment may privilege electoral convenience over a House’s independent democratic mandate.

**Reply:** supporters emphasise reduced election frequency and administrative disruption. The unresolved constitutional question is how to preserve genuine confidence responsibility when terms are artificially aligned.

### UPSC application

- Distinguish dissolution of the House from resignation of the ministry.
- A dissolved House does not leave the President without a Council.
- Caretaker restraint is convention, not an express constitutional chapter.
- Alternative-government exploration precedes partisan dissolution.
- **Answer line:** *Parliamentary tenure is flexible, but constitutional continuity is unbroken: the House may dissolve, the State cannot stop governing.*

4. Loss of confidence should ordinarily be established on the floor.
5. A viable alternative ministry should be explored.
6. *U.N.R. Rao* confirms ministerial continuity after dissolution.

8. Caretakers manage continuity and avoid unnecessary irreversible policy.
9. Urgent national interest may require action.
10. The 129th Amendment Bill is only a proposal.

12. Synchronisation raises a responsibility-versus-stability design question.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does dissolution of the Lok Sabha not terminate the Council of Ministers automatically?

**Model answer:** Article 74 requires a Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President, so executive government must continue despite dissolution. *U.N.R. Rao* confirms this continuity. The outgoing Council may function in a caretaker capacity, but convention restrains major avoidable decisions until a successor assumes office.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not infer that absence of a sitting Lok Sabha authorises personal presidential government.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Examine the constitutional and conventional principles governing dissolution and caretaker government in India.

**Model answer — 210 words:**
Article 83(2) gives the Lok Sabha a normal five-year term unless sooner dissolved. Dissolution is a feature of parliamentary government because executive tenure and lower-House tenure are politically flexible. The President ordinarily acts on ministerial advice, but a ministry that has lost confidence should not secure dissolution merely to prevent a viable alternative from forming.

The first principle is therefore **floor determination** of disputed majority. The second is exploration of an alternative ministry capable of commanding confidence. Dissolution and a fresh election become appropriate when no credible alternative exists or a new mandate is otherwise necessary.

Executive continuity survives dissolution. In *U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi*, the Supreme Court held that the Council of Ministers does not automatically cease because Article 74 requires ministerial aid and advice. The outgoing Council may consequently continue as a caretaker.

Caretaker government is not separately codified. Convention requires routine administration, electoral neutrality and avoidance of major irreversible commitments, while permitting urgent action in national interest. This restraint protects the successor’s democratic freedom without paralysing the State.

The central tension is between stability and responsibility. Flexible dissolution can create election frequency, but rigid tenure can preserve an executive that no longer commands confidence. Constitutional practice must therefore favour floor tests, viable alternatives and restrained continuity.

### Visual: The choice the framers confronted

```text
                    DEMOCRATIC EXECUTIVE
                           │
             ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
             ▼                           ▼
      MORE RESPONSIBILITY          MORE FIXED STABILITY
```

The framers did not believe the parliamentary system was cost-free. They chose its trade-off knowingly.

### 1. Familiarity

The Constitution-makers had experience with legislative responsibility under late colonial constitutional development, especially the Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935.

K.M. Munshi’s argument was practical: after developing parliamentary traditions, India should not “buy a novel experience.”

Familiarity reduced transition risk, but it was not mere colonial imitation. India changed the model through republicanism, federalism, Fundamental Rights, judicial review and constitutional supremacy.

### 2. Responsibility over stability

B.R. Ambedkar framed the central choice:

- the American system offered greater stability but less continuing responsibility;
- the British system offered greater responsibility but less guaranteed stability;
- the Draft Constitution preferred responsibility.

This is the most important interpretive key. When critics complain that a parliamentary ministry can fall, they identify a cost the framers consciously accepted.

### 3. Avoiding executive–legislative deadlock

In a presidential system, legislature and executive possess separate democratic mandates and fixed tenure. This may produce strong checks, but it may also create sustained deadlock.

India’s framers feared that a new democracy confronting Partition, integration, poverty and institution-building could not afford continuous conflict between two independently mandated branches.

Fusion was intended to make coordinated government possible.

### 4. Representation in a plural society

A Cabinet can include:

- different regions;
- social groups;
- coalition parties;
- linguistic and community interests;
- members of either House.

This does not guarantee social inclusion, but it gives the political executive a group form capable of broader representation than a single-person presidency.

### 5. Formal reconsideration after Independence

The Swaran Singh Committee in 1975 considered the parliamentary-versus-presidential debate and supported continuation of the parliamentary model.

The recommendation is evidence that the question was formally reconsidered. It does not prove that every feature of parliamentary practice is satisfactory.

### 6. Merits

| Merit | Mechanism | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Harmony | Executive sits in and leads legislature | May become executive domination |
| Responsible government | Questions, motions, finance and confidence | Party discipline may blunt use |
| Check on personal rule | Executive power distributed through a Council | Strong PM may recentralise power |
| Ready alternative | New ministry may form without immediate election | Alternative may lack durability |
| Wide representation | Group executive accommodates diversity | Representation can become patronage |

Jennings’s description of the opposition leader as the “alternative Prime Minister” captures replaceability without constitutional rupture.

### 7. Demerits

#### Instability

Short-lived Union governments associated with Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, Chandra Shekhar, H.D. Deve Gowda and I.K. Gujral demonstrate the vulnerability of fragmented mandates.

#### Policy discontinuity

Uncertain tenure may shorten planning horizons or cause reversal after changes of government.

#### Cabinet dictatorship

Laski and Ramsay Muir warned of Cabinet domination. A disciplined majority can allow the executive to control the House that is meant to scrutinise it.

#### Fusion against strict separation

Bagehot described the Cabinet as the “hyphen” joining executive and legislature. The same fusion that produces coordination may weaken institutional checking.

#### Government by generalists

Ministers are political representatives, not necessarily technical specialists. Yet democratic government requires policy judgement and accountability, while professional civil services and expert advice supply specialised capacity.

### 8. Has coalition experience disproved the instability charge?

Stable coalition and single-party governments after 1999 show that parliamentary government is not inherently short-lived. Stability depends on:

- party-system structure;
- coalition agreements;
- anti-defection discipline;
- leadership;
- electoral arithmetic.

**Strongest objection:** stability achieved through strict whips may hollow out the very responsibility the system values.

**Reply:** Narrowing whips to confidence and financial survival questions could protect stability while restoring ordinary legislative deliberation.

### UPSC application

- Use merits and demerits as linked trade-offs, not two unrelated lists.
- Explain why the same feature—fusion—produces both harmony and domination.
- Treat instability as contingent on party structure, not an inevitable constitutional result.
- **Answer line:** *India chose the risk of a removable government over the risk of an unanswerable one.*

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is instability not by itself proof that India made the wrong constitutional choice?

**Model answer:** The framers knowingly preferred continuing responsibility to guaranteed executive tenure. Parliamentary instability is therefore a possible cost of removability, not an unforeseen defect. Its severity also depends on party fragmentation and coalition practice; stable governments demonstrate that the Constitution permits stability without guaranteeing it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not answer the system-choice debate by treating stability as the only democratic value.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** “India chose responsibility over stability, but political practice has often delivered neither.” Critically examine.

**Model answer — 212 words:**
Ambedkar explained that the presidential model offered greater stability with less continuing responsibility, whereas the parliamentary model offered greater responsibility with less fixed stability. India chose the latter because of institutional familiarity, the need to avoid executive–legislative deadlock and the representative capacity of a group executive.

The design has delivered important responsibility. Governments answer questions, require legislative supply and may be removed through loss of Lok Sabha confidence. Coalition transitions have occurred without constitutional breakdown, and stable governments after 1999 qualify the claim that the system is inherently unstable.

Yet practice may weaken both values. Fragmented coalitions can produce short-lived ministries and policy discontinuity. Conversely, a cohesive majority combined with the whip and anti-defection law may secure stability by reducing Parliament to ratification. Cabinet control of time, ordinances, money-bill routing and weak committee referral can make formal responsibility ineffective.

The strongest case for reform therefore lies between system replacement and complacency. Confidence votes should preserve government survival, while ordinary legislative questions should permit greater dissent. Committee scrutiny, predictable sitting calendars and internal party democracy can deepen responsibility without manufacturing instability.

India’s constitutional choice remains defensible because removability suits a plural democracy. The failure lies not in preferring responsibility, but in allowing coalition opacity at one extreme and majority domination at the other.

### Visual: Three ways to organise democratic executive power

| Test | Parliamentary: India | Presidential: USA | Semi-presidential: France |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive form | Dual: nominal President + real ministry | Single elected President | Dual: elected President + PM |
| Selection | Government emerges from legislature | President elected separately | President elected; PM appointed |
| Confidence | Ministry depends on lower House | Executive independent of congressional confidence | Government responsible to National Assembly |
| Tenure | Politically flexible | Fixed terms | Fixed President; variable government/Assembly relationship |
| Membership | Ministers are/become legislators | Secretaries cannot sit in Congress | Institutional combination varies |
| Power relation | Fusion with responsibility | Separation with checks and balances | Balance shifts with legislative majority |
| Main gain | Accountability and replaceability | Stability and decisional independence | Dual legitimacy and flexibility |
| Main risk | Cabinet domination or instability | Deadlock or personalised executive | Rival executive legitimacy |

The differences matter because institutional incentives change. A comparison is incomplete unless it explains the consequence of each design.

### 1. Parliamentary model

Its logic is:

```text
legislative majority
      ↓
government formation
      ↓
fusion and coordination
      ↓
continuous confidence responsibility
```

Political tenure is flexible. A government may change without the constitutional system changing.

### 2. Presidential model

The United States President is both head of State and head of government. The President:

- is chosen separately through the electoral process;
- serves a fixed four-year term;
- cannot dissolve the House of Representatives;
- appoints secretaries who are not members of Congress;
- is not removable through an ordinary political no-confidence vote;
- is subject to impeachment and conviction for constitutionally serious grounds.

Separation is not isolation. Vetoes, legislative approval, appropriations, oversight, judicial review and impeachment create checks and balances.

### 3. Semi-presidential model

France combines:

- a directly elected President;
- a Prime Minister and government responsible to the National Assembly.

When the President and legislative majority align, the President is generally dominant. During **cohabitation**, the Prime Minister and parliamentary majority gain greater control over domestic government.

Semi-presidentialism therefore does not mean two executives with permanently equal power.

### 4. Stability with responsibility: Germany’s constructive vote

Germany permits removal of the Chancellor only when the Bundestag simultaneously elects a successor. This **constructive vote of no confidence**:

- preserves legislative responsibility;
- prevents purely destructive majorities;
- reduces caretaker uncertainty;
- offers a possible reform idea, not an Indian constitutional rule.

### 5. Indian PM versus US President

| Dimension | Indian Prime Minister | US President |
|---|---|---|
| Formal position | Head of government within dual executive | Head of State and government |
| Democratic base | Parliamentary majority/coalition | Separate national electoral mandate |
| Legislature relation | Sits in or participates in Parliament | Institutionally separate from Congress |
| Survival | Depends on Lok Sabha confidence | Fixed term, exceptional impeachment route |
| Legislative influence | Often controls agenda through majority | Must bargain across separated institutions |
| Dissolution | May advise Lok Sabha dissolution | Cannot dissolve Congress |
| Party effect | Majority may fuse branches strongly | Separation persists even under unified party control |

The Prime Minister may exercise greater day-to-day legislative control when backed by a disciplined majority. The US President possesses a stronger independent constitutional mandate but may face greater institutional blockage.

### 6. Strongest objections and replies

**Against parliamentary government:** instability and Cabinet dictatorship.

**Reply:** it supplies continuing responsibility, easy replacement and representative government; instability is contingent, while domination can be corrected through legislative reform.

**Against presidential government:** deadlock and personalisation.

**Reply:** fixed tenure and separation may protect institutional independence and long-term policy.

**Qualified verdict for India:** Pluralism, federal pressures and the need for continuous legislative responsibility favour the parliamentary model. Borrowing specific stability devices is safer than replacing the entire executive design.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can the Indian Prime Minister be politically stronger in the legislature than the US President while remaining constitutionally less independent?

**Model answer:** A disciplined parliamentary majority may let the Prime Minister control legislative business and party voting, producing strong practical influence. Yet the ministry’s survival still depends on Lok Sabha confidence. ### Original Mains practice

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Compare parliamentary and presidential government as alternative methods of reconciling democratic authority, stability and accountability. Which design better suits India?

**Model answer — 217 words:**
Parliamentary and presidential systems organise the same democratic problem differently: how to empower an executive while preventing unaccountable rule.

In parliamentary government, the nominal and real executives are separated, ministers emerge from the legislature and the ministry survives only while it commands lower-House confidence. Fusion promotes coordination, replaceability and continuing responsibility. Its costs are possible instability and Cabinet domination when a disciplined majority weakens scrutiny.

In presidential government, one President heads State and government, possesses a separate mandate and serves a fixed term. Separation protects decisional independence and institutional checking, but can create executive–legislative deadlock. Secretaries remain outside Congress, and ordinary political disagreement cannot remove the President.

France demonstrates a semi-presidential alternative, though cohabitation may produce rivalry between President and Prime Minister. Germany’s constructive vote of no confidence shows that stability devices can be introduced without abandoning parliamentary responsibility.

India’s Constituent Assembly preferred responsibility over stability. Familiarity, plural representation and avoidance of dual-mandate conflict supported that choice. Experience reveals both coalition instability and majority-driven Cabinet dominance. Yet India’s federal and social diversity still favours a group executive answerable to Parliament.

The better response is institutional repair: stronger committees, freer ordinary legislative voting, disciplined confidence procedures and possible constructive no-confidence. India should improve its parliamentary model rather than exchange continuing accountability for a fixed and personalised executive mandate.

### 1. India–United Kingdom distinctions

| Dimension | India | United Kingdom |
|---|---|---|
| Head of State | Elected President; republic | Hereditary monarch |
| Higher constitutional authority | Written supreme Constitution | Parliamentary sovereignty within an uncodified constitution |

Federalism is separated into its own row for teaching clarity. In Laxmikanth’s six-point textbook list, it forms part of the wider explanation of why India rejects British parliamentary sovereignty.

### 2. Parliamentary sovereignty versus constitutional supremacy

British parliamentary sovereignty broadly means courts do not ordinarily invalidate a Westminster Act of Parliament.

Indian Parliament is limited by:

- the written Constitution;
- Fundamental Rights;
- federal distribution of legislative power;
- judicial review;
- the basic-structure limitation on amendment power.

Therefore:

```text
India has parliamentary GOVERNMENT
             but
India does not have British parliamentary SOVEREIGNTY
```

A question on the “marginalisation of parliamentary supremacy” in India may refer to Parliament’s control over the executive, not legal omnipotence. The context must be identified.

### 3. State parliamentary government

India applies parliamentary government at both levels:

| Union | State |
|---|---|
| President | Governor |
| Prime Minister | Chief Minister |
| Articles 74–75 | Articles 163–164 |
| Responsibility to Lok Sabha | Responsibility to Legislative Assembly |
| Article 78 communication | Article 167 communication |
| Union ministry cap: 15% of Lok Sabha | State cap: 15% of Assembly, minimum 12 |

The State model is not a perfect photocopy. The Governor possesses constitutionally recognised discretionary fields that the President does not possess in the same textual form.

### 4. Judicial control of State confidence

State politics has generated the strongest floor-test doctrine:

- *Shamsher Singh*: advice is the norm for a constitutional Governor.
- *Bommai*: majority should be tested on the floor.
- *Rameshwar Prasad*: apprehended horse-trading cannot justify premature dissolution.
- *Subhash Desai*: objective material is required; intra-party dissent alone is inadequate.

These cases protect the federal adaptation of parliamentary government from partisan manipulation by the appointed head of State.

### 5. Conventions travel imperfectly

Some British conventions did not take deep root in India:

- “once a Speaker, always a Speaker” is not established practice;
- the British shadow Cabinet has no equivalent formal place;
- party defection required a constitutional Tenth Schedule response;
- written Articles and judicial review replace some reliance on unwritten understandings.

**Strongest objection:** constitutionalising conventions may reduce useful flexibility.

**Reply:** India’s social scale, federal complexity and party fragmentation required more written safeguards. Yet excessive legalisation cannot replace political restraint.

Its weakness is that written safeguards cannot ensure neutral conventions: Governors, Speakers and party whips may still distort operation. India therefore constitutionalised Westminster government without eliminating the need for Westminster-style restraint.

### Visual: The paradox of disciplined majority government

```text
PARTY MAJORITY
      +
WHIP / TENTH SCHEDULE
      +
EXECUTIVE CONTROL OF BUSINESS
      ↓
STABLE GOVERNMENT AND POLICY COORDINATION
      ↓
BUT ALSO
      ↓
WEAKER BACKBENCH DISSENT
LESS EFFECTIVE SCRUTINY
CABINET / PM DOMINANCE

CORRECTIVES:
questions + committees + finance + no-confidence
+ opposition + courts + elections + freer ordinary votes
```

The same discipline that allows a ministry to govern may prevent legislators from holding it meaningfully accountable.

### 1. Conventions complete the constitutional text

The Constitution states broad rules but does not fully codify:

- whom to invite in a hung House;
- which defeat counts as a confidence defeat;
- caretaker restraint;
- Cabinet solidarity;
- when a minister should resign for departmental failure;
- political neutrality expected from constitutional heads.

Conventions work because political actors accept restraint. Courts may police constitutional boundaries, but they cannot manufacture every convention.

### 2. Anti-defection at the parliamentary-system boundary

The Tenth Schedule was inserted by the Fifty-second Amendment in 1985. A party member may be disqualified for voluntarily giving up membership or defying a valid party direction in the specified manner.

For this topic, the relevant mechanism is narrow:

```text
CONFIDENCE DEPENDS ON PARTY NUMBERS
            ↓
WHIP DISCIPLINES LEGISLATORS
            ↓
GOVERNMENT STABILITY INCREASES
            ↓
INDIVIDUAL LEGISLATIVE AUTONOMY FALLS
```

The complete grounds, exceptions and adjudication process belong to the anti-defection topic. Here the concern is the effect on responsible government.

### 3. Why the whip changes collective responsibility

Collective responsibility assumes Parliament can hold the ministry accountable. A broad whip creates a reverse pressure: the ministry, through party leadership, controls how its legislative supporters vote.

This produces:

- reliable confidence;
- coherent legislative programme;
- reduced opportunistic individual defection;
- weaker policy dissent;
- reduced backbench capacity to scrutinise ministers.

The Dinesh Goswami Committee and Law Commission reform tradition favoured confining disqualification-producing whips to high-stakes votes such as confidence or financial survival. These remain reform proposals, not enacted law.

### 4. Cabinet dominance over Parliament

The mechanism extends beyond the whip:

| Executive advantage | Effect on Parliament |
|---|---|
| Majority controls business and time | Government determines legislative priorities |
| Guillotine on demands for grants | Expenditure may pass without individual discussion |
| Delegated legislation | Details move outside plenary enactment |
| Ordinance power under Article 123 | Temporary law precedes legislative debate |
| Money-Bill routing under Article 110 | Rajya Sabha role may be curtailed |
| PM-centred party discipline | Cabinet and backbench independence may decline |

This is why Laski and Ramsay Muir warned of “Cabinet dictatorship,” while Bagehot’s “hyphen” illustrates fusion.

### 5. Why parliamentary supremacy is not extinguished

Cabinet dominance is a claim about practical scrutiny, not legal sovereignty.

Correctives remain:

- Article 75(3) and no-confidence;
- questions and debates;
- financial approval and cut motions;
- Departmentally-Related Standing Committees;
- CAG-supported Public Accounts Committee scrutiny;
- judicial review of constitutional limits;
- elections and opposition mobilisation.

The problem is often underuse, not complete absence, of instruments.

### 6. Cases at the boundary

- *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992): the Speaker acts as a reviewable tribunal in defection matters.
- *Keisham Meghachandra* (2020): decisions should ordinarily be made within about three months; an independent tribunal was suggested.
- *Subhash Desai* (2023): the party’s authorised whip, not a self-created legislative faction’s whip, controls; disqualification and symbol proceedings perform distinct functions.

These cases should not be expanded into a complete anti-defection answer here. Their system-level significance is that partisan delay or factional control can determine government survival.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Without a broad whip, governments would face constant blackmail and unstable voting.

**Reply:** Confidence, appropriation and core survival votes can remain disciplined while ordinary Bills permit greater legislative judgement. Responsibility requires both a governable executive and legislators capable of scrutiny.

**Residual:** Whip reform alone will not solve executive dominance. Sitting time, committee referral, information quality, party democracy and presiding-officer neutrality also matter.

6. The Ninety-first Amendment separately capped ministry size and barred defectors from office.
7. Executive control of business contributes to Cabinet dominance.

14. Cabinet dominance weakens scrutiny, not constitutional supremacy.
15. Whip reform must be paired with wider parliamentary reform.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the anti-defection law create a paradox for parliamentary responsibility?

**Model answer:** Parliamentary government needs party cohesion so the ministry can retain confidence and implement a programme. The anti-defection law strengthens that cohesion through the whip. But if the whip extends broadly, legislators lose freedom to scrutinise the same ministry they are constitutionally expected to hold accountable. Stability rises while deliberative responsibility may fall.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not argue that every whip is undemocratic or that confidence can function without any party discipline.

### Original Mains practice

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Analyse how party discipline, anti-defection and executive control of parliamentary business have altered the working of responsible government in India.

**Model answer — 211 words:**
Responsible government assumes that the ministry leads Parliament while remaining answerable to it. Party discipline is necessary to sustain confidence and implement an electoral programme, but its modern form can reverse the accountability relationship.

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the Fifty-second Amendment, discourages legislators from abandoning their party or defying its direction. It has reduced opportunistic individual defection and stabilised ministries. The Ninety-first Amendment further barred disqualified defectors from ministership and capped ministry size.

However, a broad whip subordinates the legislator’s independent judgement to party leadership. Combined with executive control of parliamentary time, the guillotine on grants, delegated legislation, ordinances and disputed Money-Bill routing, this may convert scrutiny into ratification. Prime-Ministerial control of portfolios and party organisation intensifies the trend.

The Constitution retains correctives: Article 75(3), no-confidence, questions, financial approval, committees, CAG-PAC scrutiny, judicial review and elections. *Kihoto Hollohan* subjects the Speaker’s decision to review; *Keisham Meghachandra* addresses delay; *Subhash Desai* protects the political party’s authorised whip while limiting factional manipulation.

The answer is not undisciplined voting. Whips should principally protect confidence and financial survival, while ordinary legislation permits greater debate. Mandatory committee scrutiny, adequate sitting days and neutral presiding officers are equally necessary. Responsible government requires a ministry stable enough to govern and a legislature free enough to question it.

**Rubric — 20 marks:** responsible-government baseline 2; discipline and stability gains 4; anti-defection/executive-dominance mechanisms 5; cases and correctives 4; reform design 3; qualified conclusion 2.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Architecture

**Question:** A ministry commands a large Lok Sabha majority and therefore cannot realistically be defeated. Has collective responsibility become constitutionally irrelevant?

**Model answer:** No. Article 75(3) continues to make the ministry collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, and its legal survival remains conditional on confidence. A large majority may weaken the practical likelihood of removal and blunt scrutiny, but it does not convert the executive into a fixed or constitutionally independent authority. The correct criticism concerns effectiveness, not disappearance, of responsibility.

**Misconception to avoid:** Political improbability of defeat is not legal immunity from confidence.

## Check 2 — Formation and confidence

**Question:** Can the President reject the leader of the largest party and appoint another claimant after a hung election?

**Model answer:** The Constitution does not prescribe an automatic largest-party rule. The President must identify the person most likely to command Lok Sabha confidence using objective support material. Any uncertain appointment should be validated promptly through a floor test. The President may facilitate a viable coalition but cannot select a government on personal ideological preference.

**Misconception to avoid:** “Largest party” and “demonstrable majority coalition” are not always the same claimant.

## Check 3 — Responsibility and discipline

**Question:** Why would narrowing the whip not necessarily destroy government stability?

**Model answer:** Government survival can remain protected by binding party directions on confidence and essential financial votes. Allowing greater freedom on ordinary legislation would restore deliberation without permitting legislators casually to bring down the ministry. Stability requires discipline on survival questions, not automatic obedience on every policy detail.

**Misconception to avoid:** The only alternatives are not total party control and complete voting anarchy.

## Check 4 — Comparative synthesis

**Question:** Which system is “stronger”—one where the executive controls the legislative majority or one where the executive has a fixed independent term?

**Model answer:** Strength depends on the axis. A parliamentary Prime Minister with a disciplined majority may possess greater legislative capacity but remains removable through confidence. A presidential executive has greater constitutional independence and fixed tenure but may face legislative deadlock. Comparative analysis must separate agenda control, tenure security, democratic mandate and checking institutions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Executive strength is not a single measurable quality.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question

**Question — 10 marks, 150 words:** “Cabinet secrecy is justified only when accompanied by Cabinet accountability.” Explain.

**Model answer — 136 words:**
Cabinet secrecy permits candid internal discussion, protects sensitive information and allows ministers to test competing options before adopting a common policy. The Third Schedule oath therefore supports effective collective decision-making.

Secrecy is constitutionally defensible, however, only because the resulting government remains accountable. Article 75(3) makes the Council collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha; ministers must defend the final decision, answer questions, obtain legislative finance and resign after loss of confidence. Article 74(2) protects the content of advice from judicial inquiry but does not immunise every resulting executive act from review.

The danger arises when confidentiality becomes concealment—shielding illegality, misleading Parliament or avoiding statutory disclosure. Cabinet solidarity also cannot excuse personal misconduct.

Thus secrecy protects the process of deliberation, not freedom from constitutional control. Its democratic legitimacy depends on identifiable public responsibility for the policy eventually adopted.

**Why this earns marks:** It links oath, deliberative purpose, Article 75(3), Article 74(2), limits and a direct verdict.

**Quantified rubric — 10:** concept and thesis 2; constitutional evidence 3; accountability mechanism 2; misuse limitation 2; conclusion 1.

## Final 15-mark question

**Question — 15 marks, 250 words:** Coalition and minority governments reveal both the representative strength and the structural fragility of parliamentary government. Analyse.

**Model answer — 204 words:**
Parliamentary government translates legislative support into executive authority. Where no party commands a majority, coalition and minority ministries allow a fragmented electoral verdict to produce government without abandoning lower-House confidence.

Their representative strength lies in accommodation. Coalition Cabinets include regional parties and diverse interests, while negotiated programmes restrain unilateral rule. Minority governments must bargain across party lines and may increase legislative deliberation. They demonstrate Jennings’s idea that an alternative government can emerge without constitutional rupture.

The same arrangements create fragility. External support may be withdrawn, small allies may acquire disproportionate bargaining power and public disagreements may weaken Cabinet solidarity. Short-lived ministries headed by Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, Chandra Shekhar, H.D. Deve Gowda and I.K. Gujral illustrate the risk. Caretaker periods and repeated elections may disrupt continuity.

Yet instability is not inevitable. Full-term coalitions after 1999 show that agreements, leadership and predictable confidence rules can produce durability. Anti-defection discipline also protects survival, though an overbroad whip reduces legislator autonomy.

The appropriate response is not compulsory majoritarianism. Transparent coalition agreements, prompt floor tests, constructive no-confidence and whips confined mainly to survival votes can reconcile representation with continuity. Coalition government is fragile when bargaining is opaque, but constitutionally valuable when negotiation converts pluralism into an accountable majority.

**Why this earns marks:** It analyses both sides with named evidence, mechanism, counter-evidence, reform and a graded conclusion.

**Quantified rubric — 15:** representative mechanisms 3; fragility mechanisms 3; named historical evidence 3; counter-evidence and qualification 2; reforms 2; conclusion 2.

## Final 20-mark question

**Question — 20 marks, 250 words:** Is the crisis of Indian parliamentary government a defect of constitutional design or a failure of political practice? Evaluate.

**Model answer — 239 words:**
India’s constitutional design deliberately combines executive–legislative fusion with continuing responsibility. Articles 74–75 create a real ministry responsible to the Lok Sabha, while confidence, financial control and legislative scrutiny make authority removable. The design’s recognised costs are flexible tenure and the possibility of Cabinet dominance.

Several contemporary weaknesses arise mainly from political practice. Broad party whips and the Tenth Schedule reduce backbench judgement. Executive control of parliamentary time, the guillotine, ordinances, delegated legislation and disputed Money-Bill use may replace scrutiny with ratification. Weak committee referral, disruption and poor enforcement of individual ministerial responsibility further hollow out accountability. Coalition opacity at one extreme and majority centralisation at the other aggravate the problem.

Design nevertheless contributes to the incentives. Fusion allows a cohesive majority to dominate the checking institution; the Speaker’s partisan location complicates defection adjudication; flexible dissolution can encourage tactical elections. Hence practice cannot be separated completely from structure.

The constitutional reply already exists in part: no-confidence, judicial review, federal politics, elections, questions, CAG-PAC scrutiny and Departmentally-Related Standing Committees. *Bommai*, *Kihoto Hollohan*, *Keisham Meghachandra* and *Subhash Desai* also police the boundaries of confidence and party control.

Reform should preserve responsible government while correcting incentives: constructive no-confidence, whips limited to survival votes, mandatory committee scrutiny, adequate sitting days, neutral presiding officers and stronger party democracy.

The crisis is therefore primarily a failure of political practice amplified by structural incentives—not proof that India should abandon a system chosen for accountability and plural representation.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates design from operation, connects mechanisms to consequences, uses cases and institutions, offers proportionate reform and reaches a qualified verdict.

**Quantified rubric — 20:** constitutional design 3; practice failures 5; structural incentives 3; checks and cases 3; reform package 4; evaluated verdict 2.

# REMEDIATION

## Common error 1 — Government versus sovereignty

**Faulty move:** “India follows parliamentary sovereignty because it has parliamentary government.”

**Repair:** India follows parliamentary government under constitutional supremacy. British parliamentary sovereignty concerns the legal authority of Westminster, not merely confidence government.

## Common error 2 — Responsibility to both Houses

**Faulty move:** “The Council is collectively responsible to Parliament.”

**Repair:** In broad political language ministers answer both Houses, but Article 75(3) makes collective responsibility and government survival specific to the Lok Sabha.

## Common error 3 — Non-member cannot be minister

**Faulty move:** “Only an existing MP may be appointed minister.”

**Repair:** Article 75(5) permits temporary non-membership for six consecutive months, subject to legislative qualification and other disqualifications.

## Common error 4 — President personally governs

**Faulty move:** “Because executive power is vested in the President, policy discretion belongs personally to the President.”

**Repair:** Articles 74–75 locate ordinary governing authority in the responsible ministry; the President’s situational judgement is narrow.

## Common error 5 — Coalition abolishes responsibility

**Faulty move:** “Public bargaining means coalition ministers are not collectively responsible.”

**Repair:** Solidarity may weaken politically, but confidence defeat still removes the entire Council.

## Common error 6 — Dissolution ends government

**Faulty move:** “After Lok Sabha dissolution, the President governs until elections.”

**Repair:** *U.N.R. Rao* confirms continued ministerial aid and advice. The outgoing Council functions with caretaker restraint.

## Common error 7 — Every floor test is democratic

**Faulty move:** “A floor test can always be ordered whenever political instability is alleged.”

**Repair:** *Subhash Desai* requires objective material indicating loss of confidence; intra-party dissent alone is insufficient.

## Common error 8 — Anti-defection is wholly beneficial or wholly harmful

**Faulty move:** One-sided claims.

**Repair:** It stabilises ministries and limits opportunistic defection, but broad whip enforcement weakens deliberation and intra-party democracy.

## Common error 9 — US President always more powerful

**Faulty move:** Compare titles rather than dimensions.

**Repair:** The US President has greater tenure independence; an Indian PM with a disciplined majority may have greater day-to-day legislative capacity.

## Common error 10 — Proposal treated as law

**Faulty move:** Cite proposed Article 82A as operative constitutional text.

**Repair:** The 129th Amendment Bill remains a proposal on the verified official record; no implementation year is safe to assert.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Core identity and constitutional spine

- Parliamentary government classifies the executive by its **responsibility to the legislature**.
- Union: Articles **74–75**; State: Articles **163–164**.
- Also called responsible, Cabinet or Westminster government.
- India has parliamentary **government**, not British parliamentary **sovereignty**.
- Eight features: nominal/real executive; majority rule; collective responsibility; political homogeneity; executive–legislative membership link; PM leadership; dissolution; secrecy.

## Nominal and real executive

- President/Governor = constitutional or nominal head.
- PM/CM-led Council = real political executive.
- Forty-second Amendment made Article 74 advice binding.
- Forty-fourth Amendment permits one reconsideration; reconsidered advice binds.
- Article 74(2) protects the content of advice from judicial inquiry.
- *Shamsher Singh* makes advice the ordinary rule for constitutional heads.
- Situational judgement is narrow: hung House, demonstrated loss of confidence and dissolution questions.

## Government formation

- Clear majority leaves no meaningful choice.
- Coalition replaces party homogeneity with negotiated consensus.
- Minority government may survive through outside support or abstention.
- Appointment legitimacy comes from the Constitution; continuation legitimacy comes from the House.
- *Bommai*: floor test is the proper majority test.
- *Rameshwar Prasad*: anticipated horse-trading cannot justify premature dissolution.
- *Subhash Desai*: objective material required; intra-party dissent alone is insufficient.

## Responsibility

- Article 75(3): collective responsibility to Lok Sabha.
- Whole Council stands or falls together, including Rajya Sabha ministers.
- No-confidence tests the survival of the ministry.
- Cabinet decisions bind ministers publicly.
- Article 75(2): individual tenure during President’s pleasure, ordinarily operated on PM advice.
- India lacks British-style ministerial countersignature.
- Legal review of resulting executive action remains possible.

## Secrecy and solidarity

- Oath of secrecy protects candid deliberation and sensitive information.
- Secrecy is not authority to conceal illegality or mislead Parliament.
- Internal disagreement is permissible.
- Public rejection of settled Cabinet policy requires resignation.
- Collective responsibility needs solidarity because Parliament must identify one accountable government.

## Minister–legislator relation

- Article 75(5): non-member minister must become member of either House within six consecutive months.
- Article 164(4): State parallel.
- *S.P. Anand*: non-member PM permissible subject to the six-month rule.
- Indian PM may belong to either House.
- Article 88: minister may speak in either House but vote only in the House of membership.
- Voter eligibility, legislative qualification and ministerial eligibility are distinct.
- Defection disqualification creates a separate ministerial bar.

## PM, Council and Cabinet

- Council = wider constitutional ministry.
- Cabinet = smaller decision core.
- Cabinet expressly appears in Article 352(3) after the Forty-fourth Amendment.
- PM controls portfolios, ministerial continuance, agenda and Article 78 communication.
- PM normally leads majority party/coalition and may advise dissolution.
- *Primus inter pares* describes collegial leadership.
- “Prime-ministerial government” describes political concentration, not a new constitutional system.
- PMO, Cabinet Committees and kitchen cabinet are not constitutional substitutes for the Council.

## Dissolution and caretaker government

- Lok Sabha normal term: five years unless sooner dissolved.
- A ministry that loses confidence may resign; viable alternatives should be explored.
- *U.N.R. Rao*: Council continues after dissolution because Article 74 is mandatory.
- Caretaker government is a convention and functional necessity.
- Caretaker principles: continuity, electoral neutrality and restraint on avoidable irreversible policy.
- Urgent national interest may justify action.
- Proposed Article 82A remains non-operative because the 129th Amendment Bill is not enacted.

## Why India chose the model

- Familiarity with parliamentary responsibility.
- K.M. Munshi: avoid buying a novel experience.
- Ambedkar: responsibility preferred over fixed stability.
- Avoid executive–legislative deadlock in a new democracy.
- Group Cabinet better accommodates plural representation.
- Swaran Singh Committee supported retaining the model.

## Merits and demerits

### Merits

- Harmony and coordination.
- Continuing responsible government.
- Group executive checks personal despotism.
- Ready alternative government.
- Wide representation.

### Demerits

- Possible instability and policy discontinuity.
- Cabinet dictatorship under disciplined majority.
- Fusion weakens strict separation.
- Generalist political ministers may lack technical expertise.
- Strong PM may eclipse Cabinet collegiality.

### Balanced line

- Stable governments after 1999 show instability is contingent.
- A strong majority improves coordination but may weaken scrutiny.
- Coalition breadth improves representation but may create veto points.

## Comparative essentials

### Parliamentary

- Dual executive.
- Flexible tenure.
- Lower-House confidence.
- Executive–legislative membership link.
- Dissolution possible.
- Fusion with accountability.

### Presidential

- Single political executive.
- Fixed term.
- No ordinary legislative confidence removal.
- Secretaries outside Congress.
- No presidential dissolution of Congress.
- Separation with checks and balances.

### Semi-presidential

- Elected President plus responsible Prime Minister/government.
- French cohabitation shifts domestic authority towards parliamentary majority.

### Germany

- Constructive no-confidence removes Chancellor only by electing successor.
- Reform idea, not current Indian law.

## India–UK distinctions

- Republic versus monarchy.
- Constitutional supremacy versus parliamentary sovereignty.
- Federal Constitution versus legally unitary devolution.
- Indian PM may belong to either House; UK PM by convention belongs to Commons.
- Temporary non-member minister permitted in India.
- No British countersignature system in India.
- No institutionalised British-style shadow Cabinet equivalent.

## State application

- Governor nominal; CM-led Council real.
- Collective responsibility to Legislative Assembly.
- Article 163 discretion is limited, not general.
- State ministry cap: 15% of Assembly, minimum 12.
- Article 167 parallels Article 78.
- Floor-test jurisprudence protects elected government against subjective gubernatorial assessment.

## Anti-defection interface

- Tenth Schedule inserted by Fifty-second Amendment, 1985.
- Ninety-first Amendment, 2003 tightened the system, capped ministries and barred defectors from office.
- Whip stabilises confidence but may suppress ordinary legislative judgement.
- *Kihoto*: Speaker is a reviewable tribunal.
- *Keisham*: ordinary three-month decision norm; independent tribunal suggested.
- *Subhash Desai*: authorised political-party whip controls; factional numbers alone do not.
- Whip-limiting and tribunal proposals are not enacted law.

## Cabinet dominance answer spine

```text
Fusion under Articles 74–75
→ disciplined majority and whip
→ government control of business
→ delegated legislation / ordinance / guillotine / Money-Bill route
→ weaker scrutiny
→ qualification: confidence, questions, committees, finance, courts and elections
→ verdict: scrutiny marginalised, constitutional supremacy not extinguished
```

## Rapid traps

- Collective responsibility: Lok Sabha, not Rajya Sabha.
- Non-member minister: permitted temporarily, not prohibited absolutely.
- Indian PM: either House, not necessarily Lok Sabha.
- Article 74: advice; Article 75: ministers and responsibility.
- Fifty-second Amendment created anti-defection; Ninety-first tightened it.
- Parliamentary system means fusion, not rigid separation.
- Caretaker restraint is convention.
- Floor test requires a genuine, objectively supported confidence doubt.
- Proposed Article 82A is not current constitutional law.
- UK Constitution is uncodified, not literally unwritten.
