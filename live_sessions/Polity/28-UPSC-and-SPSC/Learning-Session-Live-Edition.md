### The problem before the doctrine

Imagine a new government with no constitutional recruitment rule at all. Every appointment —
clerk, engineer, magistrate — is made at the pleasure of whichever minister or officer controls
the post. Who gets hired depends on family connection, party loyalty, caste network or personal
favour, not on who can actually do the job. Two things go wrong very quickly: capable people are
shut out, and the state's own machinery stops working for the public and starts working for
whoever controls appointments.

A written Constitution has to answer a hard design question: **how do you keep recruitment
honest without creating a second, unelected government that runs personnel decisions forever?**
India's answer was not to abolish executive control over hiring. It was to insert a permanent,
tenured, advisory institution between the executive and the appointment — a body strong enough to
resist patronage, but answerable enough that elected government still owns administration.

### Visual 1 — Why advice, not command

```text
PATRONAGE MODEL                    CONSTITUTIONAL MODEL
Minister decides alone      ->     Minister/Government decides
       |                                    |
No independent check                Commission examines/advises first
       |                                    |
Loyalty beats merit                 Merit-based recommendation
       |                                    |
No public accountability            Non-acceptance must be reported (Art 323)
```

*Caption: the Constitution inserts an insulated advisory step into the recruitment chain; it does
not remove the executive from the chain altogether.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of the Public Service Commission as a referee who checks eligibility and merit before a
match, not as the team owner who picks who plays. The referee cannot be sacked by either team
captain on a whim (tenure protection), must apply the same rulebook to everyone (equality), and
must explain in public if a captain overrides a recommendation (the reporting duty). That is the
entire logic of **insulated advice**: strong enough to say no to patronage, accountable enough
that the public can see when the executive disagrees with it.

### Technical doctrine: constitutional rationale

✅ Fact / canonical doctrine: a modern state needs permanent officials selected through criteria
that do not merely reproduce patronage, family connection, party loyalty or personal favour.
✅ Fact / canonical doctrine: the constitutional design is **neutrality without executive
paralysis** — the Commission must be strong enough to resist patronage, but not so dominant that
elected government loses responsibility for administration.
⚠️ Analytical inference: this is why the Commission's core function (Article 320) is phrased as
**advice and consultation**, not command — the Constitution chose insulated advice, not an
unelected personnel government.

### Full doctrine: the evolutionary argument

The 1950 design was not invented from nothing. It is the end point of roughly a century of
institutional learning about what goes wrong without it:

```text
Company patronage (pre-1853)
        |
Charter Act, 1853 — the competitive-recruitment turn
        |
Macaulay Committee, 1854 — competitive civil-service framework
        |
Government of India Act, 1919 — statutory provision for a Public Service Commission
        |
Lee Commission, 1924 — institutional recommendation
        |
1 October 1926 — first Public Service Commission begins work
        |
Government of India Act, 1935 — Federal + Provincial + Joint PSC design
        |
26 January 1950 — Articles 315-323 constitutionalise the UPSC/SPSC architecture
```

✅ Fact: each stage solved a specific failure of the previous one. The 1853-54 reforms answered
pure patronage with competitive examination, but examination alone does not guarantee
institutional independence from the executive that administers it. The 1919 Act's statutory
Commission and the 1935 Act's federal design answered that by giving the Commission its own
constitutional/statutory footing, separate from the department whose vacancies it filled. By 1950,
the framers converted a colonial administrative mechanism into a constitutionally entrenched,
federally distributed, publicly accountable institution.

⚠️ Analytical inference: the constitutional innovation of 1950 was not "inventing an exam body." A
Public Service Commission already existed from 1926. The innovation was **entrenchment** —
removing the Commission's tenure, composition and reporting duty from ordinary legislative or
executive control and fixing them in the Constitution itself (Articles 315-323), so that no future
government could quietly weaken the institution by ordinary law.

### Limit of the example

Competitive recruitment under colonial rule did not itself create social equality — access and
representation remained restricted under colonial administration. Do not present 1853-1926 as a
golden age of merit; present it as the technical ancestor of today's examination method, which the
post-1950 constitutional order then had to combine with equality and reservation law (a separate
owner — see the boundary note below).

### Technical doctrine: Part XIV master map

✅ Fact: Articles 315-323 sit inside **Part XIV, Chapter II (Public Service Commissions)**, which
is itself part of the wider Part XIV architecture governing Services under the Union and the
States.

| Article | Subject | Owner |

| 308-309 | definition of "the State" for this Part; recruitment/service-condition law | Public Services (separate canonical owner) |
| 310 | doctrine of pleasure | Public Services |
| 311 | dismissal/removal safeguards for civil servants | Public Services |
| 312 | All-India Services | Public Services |
| **315** | **UPSC, SPSC and Joint Commission** | **this topic** |
| **316** | **appointment, composition and term** | **this topic** |
| **317** | **removal and suspension** | **this topic** |
| **318** | **member/staff service-condition regulations** | **this topic** |
| **319** | **post-tenure office restrictions** | **this topic** |
| **320** | **functions — examinations, joint schemes, consultation** | **this topic** |
| **321-323** | **extension, charged expenditure, reports** | **this topic** |

> **Prelims rule:** Articles 315-323 are the Commission chapter. Do not mix them with Article 323A
> tribunals merely because the numbering is adjacent — a Commission *recommends appointments*; a
> tribunal *adjudicates service disputes*. They do not perform the same function, and conflating
> them is a standard distractor design (see Lesson 9).

### Comparative treatment: why this is not duplicated elsewhere

`basic/Public-Services.md` governs Articles 308-314 (service-condition law, the pleasure doctrine,
dismissal safeguards and the All-India Services) and explicitly hands Articles 315-323 to this
topic — there is no overlap, because Public Services asks *on what terms does a civil servant
hold office*, while this topic asks *who certifies that a person deserves to be recruited or
promoted in the first place, and who guards that certifying body's own independence*.
`basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md` separately classifies UPSC/SPSC as
**constitutional bodies**, in the same row as the Election Commission, CAG and the Finance
Commission, and distinct from statutory bodies such as the CVC or CBI — the difference matters
because a constitutional body's existence, composition and removal protection cannot be altered by
ordinary legislation, while a statutory body's can.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** if the Commission is merely advisory (Article 320 consultation is directory, not
binding — you will meet *Manbodhan Lal Srivastava* properly in Lesson 8), what did entrenchment
actually buy? A government that is determined to ignore merit can simply not consult, or consult
and then reject the advice.

**Reply:** entrenchment does not operate through a legal veto; it operates through three harder-to-
evade mechanisms taught across this topic — (i) the Commission's own tenure and removal protection
cannot be altered by ordinary law (Lessons 3-5), so the advisors themselves cannot be easily
replaced for giving inconvenient advice; (ii) non-acceptance of advice must be reported to the
legislature with reasons (Article 323 — Lesson 8), converting a private executive departure into a
matter of public and legislative record; and (iii) the underlying recruitment process remains
subject to judicial review for arbitrariness and bias even where the Commission's advice was not
binding (Lesson 10). The qualified, honest verdict is that the 1950 design trades a legal veto for
durable independence plus transparency-forced accountability — a real but incomplete check, not a
toothless one.

### Revision notes

1. A Public Service Commission is an **insulated advisory** institution, not a personnel government.
2. Constitutional design goal: neutrality without executive paralysis.
3. Evolution: 1853 Charter Act -> 1854 Macaulay Committee -> 1919 Act -> 1924 Lee Commission -> 1
   October 1926 first PSC -> 1935 Act Federal/Provincial/Joint design -> 1950 Articles 315-323.
4. 1950's innovation was **entrenchment**, not invention — a Commission already existed from 1926.
5. Entrenchment means tenure, composition and reporting are fixed in the Constitution, not ordinary
   law.
6. Articles 315-323 sit in Part XIV, Chapter II, distinct from 308-314 (Public Services) and 323A
   (Tribunals).
7. Competitive examination under colonial rule solved patronage but not equality of access.
8. UPSC/SPSC are classified as constitutional bodies, alongside ECI, CAG and the Finance Commission.
9. The Commission's power is advisory; its durability comes from tenure protection and the Article
   323 reporting duty, not from a legal veto.
10. Judicial review (Lesson 10) is the third leg of accountability alongside tenure and reporting.

### Concept check

**Question:** A friend says, "Since the Commission only advises and the government can reject its
advice, constitutionalising it in 1950 achieved nothing beyond what the 1926 statutory Commission
already did." Is this correct?

**Model answer:** No. The *function* (examine and advise on recruitment) is similar to 1926, but
the *durability* of the institution changed completely. A 1926 statutory body's composition, tenure
and powers could be altered by ordinary legislation at any time. After 1950, Articles 315-323 fix
the Commission's existence, appointment process, tenure protection, removal procedure and reporting
duty in the Constitution itself, so no ordinary law can weaken them. The 1950 change is about
**institutional insulation**, not about making the advice legally binding.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not equate "advisory, non-binding function" with "constitutionally
unimportant." Entrenchment protects the *advisor's independence*, not the *bindingness of the
advice* — these are two different protections, and this topic repeatedly tests whether you can
keep them separate.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (10 marks):** "The constitutional entrenchment of the Public Service
Commission in 1950 was about institutional insulation, not about making its advice binding."
Examine. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Mains model answer:** The Constitution (Arts 315-323) did not alter the Commission's core 1926
function — examining candidates and advising on recruitment, promotion, discipline and related
matters (Art 320). What changed was the institution's durability. Tenure (Art 316), a differentiated
removal process (Art 317), service-condition protection (Art 318) and post-tenure restrictions
(Art 319) place the Commission's independence beyond ordinary legislative or executive alteration.
Article 323 converts any executive departure from its advice into a reported, legislature-facing
fact rather than a silent decision. Yet Article 320 consultation remains directory, not mandatory
(*Manbodhan Lal Srivastava*), so government retains final authority over appointments. The
entrenchment therefore secures an independent advisor, not a binding adviser — insulated advice
operating alongside, not above, elected executive responsibility for administration.

**Quantified rubric (10 marks):** Correct articulation of "insulation vs bindingness" distinction
(3); naming at least three of Arts 316-319 with their protective function (3); accurate statement
that Art 320 advice remains directory (2); coherent 150-word structure with a stated verdict (2).

## Lesson 2/12 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Three Commissions, One Article

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The problem before the doctrine

Article 315 sounds like it creates exactly one institution per government level. In fact, it
quietly authorises **three different institutional shapes**, and the Constitution gives you two
more cooperative devices elsewhere that are easy to mistake for a fourth and fifth shape. If you
cannot keep these five apart — one Article, three institutions, two more look-alike devices — you
will lose easy Prelims marks to a well-built distractor and write a confused Mains paragraph.

### Visual 2 — One Article, three institutional forms

```text
ARTICLE 315
    |
    |-- 315(1): a Commission "for the Union" and "for each State"  -> UPSC, SPSC
    |
    |-- 315(2): two or more States agree on ONE Commission          -> Joint State PSC
    |              (State legislatures resolve -> Parliament legislates)
    |
    |-- 315(4): UPSC agrees, on a State's request, to serve that State -> UPSC-serves-a-State
                   (Governor requests + President approves + UPSC agrees)
```

*Caption: three forms, three different trigger mechanisms, one shared Article.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of three ways a small town could get policing. It can run its own police force (SPSC — a
State runs its own Commission). Two neighbouring small towns can agree to jointly fund and run one
shared police force covering both (Joint State PSC — Article 315(2)). Or a small town, instead of
building its own force, can simply ask the larger regional police force to cover its area too
(UPSC-serves-a-State — Article 315(4)). All three solve the same underlying need — someone must
examine candidates and advise on State recruitment — but the institutional route, and therefore the
legal process to set it up, is completely different each time.

### Technical doctrine

✅ Fact: **Article 315(1)** requires a Public Service Commission for the Union and one for each
State, subject to the rest of the Article — this is the ordinary case: UPSC for the Union, one
SPSC per State.

✅ Fact: **Article 315(2)** allows two or more States to agree on a single Commission. Each
relevant State legislature must pass the required resolution; **Parliament may then by law provide
for the appointment** of a Joint State Public Service Commission. This is the **Joint State PSC**.

✅ Fact: **Article 315(4)** lets the UPSC, on a request by a State's Governor and with the
President's approval, agree to serve all or any of that State's needs. This is **UPSC serving a
State** — a different device from a Joint Commission.

⚠️ Analytical inference: a Joint Commission is constitutionally **contemplated** by Article 315(2)
but is **activated through a parliamentary statute**. Calling it "purely statutory" is incomplete
(it would suggest an extra-constitutional body, which it is not); calling it "automatically
constitutional" without any legislative step is equally incomplete. The correct formulation is:
constitutional source, statutory creating instrument.

### Visual 3 — Constitutional source versus creating instrument

```text
CONSTITUTION, ARTICLE 315(2)
authorises a Joint State PSC
          |
State legislatures pass the required resolutions
          |
PARLIAMENT passes a law providing for its appointment
          |
Joint State Public Service Commission begins to function
```

### A third device that is NOT a new Commission: Article 320(2)

✅ Fact: if requested by two or more States, the **UPSC must assist them in framing and operating
joint recruitment schemes** for services requiring candidates with special qualifications. This is
Article 320(2) — it is a **function**, not an institution. No new Commission is created; the UPSC
simply helps design and run a shared recruitment scheme for specialist posts.

### Comparative treatment: three easily confused cooperative devices

| Device | Constitutional trigger | What is created | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joint State PSC — Art 315(2) | State legislative resolutions + parliamentary law | one new **Joint Commission** | serves a defined group of States |
| UPSC serves a State — Art 315(4) | Governor's request + President's approval + UPSC's agreement | nothing new — **existing UPSC** takes on the work | UPSC covers all/some of that State's Commission needs |
| Joint recruitment scheme — Art 320(2) | request by two or more States | nothing new — a **scheme**, run with UPSC assistance | shared scheme for specially-qualified posts, not a Commission |

⚠️ Analytical inference: the examiner-favourite distractor is to describe Article 320(2) as
"creating a Joint Commission," or to describe Article 315(4) as "a Joint Commission in which the
UPSC and the State share membership." Neither is correct: 320(2) creates a scheme, not a body;
315(4) simply re-assigns the UPSC's workload without altering its own identity or membership.

### Visual 4 — Article 315(2) versus Article 320(2), side by side

| Test | Art 315(2) | Art 320(2) |
|---|---|---|
| Object | a common **Commission** | a common **recruitment scheme** |
| Trigger | legislative resolutions + parliamentary law | requests by two or more States |
| Institution created? | yes — Joint Commission | no — UPSC merely assists |
| Special qualifications required? | not a textual condition | expressly relevant — "special qualifications" |

### Full institutional comparison

| Dimension | UPSC | SPSC | Joint State PSC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutional basis | Art 315(1) | Art 315(1) | Art 315(2) |
| Operational creation | Constitution | Constitution | parliamentary law after State resolutions |
| Chair/members appointed by | President | Governor | President |
| Removal authority | President | President | President |
| Suspension after SC reference | President | Governor | President |
| Age ceiling | 65 | 62 | 62 |
| Annual report goes to | President | Governor | Governor of each served State, for that State |
| Charged on | Consolidated Fund of India | State Consolidated Fund | law/arrangement read with its serving design |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** if a Joint Commission needs a parliamentary law to exist, isn't it really a
**statutory body** dressed up in constitutional language, no different from the CVC or CBI?

**Reply:** no — the *authorisation* to create it, the *category of body* it becomes once created,
its members' appointment by the President, their removal exclusively through Article 317, and
their tenure/service-condition protection under Articles 316-319 are all fixed directly by the
Constitution, not by the activating statute. The statute only triggers the Commission into
existence and may fix administrative detail; it cannot alter the constitutional rules governing
removal, tenure or post-tenure restriction the way it could for a genuinely statutory body like the
CVC. This is why `basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md` still places even the
Joint Commission in the constitutional-body category, not the statutory-body category.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, "Constitutional Bodies" — the three-form architecture is the most
  commonly tested structural fact about this topic.
- **Verified PYQ linkage through 2026:** none found routed here in the audited ledgers (see final
  PYQ section).
- **Probable framing:** a Prelims statement-based question listing three or four claims about
  "Joint Commission," "UPSC serving a State" and "joint recruitment scheme" and asking which are
  correct — exactly the kind of fact-matching the comparison tables above are built to defeat.
- **Common trap:** assuming "Joint" in "Joint State PSC" and "joint recruitment scheme" refers to
  the same constitutional mechanism because both use the English word "joint."

### Revision notes

1. Article 315(1) is the ordinary rule: one Commission for the Union, one for each State.
2. Article 315(2) authorises a Joint State PSC for two or more States.
3. A Joint Commission needs State legislative resolutions **and** a parliamentary law to exist.
4. Article 315(4) lets UPSC serve a State's needs on Governor's request + President's approval —
   no new body is created.
5. Article 320(2) is a function (joint recruitment scheme assistance), not an institution.
6. Article 320(2) schemes are specifically for posts needing **special qualifications**.
7. President appoints UPSC and Joint Commission members; Governor appoints SPSC members.
8. President removes members of all three forms — appointment power is not removal power.
9. Age ceiling: 65 for UPSC, 62 for SPSC and Joint Commission members.
10. UPSC and Joint Commission expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India; SPSC expenses
    on the State Consolidated Fund.
11. A Joint Commission remains a constitutional body despite needing a statute to come into being.
12. Do not confuse "Joint State PSC" (an institution) with "joint recruitment scheme" (a function).

### Concept check

**Question:** Two States, X and Y, want to share recruitment machinery for a small cadre of
forensic chemists who are hard to find separately in either State. Which Article 315/320 device
fits, and why would the "special qualifications" language matter here?

**Model answer:** This fits **Article 320(2)** — a joint recruitment scheme, not a Joint State PSC
under Article 315(2). The posts (forensic chemists) are a classic case of candidates requiring
special qualifications that neither State's own SPSC may attract efficiently alone. The UPSC would
assist X and Y in designing and operating a shared recruitment scheme for that cadre; this does not
require either State's legislature to pass a resolution creating a new Commission, and no new body
comes into existence — only a shared scheme, run with UPSC's help.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not reach for "Joint State PSC" every time two States cooperate on
recruitment. Article 315(2) is for replacing two separate Commissions with one shared Commission;
Article 320(2) is for a shared scheme for a specific specialised cadre while each State keeps its
own SPSC.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (10 marks):** Distinguish a Joint State Public Service Commission under Article
315(2) from a joint recruitment scheme under Article 320(2). (Answer in 150 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Both Articles 315(2) and 320(2) allow States to cooperate on recruitment, but
they create different things. Article 315(2) authorises an entirely new institution — a Joint State
Public Service Commission — triggered when the legislatures of two or more States pass resolutions
and Parliament then enacts a law providing for its appointment; it replaces the separate SPSCs for
its member States on matters within its remit. Article 320(2), by contrast, creates no new body: if
two or more States request it, the UPSC must assist them in framing and operating a joint
recruitment scheme for services requiring candidates with special qualifications, while each
State's own Commission continues to exist. The practical test is institutional: did a new Commission
come into being (315(2)), or did the existing UPSC simply lend assistance to a shared scheme while
each State's Commission remains unchanged (320(2))?

**Quantified rubric (10 marks):** Correctly identifies Art 315(2) trigger — legislative resolutions
plus parliamentary law (2); correctly identifies Art 320(2) as UPSC-assisted scheme, not a new body
(2); explicit "special qualifications" condition for 320(2) (2); explicit statement that 315(2)
replaces SPSCs while 320(2) leaves them intact (2); clear comparative structure within 150 words (2).

### The problem before the doctrine

Once you know a Commission exists, three practical questions follow: who sits on it, for how long,
and who has the power to put them there in the first place? Get the appointment power wrong and you
will also get the removal power wrong later (Lesson 4), because the Constitution deliberately
separates who *appoints* from who *removes* for the State Commission — a asymmetry that is a
favourite trap.

### Visual 5 — Appointment and removal, side by side

```text
                 UPSC / Joint Commission        SPSC
APPOINTS   -->   President                      Governor
REMOVES    -->   President                      President   <-- asymmetry
```

*Caption: appointment power for an SPSC sits with the State executive, but final removal power for
every Commission — Union, State or Joint — sits only with the President.*

### Plain-language intuition

A State Governor can choose who sits on the State's own Commission — that is State-level
ownership of its own institution. But once appointed, no Governor can sack that member. Only the
President can, and only through the Article 317 process you will learn in Lesson 4. This design
protects an SPSC member from retaliation by the very State government whose recruitment decisions
the member is supposed to scrutinise.

### Technical doctrine: appointment

✅ Fact: the President appoints the Chairman and other members of the Union Commission and of a
Joint Commission. The Governor appoints the Chairman and other members of a State Commission.

⚠️ Analytical inference: appointment and removal are **intentionally asymmetric** for an SPSC —
**Governor appoints; President removes**. This is not an oversight; it is the device that keeps a
State's own political executive from being able to dismiss an inconvenient Commission member.

### Technical doctrine: the one-half experience rule

✅ Fact: the Constitution does not fix the total strength of any Commission — size is left flexible.

✅ Fact: under the **proviso to Article 316(1)**, **as nearly as may be one-half** of the members of
every Commission must, at their appointment dates, have held office for at least **ten years** under
the Government of India or a State. Pre-Constitution service under the Crown in India, or under the
Government of an Indian State, counts toward that ten years.

⚠️ Analytical inference: this is a **near-half experience quota**, not a requirement that half the
members be serving IAS officers, and not a fixed numerical seat count — a common distractor
rewrites "as nearly as may be one-half... ten years in specified government office" as "half the
members must be career civil servants," which overstates and mislabels the rule.

### Technical doctrine: tenure and age

✅ Fact: **Article 316(2)** gives a term of **six years from the date the member enters upon
office**, or until the age ceiling, whichever occurs earlier.

✅ Fact: the age ceiling is **65** for the Union Commission and **62** for a State or Joint
Commission.

### Visual 6 — Tenure calculation flow

```text
Member enters upon office
          |
          |---> Age ceiling reached first (65 UPSC / 62 SPSC+Joint)  -> term ends
          |
          |---> Six years completed first                            -> term ends
          |
Whichever happens EARLIER controls the exit date
```

⚠️ Limit: appointment date, notification date and the date of actually **entering office** need not
always be identical. Use the precise constitutional phrase — "from the date on which the member
enters upon office" — whenever a commencement calculation genuinely matters, rather than assuming
the appointment order date and the entering-office date are the same.

### Technical doctrine: resignation and the no-reappointment rule

✅ Fact: a UPSC or Joint Commission member resigns by writing addressed to the **President**. An
SPSC member resigns by writing addressed to the **Governor**.

✅ Fact: **Article 316(3)** makes a person, on expiry of the term, **ineligible for reappointment to
that office** — i.e., no second term in the *same* Commission office.

⚠️ Analytical inference: "no second term" is a useful shorthand only if immediately qualified —
Article 319 (Lesson 5) still permits specified movement to a **different, eligible** Commission
office. Do not let a learner conclude that a former Commission member can never hold any further
public role; the bar is office-specific, not person-wide.

### Technical doctrine: acting Chairman

✅ Fact: **Article 316(1A)** — if the Chair's office is vacant, or the Chair cannot perform duties
because of absence or another reason, **another member may be appointed to perform the duties**.

✅ Fact: the appointing authority for this acting arrangement is the **President** for the
Union/Joint Commission and the **Governor** for an SPSC.

✅ Fact: the arrangement lasts until the new Chair enters upon duties, or the existing Chair resumes.

### Visual 7 — Acting-chair trigger and exit

```text
Chair's office vacant OR Chair unable to act
                 |
President (UPSC/Joint) or Governor (SPSC)
appoints another MEMBER to act as Chair
                 |
Acting arrangement continues
                 |
         New Chair enters upon duties
                       OR
         Existing Chair resumes duties
                 |
Acting arrangement ends
```

⚠️ Limit: Article 316(1A) does **not** create an automatic seniority-based succession rule; the
appointing authority actively appoints an acting Chair from among the existing members, rather than
the senior-most member automatically stepping in by operation of law.

### Comparative treatment: appointment/removal trap board

| Commission | Who appoints | Who removes |
|---|---|---|
| UPSC | President | President |
| SPSC | Governor | President |
| Joint State PSC | President | President |

The SPSC row is the one worth memorising precisely: it is the only row where the two columns
differ.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** if the President can remove an SPSC member regardless of who appointed them, doesn't
that make the "State appoints its own Commission" principle symbolic — real control still sits with
the Union?

**Reply:** not quite, and the distinction matters for a federal-design answer (developed fully in
Lesson 12). Appointment is a genuine State power exercised routinely, without Union involvement, and
it shapes who actually sits on the body day to day. Removal, by contrast, is a **rare, judicially
supervised, high-threshold event** under Article 317 — not an ordinary administrative lever the
Union can use casually. Centralising only the *extraordinary* removal power, while leaving routine
appointment fully with the State, is a deliberate way of protecting a Commission member from
*State-level* retaliation without handing the Union routine control over *State-level* staffing.
The qualified verdict: this is a genuine federal asymmetry with a specific protective purpose, not
a disguised centralisation of real power.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, "Constitutional Bodies" — composition and appointment detail is
  the most frequently tested factual layer of this topic.
- **Verified PYQ linkage through 2026:** none found routed here in the audited ledgers (final PYQ
  section).
- **Probable framing:** a Prelims question combining "one-half experience rule," tenure and age
  ceiling, and the appointment/removal asymmetry into one multi-statement item.
- **Common trap:** stating "a UPSC member serves for six years or until age 65" without the
  "whichever is earlier" qualifier, which silently changes the rule's meaning.

### Revision notes

1. President appoints UPSC/Joint Commission members; Governor appoints SPSC members.
2. President removes members of all three forms, including SPSC — appointment and removal are
   asymmetric only for the SPSC.
3. No fixed Commission size is specified in the Constitution.
4. Proviso to Art 316(1): as nearly as may be one-half of members need ten years of specified prior
   government service (Union or State, including pre-Constitution Crown/Indian-State service).
5. Art 316(2): term is six years from entering office, or age ceiling, whichever is earlier.
6. Age ceiling: 65 for UPSC, 62 for SPSC and Joint Commission.
7. Resignation: to the President (UPSC/Joint) or the Governor (SPSC).
8. Art 316(3): no reappointment to the *same* office after term expiry.
9. Article 319 still permits movement to a *different* eligible Commission office — "no second
   term" is office-specific, not a lifetime bar.
10. Art 316(1A): acting Chair is appointed by President (UPSC/Joint) or Governor (SPSC) when the
    Chair's office is vacant or the Chair cannot act.
11. Acting arrangement ends when a new Chair enters office or the existing Chair resumes.
12. The current UPSC Chairman is a date-controlled administrative fact, not examinable doctrine —
    never freeze a current officeholder's name as a syllabus point.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State Governor appoints a new SPSC member in year one. In year three, the State
government becomes unhappy with the member's recommendations and wants the member removed. Can the
Governor remove the member directly?

**Model answer:** No. Although the Governor appointed the member, **only the President** can remove
any Public Service Commission member, including an SPSC member, and only through the Article 317
process (Lesson 4) — either the misbehaviour route (Presidential reference, Supreme Court inquiry,
Presidential decision) or one of the direct Presidential grounds under Article 317(3). The State
government's dissatisfaction with recommendations is not itself a constitutional removal ground.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not assume appointment power implies removal power. For the SPSC
specifically, these two powers are deliberately held by different constitutional authorities.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (10 marks):** Explain the significance of the appointment-removal asymmetry for
a State Public Service Commission. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Article 316 vests appointment of an SPSC's Chairman and members in the Governor,
giving each State genuine, routinely exercised control over who serves on its own Commission. Yet
Article 317 vests final removal power for every Commission — Union, State or Joint — exclusively in
the President, exercised only through a high-threshold process: a Presidential reference and Supreme
Court inquiry for misbehaviour, or direct Presidential action only on the narrow grounds in Article
317(3). This asymmetry protects an SPSC member from retaliatory removal by the very State government
whose recruitment and disciplinary decisions the member is expected to scrutinise impartially,
while leaving ordinary staffing fully with the State. It illustrates a deliberate federal design:
decentralised appointment for legitimacy and local ownership, centralised removal for independence
and protection against local capture.

**Quantified rubric (10 marks):** Correctly states Governor appoints, President removes (3); names
Article 317's high threshold for removal (3); explains the protective rationale against State-level
retaliation (2); situates this as deliberate federal design, not an oversight (2).

### The problem before the doctrine

If removal of a Commission member were easy, tenure protection would be meaningless — any
government could simply remove an inconvenient member whenever it liked. If removal were
*impossible*, a genuinely corrupt or incapacitated member could never be removed, which is just as
damaging to institutional credibility. Article 317 solves this by building **three separate removal
routes with different thresholds**, not one single process — and conflating them is the single
most common error students make with this Article.

### Part A — Misbehaviour and the Supreme Court inquiry

#### Visual 8 — Removal decision tree

```text
ALLEGATION AGAINST A COMMISSION MEMBER
            |
   +---------------------+--------------------------+
   |                      |                          |
"Misbehaviour"      Insolvency / outside paid   Deemed misbehaviour:
 (general,           employment / infirmity       interest in a
 Art 317(1))         of mind or body               government contract
   |                 (Art 317(3))                   (Art 317(4))
   |                      |                          |
President refers    President may remove       Treated as misbehaviour
to Supreme Court     DIRECTLY — no separate      -> routed through the
for inquiry          317(1) inquiry required      317(1) inquiry route
   |
SC conducts inquiry
(Art 145 procedure)
   |
SC reports whether member
"ought to be removed"
   |
President decides and acts on the report
```

### Plain-language intuition

Think of misbehaviour as a *disputed, serious allegation* — the kind of claim that genuinely needs
an independent, judicial fact-finder before anyone's career is ended. The Constitution therefore
inserts the Supreme Court as an impartial inquiry body between the allegation and removal. By
contrast, insolvency, outside paid employment, or medical/mental unfitness are *largely verifiable
facts*, not disputed value judgments, so the Constitution lets the President act on them directly,
without routing every such case through a full Supreme Court inquiry.

### Technical doctrine: misbehaviour (Article 317(1))

✅ Fact: under **Article 317(1)**, removal for misbehaviour follows a **Presidential reference** and
a **Supreme Court inquiry** conducted under the Article 145 procedure. Removal follows only where
the Court **reports** that the member ought to be removed on that ground. The President then acts
on that report.

✅ Fact: **Article 317(4)** **deems** a member guilty of misbehaviour if the member is concerned or
interested in a government contract or agreement, or participates in its profit or benefit, **except
as a member in common with other members of an incorporated company**. This conflict-of-interest
ground is routed through the same 317(1) inquiry process, because it is legally classified as
misbehaviour.

### Part B — The direct Presidential grounds (Article 317(3))

✅ Fact: **Article 317(3)** separately authorises Presidential removal, **without** the 317(1)
inquiry route, where the member:

1. is adjudged **insolvent**; or
2. engages, during the term of office, in **paid employment outside the duties of the office**; or
3. is, in the **President's opinion**, unfit to continue in office by reason of **infirmity of mind
   or body**.

#### Visual 9 — Grounds must not be flattened into one list

| Ground | Needs a Supreme Court inquiry under 317(1)? | Final decision route |
|---|---|---|
| Misbehaviour (general) | Yes | President acts on SC report |
| Interest in a government contract | Yes — deemed misbehaviour (317(4)) | President acts on SC report |
| Adjudged insolvent | No | President directly, under 317(3) |
| Paid outside employment | No | President directly, under 317(3) |
| Mental/physical infirmity | No | President directly, under 317(3), on the President's own opinion |

⚠️ Analytical inference: this is the single most tested structural fact in Article 317. A common
distractor claims "all Commission-member removal requires a Supreme Court inquiry." That is false —
only the misbehaviour route (including the deemed-misbehaviour contract-interest ground) requires
it; the three Article 317(3) grounds are direct Presidential powers.

### Technical doctrine: suspension during inquiry

✅ Fact: once a reference has been made to the Supreme Court, the **President** may suspend a UPSC or
**Joint Commission** Chair/member, and the **Governor** may suspend an **SPSC** Chair/member, until
the President acts on the Supreme Court's report.

#### Visual 10 — Suspension authority, mapped against removal authority

| Commission | Suspends (pending SC inquiry) | Finally removes |
|---|---|---|
| UPSC | President | President |
| SPSC (including, e.g., the Jharkhand PSC) | Governor | President |
| Joint State PSC | President | President |

> **Terminology caution:** "JPSC" is commonly used today to mean the **Jharkhand Public Service
> Commission** — that is an ordinary SPSC, not the constitutional "Joint Commission" discussed in
> Lesson 2. Do not let a State's own abbreviation collapse these two very different concepts.

### Example with limit

Suppose an SPSC member is found, on investigation, to hold a continuing financial interest in a
government supply contract. This is **not** treated as a simple administrative irregularity — Article
317(4) deems it misbehaviour, which means it must go through the full 317(1) route: Governor may
suspend the member immediately, but final removal requires a Presidential reference, a Supreme
Court inquiry, and a Presidential decision based on the Court's report. The limit: this heavier
process exists specifically because 317(4) *reclassifies* a conflict-of-interest fact as
misbehaviour; a lesser irregularity not falling within 317(1)/(4) would not automatically trigger
the same procedure unless it independently qualifies as misbehaviour or fits a 317(3) ground.

### Comparative treatment: why not borrow the judges' removal process?

Students often try to import the parliamentary "address" procedure used to remove a Supreme Court
or High Court judge into Article 317. ⚠️ Limit: do not do this. Judicial removal is a
**legislative-cum-judicial** process requiring a parliamentary motion with special majorities.
Commission-member removal under Article 317 is an **executive-cum-judicial** process: the President
refers the matter, the Supreme Court inquires, and the President alone decides and acts — Parliament
has no formal vote in the removal of a Commission member. Confusing the two institutions' removal
architecture is a classic cross-topic contamination error.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** giving the President sole final decision-making power even after an adverse Supreme
Court report (rather than making the SC's finding automatically binding) seems to leave room for
political discretion at the last step.

**Reply:** the Supreme Court's inquiry constrains that discretion heavily in practice — the
President acts "on the report," and a Presidential refusal to remove a member despite an adverse
report would itself be a highly unusual, legally exposed act, inconsistent with the structure and
purpose of Article 317. The residual formal discretion exists because the Constitution generally
avoids making a court's advisory finding automatically self-executing against the executive without
any final decision-maker; in practice, the Supreme Court's inquiry is the operative safeguard,
and the President's final act is close to formal implementation rather than an independent veto on
the merits.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, "Constitutional Bodies" — this is the core "independence
  safeguard" doctrine examiners test directly.
- **Verified PYQ linkage through 2026:** none found routed here (final PYQ section).
- **Probable framing:** "The removal of a member of a Public Service Commission is not a single
  undifferentiated process." Examine with reference to Article 317. This is a natural, high-value
  original Mains framing even without a verified historical PYQ on it.
- **Common trap:** writing "a Commission member can only be removed by the President after a
  Supreme Court inquiry" as a universal statement — this is true only for the misbehaviour branch.

### Revision notes

1. Article 317 is not one process — it has a misbehaviour route and a set of direct grounds.
2. Art 317(1): misbehaviour -> Presidential reference -> Supreme Court inquiry (Art 145 procedure)
   -> SC reports -> President decides and acts.
3. Art 317(4): interest/participation in a government contract/agreement is *deemed* misbehaviour,
   except as a member in common with other members of an incorporated company.
4. Art 317(3) grounds (insolvency, outside paid employment, infirmity of mind/body) allow **direct**
   Presidential removal — no separate 317(1) inquiry is required.
5. Only the President finally removes a Commission member, in every one of the three institutional
   forms (UPSC, SPSC, Joint Commission).
6. Suspension pending inquiry: President suspends UPSC/Joint Commission members; Governor suspends
   SPSC members.
7. Suspension lasts until the President acts on the Supreme Court's report.
8. "JPSC" today commonly means Jharkhand PSC (an SPSC) — not the constitutional Joint Commission.
9. Do not import the judges' parliamentary-address removal process into Article 317.
10. The President's final act after an adverse SC report is close to implementation, not an
    independent merits veto, despite formally residual discretion.
11. Removal architecture differs sharply from appointment architecture (Lesson 3) for the SPSC.
12. A single clean Mains thesis: Article 317 centralises final removal in the President while
    differentiating misbehaviour from direct incapacity/conflict grounds — balancing tenure security
    with workable incapacity and conflict controls.

### Concept check

**Question:** An SPSC Chairman is adjudged insolvent during their term. Does removal require a
Presidential reference to the Supreme Court?

**Model answer:** No. Adjudged insolvency is one of the three direct grounds under **Article
317(3)**, alongside outside paid employment and infirmity of mind or body. These grounds authorise
the President to remove the member **directly**, without the separate Supreme Court inquiry that
Article 317(1) requires for misbehaviour. The Supreme Court inquiry route is reserved for
misbehaviour allegations, including the deemed-misbehaviour contract-interest ground under Article
317(4).

**Misconception to avoid:** do not assume every Article 317 removal needs a Supreme Court inquiry.
Only the misbehaviour branch does; the three 317(3) grounds are direct Presidential powers.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (10 marks):** "Article 317 differentiates removal of a Public Service Commission
member rather than prescribing one uniform process." Examine. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Article 317 creates two distinct removal tracks. Misbehaviour, under clause (1),
requires a Presidential reference, a Supreme Court inquiry under the Article 145 procedure, and a
Presidential decision based on the Court's report; clause (4) extends this route by deeming
interest in a government contract or agreement to be misbehaviour, subject to the incorporated-
company exception. Clause (3), by contrast, authorises direct Presidential removal — without a
separate Supreme Court inquiry — for adjudged insolvency, outside paid employment, or infirmity of
mind or body making the member unfit. Suspension pending a clause (1) inquiry is itself split: the
President suspends a UPSC or Joint Commission member, while the Governor suspends an SPSC member,
though the President alone finally decides every case. This differentiation balances tenure
security for disputed allegations against workable removal for largely verifiable incapacity or
conflict-of-interest facts.

**Quantified rubric (10 marks):** Correctly separates 317(1)/(4) misbehaviour route from 317(3)
direct grounds (4); names all three 317(3) grounds accurately (2); states suspension asymmetry
correctly (2); concludes with the balancing rationale (2).

📚 Book context: Queried — Complete Topic Package §17-19, `advanced/28_UPSC-and-SPSC.md`

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The problem before the doctrine

Tenure protection during service (Lesson 4) is only half the independence design. The other half
asks: what happens to a Commission member's *career incentives* after they leave? If a departing
member could simply be rewarded with a lucrative government post for having been "cooperative"
while in office, the independence protected during service could be undone by expectations about
what comes afterward. Article 319 closes this door — but it closes it **differently** for each of
four distinct offices, and treating it as one blanket rule is the most common error here.

### Visual 11 — Four-office eligibility matrix

| Former office | May become | What remains barred |
|---|---|---|
| UPSC Chairman | **nothing further** — no further government employment at all | every other Union or State government employment |
| SPSC Chairman | UPSC Chairman or UPSC member; Chairman of **another** SPSC | every other Union/State government employment |
| UPSC member | UPSC Chairman, or Chairman of **an** SPSC | every other Union/State government employment |
| SPSC member | UPSC Chairman/member; Chairman of the **same or another** SPSC | every other Union/State government employment |

*Caption: read down the first column before reading across — each row is its own complete rule;
there is no single sentence that accurately describes all four offices at once.*

### Plain-language intuition

Picture four ladders of different heights leaning against the same wall (further PSC-related
office), but each ladder forbidden from touching any *other* part of the building (ordinary
government employment). The UPSC Chairman's ladder has been removed entirely — having reached the
very top PSC office, there is nowhere further within government for that person to go. A member of
a State Commission has the most room to move: upward to the UPSC, or sideways/upward within the
SPSC system. The design rewards upward institutional mobility *within the Commission family* while
blocking any route back into the ordinary civil or political executive machinery that a Commission
member might have had reason to favour while serving.

### Technical doctrine: Article 319

✅ Fact: Article 319 differentiates each office — there is no single sentence accurately describing
all four at once (see the matrix above for the complete, accurate statement office by office).

⚠️ Limit: eligibility for further PSC office does **not** create a right to elevation — Article 319
only removes a constitutional bar; it does not guarantee that any particular former member will in
fact be appointed to the office for which they remain eligible.

⚠️ Limit: the text bars "employment" under government. A constitutional office (such as Governor)
must be analysed by its own legal character rather than mechanically assumed to count, or not
count, as "employment" — this is why the illustrative case below is introduced with care rather
than as a settled universal rule.

### Illustrative case (flagged with caution by the canonical source)

The canonical advanced file notes, with its own caution marker rather than full certainty, an
illustrative instance in which a former UPSC Chairman's subsequent appointment as a State Governor
was treated as compatible with Article 319's bar on further government "employment," on the
reasoning that a Governorship is a **constitutional office**, not ordinary "employment under
government." Treat this as useful illustrative doctrine about how "employment" should be read
narrowly against a constitutional office — but because the source itself flags the citation with
caution, do not present the case name or year in a Mains answer as a fully pinned, independently
re-verified citation; present the *principle* (constitutional office ≠ government employment for
Article 319 purposes) as the safe, examinable takeaway.

### Technical doctrine: Article 318

✅ Fact: the President (for the Union/Joint Commission) and the Governor (for an SPSC) may regulate
(1) the number of Commission members and their conditions of service, and (2) the number and
conditions of service of Commission staff.

✅ Fact (non-disadvantage proviso): the conditions of service of a member may **not be varied to the
member's disadvantage after appointment**.

#### Visual 12 — Article 318's protective shape

```text
Regulation power (President/Governor)
         |
         |---> Member strength + member conditions of service
         |            |
         |            PROVISO: cannot be varied to a MEMBER's disadvantage
         |                      after appointment
         |
         |---> Staff strength + staff conditions of service
                      (no equivalent member-style non-disadvantage proviso)
```

⚠️ Limit: the non-disadvantage proviso protects **members**, not staff — ordinary Commission staff
conditions of service remain governed by the applicable general legal framework, without the
member-specific constitutional shield.

### Technical doctrine: Article 316(3) read with Article 319

✅ Fact: Article 316(3) bars reappointment **to the same office**; Article 319 separately permits
*specified* movement to a *different* eligible Commission office.

#### Visual 13 — Same office versus permitted progression

```text
EXPIRY OF TERM
       |
       |---> Same office again?              -> BARRED (Art 316(3))
       |
       |---> Different eligible PSC office?  -> PERMITTED, per the Art 319 matrix above
       |
       |---> Any other government employment? -> BARRED (Art 319), except where a
                                                   constitutional-office argument genuinely applies
```

### Comparative treatment: why a UPSC Chairman is treated most strictly

| Office | Direction of permitted movement | Underlying logic |
|---|---|---|
| UPSC Chairman | none | having reached the apex PSC office, no further government incentive should be dangled |
| SPSC Chairman | lateral/upward within the PSC family | still allows institutional continuity without opening ordinary government employment |
| UPSC member | upward within the PSC family | mirrors ordinary promotion logic inside the Commission system |
| SPSC member | lateral/upward within the PSC family, widest range | reflects that State-level members have the most natural further PSC-system destinations |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** if a UPSC Chairman can never receive any further government employment, doesn't this
discourage the most able serving officers from ever accepting the Chairmanship, since it ends their
further government career entirely?

**Reply:** this is a genuine, acknowledged trade-off rather than a design flaw to be explained away.
The Constitution accepts a real cost (reduced attractiveness of the apex office to someone still
seeking further government roles) in exchange for a real benefit: a UPSC Chairman's decisions
during office cannot be influenced, even subtly, by hope of a future government posting, because no
such posting is constitutionally available. The qualified verdict is that Article 319 trades future
career flexibility for decision-making purity at the single most sensitive PSC office, and applies
progressively lighter versions of the same trade-off further down the office hierarchy.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II, "Constitutional Bodies" — post-tenure restriction is a classic
  "independence safeguard" sub-theme.
- **Verified PYQ linkage through 2026:** none found routed here (final PYQ section).
- **Probable framing:** a Prelims matching question pairing each of the four offices with its
  correct permitted-destination list; or a Mains question on "post-tenure restrictions as an
  independence safeguard, with practical limits."
- **Common trap:** writing "no PSC member can ever hold any other office again" as a universal rule
  — this is true only for a UPSC Chairman; the other three offices retain defined onward routes.

### Revision notes

1. Article 319 governs post-tenure restrictions, distinct from Article 316(3)'s same-office bar.
2. UPSC Chairman: no further government employment at all — the strictest row.
3. SPSC Chairman: may become UPSC Chairman/member, or Chairman of another SPSC.
4. UPSC member: may become UPSC Chairman, or Chairman of an SPSC.
5. SPSC member: may become UPSC Chairman/member, or Chairman of the same or another SPSC.
6. Every row also bars all other Union/State government employment.
7. Eligibility under Article 319 does not create a right to actual appointment.
8. "Employment" under Article 319 must be read against the legal character of the later post — a
   constitutional office is analysed differently from ordinary government employment.
9. Article 318 lets the President/Governor regulate member and staff numbers and conditions.
10. The Article 318 non-disadvantage proviso protects members' conditions of service after
    appointment; it does not extend the same constitutional shield to staff.
11. Article 316(3) (no same-office reappointment) and Article 319 (limited different-office
    movement) must be read together, not treated as one rule.
12. The strictness gradient (strictest for UPSC Chairman, widest for SPSC member) reflects a
    deliberate trade-off between institutional continuity and decision-making purity.

### Concept check

**Question:** A former SPSC member is later offered the Chairmanship of a *different* State's
SPSC. Does Article 319 permit this?

**Model answer:** Yes. The Article 319 matrix explicitly allows a former SPSC member to become
Chairman of the same or another SPSC, in addition to UPSC Chairman or member. This is one of the
widest permitted-destination rows in the matrix, reflecting that State-level members have the
broadest range of natural further-PSC-system destinations.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not assume a stricter rule (such as the one applying to a UPSC
Chairman) applies uniformly to all four offices. Each row of the Article 319 matrix must be checked
independently.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (15 marks):** Examine Article 319's post-tenure restrictions as an independence
safeguard, along with their practical limits. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Article 319 bars a former Commission office-holder from most further
government employment, differentiated precisely by office. A former UPSC Chairman may take no
further government employment at all — the strictest rule, so that no future posting can influence
the apex office's decisions. A former SPSC Chairman may become UPSC Chairman/member or Chairman of
another SPSC; a former UPSC member may become UPSC Chairman or an SPSC Chairman; a former SPSC
member may become UPSC Chairman/member or Chairman of the same or another SPSC. Every row also bars
other Union/State government employment. Read with Article 316(3)'s separate bar on reappointment to
the *same* office, a member's only legitimate onward path is upward or lateral within the Commission
family, never into ordinary executive employment.

This is a genuine independence safeguard: it removes the incentive to curry executive favour for a
post-tenure reward. Its limits are real. Eligibility is not a right to elevation — an eligible former
member may simply never be appointed further. "Employment" must be read against the legal character
of the later post, since a constitutional office is not automatically equivalent to government
employment, creating boundary disputes at the margins. The rule also constrains only the
office-holder's *own* future incentive — it says nothing about whether the appointing authority's
discretion in choosing whom to elevate is itself free of political favour. Article 319 therefore
closes one channel of capture while leaving appointment discretion to be checked by other means,
such as transparency and judicial review (Lesson 10).

**Quantified rubric (15 marks):** Correct, office-differentiated statement of all four Article 319
rows (6); correct cross-reference to Article 316(3) same-office bar (2); clear statement of the
independence rationale (3); at least two distinct, correctly reasoned practical limits (3); coherent
250-word structure with a stated verdict (1).

### The problem before the doctrine

You have now met five separate safeguards — appointment design, tenure, differentiated removal,
service-condition protection, and post-tenure restriction — across four lessons. A Mains examiner
rarely asks about Article 316 or Article 319 in isolation; the typical high-value question asks you
to **argue** whether the Commission is genuinely independent. Answering that well requires treating
independence as a connected *lifecycle* running from entry to exit, not a list of unconnected
Article numbers, and then honestly stating where that lifecycle's protection runs out.

### Visual 14 — The independence lifecycle, as an argument tree

```text
ENTRY
  Art 316: fixed term (6 yrs/age ceiling) + one-half experience rule
       |
       v
DURING SERVICE
  Art 317: removal only through differentiated, high-threshold process
  Art 318: service conditions cannot be varied to a member's disadvantage
       |
       v
EXIT
  Art 319: post-tenure restrictions remove the incentive to curry future favour
       |
       v
RESULT CLAIMED
  A member can examine and advise without fear of casual removal,
  without fear of punitive service-condition changes, and without
  hope of post-tenure reward for compliance
```

*Caption: each safeguard answers a different moment in a member's career — entry, service, and exit
— and the claim of independence depends on all three holding together, not on any single Article.*

### Plain-language intuition

Independence, for an institution like this, is not a single switch that is either "on" or "off." It
is closer to a **chain with several links** — fixed entry terms, a hard-to-trigger removal process,
protected service conditions, and controlled exit incentives. A chain is only as strong as its
weakest link: even perfect removal protection (Article 317) would mean little if the executive
could simply degrade a sitting member's service conditions (if Article 318's proviso did not exist),
or dangle a lucrative post-retirement job for cooperative behaviour (if Article 319 did not exist).

### Technical doctrine: the synthesised safeguard set

✅ Fact: the independence design rests jointly on Articles 315 (institutional existence beyond
ordinary statute), 316 (fixed tenure and controlled appointment), 317 (differentiated, high-
threshold removal), 318 (non-disadvantage in service conditions) and 319 (post-tenure restriction),
reinforced by Article 322 (charged expenditure, not subject to annual legislative vote — covered
fully in Lesson 8) and Article 323 (mandatory reporting, converting silent non-acceptance of advice
into a recorded, legislature-facing fact).

### Full argument: why this combination, and not just one safeguard

⚠️ Analytical inference: no single Article could have done this work alone.

- Tenure (316) alone, without removal protection (317), would be meaningless — a government could
  simply remove an inconvenient member before the term naturally expires.
- Removal protection (317) alone, without service-condition protection (318), would be meaningless —
  a government could keep a member in office while punishing them through salary cuts or degraded
  conditions.
- Both of the above, without post-tenure restriction (319), would still leave open a subtler
  channel: rewarding compliant behaviour with a lucrative post-retirement government post.
- All three, without the charged-expenditure and reporting duties (322-323), would still leave the
  Commission's *budget* vulnerable to executive pressure and its *rejected advice* invisible to the
  public.

The five-plus-two safeguard combination is therefore best understood as **closing successive
channels of executive leverage**, each one targeting a different point at which independence could
otherwise be quietly undermined.

### Comparative treatment: independence here versus elsewhere

| Institution | Primary independence technique | Shared feature with UPSC/SPSC |
|---|---|---|
| Election Commission | fixed tenure + removal akin to a Supreme Court judge for the Chief Election Commissioner | tenure + high-threshold removal |
| CAG | fixed tenure + judge-like removal + no further government office after tenure | tenure + removal + post-tenure restriction |
| UPSC/SPSC | fixed tenure + Art 317's own differentiated removal + non-disadvantage service conditions + office-specific post-tenure matrix | the most granular, office-differentiated version of the same basic technique |

⚠️ Analytical inference: this is why the GS-II syllabus groups these institutions together as
"Constitutional Bodies" — they share a *family* of independence techniques (tenure, removal
insulation, post-tenure restriction) even though the exact mechanics differ by institution.

### Strongest objection and reply (the honest limits)

**Objection:** list every genuine way the "fully independent" claim can still be undermined in
practice, even with all five-plus-two safeguards intact.

**Reply — the practical limits, stated without exaggeration or understatement:**

1. **Appointment discretion is not neutralised.** Articles 316-319 protect a member *once
   appointed*; they do nothing to guarantee that the appointing authority's initial choice of whom
   to appoint is itself free from political consideration. No adopted constitutional "collegium" or
   equivalent appointment-screening mechanism currently governs PSC appointments — this is a
   genuine, unresolved governance gap, not a settled safeguard (developed fully in Lesson 12).
2. **Advice remains directory, not binding** (*Manbodhan Lal Srivastava* — Lesson 8). Independence
   protects the *advisor*, not the *weight* given to the advice.
3. **Operational dependence persists.** Staff strength, technology procurement, and the pace of
   filling vacancies on the Commission itself often remain practically dependent on the executive's
   administrative cooperation, even though the Constitution does not formally subordinate the
   Commission on these points.
4. **SPSC capacity and integrity vary by State** (Lesson 11) — constitutional insulation does not
   by itself guarantee administrative capacity, exam security, or freedom from local political
   capture.

### UPSC application

- **Syllabus relevance:** GS-II — this lesson is the natural home for the classic "independence of
  constitutional bodies" essay-style question applied to the Commission.
- **Verified PYQ linkage through 2026:** none found routed here (final PYQ section); this synthesis
  is original teaching built from the audited Article-by-Article doctrine, not from an existing PYQ.
- **Probable framing:** "The independence of the Public Service Commission is constitutionally
  robust but administratively incomplete." Discuss.
- **Common trap:** writing a purely descriptive answer (listing Articles 316-319) without ever
  reaching the "but administratively incomplete" qualification — examiners reward the honest limit
  as much as the correct list.

### Revision notes

1. Independence is a lifecycle: entry (Art 316) -> service (Art 317, 318) -> exit (Art 319).
2. No single safeguard works alone — each closes a different channel of executive leverage.
3. Tenure without removal protection would be meaningless; removal protection without service-
   condition protection would be meaningless; both without post-tenure restriction would still leave
   a reward channel open.
4. Articles 322 (charged expenditure) and 323 (mandatory reporting) reinforce the five core
   safeguards by protecting the budget and making rejected advice visible.
5. The Election Commission and CAG use a recognisably similar "tenure + removal + post-tenure
   restriction" family of techniques — hence the shared "Constitutional Bodies" syllabus category.
6. Genuine limit 1: appointment discretion itself is not neutralised by these safeguards.
7. Genuine limit 2: no adopted constitutional collegium or equivalent exists for PSC appointments.
8. Genuine limit 3: advice remains directory, not binding, even from a fully independent Commission.
9. Genuine limit 4: operational dependence (staff, technology, vacancy-filling pace) can persist in
   practice despite formal constitutional insulation.
10. Genuine limit 5: SPSC capacity and integrity vary by State — insulation is not the same as
    administrative capacity.
11. A strong Mains answer always pairs the safeguard list with at least two of these honest limits.
12. This lesson is a synthesis, not a new Article — do not present it as introducing new textual law.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why would it be inadequate for a Mains answer to argue "the UPSC is completely
independent because Articles 316-319 protect its members"?

**Model answer:** It is inadequate because it treats constitutional tenure/removal/service-
condition/post-tenure protection as the *entire* independence question, when genuine limits remain
outside those Articles' reach — most importantly, the absence of any adopted appointment-screening
mechanism (no collegium), the directory (non-binding) character of the Commission's advice, and
practical operational dependence on the executive for staff and administrative resources. A
complete answer states the strong textual protection *and* these residual limits together.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not conflate "the member, once appointed, cannot be easily removed"
with "the institution faces no independence risk at all." These are different claims, and only the
first is fully secured by Articles 316-319.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (15 marks):** "The independence of the Public Service Commission is
constitutionally robust but administratively incomplete." Discuss. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Mains model answer:** The Constitution builds a genuinely strong independence architecture around a
Commission member's career. Article 316 fixes tenure (six years or an age ceiling, whichever is
earlier) and a near-half experience requirement for appointment. Article 317 differentiates removal
into a high-threshold misbehaviour route, requiring a Presidential reference and a Supreme Court
inquiry, and three narrower direct Presidential grounds, preventing casual dismissal on either
front. Article 318's non-disadvantage proviso stops punitive service-condition changes during
service. Article 319 removes the incentive to curry post-tenure favour by sharply restricting
further government employment, in an office-differentiated manner. Articles 322 and 323 add charged
expenditure and mandatory reporting of rejected advice.

Together these make the *sitting member* hard to pressure through removal, service conditions, or
future reward — a textually robust design comparable to the Election Commission and CAG's
independence architecture. Yet the design is administratively incomplete in at least three
respects. First, no adopted constitutional collegium or equivalent mechanism screens *appointment*
itself, leaving the Commission's composition exposed to executive discretion at the point of entry.
Second, Article 320 advice remains directory, not binding, so independence protects the advisor
without guaranteeing the advice is followed. Third, staff strength, technology and the pace of
filling vacancies often depend practically on executive cooperation. The accurate verdict is
textual strength paired with administrative and appointment-stage incompleteness, not unqualified
independence.

**Quantified rubric (15 marks):** Correctly synthesises Articles 316-319 and 322-323 as a connected
lifecycle, not a list (5); explicit "robust but incomplete" dual thesis (2); at least three distinct,
accurately reasoned limits (6); comparative reference to EC/CAG as the same safeguard family (1);
coherent 250-word structure with a stated verdict (1).

### The problem before the doctrine

So far you have learned who the Commission is and how its members are protected. This lesson asks
the functional question: **what does the Commission actually do, and on what exactly must it be
asked?** Article 320 is longer and more varied than most students expect — it is not only about
conducting examinations. It also carries the single most misquoted clause in this entire topic:
the reservation "exception," which is routinely overstated into something far broader than its
actual text.

### Part A — Article 320(1) and (2)

#### Visual 15 — What Article 320 actually governs

```text
ARTICLE 320
   |
   |-- (1) Duty to conduct examinations for Union/State service appointments
   |
   |-- (2) On request of 2+ States: UPSC must assist a joint recruitment scheme
   |        for posts requiring special qualifications
   |
   |-- (3) Consultation catalogue (methods, principles, suitability, discipline, claims)
   |
   |-- (4) What clause (3) does NOT require consultation about
   |
   |-- (5) Consultation-exemption regulations must be laid before the legislature
```

### Plain-language intuition

Article 320(1) is the job most people already associate with "UPSC" — running the entrance
examinations. But treat this as only one function among several, not the Commission's entire
identity. Article 320(2) is a *helping* function for States that want to run a shared recruitment
scheme for a hard-to-find specialist cadre. Article 320(3)-(5) is the Commission's *advisory*
function, running far beyond examinations into recruitment design, discipline and even certain
compensation claims — and it is this advisory function, not the examination function, that contains
the heavily tested reservation exception.

### Technical doctrine: the examination duty (320(1))

✅ Fact: it is the duty of the Union and State Commissions to **conduct examinations** for
appointments to Union and State services respectively.

⚠️ Limit: not every government appointment must be made through one common competitive
examination. Recruitment rules may prescribe examination, direct recruitment by selection,
promotion, transfer, deputation, contract, or another lawful mode — Article 320(1) is a duty to
conduct examinations *where the recruitment rules call for one*, not a monopoly clause requiring
every post to be filled only by competitive examination.

### Technical doctrine: joint recruitment assistance (320(2))

✅ Fact: if requested by two or more States, the UPSC must **assist them in framing and operating
joint recruitment schemes** for services requiring candidates with special qualifications.

⚠️ Limit: this clause does not create a Joint State PSC (you already distinguished this precisely
from Article 315(2) in Lesson 2 — revisit Visual 4 if this distinction feels unfamiliar).

### Part B — Article 320(3), (4) and (5): the consultation catalogue

#### Visual 16 — The consultation wheel

```text
Article 320(3): the Commission shall be consulted on —
         |
   +-----+-----------------+------------------+------------------+
   |                        |                  |                  |
(a) recruitment       (b) suitability of   (c) disciplinary   (d)/(e) specified
methods, principles    candidates for        matters           legal-cost and
```

✅ Fact: the Commission must advise on referred matters and the listed classes, subject to the
constitutional exceptions and valid regulations.

### Technical doctrine: three different consultation questions

✅ Fact: Article 320(3)(a) covers **methods of recruitment** and **principles to be followed** in
making appointments and in promotions and transfers from one service to another, and on suitability
of candidates. Article 320(3)(c) covers **disciplinary matters** affecting persons serving the
Union/a State in a civil capacity, including memorials or petitions. Clauses (d) and (e) cover
specified official-duty legal-cost claims and injury-pension claims.

| Question | Example | Commission's role |
|---|---|---|
| Methods | should promotion use a written test, an interview, or seniority-cum-fitness? | designs/advises on the recruitment route |
| Principles | what general criteria should govern inter-service transfer? | sets the governing rule |
| Suitability | do named candidates satisfy lawful criteria? | assesses within an already-set process |

⚠️ Analytical inference: a strong answer keeps these three questions separate — designing the
*route* (methods), setting the *rule* (principles), and evaluating a *candidate* (suitability) are
different tasks, and conflating them hides exactly where arbitrariness occurred in a given dispute.

### Technical doctrine: the exact reservation exception (320(4))

✅ Fact: the proviso to Article 320(3) allows the President or Governor to make **regulations**
specifying matters, classes of matters, or circumstances in which consultation is unnecessary.

✅ Fact: **Article 320(4)** separately states that clause (3) does **not** require consultation about
the **manner** in which effect is to be given to any reservation-related provisions under Articles
16(4) or 335 (i.e., reservation of appointments/posts for backward classes, SCs and STs, and the
efficiency-of-administration proviso) — or about matters affecting the claims of reservation
beneficiaries in connection with such appointments or posts.

#### Visual 17 — What 320(4) actually exempts

```text
WITHOUT the exemption: UPSC/SPSC consultation would ordinarily

Consultation is NOT required specifically on the MANNER of

       |
       does NOT abolish reservation law itself
       does NOT remove reservation from UPSC's broader advisory field entirely

```

⚠️ Limit: the familiar classroom shorthand "UPSC is not consulted on reservations" is dangerously
imprecise and must be written with its **exact textual scope** — it is only the *manner of giving
effect* to Articles 16(4)/335 reservation that is exempted from mandatory consultation, not
reservation policy as a whole, and not every reservation-adjacent recruitment question.

### Technical doctrine: regulations must face the legislature (320(5))

✅ Fact: Article 320(5) requires consultation-exemption regulations to be **laid for at least
fourteen days** before Parliament or the State legislature, as applicable, and subjects them to
**legislative modification, repeal or amendment** during the relevant session.

#### Visual 18 — Delegated-accountability chain

```text

```

⚠️ Analytical inference: this is the same accountability logic you saw in Lesson 1's "insulated
advice" argument, applied specifically to delegated exemption power — the executive can exempt
itself from consultation only through an instrument the legislature can later undo, not through a
silent administrative circular.

### Comparative treatment: Article 320(2) versus Article 320(3)-(5)

| Dimension | Art 320(2) | Art 320(3)-(5) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | an assistance duty | a consultation/advisory relationship |
| Trigger | two or more States' request | ordinary recruitment/discipline/claims process |
| Creates new institution? | no | no |
| Subject to exemption regulations? | not applicable | yes, under 320(3) proviso and 320(4) |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** if the executive can make regulations exempting itself from consultation (320(3)
proviso), doesn't this let government quietly hollow out the Commission's advisory role over time,
one exemption at a time?

**Reply:** the design anticipates exactly this risk and responds with Article 320(5)'s mandatory
14-day legislative laying and amendment power — every exemption is time-bound to legislative
visibility and revisability, not executive discretion alone. It is also worth distinguishing this
general exemption-regulation power from the *specific*, narrower Article 320(4) reservation
exception, which the Constitution itself writes directly into the text rather than leaving to
delegated regulation — meaning 320(4)'s scope cannot be silently expanded by executive regulation
the way an ordinary 320(3)-proviso exemption theoretically could be contested or reversed by the
legislature. The qualified verdict: the exemption mechanism carries a real legislative check, but it
remains a genuine point of potential erosion if legislatures fail to scrutinise laid regulations
actively — a governance quality question, not a textual gap.

  the precise scope every time this appears.

### Revision notes

1. Article 320(1): duty to conduct examinations — not a monopoly over every recruitment mode.
2. Article 320(2): UPSC must assist 2+ States' joint recruitment scheme for specially-qualified
   posts; creates no new Commission.
3. Article 320(3): consultation catalogue — methods/principles, suitability, disciplinary matters,
   and specified legal-cost/injury-pension claims, plus referred matters.
4. Methods, principles and suitability are three *different* questions — route, rule, and candidate
   evaluation respectively.
5. The 320(3) proviso lets the President/Governor make regulations exempting specified matters from
   consultation.
6. Article 320(4): consultation is not required specifically on the *manner* of giving effect to
   Art 16(4)/335 reservation, or on claims of reservation beneficiaries connected with such posts.
7. Article 320(4) does not abolish reservation consultation entirely — its scope is narrow and
   precise.
8. Article 320(5): exemption regulations must be laid for at least 14 days before the legislature
   and remain subject to legislative modification, repeal or amendment.
9. The 320(5) legislative check applies to the general 320(3)-proviso exemption power; Article
   320(4)'s reservation exception is itself written directly into the constitutional text.
10. "UPSC is not consulted on reservations" is an imprecise shorthand — always state the exact
    manner-of-implementation scope.
11. Article 320's advisory catalogue is broader than examinations — discipline and compensation
    claims are also covered.
12. The exemption-regulation mechanism carries a real but governance-dependent legislative check.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State government argues that, because of Article 320(4), it need not consult its
SPSC at all when designing the overall reservation policy for a new recruitment rule. Is this
correct?

**Model answer:** No. Article 320(4) exempts consultation only on the **manner** of giving effect
to Articles 16(4)/335 reservation provisions, and on claims of reservation beneficiaries connected
with such appointments or posts. It does not exempt the SPSC from consultation on the *recruitment
rule as a whole*, nor does it remove reservation-adjacent matters from the Commission's broader
advisory field under Article 320(3) wherever they fall outside this narrow, precisely worded
exception.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not read Article 320(4) as a blanket reservation carve-out. Its text
is narrow and specific — "manner of giving effect" and "claims of reservation beneficiaries" — not
"all reservation matters."

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (10 marks):** Explain the exact scope of the Article 320(4) exception to
Commission consultation. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Article 320(3) ordinarily requires Union and State Public Service Commissions to
be consulted on recruitment methods and principles, candidate suitability, disciplinary matters and
specified legal-cost or injury-pension claims. Article 320(4) carves out a narrow exception: clause
(3) does not require consultation on the **manner** in which effect is to be given to the
reservation provisions under Articles 16(4) and 335, nor on matters affecting the claims of
reservation beneficiaries in connection with such appointments or posts. This is textually precise
and limited — it removes only the implementation-manner question and connected beneficiary claims
from mandatory consultation; it does not exempt the Commission from consultation on the recruitment
rule as a whole, and it does not abolish reservation policy or Commission involvement in recruitment
more broadly. The popular shorthand "UPSC is not consulted on reservations" must always be qualified
to this exact scope.

### The problem before the doctrine

You already know Article 320 consultation is a duty. But what actually happens if the executive
consults the Commission and then does something different from what it advised? Is the decision
automatically invalid? This lesson answers that question through a 1957 precedent, and then
completes Part XIV with three remaining Articles: extension of Commission functions (321), charged
expenditure (322), and the reporting duty (323) that converts non-acceptance of advice into a
public, legislature-facing fact.

### Technical doctrine: advice is generally advisory, not binding

✅ Fact: in *State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava* (1957), the Supreme Court held Article
320(3) consultation to be **directory rather than mandatory** — non-consultation does not, by
itself, invalidate the disciplinary action in question.

⚠️ Limit: "directory" does not mean the executive may casually ignore the Commission. The judgment
treated consultation as an important constitutional safeguard even while refusing automatic
invalidation for its absence — the word "directory" describes the *legal consequence of breach*,
not the *constitutional importance* of the duty.

### Visual 19 — What "directory, not mandatory" actually means

```text
Government fails to consult the Commission where Article 320(3) applies
                 |
Does the decision become automatically void?  -> NO
                 |
Is the consultation duty therefore meaningless? -> NO
                 |
What actually follows:
  - the decision is not automatically invalidated on this ground alone
  - but failure to consult remains a constitutionally significant omission
  - and other grounds (bias, arbitrariness, bad faith, statutory-rule
    violation) remain fully available for judicial review (Lesson 10)
```

### Plain-language intuition

Think of Article 320 consultation as a mandatory step in a recipe that, if skipped, does not by
itself spoil the finished dish in the eyes of the law — but skipping it is still bad practice, and
if the dish turns out spoiled for an *independent* reason (say, bias in the final decision), the
cook cannot hide behind "I did eventually consult someone." *Manbodhan Lal* keeps these two
questions separate: was consultation skipped (a directory formality), and was the final decision
otherwise fair and lawful (a substantive question courts do examine closely, as you will see fully
in Lesson 10).

### Technical doctrine: Article 323 — advice becomes accountability

✅ Fact: the UPSC reports **annually to the President**. The President lays the report before each
House of Parliament **together with a memorandum** explaining cases where advice was not accepted,
and the reasons for non-acceptance.

✅ Fact: an SPSC reports to the **Governor**, who lays the report and the corresponding memorandum
before the State legislature.

#### Visual 20 — From private departure to public record

```text
Government decides not to accept Commission's advice on a matter
                 |
Article 323 requires this to surface in the annual report/memorandum
                 |
President lays report+memorandum before Parliament (for UPSC)
Governor lays report+memorandum before State legislature (for SPSC)
                 |
Legislature and public can now scrutinise WHY advice was rejected
```

⚠️ Analytical inference: Article 323 is the mechanism that makes Lesson 1's claim — "entrenchment
operates through transparency-forced accountability, not a legal veto" — concretely true. A
directory consultation duty (per *Manbodhan Lal*) would be a weak safeguard on its own; pairing it
with a mandatory reasons-giving and legislative-laying duty converts "the government ignored our
advice" from an invisible private fact into a recorded, debatable public one.

### Technical doctrine: Article 321 — legislative extension

✅ Fact: Parliament or a State legislature may **extend** Commission functions to services of the
Union/State and to services of a **local authority, statutory body corporate, or public
institution**.

⚠️ Limit: Article 321 is an **enabling legislative route**, not an automatic extension to every
public-sector entity — extension requires an actual legislative act; it is not a default rule
applying to every body merely because it performs a public function.

### Visual 21 — Constitutional core and legislative extension

### Technical doctrine: Article 322 — charged expenditure

✅ Fact: expenses of the UPSC or an SPSC, including salaries, allowances and pensions payable to or

```text
                    vs

```

⚠️ Analytical inference: charged expenditure is a financial-independence safeguard working

⚠️ Limit: for a Joint Commission specifically, the text of Article 322 expressly names Union and

law alongside this general design, rather than assumed identical without checking that law.

### Comparative treatment: four accountability instruments, one purpose

| Instrument | What it protects | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Art 316 tenure | the individual member's job security | fixed term + controlled removal |
| Art 318 service conditions | the individual member's compensation/conditions | non-disadvantage proviso |
| Art 322 charged expenditure | the institution's budget | Consolidated Fund, no annual vote |
| Art 323 reporting | the institution's advisory credibility | mandatory reasons + legislative laying |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** if Article 323 only requires the government to *explain* why it rejected advice, and
*Manbodhan Lal* confirms consultation itself is directory, hasn't the Constitution built an elaborate
reporting ritual with no real teeth?

**Reply:** the "teeth" here are political and institutional, not a court-enforced veto, and that is
a deliberate design choice consistent with the rest of this topic's logic (Lesson 1). A government
that must publicly justify, session after session, why it repeatedly rejects the advice of a
constitutionally insulated expert body faces a real and cumulative political cost — especially
combined with judicial review remaining available wherever the underlying decision is independently
arbitrary or biased (Lesson 10). The honest, qualified verdict is that Article 323 converts
disagreement into a *recorded, contestable* fact rather than guaranteeing a particular outcome —
this is weaker than a veto, but materially stronger than silence.

   (explaining non-acceptance and reasons) before Parliament.
5. Article 323: SPSC reports to the Governor, who lays report + memorandum before the State
   legislature.
6. Article 323 converts private executive departure from advice into a public, legislature-facing
   record.
7. Article 321: Parliament/State legislature may extend Commission functions to local authority,
   statutory body corporate, or public institution services — an enabling route, not an automatic
   extension.
8. Article 322: UPSC/SPSC expenses, including member/staff salaries, allowances and pensions, are

9. Charged expenditure protects the institution's budget, parallel to how tenure protects the

10. For a Joint Commission, Article 322's financial arrangement should be checked against its own

11. Four accountability instruments work together: tenure, service conditions, charged expenditure,

### Concept check

**Question:** A State government disciplines a civil servant without consulting its SPSC, contrary
to Article 320(3)(c). Is the disciplinary action automatically void?

**Model answer:** Not automatically. Following *State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava*, Article
320(3) consultation is directory, not mandatory, so the absence of consultation does not by itself
invalidate the action. However, the action remains open to challenge on independent grounds — bias,
arbitrariness, bad faith, or violation of the governing statutory rules — and the omission to
consult remains a constitutionally significant failure even though it is not automatically fatal to
the decision's validity.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not equate "directory" with "optional" or "without consequence."
Directory means the *automatic invalidity* consequence does not follow from non-consultation alone —
it does not mean the duty carries no constitutional weight.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (15 marks):** Examine how Article 323 converts the Public Service Commission's
advisory, non-binding status into a mechanism of real institutional accountability. (Answer in 250

**Mains model answer:** Article 320 makes Commission consultation an advisory duty, and *State of U.P. v.

does not automatically invalidate a decision. Read alone, this could suggest the Commission's advice
carries little real weight. Article 323 changes that calculus. It requires the UPSC to report
annually to the President, who must lay the report before each House of Parliament together with a
memorandum explaining every case where advice was not accepted and the reasons for non-acceptance;
an SPSC follows the parallel route through the Governor and the State legislature. This converts
what would otherwise be a private, invisible executive departure from expert advice into a
recorded, legislature-facing fact, exposed to parliamentary and public scrutiny session after
session.

This accountability is reinforced by two further Part XIV provisions. Article 322 charges
Commission expenditure, including member and staff salaries and pensions, directly on the
Consolidated Fund, insulating the institution's budget from annual legislative leverage. Article 321
allows Parliament or a State legislature to extend Commission functions to local authorities,
statutory corporations or public institutions, widening the Commission's reach where legislatively
authorised. The combined effect is a Commission whose advice is not legally binding but whose
rejection is never silent — transforming "directory consultation" from a potential weakness into an
institution that competes for legitimacy through enforced transparency rather than legal command.

### The problem before the doctrine

allocation, postings and disciplinary finality just as reliably as understating its role does. This
lesson draws the Commission's actual boundary against DoPT and the appointing government, maps the
full recruitment chain, and then applies all of it to the one genuinely live current controversy in
this topic: lateral entry recruitment.

### Visual 23 — UPSC versus DoPT: who does what

|---|---|

✅ Fact: Article 312 and the All-India Services framework belong to the **Public Services** owner,

numbers, and treating "CAT and UPSC perform the same role" is a standard, clearly wrong distractor
(as both Lesson 1's Prelims rule and the canonical cross-topic owner independently confirm).

### Technical doctrine: lateral recruitment's precise constitutional relationship

✅ Fact: lateral recruitment is a **mode of filling identified posts** — it is not a constitutional
amendment, and it does not displace Articles 14, 16, 309, or the applicable reservation and

✅ Fact: a 15 March 2024 amendment to the **UPSC Exemption from Consultation Regulations**

⚠️ Limit: this instrument is **not** a general constitutional exemption called "lateral entry." It

modes — presenting it as a blanket lateral-entry license for all posts is a serious overstatement.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### The problem before the doctrine

If courts could freely re-mark interviews, re-rank candidates, or second-guess an expert
Commission's comparative judgment of merit, the Commission's entire advisory expertise would become
pointless — litigation would simply replace selection. But if courts could *never* intervene, bias,
shifting criteria and arbitrary post-selection surprises would go unchecked. Indian courts have
built a precise middle path across four decisions, each policing a different *type* of defect while
consistently refusing to become a second selection committee.

### Visual 27 — The court's actual question set

```text
Were the rules followed?            -> courts intervene if NOT
Was there bias or mala fide?        -> courts intervene if YES
Was equality (Art 14/16) violated?  -> courts intervene if YES
                 vs
Was the comparative merit assessment itself wrong?
                 -> courts generally DO NOT re-conduct this assessment
```

*Caption: courts review legality and fairness of process; they do not re-run the expert merit
comparison themselves.*

### Plain-language intuition

Think of judicial review here like a sports tribunal reviewing a championship result. The tribunal
will intervene if the referee was bribed, if the rulebook was changed mid-match, or if one team was
denied a fair chance to compete. It will not re-watch the match and re-score every play according to
its own taste — that would require the tribunal to become a second set of judges, duplicating
exactly the expertise the original judges were appointed to exercise. Indian courts apply the same
discipline to Commission selections.

### Technical doctrine and full case law

#### Case 1 — *Ashok Kumar Yadav v. State of Haryana* (1985): bias and interview fairness

✅ Fact: the case scrutinised Haryana Public Service Commission selection, including interview
weight and participation concerns involving relations of Commission members.

⚠️ Analytical inference: its enduring lesson is institutional, not merely case-specific — a
formally expert body can still produce an unconstitutional selection if bias safeguards and
assessment design are weak. ⚠️ Limit: do not turn a case-specific discussion of interview
percentages into an inflexible constitutional number applicable to every service; the doctrinal
takeaway is the *bias/fairness principle*, not a fixed interview-weight ceiling.

#### Case 2 — *K. Manjusree v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (2008) and *Tej Prakash Pathak v. Rajasthan High Court* (2024 INSC 847): stable rules

✅ Fact: *K. Manjusree* disallowed introducing a minimum interview benchmark **after** the selection
process, where the governing framework had not prescribed it in advance.

✅ Current fact: the Constitution Bench in *Tej Prakash Pathak*, **2024 INSC 847**, reaffirmed the
core stability principle while locating it firmly in **Articles 14 and 16** — eligibility and
selection criteria cannot be changed after the process commences, unless the governing rules
themselves permit a lawful, non-arbitrary course.

#### Visual 28 — The "rules-of-the-game" test

```text
Selection process begins under published criteria
            |
Can the criteria change mid-process or after results?
            |
   +--------+---------+
   |                  |
  NO                 YES, but only if
(ordinary rule)      the governing rules themselves
                      lawfully permit a non-arbitrary change
            |
Otherwise: violates Articles 14/16 — rules-of-the-game doctrine
```

#### Case 3 — *Shankarsan Dash v. Union of India* (1991): select list is not an indefeasible right

✅ Fact: notification of vacancies and inclusion in a select list do **not** create an indefeasible
right to appointment.

✅ Fact: government may decide not to fill all vacancies for **bona fide reasons**, but the decision
cannot be **arbitrary**.

⚠️ Analytical inference: the candidate has a right against unconstitutional decision-making, not
ownership of the post — this is a precise distinction: equality and non-arbitrariness are
enforceable; a guaranteed appointment is not.

#### Case 4 — *M.V. Thimmaiah v. UPSC* (2007): limited review of expert recommendations

✅ Fact: selection-committee recommendations are ordinarily **not challengeable** except on grounds
such as **mala fides or serious violation of statutory rules**; courts do not act as appellate
authorities over expert comparative assessment.

#### Visual 29 — The complete four-case toolkit, side by side

| Case | What it protects against | What it does NOT authorise |
|---|---|---|
| *Ashok Kumar Yadav* (1985) | bias, unfair interview design | re-scoring every interview |
| *K. Manjusree* / *Tej Prakash Pathak* (2024 INSC 847) | mid-process or post-process rule changes | freezing rules that the framework itself allows to change lawfully |
| *Shankarsan Dash* (1991) | arbitrary refusal to appoint from a valid list | a guaranteed right to appointment |
| *M.V. Thimmaiah* (2007) | mala fides, serious rule violation | appellate re-ranking of candidates |

### Full argument: why this exact division of labour

⚠️ Analytical inference: the four cases together draw one consistent line — courts police
**legality and process fairness** (bias, rule stability, non-arbitrariness, statutory compliance);
they decline to police **comparative merit judgment** (who scored better in an expert's assessment).
This mirrors the institutional logic of Lesson 1: the Commission exists precisely because comparative
merit assessment requires specialised judgment the judiciary is not designed to duplicate, while
legality and fairness are exactly the questions courts are institutionally equipped to answer.

### Comparative treatment: restraint versus abdication

| Court's question | Reviewable? | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Were the rules followed? | yes | legality is a judicial question |
| Was there bias/mala fide? | yes | fairness is a judicial question |
| Was equality violated? | yes | constitutional rights are a judicial question |
| Was the comparative merit assessment itself "wrong"? | generally no | expert, discretionary, and institutionally reserved to the Commission |

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** if courts refuse to re-examine the merits of a comparative assessment, doesn't this
leave a genuinely incompetent or badly designed selection process — one that is merely "not
technically illegal" — completely unchecked?

**Reply:** the four-case toolkit captures more than pure procedural technicality. *Ashok Kumar
Yadav* reaches *design-level* unfairness (how interviews are structured, not just whether a form was
filled correctly); *K. Manjusree*/*Tej Prakash Pathak* reach substantive criteria-stability, which is
itself a safeguard against results-driven manipulation of standards; and *M.V. Thimmaiah*'s "serious
violation of statutory rules" ground reaches real process defects beyond mere paperwork. What
remains genuinely unreviewed is the Commission's **comparative judgment call** within a fair,
stable, rule-compliant process — for example, whether one strong candidate's answer was marginally
better than another equally strong candidate's. The qualified verdict: judicial restraint here
targets only the narrow band of genuine expert discretion, not the much wider band of legality and
fairness, which remains fully open to review.

### UPSC application

  action — a frequently tested combination topic.
- **Verified PYQ linkage through 2026:** none found routed to this specific topic in the audited

  judgment relevant to recruitment law generally and should be checked independently against any

- **Probable framing:** "Judicial review of Public Service Commission selections is calibrated

### Revision notes

1. Courts police legality, bias and fairness; they do not re-conduct the expert comparative-merit
   assessment itself.
2. *Ashok Kumar Yadav* (1985): bias and unfair interview design remain reviewable, without fixing a
   universal interview-weight number.
3. *K. Manjusree* (2008): a minimum interview benchmark cannot be introduced after the process where
   the framework did not prescribe it in advance.
4. *Tej Prakash Pathak*, 2024 INSC 847 (Constitution Bench): reaffirms rule-stability, grounded in
   Articles 14 and 16 — criteria cannot change after commencement unless the governing rules
   themselves lawfully permit it.
5. *Shankarsan Dash* (1991): select-list inclusion creates no indefeasible right to appointment.
6. Government may leave vacancies unfilled for bona fide reasons, but not arbitrarily.
7. *M.V. Thimmaiah* (2007): expert recommendations are not ordinarily appellate-reviewable except

8. The four cases collectively draw one line: legality/fairness reviewable, comparative merit
   judgment generally not.
9. This division mirrors the Commission's own institutional purpose — expert comparative judgment is
   exactly why the Commission, not a court, conducts selection.
10. Judicial restraint targets only the narrow band of genuine expert discretion, not legality or
    fairness.
11. Do not rely on a single case to represent the entire doctrine — examiners expect the complete
    four-case toolkit.
12. *Tej Prakash Pathak* is the most recent authoritative restatement and should anchor any current
    answer on rule-stability.

### Concept check

**Question:** A selection board introduces a new minimum-marks cut-off in interviews only after
written results are out, even though the original notification said nothing about any interview
cut-off. A disappointed candidate challenges this. Which doctrine applies, and what is the likely
outcome?

**Model answer:** This is squarely the *K. Manjusree*/*Tej Prakash Pathak* "rules-of-the-game"
doctrine. Introducing a new interview cut-off after the process has begun, where the governing
framework did not prescribe one in advance, violates the stability principle grounded in Articles 14
and 16. The likely outcome, consistent with both precedents, is that the newly introduced cut-off
would be struck down as arbitrary and the selection would need to proceed, or be reassessed, under
the originally notified criteria.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not conclude that courts will simply reconduct the interview or
re-rank candidates themselves. The remedy targets the unlawfully introduced criterion, not a judicial
re-selection of candidates.

### Lesson-local Mains practice

**Original question (15 marks):** "Judicial review of Public Service Commission selections reflects
calibrated restraint rather than either total deference or appellate correction." Examine with
reference to the relevant case law. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Indian courts review Commission selections along a precise, consistent line:
legality and fairness are reviewable; comparative expert judgment generally is not. *Ashok Kumar
Yadav v. State of Haryana* (1985) establishes that bias and unfair interview design remain
reviewable even within a formally expert process, without fixing any universal interview-weight
rule. *K. Manjusree v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (2008) struck down a minimum interview benchmark
introduced only after the selection process, and the Constitution Bench in *Tej Prakash Pathak v.
Rajasthan High Court* (2024 INSC 847) reaffirmed this rules-of-the-game stability principle,
grounding it in Articles 14 and 16: criteria cannot change after commencement unless the governing
framework itself lawfully permits a non-arbitrary course. *Shankarsan Dash v. Union of India* (1991)
confirms that select-list inclusion creates no indefeasible right to appointment, though government
cannot arbitrarily refuse to fill vacancies for improper reasons. *M.V. Thimmaiah v. UPSC* (2007)
confines review of expert recommendations to mala fides or serious statutory-rule violation, refusing
an appellate re-ranking role for courts.

Together, these four precedents show neither abdication (courts do intervene on bias, rule
stability, arbitrariness and serious rule violation) nor overreach (courts do not re-conduct the
comparative merit assessment that is the Commission's own institutional expertise). This calibrated
restraint preserves both constitutional accountability and the specialised purpose for which the
Commission was created.

articulation of the "legality/fairness reviewable, merit judgment not" dividing line (4); coherent
250-word structure connecting the cases to one consistent principle with a stated verdict (3).

What remains comparatively unprotected is appointment screening and operational capacity. No adopted
constitutional collegium or equivalent mechanism governs how members are chosen in the first place;
proposals for greater appointment transparency, predictable vacancy-filling, stronger rule-based
staffing and cyber-security capacity, better-reasoned grievance decisions, and genuine legislative
scrutiny of Article 323 memoranda remain reform arguments, not current law, and must be labelled as
such. The Public Examinations Act and Rules, 2024, and the 15 March 2024 consultation-exemption
amendment are the operative adopted instruments in this space; a PSC-appointment collegium is not.
Genuine reform must strengthen both dimensions together — individual-member independence is already
constitutionally mature, while appointment transparency and State-level operational capacity are the
unfinished half of the reform agenda, without over-correcting into Union-controlled appointment that
would weaken federal ownership of a State-rooted institution.

**Quantified rubric (20 marks):** Accurate statement of individual-member safeguards with correct
Articles (5); accurate statement of the SPSC federal asymmetry (4); correct identification of
adopted law (2024 Act/Rules, consultation-exemption amendment) versus reform proposal (collegium,
transparency measures) (5); explicit rejection of Union-controlled appointment as the solution (3);
coherent 250-word structure with a stated verdict (3).

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

**Cumulative check 1 (Lessons 1-3 — foundation and institutional forms)**

**Question:** A State wants to reduce administrative duplication by combining its SPSC with a
neighbouring State's SPSC into one shared body for ordinary recruitment, while separately asking the
UPSC to help both States run a joint scheme for a small forensic-science cadre. Identify the two
distinct constitutional devices involved.

**Model answer:** Combining two States' SPSCs into one shared body is a **Joint State Public Service
Commission under Article 315(2)** — it requires both State legislatures to pass resolutions and
Parliament to enact a law providing for the Commission's appointment. The separate forensic-science
cadre arrangement is a **joint recruitment scheme under Article 320(2)** — the UPSC assists the two
States in framing and operating a shared scheme for posts needing special qualifications, without
creating any new Commission. The two devices can coexist: the States could have one Joint Commission
for ordinary recruitment and still ask UPSC for help with one specialised joint scheme if that fits
their specific need, though in practice a single Joint Commission would likely also handle such
specialised recruitment itself once created.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not assume "two States cooperating on recruitment" always means a
Joint Commission — check whether a new institution is actually being created (315(2)) or whether
existing institutions are merely sharing a scheme (320(2)).

**Cumulative check 2 (Lessons 4-6 — removal, service conditions, independence synthesis)**

**Question:** Explain, in one connected argument, why Articles 317, 318 and 319 must all operate
together for the independence claim in Lesson 6 to hold, using a concrete failure scenario for each
Article's absence.

**Model answer:** Without Article 317's differentiated, high-threshold removal process, a government
could remove an inconvenient member at will, making Article 316's fixed tenure meaningless. Without
Article 318's non-disadvantage proviso, a government could keep a member in office while punishing
them through degraded service conditions, achieving the same intimidating effect without formally
removing anyone. Without Article 319's post-tenure restrictions, a government could instead reward
compliant behaviour with a lucrative post-retirement posting, achieving influence through promise
rather than threat. Each Article closes a different channel through which independence could
otherwise be undermined; removing any one of the three would leave a clear, exploitable gap.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not treat these three Articles as redundant protections of the same
thing — each targets a structurally different leverage point (removal threat, service-condition
threat, post-tenure incentive).

**Cumulative check 3 (Lessons 7-9 — functions, accountability and boundary)**

**Question:** A candidate argues that because Article 320(4) exempts consultation on the "manner" of
reservation implementation, and because the 2024 lateral-entry consultation-exemption regulation
also reduces UPSC's role for certain posts, "UPSC's advisory function is steadily shrinking across
the board." Evaluate.

**Model answer:** This overstates two narrow, textually bounded exceptions into a general trend.
Article 320(4) removes only the manner-of-implementation question for Articles 16(4)/335 reservation
from mandatory consultation — the Commission's broader advisory catalogue under Article 320(3)
(methods, principles, suitability, discipline, specified claims) remains fully intact and
unaffected. The 15 March 2024 regulation similarly exempts consultation only for specified Group A/B
posts up to pay level 13A filled through named modes — it does not touch the Commission's role in
ordinary Civil Services Examination recruitment, which remains the overwhelming majority of its
work. Two narrow, specifically bounded exemptions do not establish a general shrinking trend; each
must be evaluated on its own precise textual scope.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not chain together unrelated bounded exceptions into an unsupported
general narrative — always check each exception's exact scope independently.

**Cumulative check 4 (Lessons 10-12 — review, integrity and reform)**

**Question:** Construct a one-paragraph synthesis explaining why "the Commission's biggest
vulnerability today is not legal protection but practical capacity and transparency," drawing on
material from at least three different lessons.

**Model answer:** Judicial review (Lesson 10) confirms that legality, bias and rule-stability
concerns are well-policed through the four-case toolkit, leaving comparative merit judgment properly
reserved to the Commission's own expertise. Examination-security law (Lesson 11) shows that where
genuine integrity failures occur — paper leaks, SPSC capacity gaps — they are addressed through
statutory/operational instruments (the 2024 Act/Rules, horizontal learning), not through any failure
of constitutional tenure protection. The reform matrix (Lesson 12) confirms that no adopted
collegium or equivalent appointment-screening mechanism exists, and that the genuinely open reform
agenda concerns appointment transparency, predictable vacancy-filling and operational capacity —
not weak legal protection for sitting members, which Articles 316-319 already secure robustly.

**Misconception to avoid:** do not propose "stronger constitutional protection for members" as the
solution to integrity or capacity problems that actually arise from appointment-stage transparency
gaps or operational/administrative weaknesses.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

(distinct from lesson-local questions)

**Final 10-mark question:** Distinguish the three institutional forms a Public Service Commission
can take under Article 315, and state which authority appoints each. (Answer in 150 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Article 315(1) provides the ordinary case: one Commission for the Union (UPSC,
President-appointed) and one for each State (SPSC, Governor-appointed). Article 315(2) authorises a
**Joint State Public Service Commission** when two or more States pass the required legislative
resolutions and Parliament enacts a law providing for its appointment; its Chairman and members are
appointed by the **President**, like the UPSC. Article 315(4) permits the UPSC, on a State Governor's
request and with the President's approval, to **agree to serve** all or part of that State's own
Commission needs — this creates no new body; the existing UPSC simply takes on the additional work.
The three forms differ in trigger, creating process and appointing authority, and must not be
collapsed into one undifferentiated description of "the Commission."

**Quantified rubric (10 marks):** Correctly states all three forms (315(1), 315(2), 315(4)) (4);
correctly states the appointing authority for each (3); correct trigger mechanism for the Joint
Commission (2); coherent 150-word structure (1).

**Final 15-mark question:** "Article 317 differentiates the removal of a Public Service Commission
member from the removal of a judge, even though both enjoy strong tenure protection." Examine the
basis for this differentiation and its practical significance. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Judicial removal under Articles 124/217 requires a parliamentary motion, special
majorities in each House, and a judge-conduct inquiry — a legislative-cum-judicial process placing
Parliament at the centre. Article 317 builds a different, executive-cum-judicial architecture for
Commission-member removal. For misbehaviour, the President refers the matter to the Supreme Court
for an inquiry under the Article 145 procedure; the Court reports whether the member ought to be
removed, and the President then decides and acts — Parliament has no formal vote. Article 317(4)
extends this route by deeming interest in a government contract to be misbehaviour, subject to the
incorporated-company exception. Separately, Article 317(3) authorises the President to remove a
member directly — without any Supreme Court inquiry — for adjudged insolvency, outside paid
employment, or infirmity of mind or body, since these are largely verifiable facts rather than
disputed value judgments requiring independent adjudication.

This differentiation matters practically in two ways. First, it makes incapacity-based removal
administratively workable without requiring a full judicial inquiry for every case, while still
reserving the heavier, judicially-supervised route for genuinely contested misbehaviour allegations.
Second, by keeping Parliament out of the process entirely (unlike judicial removal), it avoids
converting ordinary Commission-member removal into a politically charged legislative event, which
could itself threaten the member's independence from legislative-majority pressure rather than
protecting it. The design therefore calibrates process weight to the type of allegation, rather than
applying one uniformly heavy procedure to every ground.

**Quantified rubric (15 marks):** Accurate contrast with judicial removal process (4); correct
statement of Art 317(1)/(4) misbehaviour route (4); correct statement of Art 317(3) direct grounds
(4); clearly reasoned practical significance with two distinct points (2); coherent 250-word
structure with a stated verdict (1).

**Final 20-mark question:** "The constitutional design of the Public Service Commission protects the
sitting member far more thoroughly than it protects the integrity of the appointment and examination
processes around that member." Critically examine. (Answer in 250 words.)

**Mains model answer:** Articles 316-319 build a genuinely thorough protective architecture around a
sitting Commission member: fixed tenure with a near-half experience requirement (316), a
differentiated removal process separating misbehaviour (requiring a Supreme Court inquiry) from
direct incapacity/conflict grounds (317), a non-disadvantage proviso on service conditions (318),
and office-specific post-tenure restrictions preventing future-reward influence (319). Articles 322
and 323 reinforce this with charged expenditure and mandatory reporting of rejected advice.

By contrast, two adjacent processes remain comparatively less constitutionally secured. First,
**appointment itself** is not screened by any adopted collegium or equivalent mechanism — Governors
and the President retain unconstrained discretion in choosing whom to appoint, even though the
appointee will then enjoy the full protective architecture above. Second, **examination integrity**
— question-paper security, candidate identity verification, computer-based-test infrastructure —
depends on the Public Examinations Act and Rules, 2024, and State-level administrative capacity,
neither of which is constitutionally entrenched the way member tenure is; SPSC integrity and
capacity are acknowledged to vary materially by State.

The critical conclusion is not that the constitutional design is flawed, but that it was built to
solve a specific problem — protecting an *already-appointed* advisor from executive pressure — and
was never designed to solve the separate problems of appointment screening or examination-event
security, which require statutory and administrative solutions layered on top. A complete reform
agenda must strengthen the comparatively under-protected appointment and examination layers without
assuming they are already covered by the strong member-tenure architecture.

**Quantified rubric (20 marks):** Accurate, complete statement of Articles 316-319 and 322-323
protections (7); correctly identifies appointment-screening as comparatively unprotected, citing the
absence of an adopted collegium (5); correctly identifies examination-integrity/SPSC-capacity as a
separate, statutorily-addressed layer (5); balanced critical conclusion rejecting both "design is
flawed" and "nothing more is needed" extremes (2); coherent 250-word structure with a stated verdict
(1).

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

**Foundation and institutional design**

1. A Public Service Commission is an insulated advisory institution, not a personnel government
   (Lesson 1).
2. Evolution: 1853 Charter Act -> 1854 Macaulay Committee -> 1919 Act -> 1924 Lee Commission -> 1
   October 1926 first PSC -> 1935 Act Federal/Provincial/Joint design -> 1950 Articles 315-323.
3. The 1950 constitutional innovation was entrenchment (tenure, composition, reporting fixed beyond
   ordinary law), not invention of the institution itself.
4. Articles 315-323 sit in Part XIV, Chapter II, distinct from 308-314 (Public Services) and 323A
   (Tribunals).
5. Article 315(1): UPSC for the Union, one SPSC per State — the ordinary case.
6. Article 315(2): Joint State PSC — State legislative resolutions + parliamentary law; a new
   institution.
7. Article 315(4): UPSC serving a State — Governor's request + President's approval + UPSC's
   agreement; no new institution.
8. Article 320(2): joint recruitment scheme for specially-qualified posts — a function, not an
   institution.

**Composition, tenure and appointment**

9. President appoints UPSC/Joint Commission members; Governor appoints SPSC members.
10. President alone removes members of every Commission form — the appointment/removal asymmetry is
    deliberate, protecting SPSC members from State-level retaliation.
11. Proviso to Art 316(1): as nearly as may be one-half of members need ten years of specified prior
    government service (including pre-Constitution Crown/Indian-State service).
12. Art 316(2): six-year term from entering office, or age ceiling (65 UPSC; 62 SPSC/Joint),
    whichever is earlier.
13. Resignation: to the President (UPSC/Joint) or Governor (SPSC); Art 316(3) bars reappointment to
    the same office.
14. Art 316(1A): acting Chair appointed by President (UPSC/Joint) or Governor (SPSC) when the Chair's
    office is vacant or the Chair cannot act; ends when a new or resuming Chair takes charge.

**Removal, service conditions and post-tenure restriction**

15. Art 317(1): misbehaviour -> Presidential reference -> Supreme Court inquiry (Art 145 procedure)
    -> SC reports -> President decides.
16. Art 317(4): interest in a government contract is deemed misbehaviour, except as a member in
    common with other members of an incorporated company — routed through the 317(1) process.
17. Art 317(3): direct Presidential removal, no SC inquiry needed, for adjudged insolvency, outside
    paid employment, or infirmity of mind/body.
18. Suspension: President suspends UPSC/Joint Commission members; Governor suspends SPSC members;
    lasts until the President acts on the SC's report.
19. "JPSC" today usually means Jharkhand PSC (an SPSC), not the constitutional Joint Commission.
20. Art 318: regulates member/staff numbers and conditions; protects members (not staff) against
    disadvantageous changes after appointment.
21. Art 319: UPSC Chairman — no further government employment; SPSC Chairman — UPSC Chair/member or
    another SPSC Chair; UPSC member — UPSC Chair or an SPSC Chair; SPSC member — UPSC Chair/member or
    same/another SPSC Chair. Every row also bars other government employment.
22. Eligibility under Art 319 is not a right to actual appointment; "employment" must be read against
    the legal character of the later post.

**Independence synthesis**

23. Independence is a lifecycle: entry (316) -> service (317, 318) -> exit (319), reinforced by
    charged expenditure (322) and reporting (323).
24. Genuine limits: no adopted appointment-screening collegium; advice remains directory; operational
    dependence on the executive persists in practice; SPSC capacity/integrity varies by State.
25. The qualified verdict throughout this topic: constitutionally robust, administratively
    incomplete.

**Functions under Article 320**

26. Art 320(1): duty to conduct examinations — not a monopoly over every recruitment mode.
27. Art 320(3): consultation catalogue — methods/principles, suitability, disciplinary matters,
    specified legal-cost/injury-pension claims, referred matters.
28. Methods (route), principles (rule) and suitability (candidate evaluation) are three distinct
    questions.
29. Art 320(3) proviso: President/Governor may make regulations exempting specified matters from
    consultation.
30. Art 320(4): exempts consultation only on the manner of giving effect to Art 16(4)/335 reservation,
    and on connected beneficiary claims — a narrow exception, not a blanket exclusion.
31. Art 320(5): exemption regulations must be laid for at least 14 days before the legislature and
    remain subject to legislative modification, repeal or amendment.

**Advice, accountability and the rest of Part XIV**

32. *Manbodhan Lal Srivastava* (1957): Art 320(3) consultation is directory, not mandatory;
    non-consultation alone does not automatically invalidate a decision.
33. Art 323: UPSC reports to the President, who lays the report + non-acceptance memorandum before
    Parliament; SPSC reports to the Governor, laid before the State legislature.
34. Art 321: legislative extension of Commission functions to local authority/statutory body
    corporate/public institution services — an enabling route, not automatic.
35. Art 322: UPSC/SPSC expenditure (including salaries, allowances, pensions) charged on the
    Consolidated Fund of India/the State, bypassing the ordinary annual vote.

**Recruitment boundary and the current linkage**

36. UPSC examines, selects, recommends; it does not frame recruitment rules, issue appointment
    orders, allocate cadres, or adjudicate service disputes.
37. Recruitment chain: post sanction -> rules -> UPSC process -> recommendation -> appointment order
    -> probation/service rules.
38. Art 312/All-India Services belong to Public Services; CAT (Art 323A) adjudicates disputes — both
    are distinct from this topic's recommend-and-advise function.
39. Lateral recruitment is a mode of filling posts, not a constitutional amendment; the 15 March 2024
    regulation exempts only specified Group A/B posts up to pay level 13A, filled through named
    modes, from ordinary consultation.
40. Current linkage (re-verified 2 October 2026): the August 2024 45-post lateral-entry advertisement
    was withdrawn over the absence of a reservation mechanism for standalone specialist posts; as of
    June 2026 the Minister of State confirmed the scheme remains "in play on paper" with no agreed
    mechanism found; status unresolved through October 2026.

**Judicial review**

41. *Ashok Kumar Yadav* (1985): bias/unfair interview design remains reviewable.
42. *K. Manjusree* (2008) / *Tej Prakash Pathak*, 2024 INSC 847 (Constitution Bench): rules-of-the-
    game stability, grounded in Articles 14 and 16.
43. *Shankarsan Dash* (1991): no indefeasible right to appointment from a select list; arbitrary
    refusal remains reviewable.
44. *M.V. Thimmaiah* (2007): limited review of expert recommendations — mala fides/serious rule
    violation only.
45. Courts police legality and fairness; they do not re-conduct the Commission's comparative merit
    assessment.

**Examination integrity and reform**

46. Public Examinations Act, 2024: Schedule covers UPSC examinations; Section 3 lists unfair means;
    offences are cognizable, non-bailable, non-compoundable.
47. Public Examinations Rules, 2024: defines accountable roles; Rule 5 sets CBT security/
    infrastructure standards.
48. SPSC capacity and integrity vary by State — never generalise from one example.
49. National Conference of Chairmen of SPSCs: first held 1949; UPSC Chair as ex officio chair since
    1999 — a cooperative, non-binding forum.
50. No adopted constitutional collegium for PSC appointments exists — always label it a reform
    proposal, never current law.
51. Seven-part reform framework: appointment transparency, predictable vacancy-filling, staffing/
    cyber capacity, grievance quality, legislative scrutiny of Art 323 memoranda, horizontal learning
    without erasing State identity, lawful publication of reasons/outcomes.
52. Federal design: State-rooted appointment/accountability paired with Union-insulated removal for
    an SPSC — a deliberate asymmetry, not an inconsistency.
53. A Union-controlled appointment system would over-correct State-level capacity problems and
    weaken federal ownership — reject this as a recommended reform.
54. Answer method: claim -> evidence (exact Article/case) -> analysis (why it supports the claim) ->
    qualification (genuine limit) -> verdict.
55. No verified UPSC Prelims or Mains PYQ is routed to this topic through 2026 — state this
    transparently in any answer that might otherwise search for a precedent question.
56. The current UPSC Chairman (Dr Ajay Kumar, in office since 15 May 2025, re-verified 2 October
    2026) is a date-controlled administrative fact, never a frozen syllabus point.
