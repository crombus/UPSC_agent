# Emergency Provisions — Live Session Edition

Emergency powers answer a difficult constitutional question: how can a democracy respond to war, rebellion, State breakdown or financial collapse without abandoning constitutional government itself?

The answer is not one unlimited emergency power. Part XVIII creates three different regimes with different triggers, procedures, effects and safeguards. The learning sequence below moves from that common design to National Emergency, rights, President's Rule, judicial control and Financial Emergency.

### 1. Why emergency provisions exist

Ordinary constitutional arrangements assume that institutions can continue working through ordinary law. War, external attack, armed rebellion, collapse of constitutional government in a State or a threat to public credit may make ordinary allocation of power inadequate.

Part XVIII therefore creates temporary centralising capacity. Its stated constitutional function is protective: safeguarding sovereignty, unity, integrity, security, democratic government and the Constitution.

The underlying logic is:

```text
Normal allocation may be insufficient
                 ↓
Temporary constitutional concentration of power
                 ↓
Threat addressed under specified procedure
                 ↓
Ordinary constitutional operation restored
```

This is constitutional centralisation, not permission for an extra-constitutional regime. The emergency authority, approval process, duration and consequences all remain derived from the Constitution.

### 2. The complete Part XVIII article rail

| Article | Function |
|---:|---|
| 352 | Proclamation of National Emergency |
| 353 | Executive and legislative effects of a National Emergency |
| 354 | Modification of constitutional revenue-distribution provisions |
| 355 | Union duty to protect States and ensure constitutional government |
| 356 | Proclamation on failure of constitutional machinery in a State |
| 357 | Exercise of State legislative power during Article 356 |
| 358 | Effect of an external National Emergency on Article 19 |
| 359 | Presidential order affecting judicial enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights |
| 360 | Financial Emergency |

The intervening Articles are not decorative. Articles 353–354 explain what Article 352 does; Article 355 supplies a Union duty; Article 357 explains legislative substitution under Article 356; and Articles 358–359 create two different rights consequences.

### 3. Three regimes, three different failures

| Regime | Constitutional trigger | Basic consequence |
|---|---|---|
| National Emergency | Security of India or part threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion | Union executive and legislative capacity expands; States continue |
| President's Rule | State government cannot be carried on according to the Constitution | State Council of Ministers is displaced; State legislative power moves to Parliament |
| Financial Emergency | Financial stability or credit of India or any part is threatened | Union obtains exceptional fiscal-direction powers |

The Constitution itself uses “Proclamation of Emergency” for Article 352. “President's Rule”, “State Emergency” and “Constitutional Emergency” are conventional labels for Article 356; the constitutional text does not call Article 356 an emergency.

### 4. Federalism changes mode; it does not vanish

B.R. Ambedkar explained that India could operate federally in normal times and as though unitary during an emergency. This does not mean that every emergency produces the same unitary effect:

- Article 352 leaves State governments and legislatures functioning, but subjects them to expanded Union power.
- Article 356 displaces the elected State executive and transfers State legislative authority.
- Article 360 centralises financial direction without automatically removing State political institutions.

**Example:** If Parliament legislates on a State List subject during a National Emergency, the State legislature has not ceased to exist. Its competence becomes subject to Parliament's overriding emergency competence.

**Limit of the example:** Under Article 356, the change is sharper because Parliament exercises the State legislature's power and the Assembly may be suspended or dissolved.

### 5. Objection, reply and residual risk

**Objection:** A Constitution that can become unitary in a crisis contains the seeds of dictatorship.

**Reply:** The alternative—no capacity to respond to war, rebellion or constitutional collapse—may itself destroy constitutional government. The defensible design is therefore exceptional power combined with parliamentary renewal, judicial review, rights firewalls and restoration of ordinary government.

**Residual risk:** Formal safeguards do not eliminate partisan incentives. The history of 1975 and repeated misuse of Article 356 shows that legal powers must also be disciplined by courts, political competition and constitutional conventions.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to say that every proclamation under Part XVIII simply “suspends federalism”?

**Model answer:** The three regimes alter federal operation differently. Article 352 expands Union executive and legislative power while State institutions continue; Article 356 displaces the State executive and transfers State legislative authority; Article 360 centralises fiscal direction. None abolishes the Constitution or State identity, and each remains subject to its own procedure and limits.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat Article 356 as merely a smaller territorial version of Article 352; their triggers, institutional effects, majorities and durations are different.

### Original Mains practice

**Mains question:** “Emergency provisions permit constitutional centralisation; they do not create an extra-constitutional State.” Explain. **(10 marks, 150 words)**

**Mains model:**
Part XVIII enables the Union to meet exceptional threats while remaining within constitutional authority. Article 352 addresses war, external aggression or armed rebellion and expands Union power while State institutions continue. Article 356 responds to failure of constitutional machinery by displacing the State executive and transferring legislative authority to Parliament. Article 360 permits exceptional financial directions.

These powers are not extra-constitutional because their triggers, parliamentary approval, duration, renewal and consequences are textually regulated. The 44th Amendment strengthened Cabinet, parliamentary, rights and judicial safeguards; *S.R. Bommai* made Article 356 reviewable and protected the floor test.

However, constitutional form alone cannot prevent partisan misuse, as 1975 and the history of President's Rule demonstrate. Emergency legitimacy therefore depends on necessity, proportionality, review and restoration of normal government.

```text
                      │
             ┌────────┴────────┐
             │                 │
            No                Yes
             │                 │
     Article 352 unavailable   What is the ground?

            War       External aggression   Armed rebellion
             │                 │                 │
       External Emergency                Internal Emergency
                               │

```

The trigger is about a threat to security, not ordinary disorder, maladministration or political inconvenience.

### 1. The three constitutional grounds

Article 352 permits a proclamation where the security of India or any part is threatened by:

1. **war**;
2. **external aggression**; or
3. **armed rebellion**.

War generally indicates an organised armed conflict. External aggression is wider: a hostile external act may threaten security without a formally declared war. Armed rebellion is the internal ground and requires a grave, organised challenge involving arms.

The Constitution also allows action on **imminent danger**. The State need not wait until attack or rebellion has fully materialised, but anticipation must concern one of the constitutionally named threats.

### 2. Why “internal disturbance” was removed

The original text permitted proclamation for “internal disturbance”. That phrase was wider and less determinate than “armed rebellion”. It was used for the 1975 proclamation.

The 44th Amendment replaced it with “armed rebellion”, deliberately raising the threshold.

```text
Internal disturbance
  ├─ could include serious unrest without organised arms
  └─ broad and open to political interpretation

Armed rebellion
  ├─ organised internal challenge
  ├─ armed character
  └─ narrower constitutional threshold
```

**UPSC trap:** Article 355 still uses “external aggression and internal disturbance” when describing the Union's protective duty. The replacement occurred in Article 352, not everywhere in the Constitution.

### 3. External and internal National Emergency

| Classification | Ground |
|---|---|
| External Emergency | War or external aggression |
| Internal Emergency | Armed rebellion |

The classification matters later because Article 358 operates only during an Emergency proclaimed on grounds of war or external aggression. It does not operate merely because the proclamation is called a National Emergency.

### 4. Territorial reach

A proclamation may cover:

- all of India; or
- a specified part of India.

The 42nd Amendment enabled territorial limitation. This creates a useful distinction:

```text
Ground concerns national security
              ≠
Proclamation must cover the whole country
```

Yet executive and legislative consequences may extend beyond the territory directly placed under the proclamation where the security connection requires it. Territorial limitation therefore does not always mean that every legal effect is territorially sealed.

### 5. More than one proclamation

The Constitution permits different proclamations based on war, external aggression, armed rebellion or imminent danger even while another proclamation is operating. This possibility was added during the 1975 amendment period.

**Example:** An external-aggression proclamation could already exist when a separately grounded internal armed-rebellion threat arises.

**Limit:** The power to issue multiple proclamations does not relax the trigger or approval requirements for each proclamation.

### 6. Near-neighbour distinctions

| Situation | Correct constitutional route |
|---|---|
| Armed revolt threatening India's security | Article 352 may arise |
| Violent local disorder not reaching armed rebellion | Ordinary law, public-order machinery or Article 355 measures; not automatically Article 352 |
| State ministry loses majority | Floor test and State-government rules; possibly Article 356 only if constitutional government cannot be formed |
| State refuses a Union direction | Article 365 may support an Article 356 conclusion; it is not Article 352 |

### 7. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** “Imminent danger” permits speculative emergency declarations.

**Reply:** Preventive capacity is unavoidable where waiting for an attack would defeat national defence. The ground must still be war, external aggression or armed rebellion, and the proclamation remains subject to Cabinet advice, parliamentary approval and judicial review.

**Residual:** Courts may examine relevance, mala fides or perversity but are unlikely to replace executive security assessment with their own. The quality of the recorded material therefore remains crucial.

1. Article 352 concerns security of India or any part.
2. The grounds are war, external aggression and armed rebellion.
3. Imminent danger is sufficient; actual outbreak need not be awaited.
4. War and external aggression produce an External Emergency.
5. Armed rebellion produces an Internal Emergency.
6. The 44th Amendment replaced internal disturbance with armed rebellion.
7. Article 355 still contains the phrase internal disturbance.
8. The proclamation may cover all or part of India.
9. More than one differently grounded proclamation may coexist.
10. Public disorder is not automatically armed rebellion.

12. Every proclamation separately requires constitutional procedure.

### Concept check

**Question:** A large but unarmed civil disturbance affects several districts. Can Article 352 be justified merely because public order has seriously deteriorated?

**Model answer:** No. Serious disorder is not by itself one of Article 352's grounds. The internal ground is armed rebellion. The Union may use ordinary legal powers or discharge its Article 355 duty, but Article 352 requires evidence that the security of India or a part is threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion, including imminent danger thereof.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not import Article 355's phrase “internal disturbance” back into Article 352.

### Original Mains practice

**Mains question:** Analyse how the 44th Amendment changed the substantive threshold for proclaiming an Internal Emergency. **(10 marks, 150 words)**

**Mains model:**
The original Article 352 permitted National Emergency on the ground of “internal disturbance”, a broad expression capable of covering unrest short of organised armed revolt. The 1975 Emergency demonstrated the democratic danger of this elasticity.

The 44th Amendment substituted “armed rebellion”. The change requires an internal threat with an organised armed character and therefore excludes ordinary disorder, protest, maladministration or political instability. It also complements procedural safeguards such as written Cabinet advice, special-majority approval and periodic renewal.

The amendment does not make Article 352 purely reactive: imminent danger of armed rebellion may still justify action. Nor did it remove “internal disturbance” from Article 355, which describes the Union's protective duty.

Thus, the 44th Amendment retained preventive capacity while narrowing executive discretion and making an Internal Emergency harder to justify.

### Visual: life cycle of a National Emergency

```text
Written recommendation of Union Cabinet
                    ↓
President issues proclamation
                    ↓
Both Houses approve within 1 month
       special majority in each House
                    ↓
Valid for 6 months
                    ↓
Fresh special-majority approval every 6 months
                    ↓
President revokes voluntarily
             OR
Lok Sabha disapproves by simple majority → must revoke
```

Each stage answers a different danger: unilateral initiation, indefinite executive continuation and absence of a legislative exit.

### 1. Written Cabinet recommendation

The President may proclaim a National Emergency only after receiving the recommendation of the **Union Cabinet in writing**.

“Cabinet” here means the Prime Minister and ministers of Cabinet rank. The requirement is not satisfied by the Prime Minister acting alone.

This safeguard directly responds to 1975, when the Cabinet was informed after the proclamation had been advised.

### 2. Approval within one month

Both Houses of Parliament must approve the proclamation within one month. Approval requires a **special majority** in each House:

- a majority of the total membership of that House; and
- not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

The 44th Amendment shortened the approval window from two months and replaced the earlier simple-majority standard.

### 3. Lok Sabha dissolution during initial approval

If the Lok Sabha is dissolved when the proclamation is issued, or is dissolved within the one-month period without approving it:

1. the Rajya Sabha must approve the proclamation in the meantime; and
2. the proclamation survives until thirty days from the first sitting of the reconstituted Lok Sabha.

The new Lok Sabha must approve it within that protected period.

**Reasoning:** Dissolution should not create a national-security vacuum, but the Rajya Sabha cannot permanently substitute for democratic approval by the newly elected House.

### 4. Six-month renewal and indefinite maximum

Once approved, the proclamation continues for six months. It may continue indefinitely, but only through fresh approval by both Houses every six months, again by special majority.

“Indefinite” therefore means no fixed constitutional maximum, not executive permanence.

If the Lok Sabha is dissolved before approving a further continuation, the same Rajya Sabha plus thirty-day-new-Lok-Sabha arrangement applies.

### 5. Revocation and legislative disapproval

The President may revoke the proclamation at any time. Revocation needs no parliamentary approval.

The 44th Amendment also created a compulsory route:

- if the Lok Sabha passes a resolution disapproving continuation, the President must revoke the proclamation;
- the disapproval resolution needs only a simple majority;
- at least one-tenth of the total Lok Sabha membership may give written notice to the Speaker—or to the President if the House is not in session;
- a special sitting must then be held within fourteen days.

### 6. Approval and disapproval are not mirror images

| Question | Approval/continuation | Disapproval |
|---|---|---|
| Which House? | Both Houses | Lok Sabha only |
| Majority | Special majority | Simple majority |
| Function | Authorises exceptional power | Ends democratic consent |

The asymmetry is deliberate. Exceptional power is difficult to authorise but easier for the directly elected House to terminate.

### 7. Judicial review

The 38th Amendment attempted to make presidential satisfaction final and immune from review. The 44th Amendment removed that immunity.

In *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980), the Supreme Court recognised that a proclamation could be challenged for mala fides or where the basis was wholly extraneous, irrelevant, absurd or perverse.

Judicial review does not convert courts into war rooms. It checks constitutional relevance and good faith.

### 8. Example and limit

**Example:** Suppose the proclamation is issued while the Lok Sabha stands dissolved. Rajya Sabha approval can temporarily preserve it, but the newly constituted Lok Sabha receives a real opportunity to decide.

**Limit:** Rajya Sabha approval alone cannot continue the proclamation beyond the constitutionally protected thirty-day period after the new Lok Sabha first sits.

### 9. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** A ruling party with a large parliamentary majority can mechanically renew Emergency forever.

**Reply:** Renewal requires the demanding special-majority formula in both Houses every six months, while a simple-majority Lok Sabha disapproval compels revocation. Judicial review and electoral accountability operate alongside formal voting rules.

**Residual:** Strong single-party control can weaken political scrutiny even where formal thresholds are met. The constitutional design reduces, but cannot abolish, political risk.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the Constitution require a special majority to continue a National Emergency but only a simple majority in the Lok Sabha to disapprove it?

**Model answer:** Exceptional power should be difficult to authorise and renew, so both Houses must use a special majority. Democratic withdrawal should be easier, so the directly elected Lok Sabha may disapprove continuation by simple majority. This asymmetry favours restoration of ordinary constitutional government when consent weakens.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not say that both Houses must pass a special-majority resolution to revoke the Emergency.

### Original Mains practice

**Mains question:** Examine whether the post-44th Article 352 procedure adequately prevents unilateral executive Emergency. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Mains model:**
The post-44th procedure substantially restricts unilateral executive action. A National Emergency requires written recommendation of the Union Cabinet, preventing the Prime Minister alone from initiating it. Both Houses must approve within one month by a special majority, and continuation requires fresh special-majority approval every six months.

Democratic exit is easier than entry: Lok Sabha disapproval by simple majority compels revocation, while one-tenth of its total membership can obtain a special sitting within fourteen days. Dissolution provisions preserve security continuity but require the reconstituted Lok Sabha to approve within thirty days of its first sitting. The President may also revoke voluntarily.

Judicial review, restored after the 38th Amendment's immunity was removed, permits scrutiny for mala fides or irrelevant and perverse material.

Yet safeguards cannot fully neutralise a dominant executive controlling Parliament. Their adequacy therefore depends on opposition scrutiny, judicial independence and public accountability. The framework is robust, but political constitutionalism remains indispensable.

## Lesson 4 — Effects of National Emergency on federal government

### Visual: three channels of federal transformation

```text
NATIONAL EMERGENCY
        │
 ┌──────┼─────────────┬─────────────────┐
 │      │             │                 │
Executive          Legislative        Financial
directions on      Parliament on      President modifies
any matter         State List         revenue distribution
 │                  │                 │
States continue     State laws remain  order laid before
to administer       but Union law       Parliament; limited
                    overrides           temporal operation
        │
Terms of Lok Sabha/Assemblies may be extended
```

National Emergency changes the allocation and priority of power. It does not automatically dismiss State governments or dissolve legislatures.

### 1. Executive effect

During ordinary times, Union directions to States are limited to specified constitutional settings. During a National Emergency, Union executive power extends to directing a State on the manner in which its executive power is exercised on **any matter**.

The State government still exists and administers the State. The change is one of intensified Union control, not executive replacement.

### 2. Legislative effect

Parliament may legislate on subjects in the State List. State legislatures retain their own power, but parliamentary emergency law has overriding force.

Laws made by Parliament on State subjects under the emergency competence become inoperative six months after the Emergency ends, to the extent that Parliament would otherwise lack competence. Past operation and things already done remain protected.

When Parliament is not in session, the President may promulgate ordinances on State subjects within this enlarged field.

Parliament may also confer powers and impose duties on Union authorities for implementing laws made under the expanded competence.

### 3. Effect beyond the directly affected territory

The 42nd Amendment allowed the executive and legislative consequences to reach other States where necessary, even if the proclamation itself operates only in part of India.

The rationale is functional: a security threat concentrated in one territory may require coordination, logistics or legal measures elsewhere.

**Limit:** This is not a free-standing power to centralise unrelated State matters. The exercise must retain a connection with the Emergency.

### 4. Financial effect under Article 354

The President may modify constitutional provisions governing distribution of revenue between the Union and States.

Such modification:

- may reduce or suspend transfers otherwise due under the ordinary arrangement;
- continues no further than the end of the financial year in which the Emergency ceases; and
- must be laid before both Houses of Parliament.

Article 354 is therefore a temporary revenue-adjustment mechanism, not permanent fiscal amendment.

### 5. Life of legislatures

Parliament may extend the term of the Lok Sabha:

- by one year at a time;
- for any number of renewals while the Emergency operates;
- but never beyond six months after the Emergency ends.

The same structure applies to State Legislative Assemblies.

The **Fifth Lok Sabha (1971–77)** had its term extended during the Emergency period. This illustrates continuity of representative government under exceptional conditions, but also the democratic cost of postponing elections.

### 6. Article 250 and the State List

Article 250 is the federal-competence route usually recalled with National Emergency: Parliament may legislate on State List matters while the proclamation operates.

Do not confuse it with:

- Article 249: Rajya Sabha national-interest resolution, no Emergency required;
- Article 252: States request parliamentary legislation;
- Article 253: international obligations;
- Article 356/357: State legislative power during President's Rule.

### 7. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The combination of directions, State List legislation, revenue modification and election postponement destroys federalism.

**Reply:** The measures are temporary, State institutions continue, parliamentary authorisation must be renewed and State-subject laws face a six-month post-Emergency sunset. The design allows national coordination without formally abolishing States.

**Residual:** Temporary powers can produce durable administrative and political effects. A policy implemented during the protected period may change institutions even after its legal basis expires.

### 8. Example with limits

**Example:** During external aggression, Parliament enacts a State-subject logistics law and authorises Union officers to coordinate transport. The law may temporarily override inconsistent State law.

**Limit:** The Emergency does not transfer all State administration to the President, and an unrelated State-subject law cannot obtain protection merely by being enacted during the Emergency.

### Concept check

**Question:** During a National Emergency, does Parliament's power over the State List mean that State legislatures cease to possess legislative power?

**Model answer:** No. State legislatures continue, but Parliament acquires concurrent and overriding competence over State List subjects. The arrangement centralises priority rather than abolishing State legislative institutions. Parliamentary emergency laws also face a post-Emergency six-month sunset to the extent Parliament otherwise lacks competence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not import the institutional displacement of Article 356 into Article 352.

### Original Mains practice

**Mains question:** “Article 352 changes the operating balance of federalism without legally abolishing the States.” Analyse. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Mains model:**
Article 352 converts India's Union-weighted federation into a strongly centralised operating system. Under Article 353, the Union may direct States on any matter and Parliament may legislate on the State List. The President may issue State-subject ordinances, and Parliament may confer implementation powers on Union authorities. Article 354 permits temporary modification of revenue distribution. Parliament may also extend Lok Sabha and Assembly terms one year at a time.

Yet States are not abolished. Their governments and legislatures continue, unlike under Article 356. State legislative competence survives subject to Parliament's overriding power. Parliamentary State-subject laws ordinarily become inoperative six months after the Emergency, while revenue modification has its own temporal limit. Parliamentary renewal and judicial review further constrain the transformation.

Thus, National Emergency produces functional unitarisation rather than legal extinction of federalism. The strongest criticism is that temporary powers can leave lasting political effects; the reply is that national survival may require coordinated authority, provided necessity and proportionality are maintained.

**Rubric (15):** executive effect 3; legislative effect 3; financial/tenure effects 3; federal qualifications 3; objection-reply 2; verdict 1.

## Lesson 5 — Fundamental Rights: Articles 358 and 359

Progress: 5 / 11 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Article 19, enforcement orders and the liberty firewall

### Visual: two routes, not one suspension

```text

   │                              │
```

The central distinction is between Article 19's temporary non-restriction under Article 358 and suspension of access to courts for specified rights under Article 359.

### 1. Article 358: Article 19 restrictions cease to bind qualifying State action

Article 358 operates automatically when a National Emergency is proclaimed on grounds of **war or external aggression**.

Its effect is that Article 19 does not restrict:

- an emergency-related law that contains the constitutionally required recital; and
- executive action taken under such a law.

After the 44th Amendment:

- Article 358 does not operate during an armed-rebellion Emergency;
- unrelated laws receive no protection;
- executive action must be traceable to a qualifying law.

When the Emergency ends, inconsistent law ceases to have effect prospectively to the extent of inconsistency, but completed action during the protected period is not retrospectively undone merely because Article 19 revives.

### 2. Article 359: enforcement, not extinction, of named rights

Article 359 authorises the President to order that the right to move specified courts for enforcement of named Fundamental Rights shall remain suspended for the period and territory stated in the order.

Important limits:

- it is not automatic;
- it may operate during external or internal National Emergency;
- only the rights named in the order are affected;
- the order may cover all or part of India;
- it may operate for the whole Emergency or a shorter period;
- the order may also suspend proceedings pending in any court for enforcement of the named rights;
- every order is laid before each House of Parliament;
- Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included.

The rights remain in the Constitution. What is suspended is the specified judicial enforcement route during the order's operation.

### 3. Territorial reach: standard rule and post-44th qualification

The standard examination distinction is:

- Article 358 operates across the country during a qualifying external Emergency;
- an Article 359 order may expressly extend to the whole or only a specified part of India.

This standard line needs a territorial qualification. A National Emergency may itself operate only in part of India. In that situation, the proviso to Article 358(1) permits law or executive action in territory outside the proclaimed area only where, and only insofar as, it relates to the security threat connected with the area where the proclamation operates. The post-44th recital requirement must also be satisfied.

Therefore, avoid the absolute claim that Article 358 always applies nationwide regardless of the proclamation. The safe formulation is: Article 358 is not geographically tailored by a separate presidential order as Article 359 is, but a part-territory proclamation and the Article 358(1) proviso control any connected operation beyond that territory.

### 4. Seven distinctions

| Dimension | Article 358 | Article 359 |
|---|---|---|
| Rights | Article 19 only | Rights named in the order, except Articles 20 and 21 |
| Operation | Automatic | Presidential order |
| Emergency ground | War or external aggression only | External or armed-rebellion Emergency |
| Period | Duration of qualifying Emergency | Period stated in order |
| Territory | Standard comparison: whole country; part-territory proclamation and Article 358(1) proviso qualify connected spillover | Whole or specified part as stated in the order |
| Legal effect | Article 19 restriction does not bind qualifying law/action | Court enforcement of named rights is suspended |
| Trigger instrument | Proclamation itself | Separate presidential order |

**Similarity:** Both protect only emergency-related law carrying the required connection and executive action under it; unrelated action does not gain a constitutional shield.

### 5. *ADM Jabalpur*: the constitutional low point

During the 1975 Emergency, the presidential order affected enforcement of Article 21. In *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976), the Supreme Court majority held that a detainee lacked locus to seek habeas corpus for enforcement of personal liberty during the order.

Justice H.R. Khanna dissented. He rejected the idea that executive detention could become wholly immune from legal challenge.

### 6. The constitutional and judicial correction

The 44th Amendment made Articles 20 and 21 non-derogable under Article 359.

In *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017), the nine-judge Bench expressly held that *ADM Jabalpur* was wrongly decided and overruled it. The judgment vindicated the constitutional principle underlying Khanna's dissent: life and liberty are not gifts of the State that disappear at executive will.

### 7. Article 22 and POTA boundary

Preventive detention protections primarily belong to Article 22. The Prevention of Terrorism Act, 2002 (**POTA**) was repealed in **2004**; its former evidentiary rules raise a separate statutory question rather than an Article 358–359 rule.

The appearance of preventive detention or POTA in an objective question does not automatically make the entire question an emergency-provisions question. The Part XVIII link is limited to Article 355 language or the Articles 358–359 rights architecture.

This corrects the logic of *ADM Jabalpur* (1976), where the majority denied habeas-corpus access during the Emergency. Justice Khanna's dissent was later vindicated, and *Puttaswamy* (2017) expressly overruled the majority.

The firewall is not absolute protection against every coercive law; restrictions may still be enacted under ordinary constitutional standards. Its significance is narrower but fundamental: even in Emergency, the executive cannot place life and liberty wholly beyond courts.

The constitutional story is not simply three declarations. It is a cycle in which experience exposed weaknesses and amendment attempted to repair them.

### 1. The three proclamations

National Emergency has been proclaimed three times:

- **1962:** external aggression by China in the North-East Frontier Agency; continued until January 1968. Because it remained in operation, a fresh proclamation was not required for the 1965 war with Pakistan.
- **1971:** external aggression in the context of war with Pakistan.
- **1975:** internal disturbance; the most controversial proclamation.

The 1971 and 1975 proclamations operated concurrently and were revoked in March 1977.

### 2. Why 1975 is constitutionally decisive

The 1975 experience exposed four vulnerabilities:

1. a broad internal-disturbance ground;
2. Prime Ministerial initiation without prior Cabinet consideration;
3. weak parliamentary thresholds and continuation controls;
4. severe impairment of liberty and judicial remedy.

The Janata government appointed the Shah Commission to examine the circumstances. The Commission did not justify the declaration and documented misuse of Emergency authority.

### 3. The 38th Amendment

The 38th Amendment, enacted in 1975:

- attempted to make presidential satisfaction under emergency provisions final and non-justiciable; and
- confirmed capacity for different proclamations even while another operated.

Its design favoured executive conclusiveness. The later constitutional correction removed the review immunity.

### 4. The 42nd Amendment

The 42nd Amendment, 1976, strengthened central capacity in several ways relevant here:

- a National Emergency could be confined to a specified part of India;
- executive and legislative consequences could reach other States where required;
- the broader Emergency-era constitutional scheme strengthened Union and legislative-term control.

The capacity to localise a proclamation can be defensible, but during the 1975 setting it formed part of a wider concentration of power.

### 5. The 44th Amendment

The 44th Amendment, 1978, installed the principal safeguards:

- “armed rebellion” replaced “internal disturbance” in Article 352;
- written Cabinet recommendation became compulsory;
- approval period became one month;
- special majority replaced simple majority;
- six-month renewals became mandatory;
- Lok Sabha disapproval compelled revocation;
- one-tenth notice and fourteen-day special sitting were created;
- judicial-review immunity was removed;
- Article 358 was narrowed to external Emergency and related law/action;
- Articles 20 and 21 were protected from Article 359 orders.

### 6. Constituent Assembly criticism and defence

| Critic/defender | Constitutional concern or defence |
|---|---|
| H.V. Kamath | Risk of totalitarian or police State |
| K.T. Shah | Reaction, retrogression and arming Centre against units and people |
| T.T. Krishnamachari | Possibility of constitutional dictatorship |
| H.N. Kunzru | Threat to State financial autonomy |
| Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar | Emergency powers as the Constitution's “very life-breath” |
| Mahabir Tyagi | Emergency chapter as a safety-valve |
| B.R. Ambedkar | Accepted possibility of political abuse while defending constitutional necessity |

These views identify the enduring dilemma: powers strong enough to save constitutional government may also be strong enough to damage it.

### 7. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Post-1978 safeguards are an admission that emergency power was constitutionally misconceived.

**Reply:** They show constitutional learning rather than conceptual failure. Emergency capacity may be necessary; the original control architecture was inadequate. Amendment narrowed discretion without deleting the capacity.

**Residual:** Safeguards address known abuses. Future misuse may exploit new institutional weaknesses, so legality must be accompanied by a culture of justification.

### 8. Example and limit

The replacement of “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion” is a substantive correction because it changes what facts must exist.

The written-Cabinet rule is procedural because it changes who must formally decide.

**Limit:** Neither safeguard alone is sufficient. A properly documented Cabinet decision could still be politically abusive if the constitutional ground is absent.

**Mains question:** “The 44th Amendment constitutionalised the lessons of the 1975 Emergency.” Analyse. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Mains model:**
The 1975 Emergency exposed a broad substantive trigger, concentrated executive initiation, weak parliamentary control and the vulnerability of judicial remedies. The 44th Amendment translated these failures into constitutional safeguards.

Substantively, it replaced “internal disturbance” with the narrower “armed rebellion”. Procedurally, it required written Cabinet recommendation, one-month approval by special majority and fresh approval every six months. Democratically, Lok Sabha disapproval by simple majority compels revocation, and one-tenth of its membership may obtain a special sitting.

The Amendment removed the 38th Amendment's review immunity. It confined Article 358 to war or external-aggression Emergency and related law/action. Most importantly, Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included in an Article 359 enforcement order.

The reforms do not eliminate risk: a dominant executive may command Parliament, and security material often receives judicial deference. The three Articles perform different functions. Article 355 is a duty, Article 365 is an evidentiary route, and Article 356 is the exceptional proclamation.

### 1. Article 355

Article 355 imposes a duty on the Union:

- to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance; and
- to ensure that the government of every State is carried on according to the Constitution.

Article 355 does not itself authorise dismissal of a government. It explains the Union responsibility within which warnings, assistance, directions or—if constitutional government truly fails—Article 356 may arise.

### 2. Article 356 threshold

The President must be satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the government of a State **cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution**.

The satisfaction may rest on:

- a report from the Governor; or
- other relevant information.

“Or otherwise” means a Governor's report is not legally indispensable. It does not mean evidence is unnecessary.

### 3. Article 365

Where a State fails to comply with or give effect to Union directions, Article 365 makes it lawful for the President to hold that constitutional government cannot be carried on.

The wording creates a possible conclusion, not an automatic trigger.

```text
Non-compliance with Union direction
             ↓
Relevant to constitutional failure
             ≠
Automatic President's Rule
```

The nature of the direction, constitutional authority for it, seriousness of non-compliance and availability of less drastic correction all matter.

### 4. Parliamentary approval

A proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months. Each House uses a **simple majority** of members present and voting.

If the Lok Sabha is dissolved when the proclamation is issued, or is dissolved within the two-month period:

- Rajya Sabha approval preserves the proclamation temporarily; and
- the reconstituted Lok Sabha must approve within thirty days of its first sitting.

### 5. Duration

Once approved, President's Rule continues for six months. Parliament may renew it every six months, but the ordinary maximum is three years.

Beyond one year, every extension requires both:

1. a National Emergency operating in the whole of India, or in the whole or any part of the concerned State; and
2. Election Commission certification that Assembly elections cannot be held because of difficulties.

This is Article 356(5)'s double condition.

Historical constitutional amendments created exceptional treatment for particular situations, such as the earlier extended Punjab arrangement. Such exceptions must not be converted into the ordinary rule.

### 6. Revocation

The President may revoke President's Rule at any time by a subsequent proclamation. Parliamentary approval is unnecessary for revocation.

Unlike Article 352, the Lok Sabha has no special constitutional disapproval resolution compelling revocation.

### 7. What does not justify Article 356 by itself

- loss of popularity;
- defeat of the ruling party in a Lok Sabha election;
- maladministration or corruption allegations alone;
- strict financial difficulty alone;
- intra-party dispute;
- a Governor's untested opinion that the ministry lacks majority.

The threshold is constitutional ungovernability, not poor government.

### 8. Example and limit

**Example:** A ministry appears to lose majority. The constitutional first response is a floor test, not immediate President's Rule.

**Limit:** If no ministry can be formed after genuine attempts, or government formation is impossible after resignation or defeat, Article 356 may become constitutionally proper.

### 9. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** The phrase “cannot be carried on” and the words “or otherwise” leave excessive discretion.

**Reply:** *Bommai* requires relevant material, judicial review and a floor test where majority is disputed. Parliament must approve, and courts may restore a wrongly dismissed government.

**Residual:** Governors remain Union appointees and may frame the initial factual narrative. Judicial review often occurs after political consequences have begun.

### 10. UPSC use

Always distinguish:

- Article 355 duty;
- Article 356 proclamation;
- Article 365 possible inference;
- floor-test requirement for majority disputes.

Do not write “Article 365 automatically imposes President's Rule”.

2. It uses external aggression and internal disturbance.
3. Article 356 addresses inability to carry on State government constitutionally.
4. Governor's report is common but not mandatory.
5. “Or otherwise” still requires relevant material.
6. Article 365 concerns failure to comply with Union directions.
7. Article 365 permits, but does not compel, an Article 356 conclusion.
8. Both Houses approve within two months.
9. Approval is by simple majority.
10. Each approved period lasts six months.
11. Ordinary maximum is three years.

### Concept check

**Question:** A State refuses one Union direction. Why is immediate President's Rule not the automatic constitutional result?

**Model answer:** Article 365 permits the President to treat non-compliance as evidence that government cannot be carried on constitutionally; it does not compel that conclusion. The direction's legality, seriousness of refusal, relevant material and less drastic remedies must be examined. Article 356 remains exceptional and reviewable.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not merge Article 355's duty or Article 365's evidentiary rule into the Article 356 proclamation itself.

### Original Mains practice

**Mains question:** Distinguish Articles 355, 356 and 365 and explain why the distinction protects federalism. **(10 marks, 150 words)**

**Mains model:**
Article 355 imposes a Union duty to protect States against external aggression and internal disturbance and ensure constitutional government. It is not itself a takeover power.

Article 356 authorises proclamation where the President, on a Governor's report or otherwise, is satisfied that State government cannot be carried on according to the Constitution. It requires parliamentary approval and remains judicially reviewable.

Article 365 provides that failure to comply with Union directions may lawfully support such a conclusion. It does not make President's Rule automatic.

The distinction protects federalism by preserving a graduated response: assistance, warning and lawful direction precede exceptional displacement. It also prevents every disagreement with the Union from being recast as constitutional collapse. Under *Bommai*, relevant material and a floor test further ensure that political conflict does not substitute for the Article 356 threshold.

President's Rule is not total institutional erasure. It replaces specified political and legislative functions while protecting the High Court's constitutional position.

### 1. State executive

The President may assume:

- functions of the State government;
- powers vested in or exercisable by the Governor; and
- other executive functions identified by the proclamation.

The Chief Minister and Council of Ministers are dismissed. Administration is carried on through the Governor, usually with the Chief Secretary and advisers, subject to presidential control.

### 2. State legislature

The President declares that the State legislature's powers shall be exercisable by or under Parliament's authority.

The Assembly may be:

- kept under suspended animation; or
- dissolved.

Under *Bommai*, dissolution should occur only after Parliament approves the proclamation. Before approval, suspension preserves the possibility of restoration.

### 3. Article 357 legislative arrangements

Parliament may:

- make laws for the State itself;
- delegate the State lawmaking power to the President;
- permit delegation to another specified authority;
- confer powers and impose duties on Union officers and authorities.

Laws made through this arrangement are sometimes called President's Acts when enacted through delegated presidential authority.

### 4. Budget and expenditure

Parliament passes State legislative bills and the State budget.

When the Lok Sabha is not in session, the President may authorise expenditure from the State Consolidated Fund pending parliamentary sanction.

The President may also promulgate ordinances for the governance of the State when Parliament is not in session under the adapted constitutional arrangement.

### 5. Laws do not expire automatically with President's Rule

A law made by Parliament, the President or another authorised body continues after President's Rule ends.

The restored State legislature may later repeal, alter or re-enact it.

This differs from National-Emergency State-list laws, which face the six-month post-Emergency rule. Under Article 356, the law is made as a law for that State through substituted legislative authority.

### 6. High Court boundary

The President cannot assume the powers of the State High Court or suspend constitutional provisions relating to it.

The Court's position, jurisdiction and functions remain.

This boundary matters because judicial institutions provide legality review during political displacement.

### 7. National Emergency versus President's Rule

| Dimension | Article 352 | Article 356 |

| Trigger | National-security threat | State constitutional failure |
| State executive | Continues under Union directions | Council of Ministers dismissed |
| State legislature | Continues; Parliament gains overriding State-list power | Suspended/dissolved; power exercised by Parliament |
| Delegation of State lawmaking | Parliament legislates under enlarged competence | Parliament may delegate under Article 357 |
| Maximum | No fixed maximum with renewals | Three years ordinarily |
| Rights effect | Articles 358–359 may operate | No automatic Fundamental Rights effect |
| Lok Sabha disapproval route | Expressly available | No equivalent special route |

### 9. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Continuing centrally made laws after restoration allows temporary rule to bind a later elected legislature.

**Reply:** Immediate expiry could create a legal vacuum. The restored State legislature retains power to repeal, modify or re-enact those laws.

**Residual:** Political and administrative inertia may preserve centrally made policy even when formal State autonomy returns.

### 10. Example with limits

**Example:** Parliament authorises the President to enact an urgent State appropriation law. The law does not vanish on the day President's Rule ends.

**Limit:** The President cannot use Article 356 to assume the State High Court's judicial power.

### 11. UPSC use

For objective questions, separate four issues before choosing an option: whether the Assembly is suspended or dissolved, who exercises legislative power, whether Parliament may delegate under Article 357, and whether Fundamental Rights are affected. For Mains, compare institutional substitution under Article 356 with the concurrent Union power created by Article 352.

### Revision notes

1. The State Council of Ministers is dismissed.
2. The Governor administers on behalf of the President.
3. Assembly may be suspended or dissolved.
4. Dissolution should await parliamentary approval under *Bommai*.
5. Parliament exercises State legislative power.
6. Parliament may delegate that power under Article 357.
7. State bills and budget are handled through the substituted arrangement.
8. Expenditure from the State Consolidated Fund may be authorised pending sanction.
9. Ordinances may be promulgated when Parliament is not in session.
10. Laws continue after President's Rule ends.

12. The State High Court cannot be taken over.
13. Article 356 does not automatically suspend Article 19.
14. State Assembly dissolution is not automatic.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a law made during President's Rule continue after elected State government is restored?

**Model answer:** The law was validly made through Parliament's substituted State legislative authority or a delegation under Article 357. Its duration is not tied to the proclamation. Continuity avoids a legal vacuum, while democratic control returns because the restored State legislature may repeal, amend or re-enact it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not apply the six-month sunset for National-Emergency State-list laws to every law made during President's Rule.

### Original Mains practice

**Mains question:** Compare the institutional consequences of a National Emergency and President's Rule. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Mains model:**
National Emergency and President's Rule both strengthen the Union but operate through different institutional models.

Under Article 352, State governments and legislatures continue. The Union may direct States on any matter, and Parliament gains overriding State List competence. Emergency State-subject laws ordinarily become inoperative six months after the proclamation ends. Articles 358–359 may affect rights.

Under Article 356, the State Council of Ministers is dismissed and the Assembly is suspended or dissolved. Parliament exercises State legislative power and may delegate it under Article 357. Parliament handles State bills and the budget; presidential expenditure and ordinance arrangements may operate. Laws made during President's Rule continue until altered by the restored legislature. The State High Court remains untouched.

Article 352 may continue indefinitely through six-month special-majority renewals; Article 356 uses simple-majority approval and ordinarily ends within three years.

Thus, Article 352 overlays State institutions with Union priority, whereas Article 356 substitutes Union-controlled political and legislative authority for failed State government.

## Lesson 9 — Misuse, *Bommai* and the judicial control of Article 356

Progress: 9 / 11 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Historical misuse, proper use and judicial review

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Queried — historical use, commission tests and leading Supreme Court cases

CA found: No separate current linkage; the cases remain the governing doctrinal anchors
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: from political weapon to reviewable power

```text
1951 first use in Punjab
        ↓
repeated partisan use; over 125 proclamations
        ↓
1977 and 1980 mass dismissals after Union power changes
        ↓
State of Rajasthan (1977): broad deference
        ↓
Sarkaria Commission: last resort + warning
        ↓
S.R. Bommai (1994)
review + relevant material + floor test + restoration
        ↓
Rameshwar Prasad (2006)
```

The legal change was not deletion of Article 356. It was a change in the cost, evidence and reviewability of using it.

### 1. Historical misuse

President's Rule was first imposed in Punjab in 1951. It has been invoked **over 125 times**; false precision should be avoided because counts differ with treatment of repeat proclamations and territorial changes.

Three episodes reveal partisan danger:

- **1977:** the Janata government dismissed Congress ministries in **nine States** after winning the Lok Sabha election.
- **1980:** the returning Congress government dismissed opposition ministries in **nine States** using similar national-mandate reasoning.
- **1992:** President's Rule was imposed in BJP-ruled Madhya Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan in the aftermath of the Babri Masjid demolition and action concerning banned organisations.

Defeat in a national election does not itself prove that a State ministry has lost Assembly confidence.

### 2. From Ambedkar's “dead letter” to the “deadly weapon” critique

While defending the provision in the Constituent Assembly, B.R. Ambedkar hoped that Article 356 would remain a **“dead letter”** and, if ever used, that the President would take proper precautions before suspending a State's administration. The remark framed central intervention as an exceptional last resort in a federal Constitution.

Repeated political use reversed that expectation. Standard constitutional commentary describes the provision as having changed from a hoped-for dead letter into a **“deadly weapon”** against State governments and legislative assemblies.

H.V. Kamath later captured the contrast sharply:

> “Dr. Ambedkar is dead and the Articles are very much alive.”

Kamath's remark was not a doctrinal test for Article 356. It was a political criticism of the gap between Ambedkar's expectation of non-use and the provision's repeated use in practice.

### 3. Pre-*Bommai* deference

In *State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (1977), the Court adopted a broad, deferential approach to presidential satisfaction. The case marks the high-water point of limited judicial intervention before the later doctrine.

The 38th Amendment had attempted total immunity; the 44th removed it. Yet practical discipline required a fuller judicial test.

### 4. The ten *Bommai* propositions

*S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994), decided by a nine-judge Bench, established:

1. Article 356 proclamation is judicially reviewable.
2. Presidential satisfaction must rest on relevant material.
3. The Union bears the burden of producing material supporting the action.
4. Courts review relevance and legality, though not political adequacy as an appellate authority.
5. If the proclamation is unconstitutional, courts may restore the dismissed government and revive the Assembly.
6. The Assembly should be dissolved only after Parliament approves; before approval it should ordinarily remain suspended.
7. Secularism is a basic feature; grave anti-secular State action may justify Article 356.
8. Loss of majority must be tested on the floor of the House.
9. A new Union government cannot dismiss opposition State ministries merely because power changed nationally.
10. Article 356 is exceptional and should be used only for special situations.

### 5. Proper-use situations

The *Bommai* discussion, drawing on the Sarkaria framework, recognises possible proper use where:

1. no party obtains a majority after an election;
2. the majority party declines to form government and no viable coalition exists;
3. a ministry resigns after defeat and no alternative can command confidence;
4. a State disregards a constitutionally valid Union direction in a manner producing constitutional failure;
5. internal subversion involves deliberate action against the Constitution or violent revolt;
6. physical breakdown arises from wilful refusal to discharge constitutional obligations, endangering State security.

These are categories, not automatic warrants. Material and less drastic alternatives remain relevant.

### 6. Improper-use situations

Article 356 is improper where:

1. no alternative ministry is explored after resignation or dismissal;
2. the Governor substitutes personal arithmetic for a floor test;
3. national-election defeat is treated as loss of State Assembly confidence;
4. internal disturbance falls short of subversion or physical breakdown;
5. maladministration, corruption allegations or financial difficulty alone are used;
6. no prior warning or opportunity to correct is given, absent extreme urgency;
7. the power is used for intra-party management or an extraneous purpose.

### 7. *Rameshwar Prasad*

In *Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006), the Supreme Court held the 2005 dissolution of the Bihar Assembly unconstitutional and mala fide. Anticipated horse-trading and speculative assumptions could not justify preventing a newly elected Assembly from testing government formation.

The case reinforces a simple principle:

```text
Apprehension is not constitutional evidence
Floor process is preferred to gubernatorial prediction
```

### 8. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Judicial review occurs too late; a dismissed government may already have lost political momentum.

**Reply:** Restoration power, suspension-before-approval, relevant-material review and floor-test preference create real deterrence. The prospect of invalidation changes executive calculations even when litigation takes time.

**Residual:** Remedy cannot perfectly reverse political consequences. The best safeguard is preventing wrongful dissolution before it occurs.

### 9. Example with limits

**Example:** A Governor reports that the ministry has lost majority based solely on faction letters. The proper response is ordinarily a prompt floor test on objective material.

**Limit:** A floor test is not required where no ministry exists and no constitutional government can be formed after genuine exploration.

### 10. UPSC use

For case-law answers, do not list ten propositions without structure. Group them:

```text
Reviewability → relevant material and burden
Democratic proof → floor test
Institutional preservation → suspension before dissolution
Remedy → restoration
Substantive limits → secularism and federalism
Exceptional character → last resort
```

### Revision notes

1. Article 356 has been invoked over 125 times; first use was Punjab in 1951.
2. Ambedkar hoped Article 356 would remain a “dead letter” and be used only with proper precautions.
3. Repeated use produced the “deadly weapon” critique; H.V. Kamath remarked, “Dr. Ambedkar is dead and the Articles are very much alive.”
4. In 1977 and 1980, the new Union governments each dismissed ministries in nine States.
5. National electoral defeat does not end State Assembly confidence.
6. *State of Rajasthan* represents earlier judicial deference.
7. *Bommai* made Article 356 reviewable and requires relevant material.
8. The Union bears the burden of producing supporting material.
9. Majority is tested on the Assembly floor.
10. Dissolution should await parliamentary approval.
11. Courts may restore government and Assembly.
12. Federalism and secularism constrain Article 356.
13. *Rameshwar Prasad* rejected speculative Bihar dissolution.
14. Warning and alternative government formation should precede takeover where possible.
15. Maladministration alone is not constitutional breakdown.

### Concept check

**Question:** A Governor recommends President's Rule because coalition partners have publicly criticised the Chief Minister. What constitutional step is normally required before concluding that the government has lost confidence?

**Model answer:** Public criticism or intra-coalition dispute is not conclusive proof of lost majority. Where objective doubt exists, confidence must ordinarily be tested on the floor of the Assembly. A Governor's subjective assessment cannot replace the House, and Article 356 requires relevant material showing constitutional government cannot continue.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat every political instability as failure of constitutional machinery.

### Original Mains practice

**Mains question:** Critically evaluate the proposition that *S.R. Bommai* transformed Article 356 from a political power into a constitutional remedy. **(20 marks, 250 words)**

**Mains model:**
Before *Bommai*, Article 356 was repeatedly used against opposition governments, with judicial deference exemplified by *State of Rajasthan*. *S.R. Bommai* (1994) did not remove political judgment, but constitutionalised its limits.

The Court made presidential satisfaction reviewable, required relevant material and placed the evidentiary burden on the Union. It held that Assembly majority belongs to the floor, not the Governor's subjective assessment. Dissolution should follow parliamentary approval; an invalid proclamation may lead to restoration. Federalism and secularism were treated as basic features, and Article 356 as a last-resort power.

*Rameshwar Prasad* later applied this discipline to speculative dissolution in Bihar. These holdings raised the legal and political cost of misuse.

However, Article 356 remains initiated through political institutions, Governors may frame disputed reports and review may follow irreversible political events. Courts also avoid reassessing security or political adequacy like appellate executives.

Therefore, *Bommai* transformed an often partisan weapon into a reviewable constitutional remedy, but its effectiveness still depends on timely floors, restrained Governors and prompt judicial relief.

No single factor explains the decline. Courts changed legal risk, party politics changed political cost and commissions supplied a constitutional standard of restraint.

### 1. Legal factor

*Bommai* made Article 356 reviewable, required relevant material, privileged the floor test, delayed dissolution until approval and permitted restoration.

*Rameshwar Prasad* showed that the Court would invalidate speculative pre-emptive dissolution.

The result was deterrence: a Union government could no longer assume that Article 356 was a purely political question.

### 2. Political factor

From the late 1980s and especially the 1990s:

- Union governments increasingly depended on coalitions;
- regional parties gained national bargaining power;
- dismissal of a State government could destabilise Union alliances;
- State-level mandates became harder to treat as subordinate to national electoral results.

The decline is therefore partly “political federalism”: States gained protection through party-system incentives even without textual amendment.

#### Verified supporting PYQ linkage

**2022 GS-II, Q13**

> “While the national political parties in India favour centralisation, the regional parties are in favour of State autonomy.” Comment. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Demand:** Evaluate a tendency without treating it as an absolute law.
**Answer approach:** Connect party incentives to Union or State position, Article 356 history and coalition-era counterexamples.

### 3. Institutional recommendations

The **Sarkaria Commission (1983–88; report submitted in 1988)** and the **Punchhi Commission (2007–10)** recommended:

- Article 356 only as a last resort;
- prior warning where feasible;
- exploration of alternative government;
- a floor test for majority;
- a reasoned or speaking Governor's report;
- politically neutral gubernatorial conduct.

The NCRWC similarly supported disciplined constitutional government and reform of the Governor's role.

These recommendations are not binding law. Their value lies in supplying a standard against which conduct is politically and judicially assessed.

### 4. A recent Article 356 cycle: Manipur

On **13 February 2025**, the President issued **G.S.R. 134(E)** under Article 356 for Manipur after considering the Governor's report and other information. The proclamation:

- assumed State-government functions;
- declared State legislative power exercisable by or under Parliament;
- permitted specified operation through the Governor; and
- suspended identified constitutional provisions for the proclamation's operation.

On **4 February 2026**, **S.O. 509(E)** revoked that proclamation under Article 356(2) with effect from the same date.

As checked on **30 September 2026**, an elected government was functioning in Manipur. This is a dated status statement, not a permanent proposition.

```text

G.S.R. 134(E)                    S.O. 509(E)
President's Rule  ─────────────→ revocation
```

**Learning value:** Article 356 is not merely historical. The cycle illustrates proclamation, suspended political operation, revocation and restoration without converting the episode into a National Emergency.

### 6. Has misuse ended?

No. The form of federal conflict has changed.

Where Article 356 is harder to use, conflict may shift to:

- Governor decisions;
- timing of floor tests;
- assent to State Bills;
- Union investigative agencies;
- fiscal transfers and cesses;
- centrally designed schemes.

The decline of one power is evidence of discipline, not proof that Indian federal relations are conflict-free.

### 7. Objection, reply and residual

**Objection:** Coalition politics, not constitutional law, caused the decline; *Bommai* receives excessive credit.

**Reply:** Coalition incentives and judicial doctrine reinforced each other. Political cost discouraged use; judicial review increased the risk that use would fail and be reversed.

**Residual:** When the same party dominates the Union and many States, coalition deterrence weakens. The legal doctrine then becomes more important.

**Model answer:** *Bommai* raised legal risk through review, relevant-material requirements, floor tests and restoration. But coalition governments and strong regional parties also raised the political cost of dismissals, while the Sarkaria Commission (1983–88) and Punchhi Commission (2007–10) supplied norms of warning and last-resort use. **Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate lower Article 356 frequency with disappearance of Centre–State conflict.

The Manipur cycle—proclamation in February 2025 and revocation in February 2026—shows that Article 356 remains operational but temporary and status-sensitive.

## Lesson 11 — Financial Emergency and the complete comparison

### Visual: Article 360 control chain

```text
Threat to financial stability or credit
of India OR any part
               ↓
President issues proclamation
               ↓

simple majority
               ↓

               ↓

 ├─ canons of financial propriety

```

Article 360 is severe because it reaches State finance, public employment and judicial salaries while having no fixed maximum once approved.

### 1. Trigger

The President may proclaim Financial Emergency if satisfied that the **financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened**.

The phrase includes national and territorially limited financial danger. It is not confined to insolvency or a completed default.

The 38th Amendment attempted to make satisfaction final and non-justiciable. The 44th Amendment removed that immunity, so the proclamation is not beyond judicial review.

### 2. Approval and dissolution contingency

Both Houses must approve within two months by simple majority.

If the Lok Sabha is dissolved during the approval period:

- Rajya Sabha approval preserves the proclamation temporarily; and
- the reconstituted Lok Sabha must approve within thirty days of its first sitting.

### 3. Duration and revocation

Once both Houses approve:

- there is no maximum duration;
- periodic renewal is unnecessary;
- the proclamation continues until revoked.

The President may revoke by subsequent proclamation without parliamentary approval.

This differs from Article 352, which needs six-month renewal, and Article 356, which needs six-month renewal within an ordinary three-year maximum.

### 4. Effects on States

Union executive authority extends to directions requiring:

- observance of specified canons of financial propriety;
- other measures considered necessary for the purpose;
- reduction of salaries and allowances of all or classes of persons serving the State;
- reservation, after passage, of State Money Bills or other financial Bills for presidential consideration.

The reference to other financial Bills is wider than Money Bills alone.

### 5. Effects at Union level and on judges

The President may direct reduction of salaries and allowances of:

- all or classes of persons serving the Union; and
- judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.

This is constitutionally striking because judicial salary protection ordinarily supports independence. The emergency text expressly permits the direction, but the power's severity reinforces the need for necessity and good faith.

### 6. Why it has never been proclaimed

No Financial Emergency has been proclaimed. The 1991 balance-of-payments crisis is frequently described as the closest historical comparison, but ordinary economic, executive and legislative measures were used.

Non-use can be explained by:

- reputational cost of formally declaring national financial instability;
- breadth of Union fiscal and economic powers outside Article 360;
- severity of the consequences;
- availability of monetary, budgetary and external-finance responses.

It is an inference, not a constitutional rule, to call non-use evidence of deterrent severity.

### 7. The American reference

Ambedkar compared the provision to what the debates and standard text call the United States “National Recovery Act” of 1933. The formal American statute was the **National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933**.

The comparison concerned exceptional presidential measures during the Great Depression. It should not be read to mean that the American statute and Article 360 were legally identical.

### 8. Constituent Assembly concern

H.N. Kunzru warned that financial-emergency provisions threatened State financial autonomy.

The strongest defence is coordination: a collapse of sovereign credit or cascading State finance may require uniform fiscal action.

The residual concern is that salary control and reservation of State financial legislation could centralise choices far beyond immediate crisis management.

Once approved by simple majority within two months, it continues indefinitely until revoked and enables far-reaching directions on State financial propriety, public salaries, State financial Bills and even judicial salaries.

Its non-use, including during the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis, reflects several factors. Governments possess substantial monetary, budgetary, borrowing and external-finance tools outside Article 360. A proclamation would signal severe sovereign weakness and impose high political and market costs. Its broad federal and salary consequences may also be disproportionate where targeted economic measures suffice.

Yet non-use does not make the Article meaningless. It remains constitutional insurance against systemic fiscal collapse and may strengthen Union bargaining capacity even without formal invocation.

The weakness is its control design: after simple-majority approval, no periodic renewal is required. If ever used, judicial review, a narrow necessity test and intense parliamentary scrutiny would be essential. Article 360 is significant precisely because it is an extreme last resort.

**Rubric (15):** trigger/procedure 3; consequences 3; reasons for non-use 4; constitutional significance 2; control critique 2; verdict 1.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Articles and regimes

**Question:** Explain the functional sequence Articles 352–360 create rather than merely listing the Article numbers.

**Model answer:** Article 352 creates National Emergency; Articles 353–354 specify executive, legislative and revenue effects. Article 355 imposes a Union protective duty; Articles 356–357 provide proclamation and substituted State legislative authority. Articles 358–359 regulate different rights consequences of National Emergency. Article 360 creates the separate Financial Emergency regime.

## Check 2 — Democratic control

**Question:** Identify the strongest entry, continuation and exit safeguards for National Emergency.

**Model answer:** Entry requires written Cabinet recommendation and one-month special-majority approval in both Houses. Continuation requires fresh special-majority approval every six months. Exit may occur through presidential revocation or compulsory revocation after simple-majority Lok Sabha disapproval, with one-tenth membership able to trigger a special sitting.

## Check 3 — Rights

**Question:** A proclamation is based on armed rebellion. Can Article 358 operate? Can Article 359 operate?

**Model answer:** Article 358 cannot operate because it is limited to war or external-aggression Emergency. Article 359 may operate through a separate presidential order because it can apply during internal or external National Emergency, but Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included.

## Check 4 — State majority

**Question:** Why is a floor test constitutionally superior to a Governor's private assessment of legislative support?

**Model answer:** Assembly confidence is an institutional fact belonging to the House. A floor test provides transparent, reviewable proof and prevents subjective or partisan prediction. *Bommai*, *Rameshwar Prasad* and later Governor cases insist on objective material and reject apprehension as a substitute for legislative determination.

## Check 5 — Three-way synthesis

**Question:** Which regime most sharply displaces elected institutions, which most directly affects rights, and which has the weakest periodic-renewal control?

**Model answer:** Article 356 most sharply displaces elected State institutions. Article 352 is the regime under which Articles 358–359 may affect rights. Article 360 has the weakest periodic-renewal control because, once approved by simple majority, it continues indefinitely until revoked without six-month renewal.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark question

**Question:** Distinguish the parliamentary approval, continuation and revocation controls under Articles 352, 356 and 360. **(10 marks, 150 words)**

**Model answer:**
Article 352 requires approval by both Houses within one month through special majority. It continues for six months at a time and needs repeated special-majority renewal. The President may revoke it, while Lok Sabha disapproval by simple majority compels revocation.

Article 356 requires approval within two months by simple majority. It operates in six-month blocks, ordinarily up to three years; extension beyond one year needs a National Emergency and Election Commission certification. The President revokes it, with no Article 352-style Lok Sabha disapproval route.

Article 360 also requires approval within two months by simple majority. Once approved, it continues indefinitely without periodic renewal until presidential revocation.

All three contain a Lok Sabha-dissolution continuity rule involving interim Rajya Sabha approval and decision by the reconstituted Lok Sabha. The design makes National Emergency hardest to authorise, President's Rule time-limited and Financial Emergency unusually open-ended after approval.

**Rubric (10):** Article 352 controls 3; Article 356 controls 3; Article 360 controls 2; dissolution/common comparison 1; conclusion 1.

## Final 15-mark question

**Question:** Assess how the post-1975 constitutional order attempts to reconcile emergency necessity with civil liberty and federalism. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Model answer:**
The post-1975 order accepts that war, rebellion and constitutional breakdown may require concentrated authority, but rejects unchecked executive emergency.

For Article 352, the 44th Amendment replaced “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion”, required written Cabinet advice, special-majority approval within one month and six-month renewals. Lok Sabha may compel revocation, and judicial-review immunity was removed. Article 358 is confined to external Emergency and related law/action; Articles 20 and 21 remain enforceable despite Article 359. *Puttaswamy* overruled the habeas-corpus denial in *ADM Jabalpur*.

For federalism, States continue under Article 352, though Union power expands. Under Article 356, *Bommai* requires relevant material, floor tests, suspension before dissolution and permits restoration; *Rameshwar Prasad* rejects speculative dissolution. Sarkaria and Punchhi recommend warning and last-resort use.

The reconciliation remains incomplete. A dominant Union executive may control Parliament, security evidence attracts deference and Governors can initiate contested narratives. Nevertheless, recurring consent, judicial review, non-derogable liberty and restoration power convert emergency authority from executive prerogative into a constrained constitutional exception.

**Rubric (15):** necessity frame 2; Article 352 safeguards 4; liberty safeguards 3; federal/judicial controls 3; limitations 2; verdict 1.

## Final 20-mark question

**Question:** “Emergency provisions are both the Constitution's safety-valve and its most dangerous centralising instrument.” Critically evaluate. **(20 marks, 250 words)**

**Model answer:**
Emergency provisions are a safety-valve because ordinary allocation may fail during war, armed rebellion, State constitutional collapse or systemic financial danger. Articles 352, 356 and 360 provide differentiated responses rather than an extra-constitutional suspension.

Their danger lies in concentration. Article 352 permits Union directions, State List legislation, revenue modification and legislative-term extension; Articles 358–359 may affect rights. Article 356 dismisses a State ministry and transfers legislative authority. Article 360 permits sweeping fiscal directions, salary reductions and reservation of State financial Bills.

History validates the criticism: the 1975 Emergency impaired liberty, while Article 356 was used over 125 times, including partisan dismissals. Constituent Assembly members warned of police State, constitutional dictatorship and loss of State autonomy.

Yet safeguards substantially alter the design. The 44th Amendment narrowed grounds, required written Cabinet advice and recurring consent, protected Articles 20–21 and restored review. *Bommai* constitutionalised relevant material, floor tests, federalism, secularism and restoration; *Rameshwar Prasad* checked speculative dissolution.

The system is therefore legitimate only as an exceptional, necessary and proportionate last resort. Its safety-valve character depends not on benevolent executive intent but on enforceable procedure, rights, review and prompt return to ordinary democracy.

**Rubric (20):** safety-valve rationale 3; three-regime centralisation 5; historical criticism 3; amendment safeguards 3; judicial safeguards 3; critical limitation 2; verdict 1.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Part XVIII at a glance

- **Articles 352–360** create three differentiated emergency regimes.
- **352:** war, external aggression or armed rebellion threatening security of India or part.
- **353–354:** executive, legislative and revenue effects of National Emergency.
- **355:** Union duty to protect States and ensure constitutional government.
- **356–357:** State constitutional failure and substituted legislative authority.
- **358–359:** distinct rights effects during National Emergency.
- **360:** threat to financial stability or credit.

## National Emergency: trigger and procedure

- Actual event or **imminent danger** may satisfy Article 352.
- War/external aggression = **External Emergency**.
- Armed rebellion = **Internal Emergency**.
- The 44th Amendment replaced **internal disturbance** with armed rebellion.
- Proclamation may cover whole or part of India.
- Differently grounded proclamations may coexist.
- Written Union-Cabinet recommendation is compulsory.
- Both Houses approve within **one month** by **special majority**.
- Approval lasts **six months**; renewed every six months without fixed maximum.
- During Lok Sabha dissolution, Rajya Sabha interim approval plus the reconstituted Lok Sabha's thirty-day decision applies.
- President may revoke at any time.
- Lok Sabha simple-majority disapproval compels revocation.
- One-tenth membership can trigger a special sitting within fourteen days.
- Review is available for mala fides, irrelevance, absurdity or perversity.

## National Emergency: federal effects

- Union executive directions may cover any matter; State governments continue.
- Parliament gains overriding State List competence.
- President may issue State-subject ordinances when Parliament is not sitting.
- Parliamentary State-subject law generally becomes inoperative six months after Emergency.
- Article 354 permits temporary revenue-distribution modification.
- Revenue modification cannot extend beyond the end of the financial year in which Emergency ceases.
- Lok Sabha and Assembly terms may be extended one year at a time, but not beyond six months after Emergency.
- The Fifth Lok Sabha (1971–77) was extended during the Emergency period.
- Effects may reach another State where constitutionally connected to the Emergency.

## Rights firewall

- Article 358 concerns Article 19, operates automatically only during war/external-aggression Emergency and protects only qualifying related law/action.
- The standard territorial comparison describes Article 358 as whole-country operation; where the proclamation covers only part of India, Article 358(1)'s proviso requires a security connection for operation beyond the proclaimed territory.
- Article 359 requires a presidential order and may apply during an external or armed-rebellion Emergency.
- An Article 359 order may extend to the whole or a specified part of India and may operate for the whole Emergency or a shorter period.
- The order may suspend both fresh court access and pending proceedings for enforcement of named rights.
- Article 359 order is laid before each House.
- Rights not named remain enforceable.
- Articles 20 and 21 remain enforceable.
- *ADM Jabalpur* majority denied habeas-corpus access during 1975.
- Justice H.R. Khanna dissented.
- *Puttaswamy* expressly overruled *ADM Jabalpur*.
- Preventive detention belongs primarily to Article 22; POTA, 2002 was repealed in 2004.

## Amendment and declaration chronology

- **1962:** external aggression; continued until January 1968.
- **1971:** external aggression.
- **1975:** internal disturbance; operated concurrently with 1971 proclamation.
- **March 1977:** both later proclamations revoked.
- **38th Amendment:** review immunity attempt; multiple proclamation capacity.
- **42nd Amendment:** territorial limitation and wider emergency effects.
- **Shah Commission:** did not justify the 1975 declaration.
- **44th Amendment:** narrower ground, collective initiation, stronger consent, rights and review.

## President's Rule threshold and duration

- Article 355 duty is not itself takeover authority.
- Article 356 requires inability to carry on State government according to the Constitution.
- President may act on Governor's report **or otherwise**.
- Article 365 non-compliance may support, but does not compel, the conclusion.
- Both Houses approve within **two months** by simple majority.
- Each period lasts six months.
- Ordinary maximum: three years.
- Beyond one year: National Emergency in whole India or whole/part of State **plus** ECI certification.
- President revokes by subsequent proclamation.

## President's Rule consequences

- CM and Council of Ministers dismissed.
- Governor administers on President's behalf.
- Assembly suspended or dissolved.
- Parliament exercises State legislative authority.
- Parliament may delegate under Article 357.
- Parliament handles State laws and budget.
- Interim Consolidated Fund expenditure may be authorised.
- Ordinance arrangements may operate when Parliament is not sitting.
- Laws continue after restoration until State legislature changes them.
- State High Court powers cannot be assumed.
- Article 19 is not automatically affected.

## Judicial control and proper use

- Ambedkar hoped Article 356 would remain a “dead letter” and be used only with proper precautions.
- Repeated political use produced the “deadly weapon” critique; H.V. Kamath remarked, “Dr. Ambedkar is dead and the Articles are very much alive.”
- In 1977 and 1980, the incoming Union governments each dismissed ministries in nine States after national changes of power.
- *State of Rajasthan* marks the earlier deferential phase.
- *Bommai*: review, relevant material, Union burden, floor test, suspension before dissolution, restoration, federalism/secularism, last resort.
- *Rameshwar Prasad*: anticipated horse-trading cannot justify speculative dissolution.
- Proper situations include genuine inability to form government, defeat with no alternative, constitutional defiance producing breakdown, internal subversion or physical breakdown.
- Improper situations include no exploration of alternatives, no floor test, national-election defeat, ordinary disturbance, maladministration, financial difficulty, no warning or intra-party purposes.

## Why Article 356 declined

- Judicial risk rose after *Bommai*.
- Coalition governments depended on regional parties.
- State mandates acquired stronger national bargaining protection.
- The Sarkaria Commission (1983–88; report 1988) and Punchhi Commission (2007–10) supplied warning, floor-test, speaking-report and last-resort norms.
- The Manipur cycle: G.S.R. 134(E), 13 February 2025 → S.O. 509(E), 4 February 2026 → elected government restored.
- Lower use does not mean Centre–State conflict disappeared.

## Financial Emergency

- Trigger: financial stability or credit of India or any part threatened.
- Approval: two months, simple majority.
- Lok Sabha dissolution contingency applies.
- Continues indefinitely after approval; no periodic renewal.
- President may revoke.
- Union may direct financial propriety.
- Salaries of State and Union personnel may be reduced.
- Salaries of Supreme Court and High Court judges may be reduced.
- State Money Bills and other financial Bills may be reserved.
- Never proclaimed; 1991 managed through ordinary measures.
- Debate wording refers to US “National Recovery Act”; formal statute was National Industrial Recovery Act, 1933.
- H.N. Kunzru warned of loss of State fiscal autonomy.

## Rapid close-option distinctions

- 352 approval = special majority; 356/360 = simple majority.
- 352 approval window = one month; 356/360 = two months.
- 352 maximum = none with renewals; 356 = three years ordinarily; 360 = none after one approval.
- 352 State institutions continue; 356 State executive displaced and legislature substituted.
- 358 = Article 19/external/automatic; standard whole-country rule qualified by the part-territory proclamation and Article 358(1) proviso.
- 359 = named enforcement/order/both Emergency grounds; whole or specified part; pending proceedings may also be suspended.
- 20 and 21 cannot be included in Article 359 order.
- 355 duty ≠ 356 proclamation ≠ 365 automatic trigger.
- 356 does not automatically dissolve Assembly.
- High Court remains unaffected by takeover.
- 360 does not suspend Fundamental Rights.
