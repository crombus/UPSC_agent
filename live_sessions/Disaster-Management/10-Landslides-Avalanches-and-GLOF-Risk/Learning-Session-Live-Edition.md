### 🖼️ Distinguishing Mountain Mass-Movement and Outburst Hazards — visual
```text
LANDSLIDE: rock/debris/earth moves downslope
AVALANCHE: unstable snow/ice mass moves downslope
GLOF: glacial-lake dam fails/overtops → flood downstream
CLOUDBURST: intense local rain → may trigger flash flood/landslide
RELATED CASCADE ≠ SAME MECHANISM
```

A landslide is gravity-driven downslope movement of rock, debris or earth. An avalanche is rapid downslope movement of snow, ice and entrained debris after snowpack strength is exceeded. A GLOF is sudden release of water and sediment when a glacial lake’s natural dam—moraine, ice, bedrock or another barrier—fails or overtops. A cloudburst is an intense local rainfall event that may initiate flash floods and slope failure.

These hazards interact but require different evidence and institutions. GSI leads landslide studies and forecasting; DGRE/DRDO issues avalanche information; NRSC/ISRO monitors glacial lakes; NDMA provides risk-management guidance. Calling every Himalayan disaster a “landslide” produces wrong maps, thresholds and protective actions.

### Decision/application lens

Use a four-column answer: **moving material → failure trigger → monitoring institution → immediate protective action**. Then add cascade arrows only after the mechanisms are separated.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Landslides move rock, debris or earth under gravity.
2. Avalanches primarily move unstable snow and ice.
3. GLOFs release water and sediment from a glacial lake.
4. Cloudbursts are intense local rainfall events.
5. Related hazards may trigger one another without becoming identical.
6. GSI is the primary landslide institutional anchor.
7. DGRE is the avalanche-warning anchor.
8. NRSC/ISRO supports glacial-lake monitoring.
9. Correct hazard naming determines the correct warning chain.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why must mountain-disaster plans distinguish landslides, avalanches, cloudbursts and GLOFs?

**Model answer:** The hazards move different materials and arise from different failure processes. Landslides involve rock, debris or earth; avalanches involve unstable snow and ice; cloudbursts are intense local rainfall events; GLOFs release water and sediment after a glacial-lake dam fails or overtops. Consequently, susceptibility inputs, lead times, institutions and actions differ: GSI supports landslide mapping and forecasts, DGRE avalanche warnings, NRSC glacial-lake monitoring and NDMA GLOF guidance. They may form cascades, but conflating them can produce wrong zones, thresholds, evacuation routes and engineering measures.

**Adjacent rubric:** Four mechanisms 4 + institutional/action differences 4 + cascade qualification 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Every fast Himalayan disaster is a type of landslide.

**Transition:** Precise distinctions allow Lesson 2 to classify landslide movement and compare India’s principal slope-risk settings.

### 🖼️ Landslide Types, Triggers and Indian Hotspots — visual
```text
MOVEMENT: fall | topple | slide | spread | flow | complex
MATERIAL: rock | debris | earth
TRIGGERS: prolonged/intense rain | earthquake | erosion | excavation | loading
SETTING: Himalaya/NE/Andaman | Western Ghats/Nilgiris | mining/subsidence
```

Landslides can be described by material and movement: rockfalls and topples, rotational or translational slides, lateral spreads, debris or earth flows and complex combinations. Trigger is not the same as underlying susceptibility. Steep slope, weak or weathered material, discontinuities, groundwater and previous movement create predisposition; intense or prolonged rain, earthquakes, toe erosion, excavation or loading may initiate failure.

The Himalaya combines young, tectonically active high-relief terrain, weak/friable rock, earthquakes, monsoon rain and intense road or settlement pressure. The Western Ghats and Nilgiris combine steep weathered slopes, high monsoon rainfall, drainage change, cutting and land-use disturbance. North-East and Andaman–Nicobar are also high-susceptibility settings. Mining and subsidence create a distinct risk in several plateau and peninsular States.

A current answer should use GSI evidence rather than repeat the owner’s document-period “15% of land area” as a live statistic.

### Decision/application lens

For any case, separate **predisposing terrain → trigger → human modification → exposed element → likely movement type**. This is stronger than writing “heavy rain caused a landslide.”

### REVISION NOTES

1. Landslides are classified by material and movement.
2. Falls, slides, flows, spreads and complex failures behave differently.
3. Susceptibility exists before the immediate trigger.
4. Rainfall raises pore pressure and may reduce effective strength.
5. Earthquakes can destabilise already susceptible slopes.
6. Himalayan risk combines tectonics, relief, weak rock and development.
7. Western Ghats risk combines weathering, steep slopes and monsoon rain.
8. Mining/subsidence is a distinct anthropogenic category.
9. The legacy 15% estimate should not be used as a current statistic.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) A rain-soaked road cut develops tension cracks above the slope and toe bulging below it. Diagnose the failure problem and specify immediate evidence-led action.

**Model answer:** Tension cracks and toe bulging indicate active deformation rather than a harmless surface wash; prolonged rain may have raised pore pressure in an already over-steepened or weak road cut. Authorities should stop traffic and work beneath the slope, establish a safe exclusion zone and inspect drainage without sending personnel onto unstable ground. A geotechnical team should map cracks, material, seepage, movement and the likely failure surface, using repeat observations or instruments where justified. Emergency drainage or unloading should follow specialist advice because excavation can accelerate failure. Reopening requires documented stability criteria, monitoring and a contingency route.

### 🖼️ Susceptibility, Hazard Zonation and GSI Mapping — visual
```text
SUSCEPTIBILITY: where can failure occur?

```

Susceptibility maps rank terrain by propensity to fail using slope, geology, structure, drainage, land cover, inventory and related inputs. Hazard assessment adds probability, magnitude or time conditions; risk adds exposed people, buildings, roads and services. A susceptibility map therefore cannot predict a specific slide or certify a project site.

GSI, India’s nodal landslide agency, completed National Landslide Susceptibility Mapping at **1:50,000 over about 4.3 lakh sq km** in parts of **19 States/UTs**, classifying mapped terrain as low, moderate or high susceptibility. The direct 20 August 2025 PIB record links the products to Bhukosh/NGDR and notes more detailed mapping for critical sectors.

Zonation becomes preventive only when translated into development controls, route alignment, drainage requirements, site investigation, monitoring priority and evacuation plans. State and local enforcement closes the map-to-action gap.

### Decision/application lens

Audit any map through **scale → inputs → date → validation → intended decision → enforcement owner → residual uncertainty**. Never use a regional map as a parcel-level engineering clearance.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Susceptibility answers where failure is more likely.
2. Hazard adds probability, magnitude or time conditions.
3. Risk adds exposure and vulnerability.
4. GSI is the nodal landslide agency.
5. NLSM scale is 1:50,000.
6. Mapped coverage is about 4.3 lakh sq km.
7. Coverage spans parts of 19 States/UTs.
8. Classes are low, moderate and high susceptibility.
9. Maps require land-use enforcement and site investigation.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Two slopes have the same susceptibility class: one is uninhabited, while the other carries the only road to a district hospital. Should they receive the same risk-management priority?

**Model answer:** Equal susceptibility does not imply equal risk. The uninhabited slope may have limited direct consequence, whereas failure on the hospital road could delay emergency care, isolate settlements and endanger travellers. Priority should combine failure propensity with traffic, population served, alternatives, likely blockage duration and vulnerability of dependent services. The hospital corridor therefore merits earlier site investigation, drainage and movement monitoring, trigger-based closure, an alternate access plan and pre-positioned medical logistics. The uninhabited slope still needs observation where failure could dam a stream or create downstream effects. Resource allocation should follow consequence and network criticality, while avoiding the assumption that lower exposure makes a slope physically stable.

**Adjacent rubric:** Susceptibility–risk distinction 4 + hospital-road consequence 4 + priority actions 4 + residual/cascade qualification 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Slopes with the same susceptibility class necessarily require identical urgency and investment.

### 🖼️ Roads, Hydropower, Mining and Mountain Urbanisation — visual
```text
CUT SLOPE + TOE REMOVAL + SPOIL DUMPING + DRAINAGE BLOCKAGE
                 ↓
fracture/water concentration → instability
SLOPE LOADING: hotels/houses | quarry/mining | reservoir/road works

```

Mountain development can create risk by steepening or undercutting slopes, blasting and fracturing rock, loading unstable terrain, blocking drainage, dumping spoil and concentrating people along narrow corridors. Roads are essential for livelihoods and emergency access, yet poorly aligned or maintained cuts can repeatedly fail. Urban hill construction adds water leakage, excavation and load; mining and quarrying create distinct slope and subsidence hazards.

Hydropower projects require catchment, slope, reservoir-bank, muck-disposal and downstream cascade screening. This does not justify a blanket anti-development conclusion. It requires carrying-capacity evidence, alternatives analysis, staged construction, drainage, engineered support, spoil accountability, monitoring and safe closure protocols.

Risk is distributed unequally: workers, roadside settlements and tourism-dependent communities may bear hazards while benefits flow elsewhere. Consultation and accessible risk disclosure are therefore engineering and governance requirements.

### Decision/application lens

Review projects through **avoidance first → alignment/design → drainage/spoil control → staged excavation → instrumentation → traffic/worker safety → independent audit → maintenance**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Road cutting can remove toe support and alter slope angle.
2. Blocked or concentrated drainage raises instability.
3. Spoil dumping can overload slopes and channels.
4. Blasting and excavation may open discontinuities.
5. Hill urbanisation adds loads, leakage and dense exposure.
6. Mining and subsidence require distinct assessment.
7. Hydropower needs slope, reservoir-bank and downstream screening.
8. Risk-informed development is not blanket prohibition.
9. Workers and local communities require disclosure and participation.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) How can Himalayan infrastructure development proceed without normalising landslide risk creation?

**Model answer:** Development should begin with regional susceptibility and project-specific geotechnical assessment, alternatives and avoidance of very-high-risk alignments. Road and hydropower works need controlled cut geometry, staged excavation, drainage, retaining or bioengineering measures, safe spoil disposal, erosion control and monitoring. Construction traffic, workers and downstream settlements require closure thresholds, warning and emergency access. Hill towns need limits on loading, excavation and water leakage. Independent design review, public risk disclosure, maintenance budgets and liability for unauthorised changes improve compliance. Because roads and power support livelihoods, the objective is not blanket prohibition but development whose benefits are tested against lifecycle slope and cascade risk.

**Adjacent rubric:** Risk-creation mechanisms 4 + design/operation measures 5 + governance/equity 4 + balanced verdict 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Any road or hydropower project in the Himalaya is either automatically safe if approved or should be categorically prohibited.

**Transition:** Development controls reduce baseline risk; Lesson 5 adds dynamic monitoring and the limits of India’s emerging forecast service.

Monitoring combines inventories, rainfall, groundwater or soil-moisture information, ground deformation, satellite observations and local reports. Thresholds relate dynamic conditions to past failures, but sparse inventories, changing slopes and uncertain rainfall limit accuracy. Forecasts communicate a probability over an area and time window; warnings attach protective decisions.

GSI’s National Landslide Forecasting Centre was inaugurated on 19 July 2024, with initial forecasts from 20 July for Darjeeling, Kalimpong and Nilgiris. The official April 2026 record states that services covered 21 districts by December 2025: **four operational and seventeen experimental**. Bhusanket disseminates bulletins and Bhooskhalan supports citizen reporting. This is expansion, not proof of nationwide operational coverage.

A credible warning protocol publishes threshold logic, uncertainty and colour meaning; connects forecasts to district control rooms, road closure and evacuation; and evaluates misses, false alarms and user response.

### Decision/application lens

Build a warning matrix with four cells: **probability × consequence**. A moderate-probability forecast may justify closure where a hospital road or crowded pilgrimage corridor has catastrophic exposure.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Inventories are the empirical base for thresholds.
2. Dynamic rainfall and movement data complement static susceptibility.
3. Forecasts estimate likelihood over an area and time window.
4. Warnings connect likelihood to protective action.
5. NLFC was inaugurated on 19 July 2024.
6. Initial forecasts began on 20 July 2024.
7. By December 2025 coverage was 21 districts.
8. Only four districts were operational; seventeen were experimental.
9. Bhusanket and Bhooskhalan support dissemination and reporting.
10. False alarms and misses must both be reviewed.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Design a usable landslide-warning chain for a hill district and state its principal limitations.

**Model answer:** GSI should combine an updated landslide inventory and susceptibility map with rainfall forecasts, gauges, soil or groundwater indicators, deformation observations and verified field reports. A district protocol must translate probabilistic forecasts into colour-coded road inspection, traffic closure, evacuation and shelter actions, with accessible messages through control rooms, local bodies and trusted community volunteers. Redundant communication and feedback are essential. Limitations include sparse inventories, local rainfall variation, altered cut slopes, sensor failure and uncertainty in thresholds. India’s expanding NLFC service should also be described accurately: by December 2025, four of 21 covered districts were operational and 17 experimental, so validation and user-response testing remain vital.

**Adjacent rubric:** Inputs 4 + action chain 5 + limitations 3 + accurate NLFC status 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** All districts covered by an experimental bulletin possess the same validated operational forecast capability.

**Transition:** Forecasts need an action-ready landscape; Lesson 6 integrates planning, engineering, ecosystems and communities across the risk cycle.

No single retaining wall manages landslide risk. Land-use measures avoid unstable slopes and preserve drainage; engineering stabilises necessary sites; ecosystem measures reduce erosion and regulate runoff where appropriate; communities identify cracks, blocked drains and unsafe spoil dumping and execute warnings.

Engineering choices follow failure mechanism: surface and subsurface drainage, slope reprofiling and benching, retaining or reinforced-earth systems, rock bolts, nets, toe protection and debris barriers. Vegetation can reinforce shallow soil and reduce erosion, but cannot stabilise every deep-seated slide. Structures need outlet maintenance and design drainage; otherwise protection can relocate risk.

NDMA’s 2009 guidelines and 2019 strategy frame hazard mapping, monitoring/EWS, awareness, capacity, regulation and mitigation. Local plans should identify safe routes and shelters, priority lifelines, equipment, trained search teams and pre-monsoon inspections.

### Decision/application lens

Choose treatment only after answering **what is moving, along which surface, because of which water/force pathway, and which asset must remain functional**.

2. Drainage is central because water often reduces stability.
3. Treatment must match the diagnosed failure mechanism.
4. Vegetation is useful but not universal stabilisation.
5. Retaining structures require outlets and maintenance.
6. Debris barriers need safe deposition and clearance plans.
7. Local reporting can reveal cracks and blocked drains early.
8. Safe routes must avoid likely slide and debris-flow paths.

### 🖼️ Avalanche Formation, Terrain and Snowpack Instability — visual
```text
WEATHER builds load: fresh snow | wind loading | rain-on-snow | warming
SNOWPACK contains layers: slab over weak layer
TERRAIN enables release: commonly 30°–45° starting zone
TRIGGER tips balance: natural loading | skier/activity
PATH → runout → exposed road/post/settlement
```

An avalanche begins when stress on a snowpack exceeds its strength. A slab avalanche commonly involves a cohesive snow slab failing along a persistent or newly formed weak layer. Fresh snowfall, wind transport, rapid warming, temperature fluctuation and rain-on-snow can increase load or weaken bonds. Release terrain is commonly around **30–45 degrees**, while tracks and runout zones extend to gentler terrain.

Useful categories include loose-snow and slab avalanches, and dry- versus wet-snow events. Hazard evaluation considers slope angle and aspect, elevation, snow stratigraphy, wind loading, weather history, terrain traps and runout—not snowfall alone. Forest may reduce initiation in some settings but cannot be assumed to stop large avalanches.

Exposure includes Himalayan roads, defence posts, workers, tourists, settlements and lifelines. Zoning must therefore map starting zones, tracks and runout together.

### Decision/application lens

Read a slope from top to bottom: **starting-zone steepness and loading → weak-layer evidence → track confinement → terrain trap/runout → time-varying exposure**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Avalanches occur when snowpack stress exceeds strength.
2. Slab avalanches fail on a weak layer beneath cohesive snow.
3. Fresh and wind-loaded snow can rapidly add stress.
4. Warming and rain-on-snow may weaken the pack.
5. Starting zones are commonly around 30–45 degrees.
6. Tracks and runout zones may be gentler than starting zones.
7. Aspect and elevation influence loading and temperature.
8. Terrain traps increase burial consequence.
9. Avalanche hazard is not measured by snowfall alone.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Explain why snowfall totals alone cannot determine avalanche danger.

**Model answer:** Snowfall adds load, but avalanche danger depends on whether the snowpack contains a weak layer, how wind redistributes snow, temperature and rain-on-snow changes, and the slope’s angle, aspect and elevation. A cohesive slab over a weak interface can fail even when total snowfall appears moderate. Conversely, deep stable snow may not release. Terrain also controls consequence: starting zones, confined tracks and terrain traps determine acceleration and burial depth. Forecasting must therefore integrate weather observations, snow profiles and stability tests with terrain and exposure rather than use a single snowfall threshold.

**Adjacent rubric:** Snowpack mechanics 4 + weather/terrain factors 4 + forecasting conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** More snow automatically means proportionately higher avalanche danger on every slope.

**Transition:** Mechanics identify the danger; Lesson 8 links DGRE forecasting to zoning, closures, shelters and user behaviour.

Its bulletins communicate area-specific danger; they do not inspect every slope or directly execute each closure. Static avalanche zoning guides settlement, road alignment and protection structures. ### Decision/application lens

Glacial lakes may be moraine-dammed, ice-dammed, supraglacial or bedrock-dammed. A GLOF occurs when stored water is released suddenly after overtopping or failure of the retaining barrier. 7. Lake size alone is not a complete danger measure.
8. Downstream exposure converts hazard into risk.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is ranking glacial lakes only by surface area an unsafe GLOF policy?

Dam geometry, seepage, drainage, lake growth and seismic setting affect breach potential. The downstream channel, sediment entrainment, settlements, bridges, hydropower and warning time determine consequence. Screening should therefore combine lake and dam condition, potential triggers, breach modelling and exposure, followed by field validation for high-priority lakes.


Mountain disasters often cross administrative and hazard silos. Rain or an earthquake can trigger landslides; slope failure can block a river or strike a glacial lake; breach water entrains debris; damaged bridges and roads isolate communities; hydropower or communication failures complicate response. Each node changes the next hazard’s timing and magnitude.

Planning should identify credible sequences, not every imaginable chain. Common-source dependencies—one road, fibre line or substation serving several valleys—deserve special attention. Monitoring must share information across GSI, DGRE, NRSC, IMD, CWC, State authorities and operators while preserving named decision accountability.

Climate change can alter glacier mass, permafrost, snow/rain regimes and extreme precipitation, thereby conditioning risk. But a specific slide, avalanche or GLOF remains multi-causal. Statements about event attribution require formal evidence and uncertainty, not intuition after a disaster.

### Decision/application lens

### REVISION NOTES

1. Mountain hazards can form multi-stage cascades.
2. A landslide can block a river and create a temporary lake.
3. Ice or rock impact can initiate lake overtopping.
4. Floodwater can entrain sediment and amplify damage.
5. Road and bridge failure can create response isolation.
6. Shared lifelines are common-source vulnerabilities.
7. Inter-agency data sharing does not erase decision accountability.
8. Climate change can condition hazard drivers.
9. Single-event attribution needs event-specific evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Why must Himalayan disaster planning move from single-hazard lists to bounded cascade scenarios?

**Model answer:** Single-hazard plans miss how one failure changes the next. Rain or earthquakes may trigger landslides; a slope or ice avalanche may block a river or strike a glacial lake; breach water can entrain debris, damage hydropower and bridges and isolate settlements. Bounded scenarios identify plausible initiation, propagation and receptor nodes rather than speculate about unlimited chains. Authorities can then diversify roads, power and communication, share monitoring data, set operator shutdown rules and pre-position response beyond likely blockage points. Climate-conditioned changes in rainfall, snow, glaciers or permafrost should inform scenarios, while attribution of a particular event remains evidence-based and uncertain.

**Adjacent rubric:** Cascade explanation 5 + bounded method 3 + resilience actions 4 + attribution caution 3 = **15 marks**

### 🖼️ Mountain-Risk Programme Evaluation — visual
```text
RESULTS CHAIN
inputs → maps/sensors/training → usable warnings/enforcement
→ timely protective action → lower mortality/disruption

MEASURE FOUR DIMENSIONS
coverage | capability | use | outcome
CHECK: equity + reliability + unintended risk + learning
```

Evaluation separates expenditure and equipment from functioning capability and reduced harm. Mapping kilometres, installed sensors and trained staff are outputs. Capability indicators include data uptime, validated thresholds, forecast skill and staffed decision chains. Use indicators include last-mile receipt, closure compliance, evacuation time and maintenance. Outcomes include mortality, isolation duration and avoided disruption, interpreted cautiously because event severity varies.

Evaluation must preserve institutional distinctions while testing interfaces: GSI landslide products, DGRE avalanche bulletins, NRSC lake monitoring and State or district action. Equity audits ask whether remote settlements, workers, tourists and persons with disabilities receive comparable warning and evacuation opportunity. Independent review should publish uncertainty, misses, false alarms and corrective action.

A counterfactual—comparable untreated corridors or before/after capability—strengthens inference. Near misses and exercises provide learning where destructive events are infrequent.

### Decision/application lens

Create a dashboard that cannot be gamed by equipment counts: require at least one indicator each for **coverage, reliability, user action, equity and outcome**, plus a corrective-action tracker.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Outputs are not identical to capabilities or outcomes.
2. Sensor uptime is more informative than installation count alone.
3. Forecast skill must include misses and false alarms.
4. Warning receipt does not prove protective action.
5. Outcome comparison must adjust for event severity.
6. Institutional interfaces deserve explicit evaluation.
7. Equity indicators test who remains outside the warning chain.
8. Exercises and near misses provide useful evidence.
9. Independent review should track corrective closure.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These short-answer checks test transfer across the completed session. Each is worth **3 marks** and has a concise responsive model plus an adjacent exact rubric.

## Cumulative check 1 — susceptibility, hazard and risk

**Question:** Distinguish susceptibility, hazard and risk for one landslide corridor. **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Susceptibility identifies corridor slopes predisposed to fail. Hazard adds the probability, magnitude or time condition of failure. Risk combines that hazard with exposed travellers, settlements, lifelines and their vulnerability.

**Adjacent rubric:** Susceptibility 1 + hazard 1 + risk application 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 2 — map scale

**Question:** Why can a 1:50,000 susceptibility map not replace parcel-level geotechnical investigation? **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** The regional map screens broad terrain and cannot resolve every local discontinuity, groundwater path, cut slope or foundation condition. A project therefore needs site investigation and design even when the regional class is known.

**Adjacent rubric:** Regional purpose 1 + local limitations 1 + site-investigation conclusion 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 3 — forecast versus warning

**Question:** Contrast a landslide forecast with the authority’s warning decision. **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** A forecast estimates likelihood over an area and period with uncertainty. A warning is the responsible authority’s action message, translating that evidence and consequence into closure, evacuation, inspection or readiness.

**Adjacent rubric:** Forecast 1 + warning 1 + action translation 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 4 — avalanche evidence

**Question:** What snowpack and terrain evidence is needed beyond snowfall totals? **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Assess weak layers, slab cohesion, wind loading, warming or rain-on-snow, and slope angle, aspect, track and runout. These determine release and consequence more reliably than snowfall depth alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Snowpack evidence 1 + weather/loading evidence 1 + terrain/runout 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 5 — GLOF impact pathway

**Question:** Trace one ice- or rock-impact pathway to GLOF. **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Ice or rock enters the lake, creates a displacement wave, overtops an erodible natural dam and initiates breach. Released water then entrains sediment and threatens downstream receptors.

**Adjacent rubric:** Impact wave 1 + overtopping/breach 1 + downstream cascade 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 6 — satellite and field evidence

**Question:** Separate satellite screening from field validation of glacial lakes. **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Satellite screening tracks lake location, area and change across remote terrain. Field validation examines dam material, freeboard, seepage, impact slopes and downstream conditions that imagery cannot fully establish.

**Adjacent rubric:** Satellite role 1 + field role 1 + complementarity/limit 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 7 — fail-safe warning

**Question:** Design a fail-safe GLOF warning chain with one telecom outage. **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Validated monitoring should reach a named authority through a primary data link and independent radio or satellite fallback. The authority then uses sirens and trained local volunteers to trigger rehearsed evacuation.

**Adjacent rubric:** Validation/authority 1 + independent fallback 1 + last-mile action 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 8 — climate language

**Question:** How does climate conditioning differ from attribution of an individual event? **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Climate conditioning means changing rain, heat, snow, glacier or permafrost conditions may alter background risk. Event attribution asks whether human-caused climate change measurably influenced a specific event and requires dedicated evidence.

**Adjacent rubric:** Conditioning 1 + event attribution 1 + evidence caution 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 9 — residual risk

**Question:** Compare avoidance, engineering and preparedness through residual risk. **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Avoidance removes exposure from the unstable site; engineering reduces failure probability or impact; preparedness protects people when failure remains possible. Residual risk normally persists after engineering and must be monitored and communicated.

**Adjacent rubric:** Avoidance 1 + engineering/preparedness distinction 1 + residual-risk conclusion 1 = **3 marks**

## Cumulative check 10 — evaluation indicators

**Question:** Propose one capability, use and outcome indicator for a mountain-risk programme. **(3 marks)**

**Model answer:** Capability: validated sensor uptime. Use: proportion of alerts producing timely closure or evacuation. Outcome: duration of isolation or mortality adjusted for event severity. Together they prevent equipment counts from masquerading as impact.

**Adjacent rubric:** Capability indicator 1 + use indicator 1 + outcome indicator 1 = **3 marks**

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark practice

**Question:** Explain why “map, monitor and maintain” is a more reliable landslide-risk strategy than isolated protective works. **(10 marks; answer in no more than 150 words.)**

**Model answer (122 words):** Mapping locates susceptible terrain, exposed settlements and critical lifelines, allowing avoidance and priority setting. Monitoring adds time-varying rainfall, groundwater, movement and field evidence, converting a static zone into a decision context. Maintenance keeps drains, retaining outlets, nets, barriers and sensors functional; neglected works may merely shift water or debris and create new failure. The three functions reinforce one another: maps place sensors and controls, observations update maps, and maintenance records reveal recurring instability. Isolated walls often fail when the movement mechanism, drainage or toe condition is misunderstood. A district should therefore connect GSI products to site investigation, land-use enforcement, trigger-based closures, community reporting and funded pre-monsoon inspection. Structural protection remains useful, but only as one diagnosed and maintained layer within residual-risk planning.

**Adjacent rubric:** Mapping logic 3 + monitoring logic 3 + maintenance/failure mechanism 2 + integrated governance 2 = **10 marks**

## Original 15-mark practice

**Question:** Examine the institutional and technological requirements of a people-centred mountain early-warning system. **(15 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (145 words):** A people-centred system begins with differentiated risk knowledge: GSI landslide zones and forecasts, DGRE avalanche information and NRSC glacial-lake monitoring cannot be collapsed into one threshold. Dynamic observations—rainfall, snowpack, deformation, lake level and field reports—need validated models, uncertainty statements and resilient data links. Technology becomes warning only when a named authority verifies evidence, selects an action and communicates it redundantly through telecom, radio, sirens, operators and trusted local volunteers. Messages should identify place, validity, severity and protective action in accessible languages and formats. District plans must connect them to road closure, worker shutdown, vertical or lateral evacuation, shelters, medical support and responder safety. Exercises should include night events, tourists, migrants, persons with disabilities, sensor failure and telecom outage. Evaluation must track data uptime, forecast skill, alert latency, receipt, compliance, evacuation time, misses and false alarms. Thus, institutions, trust and rehearsed action—not devices alone—create warning capability.

**Adjacent rubric:** Differentiated risk/technology 4 + decision architecture 4 + last-mile action/inclusion 4 + evaluation 3 = **15 marks**

## Original 20-mark practice

**Question:** “Mountain disaster risk is increasingly a problem of connected infrastructure and institutional interfaces.” Analyse with reference to landslides, avalanches and GLOFs. **(20 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (185 words):** Mountain hazards interact with corridors, dams, power, telecom and settlements concentrated in narrow valleys. A landslide can close the only road, block a river and threaten a hydropower site; an avalanche can isolate workers and responders; a GLOF can damage bridges, intakes and communications far downstream. The same infrastructure may create and carry risk: cut slopes and spoil destabilise terrain, while one road or grid becomes a common-source dependency during response.

Governance is equally connected. GSI’s susceptibility and landslide forecasts, DGRE’s avalanche bulletins and NRSC’s glacial-lake observations require State, district, road, hydropower and community decisions. Data sharing without named authority causes delay; centralisation without local observation weakens relevance. Risk reduction therefore needs alternatives and carrying-capacity appraisal, mechanism-specific engineering, drainage and maintenance, diversified lifelines, operator shutdown rules and redundant communications. Bounded cascade exercises should test night staffing, sensor failure, road blockage and accessible evacuation.

Climate-conditioned changes may alter rainfall, snow and glacial-lake risk, but individual-event attribution requires evidence. Programme evaluation should measure capability and action—uptime, forecast skill, closure and evacuation—not equipment counts. Connected risk thus requires interoperable information with clear accountability, equitable protection and planned redundancy.

**Adjacent rubric:** Connected hazard/infrastructure analysis 5 + institutional interfaces 5 + risk-reduction design 5 + caution/evaluation/equity 5 = **20 marks**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Conceptual base

- **Landslide:** gravity-driven downslope movement of rock, debris or earth; describe material and movement.
- **Avalanche:** rapid downslope movement of unstable snow/ice, often a slab failing on a weak layer.
- **GLOF:** sudden release from a glacial lake following overtopping or failure of a natural dam.
- Susceptibility asks where failure is more likely; hazard adds probability/intensity/time; risk adds exposure and vulnerability.
- Predisposition and trigger differ: weak weathered terrain may exist before rain, earthquake, erosion or excavation initiates movement.

## 2. Landslide typology and Indian geography

- Movement types: falls, topples, rotational/translational slides, spreads, debris/earth flows and complex movement.
- Himalaya: young tectonics, high relief, fractured or weak material, earthquakes, river incision, monsoon rain and rapid corridor development.
- Western Ghats/Nilgiris: steep deeply weathered slopes, intense monsoon rainfall, drainage alteration, quarrying and construction.
- North-East and Andaman–Nicobar are important susceptibility regions; mining/subsidence creates different peninsular risks.
- Do not use the legacy “15% area” owner estimate as an unqualified current statistic.

## 3. GSI mapping and forecasting

- NLSM: 1:50,000 scale, about 4.3 lakh sq km, parts of 19 States/UTs, low/moderate/high susceptibility.
- Regional susceptibility maps guide screening and priority but do not certify a site or predict timing.
- NLFC inaugurated 19 July 2024; initial forecasts from 20 July for Darjeeling, Kalimpong and Nilgiris.
- By December 2025: 21 districts covered, of which 4 operational and 17 experimental.
- Bhusanket disseminates bulletins; Bhooskhalan enables citizen reporting.

## 4. Landslide governance and mitigation

- Avoid unstable terrain where feasible; subject remaining projects to site-specific geotechnical assessment.
- Roads require safe alignment, staged cuts, drainage, spoil control, maintenance and traffic-closure thresholds.
- Engineering includes drainage, reprofiling, retaining/reinforced structures, bolts/nets, toe protection and barriers, chosen by failure mechanism.
- Ecosystem measures reduce erosion and runoff in suitable settings but do not stabilise every deep slide.
- Community reporting, pre-monsoon inspection, accessible warnings, routes, shelters and trained teams manage residual risk.
- NDMA issued landslide/snow-avalanche guidelines in 2009 and a National Landslide Risk Management Strategy in 2019.

## 5. Avalanche essentials

- Stress must exceed snowpack strength; slab failure commonly occurs over a weak layer.
- Danger reflects fresh/wind-loaded snow, weak layers, warming, temperature fluctuation, rain-on-snow, slope, aspect and elevation.
- Starting zones are commonly around 30–45°; tracks and runout extend beyond them.
- DGRE/DRDO provides specialised avalanche research and warning; local authorities/operators execute closures and preparedness.
- Zoning, bulletins, closure rules, galleries/barriers where appropriate, rescue equipment and secondary-avalanche safety form the package.

## 6. GLOF essentials

- Lake/dam types include moraine-, ice- and bedrock-dammed and supraglacial settings.
- Triggers/processes include impact waves, overtopping, piping/internal erosion, earthquakes, blocked drainage and dam degradation.
- Risk ranking needs dam condition, freeboard, lake trend, impact slopes, downstream channel, assets and warning time—not area alone.
- NRSC Bhuvan and the official atlas support repeat satellite screening; field validation remains necessary.
- NGRMP: ₹150 crore—₹135 crore NDMF plus ₹15 crore States—in Arunachal Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Sikkim and Uttarakhand.
- Official 4 December 2024 record: dynamic list of 195 high-risk lakes.
- Warning requires monitoring, validation, decision authority, redundant communication, evacuation and operator protocols.
- Lake lowering or stabilisation requires multidisciplinary review because intervention can trigger failure.

## 7. Cascades, climate and recovery

- Landslide or ice/rock avalanche may dam a river or strike a lake; breach may entrain debris and damage bridges, roads, hydropower and telecom.
- Protect common-source lifelines through redundancy, alternate routes and distributed communications.
- Climate change may condition rain, snow, glacier and permafrost drivers; an individual-event claim requires attribution evidence.
- Response prioritises scene safety and secondary-hazard monitoring.
- Build Back Better considers relocation/realignment, drainage, monitored reconstruction, livelihood continuity and inclusive loss records.

## 8. Advanced analytical tools

- Compare avoid, redesign, phase/season, and no-project options; state non-negotiable safety constraints.
- Use adaptive approval conditions, observable stop rules, independent monitoring and public reasons.
- Stress tests specify sequence, cues, decision owner, delay, fallback and corrective action.
- Evaluate programmes through coverage, capability, use, equity and outcomes—not expenditure or device count alone.
