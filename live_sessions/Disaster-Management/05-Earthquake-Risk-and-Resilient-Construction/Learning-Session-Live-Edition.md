### 🖼️ Earthquake Risk and Indian Cases — visual

```text
RISK = HAZARD × EXPOSURE × VULNERABILITY ÷ CAPACITY
FAULT RUPTURE → SHAKING/SITE EFFECTS → BUILDING + NETWORK FAILURE
               cannot prevent          can reduce
```

Earthquakes release accumulated strain through fault rupture and seismic waves. Present science cannot precisely predict their time, place and magnitude, but disaster loss is governable because siting, exposure, construction, contents, lifelines and response capacity shape outcomes.

India’s vulnerability is not Himalayan only. **Latur (1993)** exposed vulnerable masonry and low preparedness in a peninsular setting. **Bhuj (2001)** demonstrated destructive Kachchh shaking, widespread built-environment failure and the importance of mapping and safer reconstruction. **Koyna** remains a cautionary peninsular case; this lesson avoids unsourced magnitude, casualty or causation figures. The Mains thesis is therefore collapse prevention and continuity, not prediction.

### Case-to-thesis method

Use Bhuj and Latur to demonstrate different regional and built-environment vulnerabilities. Organise the answer as tectonic setting → exposed stock → failure mechanism → enforceable mitigation. Koyna rebuts the “aseismic peninsula” error but should not carry an unsourced causal narrative.

2. Precise time-place-magnitude prediction is unavailable.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) A moderate earthquake affects two Indian towns with similar populations, but one suffers far greater collapse and service disruption. Explain the risk factors that can produce this contrast.

**Model answer:** Comparable population and event size do not imply comparable loss. Local soil or slope may amplify shaking or destabilise sites; one town may have irregular frames, weak masonry, poor materials, unsafe additions and unsecured contents. Higher occupancy, narrow access, fragile power, water, hospitals and communications can multiply disruption. The better-performing town may have enforced construction, retrofitted critical buildings, protected non-structural systems, open routes, trained responders and practised continuity plans. Disaster severity therefore reflects hazard interacting with exposure, vulnerability and capacity, not magnitude or population alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Site factors 2 + building/non-structural vulnerability 3 + lifeline/exposure factors 3 + capacity-based conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Similar magnitude and population necessarily produce similar disaster outcomes.

**Transition:** These cases require Lesson 2 to distinguish source physics from vulnerability that policy can change.

### 🖼️ Indian Tectonic Sources, Magnitude and Intensity — visual

```text
INDIAN PLATE NORTHWARD (~5 cm/year) → HIMALAYAN COLLISION
ANDAMAN SUBDUCTION | KACHCHH/KHAMBHAT | PENINSULAR SOURCES
ONE EVENT MAGNITUDE → MANY LOCAL INTENSITIES
```

The Indian plate moves northward at approximately **5 cm/year**, loading Himalayan faults as it converges with Eurasia. Exam-relevant sources also include the North-East, Andaman–Nicobar subduction environment, Kachchh/Rann of Kutch, Gulf of Khambhat and peninsular intraplate sources such as Koyna. The rate is a tectonic anchor, not a short-term earthquake clock.

Magnitude estimates event size, commonly through Mw for large earthquakes. Intensity records effects at a place and varies with distance, depth, soil, slope and buildings; MMI or MSK may express it. P-wave detection after rupture can support rapid information, but it is not pre-event prediction.

### Measurement comparison

| Dimension | Magnitude | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | one event | affected place |
| Changes across city | no | yes |
| Planning value | scenario size | damage/site interpretation |
| Common error | universal “Richter” shorthand | treated as another magnitude |

### REVISION NOTES

1. Indian plate convergence is approximately 5 cm/year.
2. Himalayan collision is a dominant but not exclusive source.
3. Andaman–Nicobar belongs to an active subduction environment.
4. Kachchh and Gulf of Khambhat are important western sources.
5. Koyna and Latur demonstrate damaging peninsular seismicity.
6. Magnitude is event-level; intensity is location-specific.
7. Mw is preferred for large events over universal Richter shorthand.
8. MMI/MSK reflects observed effects and local conditions.
9. Detection after rupture is not prediction before rupture.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Distinguish magnitude, intensity and earthquake prediction and explain their planning value.

**Model answer:** Magnitude estimates the size of one earthquake, commonly through moment magnitude for large events. Intensity describes observed effects at a place and varies with distance, soil, slope and buildings; MMI or MSK may express it. Prediction would specify a future event’s time, place and magnitude, which present capability cannot reliably do. Monitoring detects and locates rupture after it begins. Managers use magnitude for event characterisation and intensity/site evidence for damage assessment and local planning, while continuing mitigation rather than waiting for prediction.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definitions 4 + distinctions 3 + planning relevance 3 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Magnitude, intensity and prediction are interchangeable.

**Transition:** Regional source knowledge feeds Lesson 3’s zonation map, which is a design input rather than a damage prophecy.

### 🖼️ Seismic Zonation and the Operative BIS Position — visual

```text
IS 1893 (PART 1):2016 — ACTIVE, REAFFIRMED 2021
ZONE V █████ highest | IV ████ | III ███ | II ██
OFFICIAL AREA SHARES: V ~11% | IV ~18% | III ~30%
```

The direct BIS record lists **IS 1893 (Part 1):2016, Criteria for Earthquake Resistant Design of Structures—Part 1**, reaffirmed in 2021 and active. India uses Zones II–V, with Zone V highest. A 30 July 2021 official record gives approximate land-area shares of Zone V 11%, Zone IV 18% and Zone III 30%—about **59%** in Zones III–V.

Zone V includes the whole North-East and Andaman–Nicobar and parts of the Himalayan belt, northern Bihar and Gujarat. Zonation supplies broad design hazard, not a building certificate or prediction. A 1 April 2026 MoES record states that a proposed revised map was withdrawn in March 2026; this session therefore does not teach an announced five-zone scheme as operative.

### Four-step map reading

1. Identify the operative standard and zone. 2. Add local soil/slope conditions. 3. Add building and occupancy vulnerability. 4. Convert the combined risk into design, audit or preparedness action.

### REVISION NOTES

1. IS 1893 (Part 1):2016 is the operative direct BIS record used here.
2. India has Zones II, III, IV and V.
3. Zone V is highest hazard; Zone II is not zero hazard.
4. Official shares are about 11% Zone V, 18% Zone IV and 30% Zone III.
5. Zones III–V together cover about 59% of landmass.
6. Zone V includes the North-East and Andaman–Nicobar.
7. Zonation is broad hazard, not building certification.
8. The proposed revision was withdrawn in March 2026.
9. Site investigation, design and compliance remain necessary everywhere.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is seismic zonation neither earthquake prediction nor a building-safety certificate?

**Model answer:** Zonation assigns broad regional design hazard under the operative standard; it does not state when or where the next earthquake will occur. Nor does a zone reveal the safety of a particular building. Local soil amplification, liquefaction, slope, configuration, materials, workmanship, occupancy and maintenance alter realised risk. A Zone II structure can be unsafe, while a properly designed Zone V structure may perform better. Authorities must combine zonation with microzonation or site investigation, applicable standards, inspection, retrofit and preparedness.

**Adjacent rubric:** Zonation meaning 3 + limits 4 + decision chain 3 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Zone II makes earthquake-resistant construction unnecessary.

**Transition:** Because regional zones cannot reveal neighbourhood amplification, Lesson 4 changes scale from India to site.

Microzonation subdivides an area using local geology, soil response, groundwater, slope, fault context and expected shaking. Soft sediment may amplify motion; saturated loose soil may liquefy; slopes can fail and access can be cut. It supports land use, critical-facility siting, audit priority and scenarios, but does not replace project-specific geotechnical investigation.

The **BMTPC Vulnerability Atlas of India, Third Edition (2019)** is a different national instrument. Its official portal combines State/UT hazard maps with housing-stock vulnerability. It supports screening and prioritisation, not parcel-level engineering certification. The edition is stated with its vintage rather than casually called “latest.”

| City land use | microzonation | update and enforce |
| Individual building | site + structural assessment | qualified professional |

1. Microzonation refines rather than replaces national zonation.
2. Local sediment can amplify shaking.

4. Slope conditions can add landslide and access risk.
5. Critical facilities require local hazard evidence.

8. The Atlas combines multi-hazard maps and housing vulnerability.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) How do microzonation and the BMTPC Vulnerability Atlas perform complementary planning functions?

**Model answer:** The BMTPC Vulnerability Atlas provides broad State/UT hazard and housing-vulnerability screening, helping governments compare areas and prioritise programmes. Microzonation works at finer urban or regional scale, combining geology, soil response, groundwater, slope, liquefaction and expected shaking to distinguish neighbourhood conditions within one seismic zone. The Atlas can indicate where deeper study is needed; microzonation can guide land use, critical-facility siting, audit priorities and emergency scenarios. Neither is an individual-building certificate. Site-specific geotechnical and structural assessment remains necessary, and maps must be updated and enforced.

### 🖼️ Building Failure through Bhuj and Latur — visual

```text
SHAKING → INERTIAL FORCE
      ├─ irregularity → torsion
      ├─ soft storey → drift concentration
      ├─ weak joints/masonry → brittle failure
      └─ unsecured contents → injury/service loss
```

Buildings are damaged as ground acceleration generates inertial force. Safety needs a continuous load path from roof and floors through walls or frames to foundations. Plan or vertical irregularity creates torsion and concentration; open soft storeys concentrate drift; weak joints, short columns, pounding, unreinforced masonry and poor materials can fail brittly.

**Bhuj (2001)** shows that urban reinforced-concrete and masonry performance depends on configuration, detailing, material quality and enforcement, not a modern-looking frame. **Latur (1993)** illustrates vulnerable masonry, heavy roofs and low perceived risk in a peninsular setting. These qualitative, owner-supported features are used without unsourced casualty or magnitude figures. “Earthquake-resistant” means controlled performance, not no damage.

### Failure-diagnosis drill

Read a damaged building through **site → configuration → load path → detailing → materials/workmanship → contents → inspection history**. This replaces the weak explanation “the earthquake was strong” with an auditable failure chain.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Ground acceleration generates inertial force.
2. A continuous load path carries force to foundations.
3. Plan irregularity can cause torsion.
4. Soft storeys concentrate drift and column demand.
5. Weak joints and unreinforced masonry fail brittly.
6. Pounding follows inadequate separation.
7. Bhuj demonstrates detailing, quality and enforcement gaps.
8. Latur demonstrates vulnerable masonry and low risk perception.
9. Earthquake resistance targets life-safety performance, not zero damage.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Using Indian cases, explain why configuration and construction quality can matter more than magnitude for disaster loss.

**Model answer:** Magnitude characterises the event, but collapse depends on local intensity, site response and the building’s force path. Irregular plans can twist; open ground storeys concentrate drift; weak joints and unreinforced masonry fail brittly; poor materials and workmanship defeat design; unsecured heavy components injure occupants. Bhuj (2001) showed major urban losses where configuration, detailing and compliance were deficient. Latur (1993) showed how vulnerable masonry and heavy roofs could create severe rural loss in a peninsular setting with low risk perception. Risk reduction therefore requires regular configuration, ductile detailing, quality control, inspection, anchorage and retrofit.

**Adjacent rubric:** Mechanisms 5 + cases 4 + magnitude qualification 3 + measures 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** More concrete or steel automatically corrects an irregular brittle system.

**Transition:** Visible failure pathways allow Lesson 6 to trace written standards into delivered structural performance.

### 🖼️ BIS/NBC and Ductile Construction — visual

```text
HAZARD INPUT → STRUCTURAL SYSTEM → LOAD PATH → DUCTILE DETAILING
      → MATERIALS → SITE SUPERVISION → INSPECTION → MAINTENANCE
written standard ≠ delivered performance
```

The direct BIS record lists **IS 1893 (Part 1):2016** as active and reaffirmed in 2021. It provides general provisions and building criteria for earthquake-resistant design. NBC 2016 integrates planning, structural sufficiency, fire, health and construction practice; other applicable BIS standards govern particular systems and materials. This lesson teaches functions, not unsourced prescriptive dimensions.

Ductility is the ability to deform and dissipate energy without sudden brittle collapse. It depends on structural system, regularity, capacity design, member and joint detailing, compatible materials, workmanship and inspection. A code is only the first link: by-law adoption, competent designers, trained masons, permit scrutiny, site checks, completion control and maintenance deliver performance.

### Answer spine: standard to safety

**Name the instrument → state its function → trace delivery → identify the failure point → assign accountability.** A copied code clause cannot compensate for changed reinforcement, weak joints or poor site execution.

### REVISION NOTES

1. IS 1893 (Part 1):2016 is active and reaffirmed in 2021.
2. NBC 2016 integrates broad building-safety practice.
3. Ductility enables controlled deformation and energy dissipation.
4. Regular configuration reduces demand concentration.
5. Continuous load paths join roof, floors, frame/walls and foundation.
6. Detailing, materials and workmanship must be compatible.
7. By-law adoption translates standards into local control.
8. Inspection must verify built work, not only drawings.
9. Code existence is an input; performance is the outcome.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Why is ductility a system property rather than a clause or material property?

**Model answer:** Ductility is the capacity of a structural system to deform and dissipate earthquake energy without sudden brittle collapse. It cannot be secured by one material label or copied clause. The chosen system, regularity, load path, capacity design, member and joint detailing, material compatibility, workmanship and inspection must work together. A ductile drawing can be defeated by changed reinforcement, weak joints, poor concrete or unapproved alterations. Standards such as IS 1893 and NBC guidance therefore require competent design, trained construction, site supervision and enforcement.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definition 3 + system mechanisms 5 + delivery failures 4 + conclusion 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Reinforced concrete or steel automatically makes a building ductile.

```text
NDMA GUIDELINES (APRIL 2007)
1 SAFE NEW CONSTRUCTION       2 SELECTIVE PRIORITY RETROFIT
3 REGULATION/ENFORCEMENT     4 AWARENESS/PREPAREDNESS

                    ↓

```

NDMA’s **National Disaster Management Guidelines: Management of Earthquakes (April 2007)** organise the strategy into six complete pillars:

1. Earthquake-resistant design and construction of new structures.
2. Selective seismic strengthening and retrofitting of existing priority and lifeline structures.
3. Regulation and enforcement.
4. Awareness and preparedness.
5. Capacity development.
6. Emergency response.

The pillars are interdependent. New-build compliance cannot protect inherited vulnerable stock; retrofit without competent professionals is unsafe; awareness without enforceable construction leaves collapse risk; response cannot compensate for mass preventable failure. This complete framework is taught here before any later assessment uses “six pillars” as evidence.

### Six-pillar diagnostic

For a State or city, mark each pillar **designed / funded / implemented / audited**. The weakest status often reveals the binding bottleneck and turns a memorised list into governance analysis.

### REVISION NOTES

1. The source is NDMA’s April 2007 earthquake guideline.
2. Pillar 1 prevents new structural vulnerability.
3. Pillar 2 reduces risk in priority and lifeline stock.
4. Pillar 3 joins regulation to enforcement.
5. Pillar 4 covers awareness and preparedness.
6. Pillar 5 builds professional, craft and administrative capacity.
7. Pillar 6 manages residual loss through emergency response.
8. No pillar can substitute for all others.
9. Use the six pillars diagnostically, not as a shopping list.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Show how NDMA’s six pillars convert non-predictability into comprehensive earthquake-risk management.

**Model answer:** Because earthquakes cannot be precisely predicted or prevented, NDMA shifts policy to reducing vulnerability and residual loss. Safe new construction prevents new risk; selective retrofit reduces dangerous existing stock; regulation and enforcement make technical rules operative; awareness and preparedness convert knowledge into protective behaviour; capacity development equips professionals, masons, officials and responders; and emergency response manages unavoidable consequences. The pillars are interdependent: codes fail without skills and inspection, retrofit fails without prioritisation and finance, and response is overwhelmed where collapse prevention is neglected. The framework therefore spans mitigation, preparedness and response rather than relying on monitoring alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** All six pillars 6 + non-predictability 3 + interdependence 4 + synthesis 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** The six pillars are independent schemes that may be implemented in isolation.

**Transition:** The second pillar creates an allocation problem, which Lesson 8 resolves through responsibility and priority criteria.

### 🖼️ Retrofitting, Federal Responsibility and Priority Structures — visual

```text
SCREEN → DETAILED ASSESS → PRIORITISE → ENGINEER → EXECUTE → VERIFY

collapse risk × occupancy × critical function × network consequence
```

Retrofitting is structure-specific and begins with screening and qualified assessment; no universal technique is safe. NDMA’s **June 2014** guideline provides the official deficient-building retrofit framework.

Central ministries and State governments facilitate seismically safe design and construction for assets under their administrative control, including bridges, flyovers, ports and harbours. **State governments**, through ULBs and PRIs, identify priority and lifeline structures for retrofitting. Named priorities in the canonical source are **Raj Bhavans, legislatures, courts, academic institutions, reservoirs, dams and multi-storeyed buildings of five or more floors**. Hospitals, schools and emergency facilities also merit consequence-based screening.

A transparent register separates identified, assessed, designed, funded, completed and independently verified work. Listing is not retrofitting.

|---|---|---|
| Centre/Ministry | safe assets under control; national guidance | compliance inventory |
| State | identify priority/lifeline stock | staged retrofit register |
| ULB/PRI | local inventory, permits and implementation | inspection record |
| Engineer | assessment, design and verification | signed technical record |

### REVISION NOTES

2. Detailed assessment precedes structure-specific design.
3. NDMA’s deficient-building retrofit guideline is dated June 2014.
4. Centre and States address assets under administrative control.
5. States identify priority structures through ULBs and PRIs.
6. Named priorities include Raj Bhavans, legislatures and courts.
7. Academic institutions, reservoirs and dams are also named.

9. Report identification, assessment, funding, execution and verification separately.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Explain how India should prioritise seismic retrofit while assigning responsibility accurately.

**Model answer:** Priority should follow screening and detailed assessment, combining collapse probability, occupancy, emergency function, network consequence, heritage value and feasibility. States, through ULBs and PRIs, identify priority and lifeline structures, including Raj Bhavans, legislatures, courts, academic institutions, reservoirs, dams and buildings of five or more floors. Central ministries and State governments remain responsible for safe assets under their control, such as bridges, flyovers, ports and harbours. Hospitals, schools and emergency facilities deserve consequence-based priority. Public registers should distinguish structures identified, assessed, designed, funded, retrofitted and independently verified; otherwise an inventory may be misreported as completed risk reduction.

**Adjacent rubric:** Priority criteria 5 + responsibility 4 + named structures 3 + accountability 3 = **15 marks**
