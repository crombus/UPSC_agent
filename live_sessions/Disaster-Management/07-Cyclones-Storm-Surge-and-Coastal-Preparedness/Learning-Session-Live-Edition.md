### 🖼️ Cyclone Risk as a Compound Coastal Hazard — visual
```text
CYCLONE SYSTEM
wind → roof/tree/power failure
rain → urban/river/landslide flooding
surge + tide → coastal inundation/salinity
waves → erosion/port damage
            ↓
       CASCADING SERVICE LOSS
```

A tropical cyclone is a rotating low-pressure system, but its disaster footprint is wider than maximum wind. The eyewall carries the most destructive winds while the eye is relatively calm. Intense rain can produce urban, river and slope failures far inland; storm surge and waves inundate and erode the coast; power, telecom, transport, health and water failures cascade.

Risk depends on track, intensity, size and speed as well as bathymetry, tide, housing, land use, livelihoods and response capacity. An intensity category is therefore not a complete impact forecast. The disaster-management task is to translate a multi-hazard forecast into different protective actions before conditions close routes or disable communication.

### Multi-hazard answer spine

Name **hazard component → exposed system → failure pathway → protective action → residual uncertainty**. Repeat for wind, rain and surge instead of writing a wind-only narrative.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Cyclone disaster risk combines wind, rain, surge, waves and flooding.
2. The eyewall, not the calm eye, carries the strongest winds.
3. Rain impacts may continue far inland after landfall.
4. Surge and waves create inundation, erosion and salinity.
5. Track, size and speed matter alongside intensity.
6. Bathymetry, tide and coastal shape influence consequences.
7. Power, telecom, roads and health services create cascades.
8. Impact-based action is more useful than a category alone.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is a wind-only cyclone plan structurally incomplete?

**Model answer:** A wind-only plan overlooks distinct exposure and action chains. Destructive winds damage roofs, trees and power; intense rain can trigger urban, river and slope flooding inland; storm surge, tide and waves inundate low coasts, erode shorelines and cause salinity; service failures then disrupt water, health, telecom and transport. These hazards have different maps, cut-off times and protective actions. Preparedness should combine impact forecasts, drainage and flood planning, evacuation from surge zones, resilient shelters and lifelines, inland alerts and backup communication. Intensity remains important, but cannot substitute for a compound-risk assessment.

**Adjacent rubric:** Compound hazards 4 + distinct consequences 3 + actions 3 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Maximum wind category alone determines the cyclone disaster footprint.

**Transition:** The compound footprint establishes why Lesson 2 retains only the meteorology needed to understand changing track, intensity and lead time.

### 🖼️ Cyclogenesis, Track and Intensity Change — visual
```text
WARM SST + INSTABILITY + MID-LEVEL HUMIDITY
+ CORIOLIS + LOW-LEVEL DISTURBANCE + LOW SHEAR
                    ↓
       organised warm-core circulation
steering flow → track | ocean/shear/internal cycles → intensity
```

Six conditions support tropical cyclogenesis: sufficiently warm sea surface, atmospheric instability, lower-to-middle tropospheric humidity, adequate Coriolis force, a pre-existing disturbance and low vertical wind shear. Warm water alone is insufficient, and organised rotation is difficult very near the equator.

Large-scale steering flow influences track, while ocean heat, atmospheric moisture and shear affect intensity. Internal structure and eyewall changes can produce non-linear intensity change. These processes matter in disaster management because forecasts update: uncertainty narrows or shifts, evacuation windows change and sector actions may need acceleration.

A forecast cone represents probable track uncertainty, not the physical size of the cyclone. Precise meteorological derivation belongs to Geography; here the science explains adaptive decisions.

### Forecast-update logic

At every update ask: **Did track, intensity, size, forward speed or uncertainty change? Which route, district, fishing zone or service trigger must now change?**

### REVISION NOTES

1. Cyclogenesis requires six interacting conditions.
2. Warm SST is necessary but not sufficient.
3. Adequate Coriolis force supports organised rotation.
4. Low shear helps align circulation and convection.
5. Steering winds largely guide track.
6. Ocean and atmosphere influence intensity change.
7. The forecast cone depicts track uncertainty, not storm width.
8. Preparedness must adapt as forecast evidence changes.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Explain why favourable sea temperature does not guarantee cyclone formation or intensification.

**Model answer:** Warm water supplies heat and moisture, but cyclogenesis also requires atmospheric instability, sufficient lower-to-middle-level humidity, adequate Coriolis force, a pre-existing low-level disturbance and low vertical wind shear. Dry-air intrusion or strong shear can disrupt convection and separate it from the circulation; weak Coriolis near the equator inhibits organised rotation. Even after formation, steering flow, ocean heat, shear and internal structural changes influence track and intensity. Disaster managers should therefore use updated multi-parameter forecasts rather than treat sea temperature as a deterministic warning.

**Adjacent rubric:** Six-condition logic 5 + disruption mechanisms 3 + management relevance 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Warm sea surface temperature by itself guarantees cyclone development.

**Transition:** Formation conditions explain the system; Lesson 3 converts observed maximum sustained wind into IMD classes without confusing class with total impact.

### 🖼️ IMD Intensity Classification and Naming — visual
```text
DEPRESSION 17–27 kt → DEEP DEPRESSION 28–33
→ CYCLONIC STORM 34–47 [NAMING BEGINS]
→ SEVERE 48–63 → VERY SEVERE 64–89
→ EXTREMELY SEVERE 90–119 → SUPER ≥120 kt
```

IMD classifies North Indian Ocean disturbances by maximum sustained wind. Depression is 17–27 kt (31–49 km/h), Deep Depression 28–33 kt (50–61), Cyclonic Storm 34–47 kt (62–88), Severe Cyclonic Storm 48–63 kt (89–117), Very Severe Cyclonic Storm 64–89 kt (118–166), Extremely Severe Cyclonic Storm 90–119 kt (167–221) and Super Cyclonic Storm at least 120 kt (at least 222 km/h).

A system receives a name only from Cyclonic Storm stage. Classification supports standard communication and wind preparedness, but unnamed depressions can still produce destructive rainfall and floods. Impact also depends on size, duration, track, surge, tide and exposure. The current RSMC/IMD record, not remembered media terminology, should govern precise class claims.

### Classification-to-impact matrix

| Classification tells | Classification does not tell alone |
|---|---|
| sustained-wind band | exact rainfall or surge |
| naming eligibility | local building performance |
| standard bulletin term | duration, size or service cascade |

### REVISION NOTES

1. IMD classification uses maximum sustained wind.
2. Depression spans 17–27 kt and Deep Depression 28–33 kt.
3. Cyclonic Storm begins at 34 kt and receives a name.
4. Severe Cyclonic Storm spans 48–63 kt.
5. Very Severe spans 64–89 kt.
6. Extremely Severe spans 90–119 kt.
7. Super Cyclonic Storm begins at 120 kt.
8. Unnamed systems may still produce extreme rain and floods.
9. Impact forecasts must supplement intensity class.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why can an unnamed depression require major disaster preparedness?

**Model answer:** A depression lies below the Cyclonic Storm naming threshold, but naming reflects wind classification rather than total impact. A slow-moving or moisture-rich depression can produce prolonged extreme rain, urban and river flooding, landslides, road isolation and power or health-service disruption. Saturated catchments and vulnerable settlements may amplify loss even without severe cyclone-force winds. Authorities should therefore issue rainfall and impact-based warnings, pre-position rescue and health capacity, protect routes and utilities and communicate inland consequences. Preparedness should follow expected impacts and exposure, not the presence of a storm name.

**Adjacent rubric:** Classification distinction 3 + non-wind impacts 4 + actions 3 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Only named cyclonic storms can produce major disaster loss.

**Transition:** Wind classification now gives one hazard dimension; Lesson 4 separates meteorological surge from tide and tsunami.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Storm Surge, Storm Tide and Coastal Inundation — visual
```text
CYCLONE WIND + LOW PRESSURE → STORM SURGE
SURGE + ASTRONOMICAL TIDE → STORM TIDE
modified by bathymetry | coast shape | approach | waves
                         ↓
          depth + velocity + salinity + erosion
```

Storm surge is an abnormal rise of sea level above the predicted astronomical tide, driven chiefly by cyclone winds and pressure. Storm tide is the observed water level produced when surge combines with astronomical tide. Shallow bathymetry, funnel-shaped coasts, approach angle, local geometry and waves influence inundation.

Surge is meteorological; high tide is astronomical; tsunami is generated chiefly by sudden water displacement from seismic or related processes and remains Topic 06 ownership. The distinction matters because monitoring, lead time and evacuation zones differ.

Risk maps need expected depth and extent, route cut-off, shelter location, salinity and erosion consequences. Uncertainty should trigger conservative action where evacuation time is long; it should not be hidden behind one deterministic water level.

### Coastal-water distinction table

| Process | Driver | Planning implication |
|---|---|---|
| surge | cyclone wind/pressure | cyclone forecast and evacuation |
| tide | astronomical cycle | timing interaction |
| tsunami | sudden displacement | separate warning architecture |

### REVISION NOTES

1. Storm surge is rise above predicted astronomical tide.
2. Storm tide combines surge and astronomical tide.
3. Onshore wind is a major surge driver.
4. Low pressure contributes to sea-level rise.
5. Bathymetry and funnel-shaped coasts influence amplification.
6. Approach and waves modify local consequences.
7. Surge and tsunami have different causes and ownership.
8. Maps should show depth, extent and route cut-off.
9. Uncertainty still supports precautionary evacuation.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Distinguish storm surge, storm tide and tsunami and show why the distinction matters operationally.

**Model answer:** Storm surge is the meteorologically generated abnormal rise above predicted astronomical tide, caused chiefly by cyclone wind and pressure and shaped by bathymetry and coastal geometry. Storm tide is the total water level when surge combines with the astronomical tide. A tsunami arises mainly from sudden displacement of seawater by seismic or related events and uses a separate monitoring and warning system. Operationally, cyclone track, intensity and surge forecasts guide coastal evacuation, while tide timing modifies expected levels and route closure. Tsunami zones and alerts cannot be inferred from cyclone bulletins. Clear terminology prevents wrong triggers, maps and public messages.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definitions 6 + physical modifiers 3 + operational distinction 4 + ownership 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Every abnormal coastal inundation is a tsunami, while surge and storm tide are synonyms.

**Transition:** With the water-level mechanisms separated, Lesson 5 maps where and when India’s coastal exposure becomes consequential.

India faces North Indian Ocean cyclones in pre- and post-monsoon periods, with strong historical east-coast exposure and important October–November risk. The owner’s document-period estimates show a marked Bay of Bengal–Arabian Sea asymmetry; they should inform, not freeze, current risk assessment. The official 2025 GIS/HWL remeasurement places India’s coastline at **11,098.81 km**. This updates the planning baseline but does not itself alter every regulatory boundary. Consequence varies across deltas, islands, estuaries, urban coasts and low-lying rural areas.

Investment should combine probability, population and livelihood exposure, evacuation difficulty, asset concentration and lead time. Category-based prioritisation must retain minimum capability in lower-frequency areas.

**Question:** (10 marks) Why should lower historical Arabian Sea frequency not justify weak west-coast preparedness?

**Model answer:** Frequency is only one component of risk. A lower-frequency Arabian Sea event can affect dense coastal cities, ports, tourism, fisheries and complex lifelines, producing high consequence and difficult evacuation. Historical climatology can also change and should be updated through current IMD evidence. West-coast States therefore need baseline warning dissemination, shelters or safe buildings, fishing and port protocols, evacuation routes, utility continuity and exercises. Differentiated investment may place more assets in higher-frequency areas, but it should not treat Category II or west-coast locations as risk-free.

### 🖼️ IMD Warning Institutions and Four-Stage Bulletin Process — visual
```text
OBSERVATION/MODEL → RSMC NEW DELHI / IMD
ACWCs: Kolkata | Chennai | Mumbai
CWCs: Bhubaneswar | Visakhapatnam | Ahmedabad
72 h WATCH → 48 h ALERT → 24 h WARNING → 12 h POST-LANDFALL OUTLOOK
```

IMD’s cyclone service uses RSMC New Delhi and a warning network of Area Cyclone Warning Centres at Kolkata, Chennai and Mumbai and Cyclone Warning Centres at Bhubaneswar, Visakhapatnam and Ahmedabad. Satellite, radar, ocean and model evidence supports progressively refined bulletins.

The four lead-time stages are **Pre-Cyclone Watch at least 72 hours ahead, Cyclone Alert at least 48 hours, Cyclone Warning at least 24 hours and Post-Landfall Outlook at least 12 hours before landfall**. They describe forecast evolution and decision time, not colour-coded action levels.

District and sector plans should assign actions to each stage: readiness and roster review; resource pre-positioning and evacuation preparation; execution of closures and evacuation; and inland readiness after coastal crossing. Updates can shift track, timing or impact, so plans must adapt rather than wait for the final stage.

### Bulletin-to-decision clock

| Stage | Operational emphasis |
|---|---|
| Watch | plans, rosters, inventories, public awareness |
| Alert | pre-positioning, transport, shelter activation |
| Warning | evacuation, closures, utility/sector protection |
| Post-Landfall | inland rain/wind and restoration readiness |

### REVISION NOTES

1. RSMC New Delhi anchors the regional cyclone service.
2. ACWCs are Kolkata, Chennai and Mumbai.
3. CWCs are Bhubaneswar, Visakhapatnam and Ahmedabad.
4. Pre-Cyclone Watch begins at least 72 hours ahead.
5. Cyclone Alert begins at least 48 hours ahead.
6. Cyclone Warning begins at least 24 hours ahead.
7. Post-Landfall Outlook begins at least 12 hours before landfall.
8. Bulletin stages communicate lead time, not colour.
9. Plans should pre-assign actions and adapt to updates.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) How should district action evolve across IMD’s four-stage cyclone bulletin process?

**Model answer:** At Pre-Cyclone Watch, districts review plans, rosters, communication, shelters, transport and stocks and begin public risk information. At Cyclone Alert, they pre-position responders and supplies, activate shelters, contact vulnerable settlements and prepare fishing, port, school and utility actions. At Cyclone Warning, location-specific evacuation, route closure, service protection and incident command should be executed before weather closes access. The Post-Landfall Outlook prepares coastal and inland districts for continuing wind, rain, flooding and restoration needs. Every stage should use current track and impact information; it should not wait for certainty or be confused with Green–Red colour codes.

**Adjacent rubric:** Four stages 6 + staged actions 6 + update/distinction 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** The four lead-time stages are the same system as the four warning colours.

**Transition:** Lead-time stages organise when decisions mature; Lesson 7 explains the separate colour vocabulary that communicates action urgency.

### 🖼️ Colour Codes and Warning-to-Action Conversion — visual
```text
GREEN: no warning → routine monitoring
YELLOW: be updated → review readiness
ORANGE: be prepared → activate preparations
RED: take action → execute protective decisions
FORECAST COLOUR ≠ guarantee | COLOUR ≠ wind class | COLOUR ≠ bulletin stage
```

IMD colour codes are an action communication layer. Green means no warning; Yellow means be updated; Orange means be prepared for very bad weather and disruption; Red means take action because severe weather and serious risk are expected. A Red warning is evidence-based and precautionary, not a guarantee that the maximum forecast impact will occur.

Colour should never stand alone. A useful message specifies area, time, wind/rain/surge impact, route or sector action and next update. Households, fishers, ports, schools, hospitals and utilities require different triggers. Trust is preserved through clear updates when the forecast changes and through review of the evidence available when the warning was issued.

The exact 2022 GS-I question is retained answer-neutral in the final PYQ section; this lesson teaches the underlying distinction without reproducing a solved PYQ.

### Action-message builder

Build every warning as **colour + hazard + place + time + expected impact + instruction + update**. Test whether a fisher, shelter manager and power utility can act differently from the same bulletin.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Green means no warning and routine monitoring.
2. Yellow means be updated and review readiness.
3. Orange means be prepared for serious disruption.
4. Red means take protective action.
5. Colour is not a wind-intensity class.
6. Colour is not a 72/48/24/12-hour stage.
7. Red is a forecast-based action signal, not a guarantee.
8. Messages require hazard, place, time, impact and instruction.
9. Updates and review support trust.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why may a Red cyclone warning be correct even when the worst forecast impact does not occur?

**Model answer:** Warnings are decisions under uncertainty. Red can be justified when forecast probability, potential consequence, exposure and available lead time require action, even if the track or intensity later changes. The correct evaluation asks whether evidence at issuance supported the warning, whether uncertainty and updates were communicated and whether precaution reduced exposure—not whether the maximum scenario occurred. Waiting for certainty can close evacuation routes and increase loss. Authorities should update or downgrade transparently and review false and missed alarms, while the public should understand Red as “take action,” not as a guarantee.

**Adjacent rubric:** Uncertainty logic 3 + decision justification 3 + evaluation/update 3 + verdict 1 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** A Red warning is valid only if the maximum predicted damage occurs.

**Transition:** Colour becomes protection only through feasible last-mile action, which Lesson 8 examines through evacuation, shelter and community participation.

```text
WARNING RECEIVED → understood/trusted → transport available

barriers: disability | livestock | livelihood | migration | language | gender safety
```

Evacuation succeeds only when people receive, understand and trust the warning and can act before routes close. Transport, disability and care support, livestock and boat security, documents, medicines, language, migration status and fear of theft all influence compliance.

A multipurpose cyclone shelter is a system, not a building. It requires all-weather access, water, sanitation, backup power, communication, accessibility, privacy, protection, trained management, supplies and accountability. Local committees, volunteers and drills build usage habits and identify route or capacity failures.

The canonical implementation gaps include failure to respond adequately to warnings because of planning and coordination weakness, limited public understanding of risk and inadequate grassroots participation. These are warning-to-action failures, not simply forecast failures. Asset counts should therefore be paired with usability and evacuation-completion evidence.

3. Transport and route cut-off determine feasibility.

6. Accessibility and privacy are operational requirements.
7. Local committees and drills build participation.

9. Usability and completion matter more than shelter counts.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Why can extensive cyclone-shelter construction coexist with incomplete evacuation readiness?

**Model answer:** Shelter construction supplies only one input. Readiness also requires warning receipt and trust, route and transport availability, accessible shelter design, water, sanitation, power, communication, privacy, supplies and trained management. Fishers, livestock owners, migrants, older persons and persons with disabilities may face distinct barriers or fear livelihood loss and theft. Weak national-local coordination and limited grassroots participation can prevent warnings from becoming household action. Authorities should maintain shelters and roads, map assisted-evacuation needs, protect livelihoods, run inclusive drills and audit evacuation completion and shelter use by group. A nominal capacity figure therefore cannot certify last-mile readiness.

**Adjacent rubric:** Asset/readiness distinction 3 + barriers 4 + functional shelter 4 + reforms/audit 4 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Nominal shelter capacity proves that every exposed household can evacuate safely.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

- Six cyclogenesis conditions: warm sea, instability, humidity, Coriolis, disturbance and low shear.
- Cyclone risk combines wind, rain, storm surge, flooding and lifeline cascades.
- IMD names systems from Cyclonic Storm stage; depressions can still cause major flooding.
- Storm surge is abnormal rise above predicted astronomical tide; bathymetry, coast shape, approach and tide matter.
- India faces Bay–Arabian asymmetry, but west-coast residual risk remains.
- IMD stages: Pre-Cyclone Watch 72h; Alert 48h; Warning 24h; Post-Landfall Outlook 12h.
- Colours: Green no warning; Yellow update; Orange prepare; Red act.
- ACWCs: Kolkata, Chennai, Mumbai; CWCs: Bhubaneswar, Visakhapatnam, Ahmedabad.
- Warning must trigger sector-specific decisions and inclusive evacuation.
- Shelters need access, utilities, sanitation, accessibility, management and drills.
- Resilient housing/lifelines address wind and water jointly.
- Ecosystem buffers complement engineered protection and land-use control.
- NCRMP Category I comprised Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Odisha, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal; Category II comprised eight lower-vulnerability coastal States/UTs.
- NDMA/MHA implemented NCRMP with participating States and NIDM, supported partly by World Bank finance.
- NCRMP combined warning dissemination, shelters, evacuation roads/bridges, saline embankments, underground cabling, technical assistance and capacity building.
- PIB PRID 2247656 (1 April 2026) verifies Phase I completion in December 2018 and Phase II in March 2023; no NCRMP-III is established here.
- The original 13-jurisdiction category architecture and eight-State phased implementation are not identical.
- Owner-identified gaps include warning-response failure, weak grassroots participation, operations-centre limitations and fragmented networks.
- Project closure does not prove present O&M funding, functionality or warning-to-action readiness.
- Fisheries, ports, agriculture and urban coasts require different impact messages.
- Recovery restores livelihoods and rebuilds safer; asset replacement alone is not BBB.
- Advanced evaluation tests rapid intensification, compound scenarios, maintenance and distribution.
