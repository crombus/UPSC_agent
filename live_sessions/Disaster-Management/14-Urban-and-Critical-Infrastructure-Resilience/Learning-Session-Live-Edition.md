### 🖼️ City as an Interdependent Risk System — visual
```text
HAZARD → ASSET FAILURE → SERVICE LOSS → SOCIAL/ECONOMIC IMPACT
                   ↘ dependency cascade ↙
POWER → water pumps → hospitals → telecom → transport control

CITY RISK = hazard × exposure × vulnerability × interdependence
RESILIENCE = resist + absorb + adapt + recover + learn
```

A city is not a collection of independent buildings. Power, water, sanitation, transport, health, communications, solid waste and digital services exchange energy, information, staff and access. Failure in one network may disable several others even when their assets remain physically intact.

Critical infrastructure is an asset, network or service whose disruption creates disproportionate consequences for life, governance, economy or other systems. Criticality is contextual: a small bridge may be more critical than a large flyover if it is the only hospital route. Resilience combines robust design with service continuity, adaptive operation, rapid repair and learning.

Hazard resistance alone is insufficient. A flood-safe data centre without telecom diversity, fuel or staff access may fail functionally. Conversely, a damaged network can sustain essential service through alternate routes, local storage or manual operation.

### Decision/application lens

Begin with the **service**, not the asset: who depends on it, what inputs it needs, what substitutes exist, and how long disruption is tolerable.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Cities are systems of interdependent services.
2. Physical survival does not guarantee functional continuity.
3. Criticality depends on consequence and substitutes.
4. Small assets may have high network criticality.
5. Power failure can cascade into water and health services.
6. Resilience includes resistance, absorption and recovery.
7. Operational adaptation complements engineering strength.
8. Manual fallback can protect essential function.
9. Service restoration time is a core metric.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is asset-by-asset hardening an incomplete urban-resilience strategy?

**Model score/word ceiling:** 10 marks | 77 words | maximum 150 words

**Model answer:** Urban services depend on one another. A hardened hospital may still fail when power, water, oxygen supply, telecom or staff transport stops; a protected pump may be useless without electricity and access. Asset-by-asset projects can also overlook common corridors and single points of failure. Cities need service-level criticality mapping, dependency analysis, alternate routes, backup supplies, interoperable command and prioritised restoration. Structural strength remains necessary, but resilience is demonstrated by continued or rapidly restored service under cascading disruption.

**Adjacent rubric:** Interdependence problem 4 + examples 2 + system measures 3 + conclusion 1 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** If each major facility meets its structural code, the city’s essential services are necessarily resilient.

**Transition:** System thinking identifies dependencies; Lesson 2 shows how land use and unequal building conditions place people and lifelines in harm’s way.

### 🖼️ Risk-Sensitive Land Use, Buildings and Informal Settlements — visual
```text
RISK-INFORMED URBAN FORM
hazard map + drainage/slope/fault/heat + building stock + people/services
                              ↓
avoid high-consequence siting → enforce safe design → retrofit priority stock

INFORMALITY LOOP
unsafe land + weak tenure + service deficit → high exposure
forced eviction without option → displacement risk, not resilience
```

Risk-sensitive land use prevents new exposure before engineering becomes expensive. Development plans should integrate multi-hazard evidence, flood and drainage paths, slope and seismic conditions, heat, evacuation access and lifeline corridors. Building permission, occupancy and change-of-use control need transparent safety requirements and inspection.

Codes protect only when design, materials, workmanship, occupancy and maintenance comply. Existing buildings require risk-based screening and retrofit, prioritising schools, hospitals, emergency facilities, dense housing and structures whose failure blocks networks.

Informal settlements often occupy floodplains, slopes, industrial edges or heat-exposed areas because safe serviced land is unaffordable. Residents may depend on informal water, power and transport with little redundancy. Resilience cannot mean eviction without secure, accessible alternatives. In-situ upgrading, tenure-sensitive services, negotiated relocation where risk is intolerable and livelihood access are part of risk reduction.

### Decision/application lens

Apply an **avoid–upgrade–retrofit–relocate** hierarchy, stating risk evidence, resident choice, service access, livelihood impact and grievance route.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Land-use decisions create long-lived exposure.
2. Hazard evidence should inform development plans.
3. Building codes require inspection and occupancy control.
4. Existing stock often dominates urban risk.
5. Critical facilities deserve priority screening.
6. Informal settlements face both hazard and service deficits.
7. Tenure insecurity can suppress reporting and upgrading.
8. Eviction without alternatives transfers vulnerability.
9. Relocation must preserve access to livelihoods and services.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) A city identifies a high-risk informal settlement beside a drain. Frame a just resilience response.

**Model score/word ceiling:** 15 marks | 89 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** The city should jointly map flood depth, velocity, drainage blockage, building condition, tenure, vulnerable residents, livelihood and service dependence. Immediate action may include warning, safe routes, drain maintenance, electrical safety and temporary shelter. Where risk can be reduced, in-situ upgrading should improve structures, water, sanitation, access and open space without using safety as a pretext for dispossession. Where residual risk is intolerable, relocation requires consultation, secure tenure, affordable services, transport, livelihood access, compensation and grievance redress. New encroachment should be prevented through safe serviced land and transparent land-use control.

**Adjacent rubric:** Risk diagnosis 4 + immediate/in-situ action 4 + relocation safeguards 5 + prevention conclusion 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Removing residents from visible hazard space automatically reduces their overall disaster risk.

**Transition:** Land-use action reduces exposure; Lesson 3 identifies which network nodes deserve highest protection and fastest restoration.

### 🖼️ Criticality Mapping and Cascading Failure — visual
```text
ASSET INVENTORY → SERVICE GRAPH → DEPENDENCY GRAPH → CONSEQUENCE
node: substation / pump / bridge / exchange / hospital
edge: power | water | data | access | staff | supplier

CRITICALITY SCORE considers:
people served + dependent systems + no substitute + restoration time + equity
```

Criticality mapping identifies assets and functions whose loss causes disproportionate cascading effects. It combines asset condition and hazard exposure with service area, dependencies, substitutes, restoration time and affected populations. It is not a secrecy-heavy list of “important buildings.”

Dependency can be physical, cyber, geographic or organisational. Power and fibre may share one tunnel; hospitals may rely on one oxygen supplier; water plants may need one access road. Common-cause failures defeat nominal backups. Geographic information, utility records, operator workshops and exercises reveal these links.

Maps should support investment and response while protecting sensitive security and personal data. Public versions can disclose service risk and preparedness without publishing exploitable details. Priority should include high-consequence neighbourhoods, not only economic value.

### Decision/application lens

Draw a dependency graph, remove one node, and trace **service lost → population affected → alternate capacity → maximum tolerable downtime → restoration owner**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Criticality is based on consequence, not asset size.
2. Dependencies may be physical, cyber or organisational.
3. Common corridors create common-cause failure.
4. Nominal backup may share the same vulnerability.
5. Restoration time affects criticality.
6. Equity belongs in prioritisation.
7. Operator knowledge complements GIS data.
8. Sensitive details require controlled access.
9. Criticality maps should guide investment and response.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) How should a city prioritise two substations when both have similar hazard exposure?

**Model score/word ceiling:** 15 marks | 89 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** The city should compare the services and populations each substation supports, dependent water pumps, hospitals, telecom and transport controls, available alternate feeds, load-transfer capacity and restoration time. A substation serving a smaller area may rank higher if it powers the only trauma centre or sewage plant. Condition and common-cause exposure of feeders, access and fuel or control systems also matter. Prioritisation should examine vulnerable populations and maximum tolerable downtime, then select hardening, network reconfiguration, local backup and restoration staging. The decision should be documented and retested through outage exercises.

**Adjacent rubric:** Service/dependency analysis 5 + substitutes/restoration 4 + equity/common-cause 3 + actions/testing 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Criticality can be ranked accurately from replacement cost alone.

**Transition:** Priority nodes need reliable performance over decades; Lesson 4 links standards and maintenance to lifecycle risk.

### 🖼️ Standards, Asset Condition and Lifecycle Maintenance — visual
```text
PLAN → DESIGN → BUILD → COMMISSION → OPERATE → MAINTAIN → RETROFIT/RETIRE
  risk       code      QA        test       inspect     fund          update

RESILIENCE GAP
nominal design capacity − degraded actual capacity
= corrosion + settlement + blocked drains + obsolete controls + misuse
```

Resilient standards translate hazard evidence and acceptable performance into design, construction and operation. Performance objectives should distinguish life safety, limited damage and continued operation for critical facilities. Codes need local adoption, competent design, quality assurance, commissioning and enforcement.

Infrastructure ages. Corrosion, leakage, settlement, vegetation, silt, blocked culverts, battery decay, software obsolescence and unauthorised modification reduce actual capacity. Deferred maintenance can erase the benefit of a resilient design. Asset registers should record condition, inspection, defects, work orders and residual life.

Lifecycle decisions compare preventive maintenance, retrofit, replacement and managed retirement. Climate and urban growth can change design assumptions; periodic review should update loads and dependencies. Maintenance finance is resilience investment, not routine expenditure outside disaster management.

### Decision/application lens

Ask for each asset: **performance objective, present condition, last functional test, deferred defect, responsible maintainer, outage window and renewal decision**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Standards need adoption and enforcement.
2. Critical assets may require continued-operation objectives.
3. Construction quality affects code performance.
4. Commissioning tests the delivered system.
5. Actual capacity degrades through age and misuse.
6. Deferred maintenance creates disaster risk.
7. Asset registers should include condition and defects.
8. Hazard assumptions require periodic review.
9. Retirement can be safer than indefinite patching.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why should maintenance be treated as a core disaster-resilience intervention?

**Model score/word ceiling:** 10 marks | 83 words | maximum 150 words

**Model answer:** Maintenance preserves the capacity assumed in risk models and design. Blocked drains, corroded supports, failed batteries, leaking pipes, obsolete software or untested generators can turn a compliant asset into a single point of failure. Condition inspection, functional testing, defect closure, spares and trained operators reduce failure probability and shorten restoration. Lifecycle records also reveal when retrofit or replacement is safer than repeated repair. Because many disasters exploit degraded everyday systems, recurring maintenance finance often protects continuity more reliably than visible post-disaster reconstruction alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Degradation mechanism 3 + maintenance functions 3 + lifecycle decision 2 + resilience conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Maintenance affects service quality but not disaster performance if the original design met code.

**Transition:** Maintained strength still cannot prevent every outage; Lesson 5 designs redundancy and safe failure around residual risk.

### 🖼️ Redundancy, Diversity, Modularity and Safe Failure — visual
```text
RELIABILITY PORTFOLIO
redundancy: second component/route
spare capacity: operate below maximum
 diversity: different technology/supplier/path
modularity: isolate failed section
manual fallback: operate without digital control
safe failure: degraded service, not catastrophic collapse
```

Redundancy provides alternate capacity, but two units in one flood basement or two fibre links in one trench are not independent. Diversity reduces common-mode failure by using different locations, technologies, suppliers or energy sources. Modularity isolates damage so the rest of the network can operate.

Spare capacity absorbs demand and repair shocks; excessive efficiency that removes every buffer may create brittleness. Manual fallback protects essential functions during cyber or power failure, but staff must practise it. Safe-failure design lets service degrade predictably rather than collapse without warning.

These measures cost money and can create idle assets. Criticality and downtime analysis should decide where redundancy is justified. Shared mutual aid or mobile assets may be better than duplicate permanent capacity for lower-frequency needs.

### Decision/application lens

Test independence by asking: **what single flood, fire, cyber event, corridor loss, supplier failure or staff shortage can disable both primary and backup?**

### REVISION NOTES

1. Redundancy requires genuine independence.
2. Co-located backups can share one hazard.
3. Diversity reduces common-mode failure.
4. Modularity limits propagation.
5. Spare capacity absorbs surge and repair.
6. Manual fallback requires trained staff.
7. Safe failure preserves minimum function.
8. Not every asset needs full duplication.
9. Mobile and mutual-aid capacity can provide alternatives.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) A hospital has two generators in the same basement. Is its power system redundant?

**Model score/word ceiling:** 10 marks | 82 words | maximum 150 words

**Model answer:** It has duplicate equipment but not resilient redundancy if one flood, fire or access failure can disable both generators, fuel pumps and switchgear. The hospital should assess elevation and separation, independent fuel and control paths, transfer-switch testing, load priority and runtime. Options include relocating one source, protected distribution, rooftop or local renewable-storage support, a mobile connection point and mutual aid. Clinical services should identify minimum loads and manual fallback. Redundancy is demonstrated by independent, tested delivery of essential power, not generator count.

**Adjacent rubric:** Common-mode diagnosis 3 + independence measures 3 + critical-load/fallback 2 + conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Two units always constitute redundancy regardless of location, controls or fuel path.

**Transition:** Reliability principles now apply to city lifelines; Lesson 6 integrates power, water, sanitation and digital dependencies.

### 🖼️ Power, Water, Sanitation and Digital Lifelines — visual
```text
POWER ↔ DIGITAL CONTROL/TELECOM
  ↓               ↓
water intake → treatment → pumping → storage → distribution
  ↓
sewer pumping/treatment → public health

PROTECT: nodes + corridors + control systems + staff + suppliers
RESTORE: black-start/feeder priority + water storage + manual operation
```

Power enables pumping, treatment, telecom, traffic control, hospitals and digital payment. Water and sanitation protect health and firefighting; digital systems monitor and coordinate all three. Their interdependence creates fast cascades.

The Central Electricity Authority’s Disaster Management Plan 2022 provides a sector anchor for power preparedness. Utilities should identify critical feeders, substations, control centres, fuel and spares; maintain black-start or restoration procedures and coordinate with districts. Water utilities need source diversity, elevated or distributed storage, protected pumps, emergency chlorination, tanker and repair plans. Sanitation requires safe bypass or containment to prevent contamination.

Cyber resilience is inseparable from physical resilience. Segmentation, backups, access control, offline procedures and tested recovery protect operational technology. Publishing outage maps must balance public information and security.

### Decision/application lens

Run a 48-hour power-loss tabletop: track **stored water, sewage overflow, telecom battery, hospital load, traffic control, fuel delivery and restoration priority**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Power is an enabling lifeline for other services.
2. Water storage can buffer pump outages.
3. Sewage failure creates secondary health risk.
4. Critical feeders require restoration priority.
5. CEA has a power-sector DMP 2022.
6. Control centres and spares need protection.
7. Cyber and physical failures can interact.
8. Offline procedures need practice.
9. Public outage information should protect sensitive details.

### Concept check

**Question:** (20 marks) Develop a continuity plan for simultaneous power and water disruption in a large city.

**Model score/word ceiling:** 20 marks | 103 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** The city should map critical substations, feeders, water sources, pumps, reservoirs, treatment plants, telecom and hospital dependencies and identify common corridors. Power utilities need protected control, restoration sequences, mobile substations or generators, spares, fuel and priority feeders. Water utilities should maximise stored supply, isolate damaged zones, deploy emergency pumping, tankers and chlorination and protect fire-service demand. Sewage overflow and contamination require monitoring and public advice. Hospitals, shelters and vulnerable neighbourhoods need minimum allocations. Redundant communications, manual operation and a unified restoration cell should publish safe service updates. Exercises must test cyber loss, blocked roads and fuel delay, with restoration time and equity reviewed.

**Adjacent rubric:** Dependency mapping 4 + power continuity 4 + water/sanitation continuity 5 + public/equity coordination 4 + testing/metrics 3 = **20 marks**

### 🖼️ Resilient Hospitals and Schools — visual
```text
THREE SAFETIES
STRUCTURAL: building/frame/foundation
NON-STRUCTURAL: equipment, pipes, shelves, ceilings, exits
FUNCTIONAL: staff, supplies, utilities, plans, communications

HOSPITAL: remain operational
SCHOOL: protect children + reunification + shelter role only if safe
```

Hospital safety requires structural integrity, secured equipment and services, and functional continuity. A hospital may stand but fail because oxygen, water, power, staff access or records are lost. NDMA’s Hospital Safety guidance supports multi-dimensional assessment, emergency plans and continuity.

Schools require safe buildings, non-structural anchoring, exits, drills, inclusive evacuation, communication and family reunification. NDMA’s School Safety Policy 2016 assigns roles across education and disaster-management institutions. A school designated as a shelter must be assessed and supplied; occupation should not unnecessarily prolong education disruption.

Retrofitting priority should reflect occupancy, vulnerability, service role and alternatives. Drills must test realistic conditions without traumatising children or disrupting clinical care. Accessibility includes ramps, visual/audible alerts, medication and assisted evacuation.

### Decision/application lens

Audit one facility through **safe structure → secured contents → lifeline autonomy → trained people → inclusive evacuation → continuity/reunification → recovery**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Structural safety is only one layer.
2. Non-structural equipment can injure or disable service.
3. Functional safety includes staff and utilities.
4. Hospitals need essential-load and supply continuity.
5. Schools require family reunification planning.
6. Shelter use needs prior safety and logistics assessment.
7. Children and patients need inclusive evacuation.
8. Drills should be realistic and safe.
9. Retrofit priority depends on consequence and alternatives.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Why can a structurally intact hospital still fail during a disaster?

**Model score/word ceiling:** 15 marks | 84 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** The building may survive while non-structural and functional systems fail. Unanchored equipment, broken oxygen or water lines, ceiling damage and blocked exits can stop care. External power, telecom, roads, suppliers and staff transport may be unavailable; a flooded generator or inaccessible pharmacy can create a complete service outage. Hospital resilience therefore requires secured equipment, protected utilities, minimum-load backup, stock and supplier alternatives, staffing and referral plans, accessible communication, drills and rapid damage assessment. Performance is continued safe care, not survival of the frame alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Non-structural failures 4 + functional/dependency failures 5 + continuity measures 4 + performance conclusion 2 = **15 marks**

### 🖼️ Transport Nodes, Mobility and Crowd Safety — visual
```text
NETWORK: roads + rail + metro + bus + walking + emergency corridors
NODE: station/interchange/bridge/tunnel

CROWD FLOW
arrival rate → holding area → controlled entry → corridor/platform capacity
             → exit/egress → dispersal
DENSITY + COUNTERFLOW + BLOCKAGE + RUMOUR = rapid compression risk
```

Transport resilience maintains emergency access, evacuation, supply and workforce movement. Network analysis should identify single bridges, tunnels, interchanges, depots, control centres and fuel or power dependencies. Alternate routes must be usable by the vehicles and populations assigned to them.

Crowd disasters are flow-capacity failures: arrivals exceed holding, entry, corridor, platform, exit or dispersal capacity, amplified by counterflow, blocked paths, delay and poor information. NDMA’s May 2014 crowd-management guide supports advance planning by authorities and organisers. Capacity-linked access, holding areas, one-way circulation, clear information, medical posts and stop-entry authority are preventive controls.

Surveillance technology helps only if a trained controller can interpret density and act. Accessibility and family separation plans matter. Post-incident crowd measures at one operator do not prove a nationwide enforceable venue regime.

### Decision/application lens

Use a flow ledger: **arrival per minute, space capacity, service rate, exit rate, trigger, authorised action and safe dispersal area**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Transport networks enable response and recovery.
2. Single bridges or tunnels can be critical nodes.
3. Alternate routes need capacity and accessibility.
4. Crowd hazard emerges from flow and density.
5. Holding areas buffer arrival surges.
6. Counterflow increases compression risk.
7. Capacity-linked entry is preventive.
8. Technology requires decision authority.
9. A guide is not proof of universal enforcement.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Design a safe operating plan for a rail station expecting a sudden festival surge.

**Model score/word ceiling:** 15 marks | 92 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** The operator should forecast arrivals by time and mode, define safe capacities for forecourt, holding areas, entries, bridges, platforms and exits, and separate incoming and outgoing flows. Ticketing and access must slow before downstream space saturates. A controller needs live counts, CCTV or field reports, clear stop-entry authority and direct coordination with police, medical, fire and city transport. Messages should explain delay and alternatives to prevent rumour or rushing. Accessible routes, family reunification and emergency egress must remain open. Drills should test train delay, one blocked bridge, power loss and communication failure.

**Adjacent rubric:** Flow/capacity plan 5 + command/triggers 4 + communication/inclusion 3 + contingency testing 3 = **15 marks**

The Gazette of India records the Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025 as **Act No. 10 of 2025**, assented to and published on **29 March 2025**. MHA notification **S.O. 1648(E), dated 8 April 2025**, appointed **9 April 2025** as its commencement date. The Act inserted **section 41A**, enabling States to constitute Urban Disaster Management Authorities for State capitals and cities having a Municipal Corporation, except the National Capital Territory of Delhi and Union territory of Chandigarh. Section 41A makes the Municipal Commissioner ex officio Chairperson and the concerned District Collector ex officio Vice-Chairperson; the Urban Authority prepares the Urban Plan, which the State Authority approves, and coordinates its implementation. Enabling law does not prove every eligible city has a functioning authority—State notification, staff, finance and prescribed powers must be verified.

Three urban-flood instruments must not be conflated. NDMA’s **2010 Guidelines on Management of Urban Flooding recommend an Urban Flooding Cell within the Union urban-development ministry for national coordination**, while **ULBs manage locally** through land use, drainage, roads, solid waste and municipal services. The Cell is a guideline recommendation, not a self-executing or verified universally functioning body. UDMA is a later enabling statutory authority where a State constitutes it. UFRMP is a sanctioned mitigation programme, not either institution.

The MHA parliamentary reply of 11 August 2026 records the same cities and outlay and provides city-wise approved outlay, central assistance and release data; neither source explicitly states a uniform Centre–State ratio. Approval and allocation do not prove completed works or avoided flood loss.

Finance should distinguish routine maintenance, capital works, retrofit, contingency and recovery. **Budget reserve funds** retain earmarked fiscal capacity for shocks; **catastrophe bonds** transfer specified event risk to capital-market investors. They are named infrastructure-risk-financing instruments in the owner but detailed structuring belongs to Topic 16, and no claim is made that Indian ULBs widely use them. Lifecycle appraisal, social safeguards and outcome budgeting should measure service downtime and exposed people rather than sanctioned money alone.

1. Spending is an input, not an outcome.
2. Retrofit counts are outputs.
3. Exercises test capability.
4. Downtime is a service outcome.
5. A baseline and theory of change are necessary.
6. Counterfactuals strengthen attribution.
7. Distribution reveals unequal benefits.
8. Maladaptation must be tested.
9. Evaluation should change future decisions.

### Concept check

**Question:** (20 marks) Construct an evaluation framework for a city lifeline-resilience programme.

**Model score/word ceiling:** 20 marks | 109 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** Begin with the hazard, service and equity problem and a theory linking finance, standards, maintenance and coordination to continuity. Record baseline asset condition, dependency exposure, fallback endurance, downtime and neighbourhood service gaps. Track inputs and outputs, but test capability through exercises and audits. Measure event or scenario outcomes: minimum service maintained, restoration time, cascading failures, casualties or disruption avoided and distribution across vulnerable groups. Use phased implementation, comparable networks and pre/post evidence cautiously to assess contribution. Examine lifecycle cost, maintenance and institutional ownership. Test for transferred risk, common-mode redundancy, exclusion or displacement. Independent review, transparent service-level reporting and scheduled retesting should feed findings into codes, budgets, procurement and plans.

**Adjacent rubric:** Theory/baseline 4 + indicator chain 5 + attribution 3 + equity/maladaptation 4 + learning/accountability 4 = **20 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Completion certificates and expenditure utilisation are sufficient proof of resilience.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — Hospital dependency

**Question:** Why can a protected hospital still fail?

**Model response:** A structurally safe hospital can lose function when external power, water, oxygen, communications, access, staff or waste services fail. Backups must have adequate capacity, autonomy and tested replenishment.

**Adjacent rubric:** External dependencies 2 + fallback limit 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 2 — Criticality

**Question:** What makes an asset critical?

**Model response:** Criticality depends on consequences of failure, network dependencies, exposed users, available substitutes and restoration time—not prestige or replacement cost alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Consequence/dependency 2 + substitutes/restoration 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 3 — Reliability design

**Question:** Distinguish redundancy from diversity.

**Model response:** Redundancy adds spare capacity of the same or similar type; diversity supplies the function through a different technical route or failure mode. Both still require separation from common causes.

**Adjacent rubric:** Redundancy 1 + diversity 1 + common-cause qualification 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 4 — Maintenance

**Question:** Why is maintenance a resilience intervention?

**Model response:** Maintenance preserves designed capacity, detects hidden deterioration and keeps gates, pumps, generators, joints and controls functional when a shock occurs.

**Adjacent rubric:** Capacity preservation 1 + defect detection 1 + emergency functionality 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 5 — Common-mode failure

**Question:** What is common-mode failure?

**Model response:** It occurs when nominally separate assets or backups are disabled by one shared hazard, location, corridor, supplier, control system or operating error.

**Adjacent rubric:** Shared cause 2 + valid example/category 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 6 — Warning outcome

**Question:** How should warning performance be measured?

**Model response:** Measure whether an accessible message was received, understood and converted into timely protective action and maintained service; alert transmission alone is an output.

**Adjacent rubric:** Receipt/understanding 1 + protective action 1 + service/outcome distinction 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 7 — Urban institutional status

**Question:** Distinguish the Urban Flooding Cell recommendation, UFRMP and UDMA.

**Model response:** NDMA 2010 recommends a national Urban Flooding Cell with ULBs managing locally; UFRMP is an NDMF-financed mitigation programme; section 41A enables a State to constitute UDMA. Recommendation, programme and authority are not interchangeable.

**Adjacent rubric:** UFC–ULB split 1 + UFRMP status 1 + UDMA status 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 8 — CDRI status and outcome

**Question:** What did the August 2022 Headquarters Agreement change, and what did it not prove?

**Model response:** It formalised CDRI’s international-organisation status and New Delhi Secretariat privileges and immunities. It facilitated international operation but did not prove domestic adoption or reduced infrastructure failure.

**Adjacent rubric:** Legal status 1 + operational implication 1 + outcome qualification 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 9 — Equity metric

**Question:** Give one equity-sensitive restoration metric and explain its value.

**Model response:** Time to minimum safe water service by neighbourhood reveals whether a citywide average hides prolonged outage among low-income or high-dependence users.

**Adjacent rubric:** Valid disaggregated metric 2 + equity interpretation 1 = **3 marks**

## Check 10 — Evaluation closure

**Question:** What converts evaluation into governance?

**Model response:** Findings must become named and funded corrective actions with deadlines, public or supervisory tracking and retesting; a report without closure does not change risk.

**Adjacent rubric:** Owner/funding 1 + deadline/tracking 1 + retest/closure 1 = **3 marks**

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark practice

**Question:** Why must urban infrastructure resilience be planned as a system rather than as a collection of assets? **(10 marks; answer in no more than 150 words.)**

**Model answer (111 words):** Urban resilience is a service outcome, not a count of robust assets. Power may depend on fuel, telecom and flooded access; water pumps depend on electricity; hospitals depend on all of them. Therefore, planning should map functional dependencies, common corridors, substitute capacity and maximum tolerable downtime. It should prioritise nodes whose failure produces large cascading and unequal consequences. Standards and retrofit reduce physical failure, while maintenance, redundancy, modular isolation, mutual aid and exercised continuity plans preserve function. Performance should be tested through service thresholds and restoration time under plausible scenarios, disaggregated by neighbourhood. This system approach prevents an apparently safe asset from failing because its upstream network or operating institution collapses.

**Adjacent rubric:** Service-system thesis 3 + dependencies/criticality 3 + intervention mix 2 + performance/equity 2 = **10 marks**

## Original 15-mark practice

**Question:** Design an accountable governance arrangement for urban disaster and lifeline resilience in India. **(15 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (140 words):** Indian cities need precise institutional status, finance and operator accountability. NDMA’s 2010 guideline recommends a national Urban Flooding Cell, with ULBs managing local drains, waste, land use and services; this recommendation is not a verified universal body. UFRMP is an NDMF mitigation programme, including Phase 2 for 11 cities with ₹2,444.42 crore in approved financial outlay, not an authority or completed outcome. Section 41A enables States to constitute UDMAs. Where notified and staffed, UDMA should prepare the Urban Plan, map dependencies, coordinate utilities through the EOC and set minimum-service and restoration priorities. Asset owners retain technical liability and condition reporting. Budgets should separate maintenance, retrofit, contingency and recovery, supported where suitable by reserve funds or catastrophe-risk transfer. State standards, district escalation, transparent service metrics and participation by informal settlements, disabled people and frontline workers should make coordination operational and equitable.

**Adjacent rubric:** Fragmentation diagnosis 3 + institutional design 5 + finance/accountability 4 + inclusion/performance 3 = **15 marks**

## Original 20-mark practice

**Question:** Suggest a lifecycle strategy for building resilient critical infrastructure in Indian cities. **(20 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (156 words):** India should move from project protection to lifecycle resilience of services. Risk-sensitive land use and code compliance must be paired with condition assessment and retrofit of priority hospitals, schools, bridges, substations, water systems and communication nodes. Regulators and procurers should require hazard information, dependency analysis, redundancy without common-mode failure, maintainability and continuity plans. ULBs, UDMA where constituted, DDMA, utilities and private operators need shared criticality maps, warning protocols, EOC interfaces and restoration priorities. Finance should distinguish new capital, maintenance, retrofit, contingency and recovery and apply lifecycle and equity tests. CDRI can supply international knowledge, standards and finance approaches, but domestic adoption and operator performance remain decisive. Multi-hazard stress tests should test minimum service, fallback endurance, staff, suppliers and vulnerable users. Evaluation must measure condition, capability, downtime, cascading failure, restoration equity and maladaptation rather than expenditure alone. Recovery should compare repair, retrofit, relocation and redesign, protect access and affordability, and close after-action findings through named, funded owners.

**Adjacent rubric:** Prevention/retrofit 4 + network governance 5 + finance/CDRI 4 + stress testing/evaluation 4 + recovery/equity 3 = **20 marks**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core frame

- Urban disaster risk is produced by hazard, exposure, vulnerability and weak capacity across dense, connected systems.
- Critical infrastructure includes assets, networks, people, data, suppliers and operating institutions needed for essential services.
- Resilience means anticipating, absorbing, maintaining or rapidly restoring priority function and adapting after learning.
- The unit of analysis is **service to people**, not only the physical asset.

## 2. Interdependence and criticality

- Power, water, sanitation, health, mobility and communications depend on one another.
- A cascade occurs when one failure propagates; a common-mode failure removes nominally separate backups.
- Rank criticality through consequence, dependency centrality, substitutes, population dependence and restoration time.
- Maintain confidential asset detail while sharing enough service information for coordination.

## 3. Land use, buildings and informal settlements

- Avoid new exposure through risk-sensitive plans, development controls and protected blue-green systems.
- Enforce safe design and construction, but also inspect alterations, non-structural hazards and maintenance.
- Retrofit priority structures using competent assessment; do not prescribe one technique without diagnosis.
- Informal-settlement improvement should reduce risk without coercive displacement and should secure basic services and access.

## 4. Reliability toolkit

- Standards set minimum design and operating expectations; condition data reveals deterioration.
- Maintenance preserves intended capacity across the lifecycle.
- Redundancy supplies spare capacity; diversity changes the failure mode; modularity isolates damage.
- Physical, operational and organisational separation prevents common-mode loss.
- Every backup needs adequate capacity, autonomy, access, trained operators, tests and replenishment.

## 5. Sector applications

- **Power:** protect substations/control, segment networks, test black start and prioritise life-critical loads.
- **Water/sanitation:** diversify sources, protect treatment and pumping, sectionalise, preserve quality and emergency access.
- **Digital:** maintain power, route diversity, cyber controls, interoperable emergency communications and manual fallbacks.
- **Hospitals:** structural, non-structural and functional safety; water, power, oxygen, staff, access, waste and evacuation continuity.
- **Schools:** safe buildings and routes, non-structural safety, plans and inclusive drills; schools may become shelters.
- **Transport:** inspect bridges/nodes, preserve emergency corridors, manage crowds and plan alternate movement.

## 6. Data, warning and decision support

- Integrate authoritative hazard observations with asset condition, dependency and population data.
- Define thresholds, accountable decision owners and uncertainty communication before crisis.
- CAP or a common alert format supports interoperability, but the chain must reach accessible protective action.
- Validate crowdsourced information and protect privacy, security and purpose limitation.
- Maintain manual and offline fallbacks when digital control fails.

## 7. Governance and finance

- NDMA’s 2010 urban-flood guidelines recommend a national Urban Flooding Cell in the urban-development ministry, while ULBs manage locally; the Cell remains a recommendation unless current constitution/operation is verified.
- ULB, DDMA/SDMA, utilities, regulators and private operators require explicit interfaces and escalation.
- Gazette Act No. 10 of 2025 was assented to on 29 March 2025 and commenced on 9 April 2025 under S.O. 1648(E); section 41A enables States to constitute UDMAs, so constitution is not automatic.
- Where notified, UDMA can align the Urban Plan, dependency maps, EOC interface, exercises and restoration priorities without displacing asset-owner accountability.
- UFRMP is an NDMF mitigation programme: Phase 2 was approved on 1 October 2025 for 11 cities and ₹2,444.42 crore in financial outlay; official records provide city-wise assistance data but do not explicitly state a uniform Centre–State ratio; sanction is not an outcome.
- Budget separately for maintenance, capital, retrofit, contingency and recovery; reserve funds and catastrophe bonds are named risk-financing instruments, with detailed design retained by Topic 16.
- Use lifecycle, equity and service-outcome tests; asset owners retain engineering and service accountability.

## 8. CDRI and wider governance

- India launched CDRI on 23 September 2019; it supports risk knowledge, standards, finance and resilient recovery.
- The Headquarters Agreement signed on 22 August 2022 formalised CDRI’s international-organisation status and New Delhi Secretariat privileges and immunities.
- International legal status facilitates cross-border cooperation, experts, partnerships and fund administration; it does not create supranational regulatory or city-command power.
- Official reporting listed 58 countries and 12 organisations as of June 2026.
- IRIS, the Global Infrastructure Resilience report and DRI Connect are important channels.
- Evaluate influence through adoption in codes, procurement, lending and operator practice, then through service performance.

## 9. Equity and multi-hazard interfaces

- Heat raises electricity/water demand; flood disables low-lying assets; earthquakes damage structures and buried networks.
- Topic 14 owns shared-system effects; detailed hazard mechanics stay with Topics 05, 08 and 15.
- Criticality changes when users lack private storage, transport, insurance or alternate care.
- Accessible alerts, universal design, public cooling/water, safe shelters and neighbourhood restoration metrics reduce unequal harm.

## 10. Continuity, recovery and evaluation

- Define minimum service, maximum tolerable downtime, alternate sites, staff/suppliers and restoration order.
- Temporary workarounds need safety limits and an exit path.
- BBB compares repair, retrofit, relocation and redesign and also reforms failed management systems.
- Stress tests specify scenario, thresholds, dependencies, fallback duration and distributional effects.
- Evaluate input → output → capability → service outcome → welfare, using baseline, counterfactual caution and maladaptation checks.

> 🔑 **Mnemonic — A resilient city:** **MAP–MAINTAIN–BACK UP–COORDINATE–RESTORE–LEARN**, always measured through equitable service.
