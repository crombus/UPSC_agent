## Lesson 1: Hazard, Event and Disaster

Progress: 1 / 16 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: When danger becomes disruption

📚 Book context: Queried — canonical Basic and Advanced owners
🔍 CA search: "UNDRR disaster risk terminology hazard disaster capacity official"
📰 CA found: UNDRR terminology remains the official vocabulary anchor; no event-specific news is needed.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Threshold sketch

```text
HAZARD EVENT
   ├── no exposed people/assets ─────────> disturbance, not disaster
   └── exposure + vulnerability
                 ├── adequate capacity ──> impact contained
                 └── coping overwhelmed ─> DISASTER
```

The physical event alone cannot explain the social outcome.

A cyclone over an uninhabited sea is hazardous but not automatically a disaster. Disaster arises when a hazardous process seriously disrupts a community after interacting with exposure, vulnerability and insufficient coping capacity.

✅ **Fact:** The Core owner defines a hazard as a dangerous condition or event with potential to cause injury or damage. It describes disaster as serious disruption causing human, material, economic or environmental losses beyond coping ability. UNDRR likewise places hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity inside disaster-risk analysis.[^TERM]

| Term | Meaning | Does not establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard | Potential source of harm | Actual social loss |
| Hazard event | Manifestation at a time/place | Failure of coping capacity |
| Emergency | Situation requiring urgent action | Necessarily widespread disruption |
| Disaster | Serious disruption from risky conditions | That nature alone caused loss |

Two settlements may receive similar rain. Wetlands, drains, safer housing, alerts and response capacity can contain impact in one; unsafe siting, blocked drainage and exclusion can turn it into disaster in another.

⚠️ **Inference:** Saying “natural hazard” is usually more precise than treating the entire disaster as natural. This does not deny physical causation; it identifies policy levers.

### UPSC application

Open with the interaction, not a calamity list: **hazard + exposure + vulnerability, conditioned by capacity, produces disaster risk.**

### REVISION NOTES

1. Hazard is potential harm; disaster is realised serious disruption.
2. A hazard event does not automatically cross the disaster threshold.
3. Exposure places persons or assets in harm's way.
4. Vulnerability raises susceptibility to damage.
5. Capacity enables anticipation, coping and recovery.
6. Equal hazards can create unequal outcomes.
7. “Natural hazard” is safer than assuming the whole disaster is natural.
8. Begin answers with interaction and coping failure.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Explain why a severe hazard need not become a disaster, using one India-centric illustration.

**Model answer:** A hazard is a potentially damaging process; disaster is serious disruption produced when it meets exposed and vulnerable people or assets and overwhelms capacity. Intense rainfall may remain manageable where wetlands, drains, safe housing, warnings and local response work, but become disastrous where floodplains are occupied, drains are blocked and poorer households lack safe shelter. Risk is therefore relational. Reducing exposure and vulnerability or improving capacity can stop a hazardous event from crossing the disaster threshold even when the hazard cannot be eliminated.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definition/distinction 2 + interaction mechanism 3 + illustration 3 + qualified conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Every extreme natural event is a disaster; damaging interaction and coping failure are decisive.

**Transition:** The next lesson unpacks the four variables hidden inside the word “risk.”

## Lesson 2: The Risk System

Progress: 2 / 16 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical risk mechanism and UNDRR terminology
🔍 CA search: "UNDRR Sendai risk dimensions exposure vulnerability capacity official"
📰 CA found: Sendai Priority 1 continues to frame risk through hazard, exposure, vulnerability, capacity and environment.[^PRIORITIES]
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Four control dials

```text
                 DISASTER RISK
      HAZARD ↑ + EXPOSURE ↑ + VULNERABILITY ↑
                       CAPACITY ↓
```

This is a diagnostic relation, not a universally measurable equation.

Ask four questions: **What can happen? Who or what is there? Why are they susceptible? What usable capacity exists?** The owner gives the working relation `Risk = Probability of Hazard × Degree of Vulnerability`. Full analysis should make exposure and capacity explicit.

⚠️ **Qualification:** `H × E × V ÷ C` is a reasoning aid, not a physical law. Variables interact non-linearly, use different units and may cross thresholds. Capacity can also be inaccessible or fail under common stress.

| Heatwave diagnosis | Ward A | Ward B |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure | Few outdoor workers | Many outdoor workers |
| Vulnerability | Insulated homes, accessible care | Tin roofs, elderly persons, insecure work |
| Capacity | Alerts, water, cooling centre | Weak alerts, distant care |
| Likely risk | Lower | Higher |

**Objection:** Capacity cannot simply divide risk because a road or digital system can create new dependencies. **Reply:** Correct; serious assessment tests whether capacity is reliable, accessible and appropriate under stress.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Hazard describes the potentially damaging process.
2. Exposure identifies persons and assets in its reach.
3. Vulnerability explains susceptibility.
4. Capacity covers resources for anticipation, coping and recovery.
5. Risk changes when any component changes.
6. Probability × vulnerability is a working owner relation.
7. Expanded equations are heuristics, not universal laws.
8. Capacity must be tested for access and reliability.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Distinguish exposure from vulnerability and explain the policy significance.

**Model answer:** Exposure asks who or what is located in the hazard zone; vulnerability asks why that exposed unit is susceptible to loss. A school on a floodplain is exposed, while weak foundations, inaccessible exits and absent continuity plans make it vulnerable. The distinction directs policy: zoning or relocation reduces exposure; retrofitting, inclusive warning and continuity planning reduce vulnerability. Capacity must also be tested because trained staff, routes and backup services determine whether remaining risk can be managed.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definitions 3 + example 3 + policy significance 3 + capacity qualification 1 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Every exposed person is equally vulnerable; susceptibility varies within the same zone.

**Transition:** Vulnerability is the most socially differentiated risk component and needs its own typology.

📚 Book context: Queried — canonical vulnerability typology and official 2019 GS-III paper
🔍 CA search: "India inclusive disaster vulnerability disability gender NDMA official"
📰 CA found: No single current event is necessary; inclusive-risk principles apply across hazards.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Vulnerability pentagon

```text
                   PHYSICAL
              site · structure
                 /         \
      ENVIRONMENTAL       SOCIAL
       ecosystems       exclusion/access
                 \         /
          ECONOMIC — ATTITUDINAL
        income/assets   trust/agency
```

The dimensions compound rather than operating as isolated labels.

| Dimension | Mechanism | Reduction lever |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Unsafe location/construction | Safe siting, codes, retrofitting |
| Social | Exclusion by age, disability, gender or status | Accessible warnings and evacuation |
| Economic | Low savings, insecure work, asset dependence | Protection and livelihood diversity |
| Environmental | Degraded buffers/ecosystems | Restoration and land management |
| Attitudinal | Distrust, fatalism or dependence | Participation and credible communication |

✅ **Fact:** The owner explicitly identifies all five. The 2019 GS-III question asks how vulnerability can be characterised and its types.

⚠️ **Inference:** Disadvantages are often compounding, not merely additive. A household may occupy unsafe housing, lack savings, be excluded from warnings and depend on a degraded livelihood base.

Categories can stereotype. People described as vulnerable also possess agency, knowledge and networks. Pair vulnerability diagnosis with capacity.

### Lesson-local verified PYQ

**2019 GS-III Q8 — exact official wording:** “Vulnerability is an essential element for defining disaster impacts and its threat to people. How and in what ways can vulnerability to disasters be characterized? Discuss different types of vulnerability with reference to disasters.” **(Answer in 150 words; 10 marks.)**

**Demand/approach:** define → five dimensions with examples → compounding → capacity-sensitive conclusion. No solved PYQ answer is provided.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Vulnerability is susceptibility produced by conditions and processes.
2. Physical vulnerability concerns site and construction.
3. Social vulnerability concerns exclusion and unequal access.
4. Economic vulnerability concerns income, assets and recovery buffers.
5. Environmental vulnerability reflects degraded protective systems.
6. Attitudinal vulnerability can inhibit adaptive action.
7. Vulnerabilities compound across households and places.
8. Classification must not erase agency and capacity.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Show how three vulnerability dimensions may compound during a heatwave.

**Model answer:** A tin-roof dwelling stores heat; insecure daily-wage work makes staying indoors costly; age, disability or isolation may obstruct water, warnings and care. Physical, economic and social vulnerabilities reinforce one another because income insecurity prolongs exposure, poor housing intensifies it and exclusion delays help. Risk reduction therefore needs cool-roof or housing measures, worker protection, accessible warnings, outreach and health preparedness rather than a forecast alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Three dimensions 4 + compounding mechanism 3 + integrated measures 2 + clarity 1 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Listing “vulnerable groups” without explaining the process producing susceptibility.

**Transition:** Once susceptibility is diagnosed, policy must choose among different treatments of risk.

## Lesson 4: Four Ways of Dealing with Risk

Progress: 4 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Accept, avoid, reduce and transfer

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical four-part risk-treatment classification
🔍 CA search: "NDMA disaster risk reduction risk transfer insurance official India"
📰 CA found: Detailed insurance architecture belongs to Topic 16; this lesson retains the conceptual boundary.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Decision fork

```text
IDENTIFIED RISK
 ├─ informed residual tolerance? ───────── ACCEPT
 ├─ stop risky activity/location? ─────── AVOID
 ├─ lower probability/consequence? ────── REDUCE
 └─ shift financial burden? ───────────── TRANSFER
```

**Acceptance** retains residual risk consciously. **Avoidance** prevents entry into the risky activity or location. **Reduction** lowers likelihood, exposure or consequence. **Transfer** reallocates financial loss, commonly through insurance or contracts.

A panchayat may prohibit building in a high-risk channel (avoid), improve drainage and warnings (reduce), insure assets (transfer) and maintain contingency reserves (accept).

Insurance can speed recovery but does not stop water entering a building. Transfer without reduction can weaken incentives if unsafe behaviour is ignored. Affordability also creates an equity constraint.

⚠️ **Inference:** A sound portfolio avoids intolerable risk, reduces preventable risk, transfers suitable residual financial risk and explicitly accepts only what remains.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Acceptance consciously retains consequences.
2. Avoidance stops or relocates the risky activity.
3. Reduction lowers likelihood, exposure or consequence.
4. Transfer shifts financial rather than physical burden.
5. Insurance cannot replace safer siting or construction.
6. Residual risk remains after feasible reduction.
7. Equity affects who can use each strategy.
8. Layered treatment is stronger than one instrument.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is risk transfer not equivalent to risk reduction? Illustrate with a hospital.

**Model answer:** Risk reduction changes expected physical or social loss; risk transfer reallocates its financial burden. Retrofitting a hospital, elevating utilities and protecting backup power reduce disruption. Insurance may finance repair but does not keep beds, oxygen or communication functioning during impact. Transfer remains useful for liquidity and residual risk, but must accompany safety, maintenance and continuity planning. Otherwise reconstruction finance may repeat the same exposure.

**Adjacent rubric:** Distinction 3 + hospital example 3 + transfer's role 2 + conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Compensation after loss proves the underlying risk was reduced.

**Transition:** Risk treatment depends partly on how quickly and through what pathway the threat develops.

## Lesson 5: Classification and Time

Progress: 5 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Rapid, slow, natural, socio-natural and anthropogenic risk

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical rapid/slow and source classifications
🔍 CA search: "UNDRR slow onset disaster terminology official"
📰 CA found: No time-sensitive claim is needed; the planning distinction remains foundational.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Two-axis planning grid

| | Rapid manifestation | Slow accumulation |
|---|---|---|
| Predominantly natural process | Earthquake, cyclone landfall | Drought evolution, desertification |
| Human-amplified/socio-natural | Flash flood worsened by land use | Land degradation and chronic water stress |
| Anthropogenic/technological | Chemical release, industrial explosion | Pollution or unsafe-waste accumulation |

The grid classifies planning problems; it does not assign blame mechanically.

Rapid events demand detection, warning, evacuation and surge response. Slow-onset “creeping emergencies” need trend monitoring, livelihood protection, trigger thresholds and sustained adaptation. Slow does not mean harmless: capacity may erode before a dramatic declaration.

The owner distinguishes natural, socio-natural and anthropogenic origins. Socio-natural is useful where development amplifies a natural process, but attribution must remain evidence-based.

**Planning trap:** A siren may suit a sudden threat but is insufficient for soil degradation or drought. Slow-onset preparedness needs indicators, food/water and health surveillance, anticipatory action and livelihood measures.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Rapid-onset events have short protective-action windows.
2. Slow-onset processes accumulate over months or years.
3. Slow onset can erode capacity before formal recognition.
4. Natural describes the process, not the complete production of loss.
5. Socio-natural risk reflects human amplification.
6. Anthropogenic hazards include technological sources.
7. Classification must change monitoring and triggers.
8. Named-hazard operations belong to later topics.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why do slow-onset disasters require a different preparedness logic?

**Model answer:** Rapid-onset preparedness concentrates on short warning windows, evacuation, communication and surge capacity. Slow-onset risk develops through trends such as rainfall deficits, groundwater decline, crop stress or degradation. Preparedness must define indicators and trigger thresholds, protect livelihoods and nutrition, coordinate water and health measures, and act before coping capacity collapses. The danger is administrative delay because no single dramatic event marks the beginning. Both need risk knowledge, but their decision clocks differ.

**Adjacent rubric:** Time distinction 2 + rapid logic 2 + slow logic 4 + governance insight 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Slow onset means low urgency; delayed recognition can make cumulative loss irreversible.

**Transition:** Classification tells us what clock we face; the cycle tells us when each family of action belongs.

## Lesson 6: The Disaster-Management Continuum

Progress: 6 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Risk management, crisis management and feedback

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical pre/post-disaster cycle
🔍 CA search: "NDMP 2019 disaster management cycle official India PDF"
📰 CA found: Revised NDMP 2019 remains the latest published national plan available through NDMA.[^NDMP]
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Cycle with a feedback hinge

```text
PREVENTION → MITIGATION → PREPAREDNESS
     ↑                         ↓
RECOVERY ← RECONSTRUCTION ← RESPONSE/RELIEF
     └── lessons, safer rules and changed land use ──┘
```

The Core owner divides the continuum into pre-disaster **risk management**—prevention, mitigation, preparedness—and post-disaster **crisis management**—response, relief, rehabilitation, reconstruction and recovery. The boundary is analytical: preparedness shapes response, while recovery changes future risk.

The older rescue–relief–restoration emphasis is reactive. Prevention–preparedness–proofing shifts attention toward avoiding and reducing risk before impact.

A hospital illustrates the cycle: avoid unsafe siting; retrofit and secure equipment; drill and plan continuity; triage during impact; restore essential care; reconstruct safer utilities; review failures and update rules.

**Limitation:** Real phases overlap. Response can begin while impact continues, and rehabilitation may coexist with relief. Use the cycle as a responsibility and feedback map, not a rigid calendar.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Pre-disaster action manages prospective risk.
2. Prevention seeks to avoid risk creation.
3. Mitigation reduces adverse effects.
4. Preparedness builds readiness.
5. Response and relief protect life and meet urgent needs.
6. Rehabilitation restores functions.
7. Recovery should reduce future risk.
8. The cycle is feedback, not a rigid wall.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is the disaster-management cycle a feedback system rather than isolated stages?

**Model answer:** Preparedness determines response speed; response choices affect recovery equity; reconstruction either corrects unsafe design or recreates it; and recovery reviews generate new maps, codes and drills. Phases therefore overlap and exchange information. Treating the cycle as a checklist encourages reactive relief. Treating it as feedback makes post-disaster learning, safer reconstruction and revised prevention integral to management.

**Adjacent rubric:** Cycle explanation 3 + feedback examples 4 + checklist critique 2 + conclusion 1 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Recovery closes the file; it must alter the next round of risk management.

**Transition:** The next lessons separate the pre-impact and post-impact terms that answers often blur.

## Lesson 7: Prevention, Mitigation and Preparedness

Progress: 7 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Three pre-disaster logics

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical cycle and Sendai fields
🔍 CA search: "UNDRR prevention mitigation preparedness official Sendai"
📰 CA found: UNDRR Priority 1 links risk knowledge to prevention, mitigation, preparedness and response.[^PRIORITIES]
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Before-impact staircase

```text
AVOID RISK CREATION? ──> PREVENTION
        ↓ residual
LOWER EXPECTED HARM? ──> MITIGATION
        ↓ residual
ENABLE TIMELY ACTION? ─> PREPAREDNESS
```

**Prevention** avoids new risk, such as refusing critical infrastructure in an intolerable zone. **Mitigation** reduces likely harm through structural measures like retrofitting or non-structural measures like codes and ecosystem protection. **Preparedness** creates readiness through warning, plans, drills, stocks, trained personnel and continuity arrangements.

Preparedness cannot fully compensate for poor prevention. A good evacuation plan does not make unsafe expansion sound. Mitigation without maintenance creates paper resilience.

| Structural | Non-structural |
|---|---|
| Retrofitting, shelters, protective engineering | Zoning, codes, awareness, planning |

Structural works may transfer risk or create false security; non-structural rules may fail through weak enforcement. Good answers name both mechanism and limitation.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Prevention avoids creation of avoidable risk.
2. Mitigation lowers expected impact.
3. Preparedness enables timely action.
4. Structural measures alter physical systems.
5. Non-structural measures alter rules and behaviour.
6. Preparedness cannot legitimise unsafe development.
7. Maintenance and enforcement determine effectiveness.
8. Residual risk requires complementary measures.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Classify zoning, retrofitting and evacuation drills and explain their complementarity.

**Model answer:** Land-use zoning is primarily preventive because it stops new exposure. Retrofitting is mitigation because it reduces damage to an exposed structure. Drills are preparedness because they improve action after warning. Together, zoning controls future risk, retrofitting treats accumulated risk and drills manage residual risk. None is sufficient alone, and each depends on enforcement, maintenance, accessible communication and participation.

**Adjacent rubric:** Classification 3 + mechanisms 3 + complementarity 3 + qualification 1 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Evacuation is mitigation merely because it reduces casualties; its primary logic is readiness.

**Transition:** After impact, the purpose shifts from prospective reduction to life safety, functional restoration and safer recovery.

## Lesson 8: Response to Recovery

Progress: 8 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Response, relief, rehabilitation, reconstruction and recovery

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical crisis-management phase
🔍 CA search: "NDMA response relief rehabilitation recovery official"
📰 CA found: No event narrative is imported; the phase logic is taught generically.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Functional timeline

```text
IMPACT → response/relief → rehabilitation → reconstruction/recovery
         save life          restore function   livelihoods + safer systems
```

The periods overlap; purpose distinguishes them.

**Response** protects life and limits secondary harm. **Relief** provides urgent necessities and protection. **Rehabilitation** restores essential functions. **Reconstruction** repairs or replaces physical systems. **Recovery** is wider: livelihoods, services, institutions and wellbeing are restored and improved while future risk is reduced.

A fast average recovery can hide unequal outcomes. Tenants, informal workers, migrants or people lacking documents may be excluded from compensation. Equity therefore belongs within assessment and design.

**Objection:** These distinctions are academic during crisis. **Reply:** Labels allocate responsibilities and time horizons. Confusing relief with recovery can normalise camps, temporary services and unresolved livelihood loss.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Response protects life and limits immediate harm.
2. Relief meets urgent survival/protection needs.
3. Rehabilitation restores essential functions.
4. Reconstruction replaces damaged physical systems.
5. Recovery includes livelihoods and institutions.
6. Post-impact phases overlap.
7. Speed alone is not an adequate recovery metric.
8. Equity and future risk must shape recovery.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Distinguish rehabilitation from recovery and explain why it matters for vulnerable households.

**Model answer:** Rehabilitation restores essential functions such as basic health, water, schooling or mobility. Recovery is broader and longer: it restores livelihoods, institutions, housing security and social wellbeing while reducing future risk. Reopening a service does not mean every household regained access or income. Tenants, informal workers and undocumented residents may remain excluded. Recovery assessment must therefore track distribution, accessibility and safer outcomes, not only repaired assets.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definitions 3 + distinction 2 + household analysis 3 + conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Reconstructed buildings prove social and economic recovery is complete.

**Transition:** The continuum now joins under the wider purpose of disaster risk reduction.

## Lesson 9: DRR as an Operating Sequence

Progress: 9 / 16 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: From policy authority to usable knowledge

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
📚 Book context: Queried — canonical DRR definition and action field
🔍 CA search: "UNDRR disaster risk reduction official definition"
📰 CA found: UNDRR terminology and Sendai priorities remain the direct framework anchors.[^TERM]
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### 🖼️ Knowledge-to-action chain

```text
POLICY/LAW → RISK ASSESSMENT → AWARENESS → PLAN
    ↑                                      ↓
LEARNING ← LOSS DATA ← SAFE ACTION ← WARNING
```

A broken upstream link weakens downstream action.

✅ **Fact:** The owner defines DRR as reducing risk through reduced exposure, lessened vulnerability, wise land/environment management and better preparedness. The operating field moves through policy/legal framework, assessment, awareness, implementation, early warning and knowledge use.

A warning built on outdated mapping may be precisely delivered but wrongly targeted. A sound assessment has little value if plans are unfunded or people cannot act.

Hazard management asks how to control or respond to the process. DRR also asks how land, infrastructure, inequality, ecosystems and information create or reduce loss. This widens responsibility beyond emergency agencies without denying hazard science.

Audit implementation: Who is responsible? Is finance available? Are rules enforced? Are outcomes monitored and plans revised?

### REVISION NOTES

1. DRR prevents new and reduces existing risk.
2. It acts on exposure, vulnerability, land and preparedness.
3. Policy authority must lead to implementation.
4. Assessment supports awareness and warning.
5. Knowledge must be accessible and actionable.
6. Warning depends on upstream risk knowledge.
7. DRR extends beyond response agencies.
8. Framework existence differs from effectiveness.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is DRR a connected operating sequence rather than a menu?

**Model answer:** DRR links authority, assessment, awareness, planning, warning and action. Weak data misdirect assessment; inaccessible awareness prevents behaviour; an unfunded plan cannot operationalise knowledge; warning without preparedness produces no safe action. Loss and near-miss evidence must then update maps, rules and plans. Treating measures independently encourages visible but disconnected projects; sequence analysis reveals the binding gap.

**Adjacent rubric:** Sequence 3 + dependency logic 4 + feedback 2 + verdict 1 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** An early-warning device alone constitutes DRR; it is one link in a knowledge-to-action chain.

**Transition:** DRR describes risk reduction; resilience describes the capabilities that preserve and restore functions across disruption.

### 🖼️ Resilience wheel

```text
RISK KNOWLEDGE → ROBUSTNESS → REDUNDANCY
       ↑                         ↓
```

Resilience is an interacting capability, not one “bounce-back” score.

### 🖼️ Network failure map

```text
PRIMARY SHOCK
   ↓
POWER FAILURE ──> COMMUNICATION FAILURE
   ↓                      ↓
WATER PUMPS STOP      WARNING WEAKENS
   └────────> HEALTH + LIVELIHOOD DISRUPTION
```

A cascade is transmitted through dependency, not merely a long damage list.

- **Compound risk:** two or more drivers/events combine simultaneously or sequentially.
- **Cascading risk:** disruption propagates from one system to dependent systems.
- **Systemic risk:** interconnected structures allow local failure to generate widespread, persistent consequences.

Development choices can create risk even if hazard frequency does not change: unsafe siting raises exposure; degraded ecosystems remove buffers; tightly coupled infrastructure creates single points of failure; exclusion blocks warning access. Recovery then reproduces or interrupts this loop.

A scenario method begins with critical functions, maps dependencies, identifies thresholds and common-cause failures, and asks who loses access first. Resilience needs modularity, redundancy, interoperability and graceful degradation, not stronger assets alone.

**Objection:** “Systemic risk” is too broad to guide action. **Reply:** Dependency maps, stress tests, continuity standards and cross-sector exercises make it operational.

1. Compound risk combines drivers or events.
2. Cascading risk propagates through dependencies.
3. Systemic risk affects connected systems widely.
4. Development choices create and accumulate risk.
5. Tight coupling magnifies disruption.
6. Dependency mapping reveals transmission channels.
7. Stress tests should include common-cause failures.
8. BBB can interrupt risk recreation.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Distinguish compound, cascading and systemic risk through an urban-services scenario.

**Model answer:** Extreme heat coinciding with drought creates compound pressure on water and electricity. When these interconnections produce city-wide, prolonged health, economic and governance disruption, the risk becomes systemic. The distinctions guide action: combine drivers in assessment, map dependencies and thresholds for cascades, and use cross-sector standards, redundancy, shared data and exercises for systemic risk. # ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** “Disaster risk is a relationship, not a synonym for hazard.” Explain with an Indian local-governance example. **(10 marks; no more than 150 words.)**

**Model answer (108 words):** Disaster risk emerges when a hazardous process interacts with exposed people or assets, vulnerability and insufficient capacity. Thus identical rainfall can produce unequal consequences. In one urban ward, protected wetlands, maintained drains, safer housing, warnings and ward response may contain impact. In another, floodplain construction, blocked drains, insecure housing and exclusion from alerts can convert the same trigger into disaster. Local government can reduce risk through land-use control, building and drainage standards, vulnerable-resident mapping, ecosystem protection and preparedness. Hazard forecasting remains necessary but cannot substitute for action on exposure, vulnerability and capacity. Calling risk a relationship identifies several policy levers even where the physical hazard cannot be prevented.

**Adjacent rubric:** Proposition 2 + mechanism 3 + example 3 + governance levers 2 = **10 marks**

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** Examine whether Build Back Better can reconcile rapid recovery with long-term disaster-risk reduction. **(15 marks; no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (162 words):** Build Back Better treats recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction as opportunities to reduce future risk rather than restore the pre-disaster condition unchanged. It can reconcile speed and safety if recovery is sequenced. Essential services and temporary protection should be restored rapidly, while permanent reconstruction follows diagnosed failure mechanisms, updated risk information and inclusive standards. Safer siting, retrofitting, redundant lifelines, livelihood restoration and accessible services convert expenditure into resilience.

Tension remains. Assessment and participation take time; stricter standards raise initial costs; relocation may separate households from work and networks. Conversely, hurried rebuilding can lock in unsafe land use and exclude tenants or informal workers.

The answer is not to choose speed or safety absolutely. Authorities should pre-agree standards, maintain recovery finance and land records, use transparent eligibility, involve communities early, and phase rebuilding while monitoring distribution. BBB succeeds when essential functions return promptly and permanent recovery produces lower exposure, lower vulnerability and stronger adaptive capacity. It fails when “better” means only faster asset replacement.

**Adjacent rubric:** Thesis 3 + reconciliation 4 + tensions 3 + policy design 3 + verdict 2 = **15 marks**

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** Evaluate the Sendai Framework as a model of global risk governance in an era of compound and cascading disasters. **(20 marks; no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (189 words):** Sendai provides a common vocabulary for preventing new risk, reducing existing risk and managing residual risk. Its four priorities connect knowledge, governance, investment and preparedness/Build Back Better; seven targets link mortality, affected people, economic loss and infrastructure disruption with strategies, cooperation and warning access. This architecture suits compound and cascading disasters because it encourages multi-hazard assessment and critical-service continuity rather than isolated response.

Its limits are substantial. Sendai is voluntary and non-binding. Reporting quality varies; strategy counts do not prove finance or enforcement; loss outcomes reflect hazard incidence and exposure as well as policy. Targets can understate interconnected failure where power, water, health, communication and livelihoods collapse through shared dependencies. The 2023 Midterm Review renewed commitment but did not create enforcement. Early Warnings for All advances Target G, not all targets.

Sendai should be strengthened through public baselines, disaggregated loss and capacity data, dependency stress tests, financed responsibilities, independent review and integration with development and climate decisions. Its value lies less in coercion than in common standards and coordination. It remains necessary but insufficient: effective systemic-risk governance depends on domestic accountability and decisions that stop creating exposure and vulnerability.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Concepts and risk

- Disaster is serious disruption produced by hazard interacting with exposure, vulnerability and insufficient capacity.
- Hazard is potential harm; exposure is location in harm's way; vulnerability is susceptibility; capacity is usable strength.
- The owner's probability × vulnerability relation is a working diagnostic aid, not a universal law.
- Same hazard, different exposure/vulnerability/capacity, different outcome.
- Physical, social, economic, environmental and attitudinal vulnerabilities can compound.
- Classification should not erase agency, local knowledge or unequal access to capacity.

## Risk treatment

- Accept informed residual risk.
- Avoid the risky activity or location.
- Reduce probability, exposure, vulnerability or consequence.
- Transfer financial burden; do not confuse it with physical reduction.
- Best portfolio: avoid intolerable → reduce preventable → transfer suitable residual → accept remainder.

## Classification and continuum

- Rapid onset requires short-window warnings and surge action.
- Slow onset requires indicators, triggers, anticipatory livelihood and health measures.
- Socio-natural risk captures human amplification of natural processes.
- Pre-disaster: prevention, mitigation, preparedness.
- Post-disaster: response, relief, rehabilitation, reconstruction, recovery.
- Phases overlap and feed lessons back into risk reduction.

## Phase distinctions

- Prevention avoids new risk; mitigation lowers harm; preparedness builds readiness.
- Response saves life and limits immediate harm; relief meets urgent needs.
- Rehabilitation restores functions; reconstruction restores assets.
- Recovery restores and improves livelihoods, systems and wellbeing.
- Structural action changes physical systems; non-structural action changes rules, knowledge and behaviour.

## DRR sequence

- DRR reduces exposure/vulnerability, improves land/environment management and preparedness.
- Sequence: policy/law → assessment → awareness → plan → warning → action → learning.
- Test responsibility, finance, enforcement, inclusion and monitoring.
- A framework can exist without being effective.

## Resilience

- Capacity to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt, transform and recover.
- Determined by risk context plus governance, infrastructure, networks, finance and knowledge.
- Risk knowledge, robustness, redundancy, resourcefulness, rapidity and adaptability are key elements.
- Robustness means resistance; redundancy means alternatives.
- “Bounce back” is incomplete if old conditions were unsafe.

## Build Back Better

- Explicit under Sendai Priority 4.
- Diagnose failure before permanent reconstruction.
- Safer standards/siting, inclusive access/livelihoods and future-risk adaptation.
- Restore essentials quickly but prevent permanent lock-in of old risk.
- Relocation requires livelihood and participation safeguards.

## Hyogo and Sendai

- Hyogo: 2005–2015, five priorities.
- Sendai: 2015–2030, adopted 18 March 2015, four priorities and seven targets.
- Sendai sharpens prevention of new risk, wider drivers/actors, measurement and BBB.
- Both are non-binding; Sendai is not a treaty.

## Sendai priorities and targets

1. Understand risk.
2. Strengthen risk governance.
3. Invest in DRR for resilience.
4. Prepare for response and BBB.

- A mortality; B affected people; C economic loss/GDP; D infrastructure/services.
- E national/local strategies (2020); F cooperation; G warning/risk information.
- A–D are loss outcomes; E–G are means.
- Indicator values require context and data-quality analysis.

## Midterm and EW4All

- 2023 Midterm Review is the dated implementation anchor.
- UNGA resolution 77/289 adopted the Political Declaration.
- Recommitment did not make Sendai binding.
- EW4All seeks universal warning protection by 2027.
- Pillars: UNDRR risk knowledge; WMO forecasting; ITU dissemination; IFRC preparedness/response.

## SDG, Paris and India

- SDG 11.b explicitly links integrated local planning to Sendai.
- Paris covers mitigation and adaptation; Sendai covers multiple hazards.
- Risk-informed investment is the practical bridge.
- DM Act 2005 and National Policy 2009 pre-date Sendai.
- NDMP first edition 2016; revised/latest published plan 2019.
- PM ten-point agenda: AMCDRR New Delhi, November 2016.
- Do not project all 2016-edition criticism onto 2019 without comparison.

## Advanced governance

- Development choices can create and accumulate risk.
- Compound risk combines drivers; cascading risk travels through dependencies; systemic risk spreads across connected systems.
- Measure capacity, performance, outcomes and distribution.
- State baselines, units, periods and uncertainty.
- Voluntary global targets need domestic finance, assigned responsibility, public monitoring and review.

## PYQ spines

- 2019: define vulnerability → five types → compounding → capacity-sensitive conclusion.
- 2018: Indian measures before/after → real Hyogo–Sendai comparison → legal qualification.
- 2024: define resilience → determine → elements → all A–G compactly.
