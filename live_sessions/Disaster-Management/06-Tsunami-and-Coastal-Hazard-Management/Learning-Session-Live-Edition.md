### Visual first

```text
ORDINARY WIND WAVE                 TSUNAMI
wind transfers surface energy     water column is displaced
shorter wavelength                extremely long wavelength
surface-dominated                 whole-depth motion offshore
weather-linked                    geological/volcanic/landslide trigger
        ↓                                  ↓
usually local surface motion      basin-scale hazard potential
```

*Visual reading:* The comparison prevents the first exam error: a tsunami is defined by its displacement mechanism, not merely by impressive wave height.

### Start with the physical identity

✅ **Fact:** NDMA describes a tsunami as a series of ocean waves produced by a large, sudden displacement of the sea. The Japanese expression means “harbour wave”; it is not a tidal phenomenon. [NDMA-TSUNAMI]

The cleanest intuition is a bathtub. Moving a hand across the surface makes a small wind-like wave. Lifting one end of the tub would displace much more of the water column. A tsunami resembles the second mechanism.

### Four distinctions that control the answer

| Near-neighbour | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|
| Tide | Astronomical and periodic; tsunami is event-triggered |
| Storm surge | Wind and pressure driven; owned in detail by Topic 07 |
| Rogue wave | Localised ocean-wave extreme; not a basin-wide displacement train |
| Seiche | Standing oscillation in an enclosed or semi-enclosed basin |

✅ **Fact:** A tsunami usually arrives as a **series** of waves. The first arrival need not be the largest, so withdrawal after one crest is unsafe. [NDMA-TSUNAMI]

⚠️ **Inference:** Public language such as “tidal wave” is dangerous because it encourages people to wait for tidal timing rather than react to an earthquake, an official warning or unusual sea behaviour.

### Exam use

For a definition question, write three elements: **sudden displacement → long wave train → coastal inundation and currents**. Do not begin with casualty figures. The mechanism earns the conceptual marks.

### Revision notes

1. A tsunami is a series of long waves caused by sudden water displacement.
2. It is not an astronomical tide.
3. Wave size alone does not define a tsunami.
4. The offshore wave involves motion through much of the water column.
5. A storm surge has a meteorological trigger and belongs primarily to Topic 07.
6. A tsunami may arrive as multiple waves.
7. The first wave is not necessarily the largest.
8. A safe definition links trigger, displacement and coastal consequence.
9. The term “tidal wave” should be avoided in an exam answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is describing a tsunami merely as a very high sea wave conceptually inadequate?

**Model answer:** Height describes an observed effect, not the cause. A tsunami is distinguished by sudden displacement of a large water volume, the production of very long waves and their transformation near the coast; tides and storm surges can also raise water but arise through different mechanisms.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate every unusual coastal rise with a tsunami; identify the generating mechanism.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks; no more than 150 words):** Define a tsunami and distinguish it from a storm surge.

**Responsive model:** A tsunami is a series of extremely long ocean waves generated by sudden displacement of a large water volume, commonly after vertical seabed movement. It differs from a storm surge in both cause and warning logic. A storm surge is produced mainly by cyclone winds and low pressure and usually accompanies a forecast meteorological system. A tsunami is commonly geological, can cross an ocean basin and may arrive as several waves. Both can inundate low-lying coasts and generate destructive currents, so evacuation and coastal planning overlap. However, cyclone and surge science belongs primarily to Topic 07; tsunami management depends on seismic and sea-level detection, scenario modelling and rapid evacuation.

**Adjacent rubric (10 marks):** definition 3 + causal distinction 3 + shared coastal effects 2 + ownership qualification 2 = 10

**Transition:** With the hazard correctly identified, Lesson 2 asks which disturbances can displace enough water to generate it.

### Visual first

```text
SUBDUCTION-ZONE RUPTURE
locked plates → stress release → vertical seabed offset
                                ↓
                     overlying water displaced
                                ↓
                       tsunami wave train

ALTERNATIVE TRIGGERS
submarine/coastal landslide · volcanic collapse/eruption · rare impact
```

*Visual reading:* The trigger tree separates the dominant earthquake pathway from less frequent but examinable non-seismic pathways.

### The dominant pathway: vertical seabed movement

✅ **Fact:** Large undersea earthquakes are the principal tsunami source when fault movement produces significant vertical displacement of the seabed. Subduction margins are especially important because one plate descends beneath another and a long fault segment may rupture. [NDMA-TSUNAMI]

An earthquake beneath the sea is therefore not enough by itself. Depth, magnitude, fault geometry, rupture area and vertical movement matter.

### A necessary-condition test

```text
Earthquake under ocean?
        ↓ yes
Shallow and sufficiently large?
        ↓ yes
Vertical seabed displacement?
        ↓ yes
Water column displaced at scale?
        ↓
Tsunami generation becomes plausible
```

✅ **Fact:** Landslides, volcanic activity and, very rarely, meteorite impacts can also generate tsunamis by rapidly moving water. [NDMA-TSUNAMI]

### Why “where” matters

Convergent plate margins and trenches provide the classic setting. For India, the **Sunda–Andaman source zone** is crucial for the eastern coast and islands; the **Makran subduction zone** is relevant to the western coast. Local landslide or volcanic sources may offer much shorter reaction time than a distant basin event.

⚠️ **Inference:** A source-based risk map is more useful than a single national label of “coastal vulnerability” because source geometry changes arrival time, likely direction and available decision time.

### Strong objection and reply

**Objection:** If magnitude is high, a tsunami warning should always follow.

**Reply:** Magnitude initiates evaluation, but threat classification must also use location, depth, mechanism, modelled wave amplitude and sea-level confirmation. Over-warning every large earthquake would erode trust.

### Revision notes

1. Most destructive tsunamis are generated by vertical seabed displacement.
2. Subduction zones are major tsunami-source environments.
3. Not every undersea earthquake is tsunamigenic.
4. Magnitude alone does not establish coastal threat.
5. Fault geometry, depth and rupture area also matter.
6. Submarine landslides can generate tsunamis.
7. Volcanic collapse or eruption can displace water.
8. Meteorite impact is a rare theoretical trigger.
9. Sunda–Andaman and Makran are India-relevant source zones.
10. Source location determines warning time as well as hazard direction.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can two earthquakes of similar magnitude produce different tsunami outcomes?

**Model answer:** Magnitude measures only one feature of the event. Tsunami generation also depends on depth, offshore location, rupture dimensions, fault mechanism and especially vertical seabed displacement; the resulting wave then interacts with basin and coastal geometry.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use a magnitude threshold as proof that a destructive tsunami has formed.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks; no more than 150 words):** Explain the conditions under which an undersea earthquake becomes tsunamigenic.

**Responsive model:** An undersea earthquake becomes tsunamigenic when it suddenly displaces a sufficiently large water column. This is most likely where a shallow, large rupture along a subduction margin causes vertical seabed movement over a broad area. Magnitude matters, but it is not sufficient: focal depth, fault type, rupture extent and the direction of displacement shape generation. A strike-slip event with little vertical offset may produce limited tsunami energy despite high magnitude. The source location also affects propagation and warning time. Hence warning centres combine seismic parameters with pre-run scenarios and sea-level observations rather than treating every strong marine earthquake as a confirmed tsunami.

**Adjacent rubric (10 marks):** core mechanism 4 + qualifying variables 3 + counter-example 2 + warning implication 1 = 10

**Transition:** Generation supplies energy; Lesson 3 follows that energy across deep water and into the coastal run-up zone.

## Lesson 3 — Propagation becomes destructive through shoaling, run-up and return flow

### Visual first

```text
DEEP OCEAN                    CONTINENTAL SHELF                 SHORE
long, low wave  ───────────→  speed falls; height rises  ───→  run-up
little visual cue              wavelength shortens              inundation
                                                               debris impact
                                                               return-flow scour
```

*Visual reading:* The cross-section explains why a modest-looking offshore wave can become a destructive coastal flow.

### Follow one packet of energy

✅ **Fact:** In deep water, tsunami waves have very long wavelengths and may have limited visible height. As depth decreases, wave speed falls, wavelength shortens and wave height can increase through **shoaling**. [NDMA-TSUNAMI]

Three terms must remain separate:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wave height | Vertical difference between crest and trough |
| Run-up | Maximum vertical elevation reached above a reference sea level |
| Inundation | Horizontal penetration of water over land |

### Consequence chain

The incoming flow can drown people, damage buildings, move vessels and containers, contaminate freshwater and soil, and sever roads, power and communications. The outgoing **return flow** can scour foundations and drag debris and people seaward. Strong currents may damage ports even where land inundation is limited.

⚠️ **Inference:** Fatality risk is governed not by water height alone but by velocity, debris load, exposure time, escape opportunity and the structural quality of the built environment.

### Exact lesson-local PYQ

**2025 GS-I Q7 — 10 marks, 150 words**

> What are Tsunamis? How and where are they formed? What are their consequences? Explain with examples. (Answer in 150 words)

**Provenance:** `books\mains\UPSC Mains 2025 GS Paper 1.pdf`, p. 2. **Ownership:** Geography owns the physical-process demand; this Disaster Management topic supplies the warning and preparedness application.

**Answer-neutral approach:** define the wave, explain displacement and source settings, trace deep-water propagation to shoaling/run-up, classify consequences and use one specified example. No solved PYQ answer is supplied.

### Revision notes

1. Deep-ocean tsunami height may be visually modest.
2. Long wavelength permits basin-scale propagation.
3. Shoaling slows the wave and can increase its height.
4. Run-up is a vertical elevation measure.
5. Inundation is horizontal landward penetration.
6. Return flow can be as destructive as incoming flow.
7. Ports face dangerous currents and debris movement.
8. Freshwater, soil and sanitation may be contaminated.
9. Multiple waves require prolonged caution.
10. The 2025 GS-I demand is Geography-owned but management-relevant.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can judging tsunami danger from the apparent offshore wave height be misleading?

**Model answer:** Deep-water height may be small even when the wave carries great energy across a long wavelength. Near shore, reduced depth slows and compresses the wave, while local bathymetry and topography can amplify run-up, inundation and currents.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat run-up, wave height and inundation distance as interchangeable measurements.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks; no more than 150 words):** Explain how offshore tsunami propagation is transformed into damaging coastal inundation.

**Responsive model:** A tsunami carries displacement energy as a train of extremely long waves. In deep water the wave may be fast and low, giving ships little visible indication. As it reaches the continental shelf, reduced depth slows the wave, shortens its wavelength and can increase its height through shoaling. Coastal bathymetry, bays, river mouths and low relief then shape local amplification. The resulting run-up and inundation create drowning, debris impact, structural failure and utility disruption. Return flow adds scour and carries people and material seaward. Therefore coastal impact cannot be inferred from offshore height alone; high-resolution bathymetry, topography and evacuation planning are essential.

**Adjacent rubric (10 marks):** propagation 3 + shoaling 3 + coastal controls 2 + consequences/implication 2 = 10

**Transition:** The physics is general; Lesson 4 places it on India's source zones, islands and exposed coasts.

### Visual first

```text
                 MAKRAN SOURCE
                      ↓

SUMATRA–ANDAMAN SOURCE
          ↓
Andaman & Nicobar → east coast → Sri Lanka-facing Tamil Nadu

```

*Visual reading:* The schematic is a risk map, not a prediction: source direction, islands and local coastal form produce unequal exposure.

### A coast is not one uniform risk unit

✅ **Fact:** India's eastern and western seaboards and island territories can face tsunami hazard from different source zones. NDMA's national guideline treats the Sunda–Andaman region and Makran region as relevant settings for preparedness. [NDMA-TSUNAMI]

The 26 December 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami exposed the Indian Ocean warning gap and caused severe impacts in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and parts of mainland India's east coast, especially Tamil Nadu. It became the institutional trigger for establishing India's modern warning system.

### Hazard × exposure × vulnerability

| Variable | Coastal question |
|---|---|
| Source | Which trench or local disturbance? |
| Exposure | Who and what occupy likely inundation zones? |
| Vulnerability | Can people receive, understand and act on warning? |
| Capacity | Are routes, shelters and responders functional? |

⚠️ **Inference:** Islands may sit closer to source zones and have fewer evacuation alternatives; mainland urban coasts may possess more institutions but also denser assets and more complex traffic.

### Use 2004 analytically

The case should establish lessons, not decorate an answer with casualty recall:

1. basin-wide science must connect to national centres;
2. national bulletins must reach local authority;
3. natural signs and community knowledge matter where warning time is short;
4. rebuilding must reduce rather than reproduce exposure.

**Counterpoint:** The existence of a post-2004 centre does not prove equal readiness along every Indian coast. Readiness is location-specific and must be exercised.

### Revision notes

1. India faces tsunami exposure on both mainland coasts and island territories.
2. Sunda–Andaman is especially relevant to eastern India and the islands.
3. Makran is relevant to western India.
4. Local bathymetry and topography alter impact.
5. Low elevation and estuarine openings can increase exposure.
6. The 2004 disaster was an institutional turning point.
7. Islands may have shorter warning time and fewer routes.
8. Dense mainland coasts combine population and asset exposure.
9. A national system does not establish uniform local readiness.
10. Use 2004 for institutional lessons rather than unsupported casualty precision.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why should India's tsunami preparedness be planned by forecast zone and locality rather than through one uniform coastal template?

**Model answer:** Source direction, travel time, bathymetry, topography, settlement density, route capacity and livelihood patterns differ sharply. A zone-based plan can align modelling, warning status and evacuation with the actual local risk system.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not describe only the east coast as vulnerable or assume equal risk along every kilometre.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks; no more than 150 words):** Assess the geographical factors that create uneven tsunami risk along India's coastline.

**Responsive model:** India's tsunami risk is spatially differentiated. The Sunda–Andaman source zone directly exposes the island territories and can affect the eastern seaboard, while the Makran zone is relevant to the west coast. Distance from source changes lead time. Near shore, bathymetry, bays, estuaries, reefs, dunes and elevation alter run-up and inundation. Exposure also varies: islands may have limited routes, fishing settlements may lie at the water's edge, and ports or tourist towns concentrate people and assets. Capacity further modifies risk through warning reception, shelters and drills. India therefore needs forecast-zone modelling and locality-specific evacuation rather than one national coastal template.

**Adjacent rubric (10 marks):** source geography 3 + local physical controls 3 + exposure/capacity 3 + conclusion 1 = 10

**Transition:** Knowing where impact may occur is still incomplete; Lesson 5 turns exposure into a usable risk and evacuation map.

### Visual first

```text
SOURCE SCENARIOS + BATHYMETRY + TOPOGRAPHY
                     ↓
             INUNDATION MODELS
                     ↓
       hazard depth / arrival / flow zones
                     +
 population · schools · hospitals · ports · routes
                     ↓
      EVACUATION MAP + PRIORITY ACTION PLAN
```

*Visual reading:* A scientific hazard layer becomes useful only after it is combined with people, assets and feasible movement.

### Four maps, four different questions

1. **Hazard map:** where water may reach under specified scenarios.
2. **Exposure map:** which people, livelihoods and assets lie there.
3. **Vulnerability map:** who will struggle to evacuate or recover.
4. **Capacity map:** which routes, shelters, communication nodes and responders are available.

✅ **Fact:** NDMA's guideline calls for coastal land-use, vulnerability and risk mapping and links bathymetric and topographic information to inundation assessment. [NDMA-TSUNAMI]

### Resolution is governance

A coarse national map can screen risk. A ward or village plan needs street-scale routes, bottlenecks, safe assembly areas and the location of persons requiring assistance.

⚠️ **Inference:** Model uncertainty should be shown rather than hidden. A slightly wider planning envelope can be justified where uncertainty is high and evacuation cost is manageable; critical facilities require conservative design assumptions.

### The map audit

| Test | Failure revealed |
|---|---|
| Is the map publicly readable? | expert-only information |
| Can routes carry peak footfall? | paper evacuation |
| Is nighttime population included? | time-of-day bias |
| Are tourists and migrant workers counted? | registry bias |
| Is the map updated after construction? | development lag |

**Objection:** Precise inundation lines create certainty.

**Reply:** They create scenario-specific estimates. Responsible plans document assumptions, uncertainty and update cycles.

### Revision notes

1. Hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity maps answer different questions.
2. Inundation modelling combines source scenarios with bathymetry and topography.
3. Evacuation mapping is an operational product, not merely a scientific map.
4. Schools, hospitals and critical infrastructure require explicit overlays.
5. Route capacity and bottlenecks must be tested.
6. Nighttime, tourist and seasonal populations can change exposure.
7. Uncertainty bands should be visible in planning.
8. New construction can rapidly stale a map.
9. Critical facilities justify conservative safety margins.
10. Community validation can reveal paths missing from official data.

### Concept check

**Question:** What additional work is needed before an inundation model can function as an evacuation plan?

**Model answer:** The model must be combined with population and asset exposure, vulnerable-group needs, route and shelter capacity, warning lead time, signage and local validation. It must then be tested under realistic time-of-day and congestion conditions.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present a coloured inundation map as proof that residents can evacuate.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks; no more than 150 words):** Explain why tsunami-risk mapping is as much a governance exercise as a scientific exercise.

**Responsive model:** Tsunami mapping begins with science: source scenarios, bathymetry and topography generate possible inundation zones. Governance determines whether that knowledge protects people. Authorities must overlay residents, tourists, fishing activity, schools, hospitals, utilities and livelihood assets; identify persons needing assistance; and test route and shelter capacity. Maps must be legible, publicly displayed, updated after coastal development and linked to drills. Uncertainty also requires explicit planning margins rather than false precision. Thus the value of a model lies not in its technical appearance but in whether local institutions convert it into credible land-use, warning and evacuation decisions.

**Adjacent rubric (10 marks):** scientific base 3 + governance conversion 4 + uncertainty/update 2 + judgement 1 = 10

**Transition:** Mapped risk tells the system what must be protected; Lesson 6 explains who generates and carries the official warning.

```text
IOC-UNESCO / IOTWMS

  ├─ Tsunami Service Provider for Indian Ocean
  └─ National Tsunami Warning Centre for India
```

*Visual reading:* The ladder distinguishes technical forecast service from sovereign public-warning and local-action responsibility.

### The institution at the scientific centre

✅ **Fact:** The Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS, Hyderabad, has operated since **15 October 2007** under the Ministry of Earth Sciences. A PIB parliamentary release dated **12 February 2026** states that it detects tsunamigenic earthquakes within ten minutes and issues advisories under standard operating procedures. [PIB-2026]

✅ **Fact:** ITEWC acts both as India's National Tsunami Warning Centre and as a Tsunami Service Provider within the UNESCO-IOC Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning and Mitigation System. [INCOIS-TSP]

✅ **Fact:** The system's establishment drew on the Department of Space, Department of Science and Technology and CSIR, while the seismic subsystem used national and international networks. This historical multi-agency construction should not obscure INCOIS's present operational responsibility.

### Responsibility does not end at the centre

The regional provider supplies forecast information. Nationally mandated authorities decide and communicate protective action. State, district and local authorities must activate sirens, police, transport, shelters, hospitals and public information.

⚠️ **Inference:** Institutional clarity matters most when technical language changes quickly. A community cannot translate model fields during an emergency; predetermined authority and message templates must do that work.

### 2025 exercise evidence

✅ **Fact:** INCOIS's **14 October 2025** release for IOWave25 described participation by coastal disaster-management organisations, Navy, Coast Guard, NDRF, ports, harbours and critical installations. ITEWC exercised its dual national and regional roles. [IOWAVE25]

### Accountability chain

Ask four questions after any exercise: Was the event detected? Was the bulletin correct and timely? Did mandated authorities receive it? Did exposed people take the right action? Each question belongs to a different link.

### Revision notes

1. ITEWC is located at INCOIS in Hyderabad.
2. It has been operational since 15 October 2007.
3. It operates under the Ministry of Earth Sciences.
4. Its historical establishment drew on the Department of Space, DST and CSIR.
5. ITEWC is India's National Tsunami Warning Centre.
6. It also serves as an Indian Ocean Tsunami Service Provider.
7. IOC-UNESCO coordinates the regional system of systems.
8. Technical forecast and public protective action are distinct functions.
9. MHA, NDMA and subnational authorities form the domestic action chain.
10. IOWave25 tested national, regional and sectoral participation.
11. End-to-end evaluation must include community action, not only bulletin speed.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is it inaccurate to say that ITEWC alone warns and evacuates every Indian coastal community?

**Model answer:** ITEWC detects, assesses and issues technical advisories. Public alerts and protective action operate through mandated national, State, district and local authorities, which control sirens, evacuation, traffic, shelters and sector response.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not collapse a regional Tsunami Service Provider, a national warning centre and a local evacuation authority into one institution.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks; no more than 150 words):** Describe the institutional chain that converts ITEWC science into coastal protective action.

**Responsive model:** ITEWC at INCOIS is the scientific core of India's tsunami-warning architecture. It receives real-time seismic and sea-level data, evaluates tsunamigenic potential, runs scenarios and issues bulletins. Internationally, it functions as a Tsunami Service Provider within the UNESCO-IOC Indian Ocean system; nationally, it is India's warning centre. Technical products then move through designated Union control arrangements to State, Union Territory, district and local authorities. These authorities translate the advisory into public messages, traffic control, evacuation, shelter and sector-specific action. Exercises such as IOWave25 test this chain. Effective warning therefore depends on both forecast skill and predetermined institutional responsibility.

**Adjacent rubric (10 marks):** ITEWC role 3 + regional role 2 + domestic chain 3 + exercise/evaluation 2 = 10

**Question:** Why is the statement “mangroves prevent tsunami damage” too categorical for a UPSC answer?

**Model answer:** Mangroves can reduce flow and debris effects under suitable width, health and site conditions, but performance varies with event size and coastal form. They reduce risk and add co-benefits; they do not eliminate inundation or the need to evacuate.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present either concrete defences or ecosystems as universally superior.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (15 marks; no more than 250 words):** Compare structural and ecosystem-based approaches to tsunami mitigation.

**Responsive model:** Structural measures such as sea walls, breakwaters, raised refuges and strengthened buildings can provide measurable protection for specified design conditions and concentrated assets. Their limits include overtopping, scour, ecological disruption, high maintenance and a false sense of security. Ecosystem measures—mangroves, dunes, shelterbelts and reefs—can increase roughness, filter debris and provide fisheries, biodiversity and livelihood co-benefits. Yet they need space and ecological health and cannot stop every tsunami. The 2004 experience, including heavier damage where dunes had been degraded, supports conservation without proving absolute protection. India should use site-specific hybrid portfolios, protect sediment systems, audit life-cycle performance and retain evacuation for residual risk.

*Visual reading:* The loss matrix shows why a false alarm and a missed destructive event cannot be treated as equal forecasting mistakes.

### Asymmetric error

A missed destructive tsunami can cause mass casualties; an unnecessary evacuation imposes disruption, congestion and trust costs. The rational threshold changes with lead time, exposure and forecast confidence.

✅ **Fact:** INCOIS's SOP embeds this asymmetry by allowing near-source warnings from earthquake information where waiting for water-level confirmation would consume the available time. [INCOIS-SOP]

### Trust is managed through explanation

Authorities should distinguish:

- preliminary information;
- precautionary action;
- observed confirmation;
- downgrade or threat passed;
- local all clear.

⚠️ **Inference:** Trust is not preserved by avoiding every false alarm. It is preserved by predictable rules, honest updates and explaining why precaution was justified.

### Avoid two extremes

**Over-warning:** repeated broad evacuation for weakly specified threats can generate fatigue.

**Under-warning:** waiting for certainty can make warning physically useless.

The answer is forecast-zone specificity, staged messages, predefined triggers and after-action transparency.

### Equity complication

Evacuation costs are unequal. Daily-wage workers, hospital patients and fishers may bear more disruption, so shelters, transport and income protection can improve compliance.

9. Evacuation burdens are socially unequal.
10. Support measures can improve compliance.

**Model answer:** It ignores the far greater potential loss from a missed or delayed warning. Policy must minimise expected harm by considering asymmetric consequences, lead time, exposure and evidence while using targeted, transparent updates.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assess warning quality from false-alarm frequency alone.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (20 marks; no more than 250 words):** Critically examine the trade-off between precaution and false-alarm fatigue in tsunami warning.

**Responsive model:** Tsunami warning is a decision under asymmetric risk. An unnecessary evacuation disrupts livelihoods, health services and trust, but a missed destructive wave can cause irreversible mass loss. Near-source events sharpen the trade-off because waiting for sea-level confirmation may consume the evacuation window. Policy should therefore use conservative triggers where consequence and time pressure are high, while limiting disruption through forecast-zone specificity, staged bulletins and rapid downgrades. Trust depends less on claiming certainty than on authenticated messages, clear explanations and consistent all-clear authority. After-action reports should explain why precaution was taken and whether thresholds need revision. Transport, shelter and livelihood support also reduce unequal evacuation costs. The objective is not zero false alarms; it is minimum expected harm with accountable uncertainty.

```text
AUTHORITY LAYER: duty roster · alternate control room · delegated action
        ↓
END-TO-END SERVICE UNDER FAILURE
```

*Visual reading:* True redundancy crosses layers; duplicating one sensor on one network leaves a common failure point.

### Common-cause failure

Two sensors connected to the same damaged cable, two sirens on one power source or two agencies relying on one unavailable officer are nominal—not functional—redundancy.

✅ **Fact:** Vandalism of unattended observation platforms is a warning-system integrity concern. NDMA and INCOIS architectures rely on distributed real-time observation and communication. [NDMA-TSUNAMI] [INCOIS-TSP]

### Reliability controls

1. geographic diversity of sensors;
2. backup and tested power;
3. alternate telemetry;
4. automated anomaly detection plus human review;
5. authenticated bulletin channels;
6. alternate control rooms and duty succession;
7. spare parts and maintenance contracts;
8. cyber incident and misinformation protocols.

⚠️ **Inference:** Cybersecurity is a life-safety issue because a forged warning, suppressed bulletin or denial-of-service attack can trigger harmful movement or delay evacuation.

### Stress-test scenario

Assume one buoy is unavailable, a cyclone has degraded mobile service, and a false bulletin circulates online. The system should still validate the event, reach authorities through alternate channels, authenticate the correct message and explain the rumour.

### Accountability

Publish uptime and exercise metrics without exposing sensitive vulnerabilities. Independent technical audit should verify that backups can carry operational load.

### Revision notes

1. Nominal duplication may share a common failure point.
2. Sensor diversity should be geographic and technological.
3. Backup power must be load-tested.
4. Alternate telemetry protects data flow.
5. Automated analysis needs human review and succession.
6. Bulletins require authenticated channels.
7. Vandalism threatens unattended platforms.
8. Cyber incidents can become life-safety failures.
9. Alternate control rooms support continuity.
10. Spare parts and maintenance contracts are preparedness tools.
11. Backups must be tested under realistic load.

### Concept check

**Question:** What distinguishes functional redundancy from simply owning duplicate equipment?

**Model answer:** Functional redundancy survives the same failure because it uses independent locations, power, telemetry, staff authority or control sites and has been tested to carry the required service.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not count two devices on one vulnerable communication and power chain as resilient duplication.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (20 marks; no more than 250 words):** Design a resilience framework for India's tsunami-warning infrastructure.

**Responsive model:** A resilient warning system must survive physical, digital and organisational failure. Sensor networks need geographic and technological diversity across seismic stations, open-ocean instruments and tide gauges. Power and telemetry require tested alternatives, while anomaly detection should combine automation with trained human review. Authenticated bulletin channels, alternate control rooms and delegated duty succession preserve authority if one centre or officer is unavailable. Maintenance contracts, spare parts and protection of unattended platforms reduce degradation. Cyber controls must address forged messages, data manipulation and denial of service, with rapid public correction of misinformation. Exercises should remove selected assets and test whether backups carry operational load. Uptime, failover time and message integrity—not equipment count—should anchor audit.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

## Check 1 — mechanism

**Question:** Build the shortest complete causal chain from source rupture to coastal damage.

**Responsive model:** Vertical seabed displacement moves a water column; long waves propagate across the basin; decreasing depth causes shoaling; coastal form controls run-up and inundation; currents, debris and return flow produce human, structural and ecological loss.

## Check 2 — warning chain

**Question:** Which link connects a seismic estimate to a public evacuation?

**Responsive model:** ITEWC combines seismic parameters with scenario modelling and sea-level evidence, issues a zone bulletin, and mandated national and subnational authorities translate it through authenticated local channels into evacuation, shelter and all-clear decisions.

## Check 3 — data

**Question:** Why do high-resolution bathymetry and topography matter?

**Responsive model:** Bathymetry shapes near-shore wave transformation while topography shapes overland flow. Their resolution affects local run-up and inundation estimates, route design, zoning and critical-facility standards.

## Check 4 — evacuation

**Question:** What determines whether horizontal evacuation is feasible?

**Responsive model:** Usable warning time must exceed realistic clearance time after accounting for population, route capacity, congestion, distance, mobility assistance and earthquake damage. Otherwise engineered vertical refuge may be required.

## Check 5 — mitigation portfolio

**Question:** Why combine engineered and ecosystem measures?

**Responsive model:** Engineered works offer designable protection for selected assets, while dunes, mangroves and reefs provide friction and daily co-benefits. Each has site-specific limits; a portfolio plus evacuation manages residual risk.

## Check 6 — community

**Question:** What does Tsunami Ready measure that a national centre cannot?

**Responsive model:** It examines local maps, exposed-population knowledge, signage, education, exercises, plans and redundant alert receipt and dissemination—the capacities that turn a national advisory into community action.

## Check 7 — sector

**Question:** Name one distinct action for a port, tourist area and fishing settlement.

**Responsive model:** A port secures hazardous operations and later surveys channels; a tourist area activates multilingual staff-guided routes; a fishing settlement applies location-specific boat and shore guidance and protects livelihood recovery.

## Check 8 — multi-hazard

**Question:** What can tsunami and cyclone plans share, and what must remain separate?

**Responsive model:** They may share control rooms, volunteers, communications and suitably assessed shelters. Trigger science, lead time, wind/rain loads, evacuation messages and all-clear criteria must remain hazard-specific.

## Check 9 — recovery

**Question:** Add one livelihood condition to Build Back Better.

**Responsive model:** Safer housing or relocation must preserve feasible access to landing centres, markets, transport and services; otherwise income loss can drive households back into exposed locations.

## Check 10 — advanced evaluation

**Question:** Give one metric for each of science, communication and community action.

**Responsive model:** Science: event-detection or model-validation performance. Communication: authenticated delivery to all designated control points. Community action: receipt-to-safe-area time, disaggregated for vulnerable groups.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question

**Question:** Distinguish tsunami detection, forecasting and warning dissemination. (10 marks; no more than 150 words)

**Model answer (118 words):** Detection establishes that an event has occurred. Seismic networks estimate origin, location, depth and magnitude, while open-ocean instruments and tide gauges observe sea-level response. Forecasting interprets these observations through source parameters and pre-run models to estimate arrival time, amplitude and threat by coastal zone. Dissemination moves the resulting bulletin through authenticated channels to mandated authorities and exposed people. The distinctions matter because speed and uncertainty differ at each stage. A centre may detect an earthquake quickly while local inundation remains uncertain; a correct forecast may still fail if the last mile is inaccessible; and a delivered message may not produce evacuation. End-to-end readiness therefore requires sensor reliability, transparent modelling, clear institutional authority, redundant communication and tested community action.

**Rubric:** definitions/distinctions 4 + chain relationships 3 + failure modes 2 + conclusion 1 = **10 marks**.

## Original 15-mark question

**Question:** “Technology is necessary but insufficient for tsunami resilience.” Discuss with reference to India. (15 marks; no more than 250 words)

**Model answer (160 words):** India has built a strong scientific core since the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami. ITEWC at INCOIS integrates seismic and sea-level monitoring, pre-run modelling and sequential bulletins and serves national and regional roles. Official performance evidence and IOWave exercises show substantial institutional capability.

Technology, however, protects only when the complete chain works. Inundation precision depends on bathymetry and topography. State and district authorities must translate technical products into understandable local action. Sirens, mobile alerts and radio require backup power, authentication and accessible language. Evacuation routes, shelters and assistance must fit actual lead time. Weak zoning can continuously add exposure, while degraded dunes or mangroves remove co-benefits. Ports, tourism and fishing settlements need distinct SOPs. Rare-event readiness also decays through staff turnover, stale maps and infrequent drills.

India should therefore judge resilience through detection and model performance plus authenticated delivery, clearance time, inclusion, route audits and corrective-action closure. Technology is the indispensable upstream capability; sustained local governance converts it into saved lives.

**Rubric:** technological strengths 4 + insufficiency analysis 5 + Indian evidence 3 + reforms/verdict 3 = **15 marks**.

## Original 20-mark question

**Question:** Design an end-to-end strategy for tsunami and coastal-hazard management in India. (20 marks; no more than 250 words)

**Model answer (206 words):** An end-to-end strategy should begin with source and coastal risk. India must update scenario-based hazard maps using high-resolution bathymetry and topography and overlay settlements, tourists, ports, lifelines and vulnerable groups. Land-use and critical-facility decisions should avoid severe inundation where possible and use safe design where occupation is necessary.

The warning core should maintain diversified seismic, bottom-pressure and tide-gauge networks, tested power and telemetry, transparent model uncertainty and authenticated sequential bulletins. ITEWC's national and regional roles must connect through clear Union, State, district and local authority. Last-mile design should combine mobile alerts, sirens, radio, police and volunteers in local and accessible formats.

Evacuation plans should compare lead time with clearance time, provide horizontal routes and engineered vertical refuge where necessary, and assign assistance. Hybrid mitigation should combine site-specific engineered protection with dunes, mangroves and reefs while retaining residual-risk evacuation. Ports, tourism, fisheries, hospitals and utilities require continuity SOPs.

Recurring Tsunami Ready indicators and scenario exercises should counter readiness decay. Response and Build Back Better must protect water, shelter, health, livelihoods, tenure and ecosystem recovery. A public dashboard should track sensor uptime, forecast validation, authenticated reach, receipt-to-safety time and closure of exercise defects. Topic 07 should retain detailed cyclone and storm-surge science while shared coastal capabilities are integrated.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Definition and formation

- Tsunami: series of extremely long waves caused by sudden displacement of a large water volume.
- It is neither an astronomical tide nor a cyclone storm surge.
- Dominant trigger: shallow large undersea earthquake with vertical seabed displacement.
- Other triggers: submarine/coastal landslide, volcanic activity and rare impact.
- Not every undersea earthquake is tsunamigenic; magnitude, depth, fault geometry, rupture area and vertical movement matter.

## 2. Propagation and impact

- Deep-ocean height may be small despite substantial energy.
- Shoaling reduces speed, shortens wavelength and can raise wave height near shore.
- Run-up is vertical reach; inundation is horizontal penetration.
- Multiple waves, debris and return-flow scour extend danger.
- Bathymetry, topography, bays, estuaries, reefs, dunes and built form control local impact.

## 3. Indian exposure

- Sunda–Andaman source zone is central to eastern and island risk.
- Makran matters to the western coast.
- Islands may have short warning time and limited routes.
- Ports, tourist centres, fishing settlements and lifelines create concentrated exposure.
- The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami drove post-disaster institution building.

## 4. Risk assessment

- Separate hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity maps.
- Build scenario inundation models from source, bathymetry and topography.
- Overlay population, schools, hospitals, ports, utilities, routes and vulnerable groups.
- Show uncertainty and update maps after development.
- Convert maps into public routes, shelters, zoning and sector SOPs.

## 5. Warning architecture

- ITEWC at INCOIS, Hyderabad, operational since 15 October 2007.
- It is India's National Tsunami Warning Centre and an Indian Ocean Tsunami Service Provider.
- Seismic networks characterise the earthquake.
- Bottom-pressure systems observe open-ocean waves; tide gauges observe coastal response.
- Pre-run scenarios estimate arrival, amplitude and threat by zone.
- The ICMAM–INCOIS Tsunami N2 model is a named Indian scenario-modelling contribution.
- Bulletins evolve from preliminary information through model and observed-water updates.
- Near-source areas may need warning before water-level confirmation.
- Local authorities, not the final technical bulletin alone, determine all clear.

## 6. Last mile and evacuation

- Measure reach, comprehension, trust and actionability.
- Use authenticated mobile, siren, radio, police, volunteers and sector channels.
- Include local languages, tourists, migrants and persons with disabilities.
- Natural signs are backup signals for near-source events.
- Horizontal evacuation is preferred where clearance is feasible.
- Vertical refuge requires engineered suitability.
- Clearance time must be less than usable warning time.
- Shelters need access, WASH, health, safety and management.

## 7. Mitigation

- Apply avoidance, land-use regulation and critical-facility standards before rescue dependence.
- Use CRZ controls only at topic-appropriate risk-reduction depth.
- Combine engineered works with dunes, mangroves, shelterbelts and reefs.
- No defence eliminates residual risk or evacuation need.
- Prevent dune mining and ecosystem degradation where they increase exposure.

## 8. Community and sectors

- Tsunami Ready is an indicator-based community recognition programme.
- Venkatraipur and Noliasahi, Odisha, were India's first recognised communities on 7 August 2020.
- Exercises should test receipt-to-action and close identified defects.
- Ports need shutdown, dangerous-goods, channel and bathymetry SOPs.
- Tourism needs multilingual guidance and trained intermediaries.
- Fisheries need location-specific warning and livelihood recovery.
- Hospitals and utilities need protected control systems, backup power and access.

## 9. Multi-hazard boundary

- Share control rooms, volunteers, communication and verified multi-purpose facilities.
- Retain distinct triggers, lead times, structural loads, messages and all-clear rules.
- Topic 07 owns cyclone and storm-surge science and detailed management.
- Tsunami inundation can cascade into fire, chemical release, sewage and utility failure.

## 10. Recovery and advanced evaluation

- Build Back Better combines safer reconstruction, services, ecosystems, livelihoods and equity.
- Livelihood-blind relocation can drive return to exposed coasts.
- Rare hazards suffer readiness decay between events.
- Warning policy balances catastrophic misses against disruption and fatigue.
- Functional redundancy spans sensor, power, telemetry, cyber and authority.
- Evaluate the chain through detection, validated modelling, authenticated delivery, clearance time, inclusion and corrective-action closure.
