### 🖼️ Combustion diagnostic — triangle plus fuel layer

```text
                         HEAT
                       /      \
            remove ignition   interrupt heating
                    /            \
                 FUEL ---------- OXYGEN

VERTICAL FUEL PROFILE
canopy ───── crown fire
shrubs ───── ladder fuel
litter ───── surface fire
organic soil ─ ground fire
wind-borne ember ─ spot fire beyond the edge
```

*The triangle explains whether combustion persists; the vertical profile explains where it spreads and which tactic is safe.*

The fire triangle explains ignition and persistence: combustible **fuel**, sufficient **oxygen** and **heat** must coincide. Oxygen is ordinarily available, so management concentrates on preventing heat introduction and modifying fuel quantity, arrangement or moisture. Fire behaviour then depends on how heat transfers by radiation, convection and contact, including wind-borne embers.

Ground fires consume organic material below or near the surface and may smoulder. Surface fires burn litter, grass and shrubs. Crown fires spread through tree canopies and can be sustained by surface fire and ladder fuels. Spot fires occur when embers create new ignitions ahead of the main front. These are operational distinctions: detection, crew access, containment and ecological consequence differ.

Not every ecological fire is a disaster. Disaster risk arises when an uncontrolled fire’s intensity, spread and exposure threaten life, livelihoods, ecosystems or infrastructure beyond coping capacity.

### Field classification drill

Diagnose an incident through **fuel layer → flame/spread mechanism → likely exposure → safe control point**. The label should change tactics, not merely decorate an answer.

### REVISION NOTES

1. The fire triangle consists of fuel, oxygen and heat.
2. Heat is the most directly preventable ignition component.
3. Fuel amount, continuity and moisture influence spread.
4. Ground fires may smoulder in organic material.
5. Surface fires consume litter, grass and low vegetation.
6. Crown fires move through tree canopies.
7. Ladder fuels can connect surface and crown layers.
8. Spotting can breach a fire line.
9. An ecological fire becomes a disaster through uncontrolled risk and exposure.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why must forest-fire management distinguish ground, surface, crown and spot fires?

**Model-answer label:** Model: 96 words; ceiling: ≤150 words for this 10-mark question.
**Model answer:** The categories describe different fuel layers and spread processes. Ground fire may smoulder below the surface and persist unseen; surface fire moves through litter and shrubs; crown fire spreads rapidly through canopies, often after surface flame reaches ladder fuels; spot fires arise when embers ignite fuel beyond the main front. Consequently, detection signatures, containment lines, water or hand-tool tactics, crew escape needs and likely damage differ. Classifying fire only as “small” or “large” can misdirect suppression and expose crews. Managers should pair the type with weather, terrain, fuel continuity and threatened assets before choosing control points.

**Adjacent rubric:** Type distinctions 4 + operational consequences 4 + integrated decision 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** All forest fires are surface fires that differ only in area burned.

**Transition:** Fire type identifies the fuel layer; Lesson 2 explains why weather, season and slope change its rate and direction of spread.

### 🖼️ Slope-and-wind behaviour map

```text
                         ridge / wind acceleration
                              ↑  spotting
                     🔥 → → → ↑
                  flame preheats uphill fuel
               / / / / / / / / /  slope
 valley wind ↗                    lee-side eddy ↺

FAST VARIABLES: fine-fuel moisture • wind • humidity • slope • aspect
SLOW VARIABLES: vegetation structure • heavy fuel • access • seasonal drying
```

*A seasonal calendar sets readiness, but the fire front responds to live fuel, weather and terrain.*

### Briefing sequence

Use a daily briefing triangle: **forecast weather and wind → observed fuel condition → terrain/choke points**. Add a trigger for patrol, public restriction or crew stand-down.

Fire behaviour emerges from a changing **fuel–weather–terrain** interaction. Fine dry fuels ignite quickly; heavy fuels retain heat. Wind supplies oxygen, bends flames toward unburnt fuel and transports embers. High temperature and low relative humidity dry fuel; rain and higher humidity may moderate behaviour but do not guarantee extinction.

On a slope, flames and convective heat preheat fuel uphill, so an upslope head fire may accelerate. Valleys and ridges channel wind unpredictably. Aspect affects solar heating and fuel moisture. Fire seasons therefore vary by region: many central and peninsular dry deciduous landscapes become vulnerable during dry pre-monsoon months, while pine litter, drought, local burning practices and wind shape Himalayan seasons. North-East patterns interact with shifting cultivation and dry spells.

A calendar is only a preparedness baseline. Daily fire weather and live fuel condition can advance, extend or interrupt the expected season.


### REVISION NOTES

1. Fuel, weather and terrain jointly control fire behaviour.
2. Fine dry fuels ignite faster than heavy moist fuels.
3. Wind accelerates spread and creates spotting.
4. Low humidity dries dead fuel.
5. Upslope fires often spread faster through preheating.
6. Aspect changes solar exposure and moisture.
7. Valleys and ridges can channel wind.
8. Indian fire seasons vary across ecological regions.
9. Seasonal calendars do not replace daily observations.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Explain why a pre-season forest-fire calendar cannot serve as an operational forecast.

**Model-answer label:** Model: 96 words; ceiling: ≤150 words for this 10-mark question.
**Model answer:** A calendar identifies months when dry fuel and customary ignition are common, but actual fire behaviour depends on daily temperature, humidity, wind, recent rain, fuel moisture and local terrain. An unusually dry spell may advance the season, while rain may temporarily reduce spread. Wind shifts can reverse a safe flank, and slope can accelerate an uphill head fire. Managers must therefore combine seasonal readiness with daily fire-danger information, patrol observations and locally defined triggers for alerts, restrictions, resource staging and crew safety. A calendar prepares capacity; it does not predict a particular ignition or fire front.

**Adjacent rubric:** Calendar value 2 + dynamic factors 4 + terrain/operational consequence 2 + conclusion 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** The officially declared fire season fixes the direction and intensity of every fire during those months.

**Transition:** Behaviour changes in space; Lesson 3 maps India’s vulnerability and uses current FSI evidence without converting detections into burned area.

### 🖼️ Evidence hierarchy for Indian fire risk

| Evidence product | Unit actually observed | Valid use | Invalid shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|
| ISFR fire-prone class | area of forest cover and scrub by long-term proneness | strategic prevention and resource planning | current incident count |
| satellite hotspot | thermal detection at an observation time | rapid alert and spatial concentration | unique fire or hectares burned |
| field incident record | verified operational event | dispatch and incident analysis | ecological severity by itself |
| burnt scar | mapped post-fire footprint | affected-area assessment | cause or health impact |
| severity assessment | degree of ecological/soil change | restoration priority | interchangeable with area |

*Each product answers a different question; combining them prevents false claims from a single number.*

India’s fire risk is spatially uneven. Dry deciduous and pine landscapes, parts of central India, the North-East, Himalayan belts and forest-fringe mosaics combine seasonal fuel, access, livelihoods and difficult terrain in different ways. ✅ **Current official fact:** ISFR 2023 Volume I, printed page 127 (PDF page 171), reports that long-term FSI analysis places **nearly 11.34% of India’s forest cover and scrub in the extremely to very highly fire-prone zone**. Its Table 4.14 separates this into **1.45% extremely fire prone** and **9.89% very highly fire prone**.[^ISFR2023V1] The same highlight identifies the North-Eastern parts of Assam, Meghalaya, Manipur, Mizoram and Tripura as showing the highest tendency, while parts of Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand and Uttarakhand contain patches in the two highest classes. These are long-term proneness classes for forest cover and scrub, not a count of current fires.

Official PIB reporting based on the current forest assessment recorded **203,544 fire hotspots in 2023–24**, compared with **223,333 in 2021–22**. These are satellite-observed hotspots, not automatically 203,544 separate field incidents or the area burned. Cloud, sensor overpass, repeat detections and non-forest heat sources require screening; conversely, small or obscured fires can be missed.

✅ **Document-period concentration evidence:** The joint MoEFCC–World Bank study reports that **20 districts**, representing about **3% of India’s land area** and 16% of its forest cover in 2000, accounted for **44% of all forest-fire detections during 2003–2016**.[^WBFFM] This is not a current district ranking. Its operational meaning is that nationally uniform monitoring should be paired with disproportionately strong prevention, staffing, access and suppression readiness in persistent high-incidence clusters.

A robust risk map combines recurrence, fire-prone class, fuels, weather, topography, settlement/lifeline exposure, crew access and response time. District totals must be normalised by forest area and detection opportunity before comparing performance.

### Evidence-use rule

Before citing any fire number, label its unit: **hotspot, alert, incident, affected area, burnt scar or severity class**. Then state the observation period and method.

7. A hotspot is not necessarily one separate fire incident.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) How should an administrator interpret a decline in satellite fire hotspots without overstating improvement?

**Model-answer label:** Model: 98 words; ceiling: ≤250 words for this 15-mark question.
**Model answer:** The decline from 223,333 hotspots in 2021–22 to 203,544 in 2023–24 is an official observation, but hotspots are sensor detections rather than unique incidents, burned area or severity. Differences may reflect weather, ignition, sensor coverage, repeat detections, cloud and screening. The administrator should compare consistent seasons and sensors, normalise by forest area, map concentration and verify major events with field reports and burnt-scar assessment. Response time, escaped fires, area and assets affected, smoke exposure and recurrence provide complementary evidence. A lower hotspot count is therefore a useful signal, not by itself proof that prevention and suppression capacity improved.

**Adjacent rubric:** Correct figures/unit 4 + measurement limitations 4 + complementary evidence 4 + qualified conclusion 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Every satellite hotspot is one independently confirmed forest fire and directly measures hectares burned.

**Transition:** Spatial evidence shows where fire is detected; Lesson 4 separates immediate ignition from underlying social, fuel and climate drivers.

### 🖼️ Attribution staircase

```text
PROXIMATE SPARK
lightning | cigarette | machinery | livelihood burn | escaped farm fire
        ↓
RECEPTIVE FUEL
dryness | continuity | invasive grass | ladder fuel
        ↓
FIRE-WEATHER WINDOW
heat | low humidity | wind | dry spell
        ↓
EXPOSURE AND GOVERNANCE
access | settlement | patrol | trust | response capacity
        ↓
LOSS

Climate conditioning may alter the middle steps; event attribution still needs evidence.
```

*The staircase separates who or what ignited the fire from the conditions that allowed damaging spread.*

### Causal-writing sequence

Write causes as a layered chain: **ignition source → fuel/weather receptivity → spread pathway → exposure → governance condition**. This prevents single-cause answers.

Many Indian forest fires originate from human activity, whether deliberate livelihood or land-use practices, accidental escapes, discarded smoking material, tourism, vehicles or machinery. Lightning provides a natural source in some settings. The canonical owner’s “more than 95% human-caused” statement is document-period evidence and is not presented here as a current national rate without fresh official verification.

Ignition is only one node. Fuel accumulation, invasive grass, disrupted traditional management, weak patrol, contested access and incentives can turn a spark into repeated risk. Criminalising communities without examining these conditions damages reporting and cooperation.

Climate change may condition fire weather through heat, dry spells, altered rainfall and fuel moisture, increasing the probability of dangerous windows in some regions. Yet a particular ignition still has a proximate cause, and attributing one fire’s occurrence or damage to climate change requires event-specific evidence. Use “climate-conditioned risk” where attribution is unavailable.


### REVISION NOTES

1. Human ignition can be deliberate or accidental.
2. Lightning is a natural ignition source in some regions.
3. The owner’s 95% figure is not treated as a current rate.
4. Ignition differs from conditions that permit spread.
5. Fuel accumulation can magnify small ignitions.
6. Livelihood conflict can reduce cooperation and reporting.
7. Climate can condition dangerous fire-weather windows.
8. A proximate ignition cause remains identifiable where evidence permits.
9. Single-event climate attribution requires formal evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Analyse the statement that forest fires are “human-caused but climate-conditioned.”

**Model-answer label:** Model: 98 words; ceiling: ≤250 words for this 15-mark question.
**Model answer:** Human actions often supply the immediate heat source through land and livelihood burning, cigarettes, campfires, machinery or escaped residue fires; lightning can also ignite vegetation. However, whether an ignition becomes a damaging fire depends on fuel continuity and moisture, wind, heat, terrain, access and response. Climate change may alter temperature, dry spells, rainfall and fuel conditions, enlarging dangerous fire-weather windows in some landscapes. This conditioning does not identify the spark or prove that an individual fire was caused by climate change. Policy must therefore combine ignition prevention and livelihood-sensitive governance with fuel management, weather-informed readiness and event-specific attribution.

**Adjacent rubric:** Human ignition analysis 4 + conditioning mechanism 4 + attribution caution 4 + policy synthesis 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** If climate change increases fire weather, the immediate ignition source and local governance no longer matter.

**Transition:** Causal clarity guides surveillance; Lesson 5 follows a hotspot from satellite sensor through FSI systems to a registered user.

*The timeline shows technical evolution; the handover chain identifies where observation must become field action.*

FSI’s Forest Fire Monitoring and Alert System converts satellite active-fire observations into near-real-time alerts. Official FSI material records MODIS-based alerting from 2004, addition of SNPP-VIIRS in 2017 and launch of FAST 3.0 on **16 January 2019**. Alerts are geolocated to forest administrative units and disseminated to registered users by SMS and e-mail.

✅ **Canonical scale and chain:** The Basic owner records the FAST 3.0 institutional chain as **NASA active-fire data → ISRO/NRSC processing → FSI forest-range labelling → alerts to approximately 40,000 registered government and public users**.[^OWNERBASIC] The figure is retained as an approximate owner/document-period scale, not asserted as the verified user count on 4 October 2026. Current FSI records directly verify the satellite-to-FSI-to-SMS/e-mail architecture and registration mechanism.[^FSITECH]

Van Agni is FSI’s geoportal for monitoring and tracking large forest fires, with pre-fire and near-real-time information. It belongs to the same broad alert architecture; it is not evidence that FAST 3.0 has been superseded by an unverified later version.

Satellite detection offers reach and consistency but not perfect situational awareness. A hotspot may need field verification for vegetation type, access, smoke, assets, fire edge and suppression need. The alert must enter a State control room or range-level dispatch workflow with acknowledgment and closure.

### Alert audit trail

### 🖼️ Product-to-decision matrix

| Product | Time position | Required decision | Closure evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| fire-danger advisory | before ignition | patrol, restriction, staging, messaging | readiness action logged |
| new active-fire alert | current observation | acknowledge, verify and prioritise | field status returned |
| large-fire tracking | escalation/persistence | scale command and mutual aid | objectives and perimeter updated |
| burnt-scar/severity product | after containment | assess loss and restoration | field-validated recovery plan |

*A dashboard becomes protective only when every information product has an owner, action and closure rule.*

### Four-card operating protocol

Build four separate standard operating cards: **danger advisory, new hotspot, escalating large fire, post-fire scar**. Give each a recipient, action and closure criterion.

Pre-fire danger products estimate where conditions may support ignition and spread; active-fire alerts detect a thermal anomaly; large-fire monitoring follows persistence and extent; burnt-scar assessment examines affected area after the event. Treating these products as interchangeable leads to wrong decisions.

✅ **Pre-warning grid evidence:** FSI’s technical record explains that its pre-warning method overlays forest cover, forest type, recent fire incidence and short-term weather variables such as temperature and rainfall. From 2017 the analysis shifted to a **5 km × 5 km grid**, with qualifying grid cells communicated to State nodal officers as pre-warning KML alerts for the ensuing week.[^FSITECH] This is a susceptibility/readiness product, not a prediction that a particular cell will ignite.

A danger category should trigger graduated action: patrol and public communication, restrictions on high-risk operations, equipment staging, crew readiness or exceptional access control. An active alert requires verification and dispatch. Post-fire mapping supports loss assessment and restoration. Thresholds must be locally validated because a dry pine slope, deciduous forest and grass–forest mosaic behave differently.

The central governance problem is the **detection-to-action gap**. FSI can disseminate nationally, while suppression rests on State forest departments, divisions, ranges, local bodies and communities with varying staff, equipment, water and road access. The remedy is not another dashboard alone but interoperable dispatch, resources and measurable response time.


### REVISION NOTES

1. Danger products precede a detected fire.
2. Active-fire alerts indicate current thermal observation.
3. Large-fire products track escalation or persistence.
4. Burnt scars are post-event products.
5. Each product needs a different decision protocol.
6. Local fuel types require threshold validation.
7. FSI detection does not equal State suppression capacity.
8. Response time and access are operational metrics.
9. A dashboard without dispatch authority has limited protective value.
10. FSI’s pre-warning method uses forest, fire-history and short-term weather layers.
11. The 2017 system uses 5 km × 5 km analytical grids.
12. A pre-warning grid triggers readiness; it does not predict a certain ignition.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Why is India’s forest-fire problem not solved merely by improving satellite detection?

**Model-answer label:** Model: 103 words; ceiling: ≤250 words for this 15-mark question.
**Model answer:** Satellite systems improve reach, speed and common situational awareness, but risk reduction begins only when a verified alert prompts action. State forest divisions may face outdated contacts, weak connectivity, inaccessible terrain, inadequate crews, equipment or water, uncertain command and competing incidents. Pre-fire danger, active alerts, large-fire tracking and burnt-scar products also require different protocols. Improvement therefore needs local threshold validation, maintained recipient lists, staffed control rooms, acknowledgment, priority classification, pre-positioned crews, community reporting and safe dispatch. Evaluation should measure alert-to-verification, dispatch and containment time, escaped fires and affected area. Better detection is necessary, but without field capacity it may only document failure sooner.

**Adjacent rubric:** Detection contribution 3 + capacity/action gaps 5 + differentiated protocols 3 + reforms/metrics 4 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** A higher number of detected hotspots necessarily means the monitoring system caused worse forest-fire management.

### 🖼️ Prescribed-burn go/no-go card

| Gate | GO requires | NO-GO signal |
|---|---|---|
| objective | defined fuel/ecological purpose | “burn because it is the season” |
| prescription | mapped plot, fuel and moisture limits | unknown heavy/organic fuel |
| weather | acceptable wind, humidity and forecast | extreme/fire-weather escalation |
| control | maintained lines, trained crew, tools and water | weak escape margin |
| receptors | smoke and livelihood safeguards | school, road or settlement exposure unmanaged |
| closure | mop-up, patrol and evaluation | no post-ignition accountability |

*Prescribed fire is a controlled decision with cancellation thresholds, not a benign label for deliberate burning.*

Prevention interrupts fuel continuity and risky ignition. Fire lines are cleared or managed strips intended to slow spread and provide access or anchor points; they do not stop wind-borne spotting and must be maintained before the season. Strategic removal or rearrangement of dry litter, grass and ladder fuels may protect settlements, plantations, roads and critical habitat.

Prescribed burning is the planned use of fire for a defined management objective under authorised conditions. It can reduce selected fuels or maintain a fire-adapted regime, but it is not universally suitable. Wrong season, wind, humidity, fuel load, smoke pathway or crew capacity can convert it into an escape. Ecological objectives belong to competent forest management; disaster planning specifies safeguards.

Blanket fire exclusion may accumulate fuel in some ecosystems, while indiscriminate burning damages soil, regeneration and wildlife. Treatment must be landscape- and ecosystem-specific, monitored and linked to local knowledge.

### Authorisation test

Require a burn prescription to name **objective, plot, fuel, weather window, ignition pattern, control line, personnel, smoke receptors, escape triggers, mop-up and evaluation**.

### Non-gameable evaluation rule

A non-gameable scorecard requires at least one measure each for **maintenance, readiness, action, crew safety, consequence, equity and corrective learning**.

Evaluation must distinguish monitoring outputs from risk-reduction outcomes. Hotspots detected, SMS messages, fire lines created, equipment purchased and people trained are inputs or outputs. Capability requires maintained lines and tools, valid contact lists, fit crews, functioning command and tested community arrangements. Use is shown by timely verification, dispatch, safe tactics and action on danger warnings.

Outcomes include escaped-fire frequency, response and containment time, injuries, area and severity, threatened assets protected, smoke-health disruption, erosion and recurrence. Event severity varies, so raw annual totals need weather, fuel and exposure context. Burned area alone may penalise ecologically appropriate managed fire or reward dangerous aggressive suppression.

Equity review asks whether remote villages, women, tribal and livelihood groups shape prevention and receive warnings, safety and compensation. Independent after-action review should publish misses, delayed dispatch, near misses and corrective completion. Suitable comparison divisions or before–after capability tests strengthen causal inference.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Fire fundamentals

- Fire triangle: fuel + oxygen + heat; prevention can target ignition and fuel condition.
- Ground fire smoulders in near-surface organic material; surface fire burns litter and shrubs; crown fire spreads through canopy; spot fire forms beyond the front from embers.
- Behaviour depends on fuel amount, continuity and moisture; wind, temperature and humidity; slope, aspect and terrain channels.
- Upslope spread often accelerates because flames preheat fuel above.
- A seasonal calendar guides preparedness but does not replace daily observations.

## 2. Indian vulnerability and evidence

- Risk varies across central and peninsular dry deciduous belts, North-East, Himalayan pine/conifer landscapes and forest-fringe mosaics.
- ISFR 2023 Volume I, printed page 127 (PDF page 171), reports nearly **11.34% of India’s forest cover and scrub** in the extremely-to-very-highly fire-prone zone.
- Table 4.14 separates the combined figure into **1.45% extremely fire prone** and **9.89% very highly fire prone**; these are long-term proneness classes, not current fires.
- Official reporting recorded 203,544 hotspots in 2023–24 versus 223,333 in 2021–22.
- Hotspot ≠ unique incident ≠ area burned ≠ severity.
- Twenty districts covering about 3% of land area accounted for 44% of detections during 2003–2016; this is concentration evidence for that period, not a current ranking.
- The owner’s older “95% human-caused” statistic is document-period evidence, not asserted as a current rate.

## 3. Drivers and climate

- Human ignition can be deliberate or accidental; lightning is a natural source in some settings.
- Unsafe livelihood practice, tourism, cigarettes, machinery and escaped agricultural fire may supply heat.
- Fuel accumulation, invasive grass, fragmented management and mistrust amplify ignition.
- Climate change may condition heat, dry-spell and fuel-moisture windows; individual-event attribution needs specific evidence.

## 4. FSI detection architecture

- FSI’s satellite-based monitoring is the national detection anchor.
- MODIS alerting began in 2004; SNPP-VIIRS was added in 2017; FAST 3.0 launched 16 January 2019.
- Alerts reach registered users by SMS/e-mail after geolocation to forest units.
- The owner records an approximately 40,000-user chain from NASA through ISRO/NRSC and FSI; the number is approximate document-period evidence, not a verified current count.
- FSI’s 2017 pre-warning design uses 5 km × 5 km grids combining forest cover/type, recent fires and short-term weather to issue week-ahead KML readiness alerts to State nodal officers.
- Van Agni supports large-fire visualisation and tracking; it complements rather than disproves FAST 3.0.
- Pre-fire danger, active alert, large-fire tracking and post-fire scar products require different actions.
- Detection becomes protection only through acknowledgment, verification, dispatch, safe tactics and closure.

## 5. Prevention and fuel management

- Fire lines interrupt surface fuel but require maintenance and may be crossed by embers.
- Treat ladder fuel, litter or grass strategically around priority receptors where ecologically suitable.
- Prescribed burning requires objective, authorisation, fuel and weather prescription, trained crew, control line, smoke plan, escape triggers, mop-up and evaluation.
- Blanket fire exclusion and indiscriminate burning can both create harm depending on ecosystem and regime.

## 6. Community and livelihoods

- Forest-dependent and tribal communities possess terrain, use and early-observation knowledge.
- JFMCs, Eco-Development Committees, Van Panchayats and other legitimate institutions can support planning, maintenance, patrol, reporting and restoration.
- Distinguish unsafe practice from livelihood itself; provide workable alternatives and grievance channels.
- Participation requires representation, training, PPE, injury cover and compensation; it is not unpaid hazardous suppression.

## 7. Suppression, health and wildlife

- Size-up covers type, head/flanks, wind, terrain, fuel, spotting, smoke, threatened receptors and access.
- Use scalable command with operations, planning, logistics, liaison and public information.
- Crew safety requires lookouts, awareness, communication, escape routes, safety zones, accountability, PPE, rest and medical support.
- Smoke planning protects crews, children, elderly people, respiratory patients, transport and clinics.
- Wildlife action protects refuges and corridors and avoids untrained capture in active fire.

## 8. Recovery and governance

- Post-fire risk includes erosion, runoff, ash/sediment, water contamination, invasive species and livelihood disruption.
- Prioritise by severity × slope × rainfall × downstream receptor; use natural regeneration where viable.
- MoEFCC’s FPM Scheme supports State/UT prevention and management; FSI informs, while State forest departments execute field action.
- The historical National Master Plan for Forest Fire Control connected prevention, community participation, detection, patrolling, communications and remote-sensing prioritisation.
- National Action Plan on Forest Fires 2018 provides the prevention, community, capacity and recovery frame.
- Finance must sustain maintenance, crews, training, communications and restoration, not equipment purchase alone.

## 9. Advanced tools

- WUI plans integrate land use, structure ignition control, landscape fuel, warning, evacuation and crew defendability.
- Extreme-fire scenarios track scarce resources, crew rest, mutual aid, smoke, power and communications.
- Evaluation follows inputs → maintained capability → action → safe outcomes and uses weather/fuel context.
- A balanced dashboard includes readiness, alert latency, response, crew safety, area/severity, health, equity, restoration and learning.
- The Advanced owner diagnoses piecemeal governance and names gaps in strategic fire centres, inter-Ministry coordination, funding, HR, research and extension.
- Named research gaps cover ignition, burn severity, season, fire size, post-fire aquatic effects and monitoring reach.
