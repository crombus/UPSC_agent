### 🖼️ Financial Protection Across the Disaster Cycle — visual
```text
BEFORE EVENT                         AFTER EVENT
avoid/reduce risk  → reserve/credit/transfer → emergency liquidity
mitigation asset     pre-arranged contract     relief + continuity
        ↓                    ↓                       ↓
prepared recovery plan → damage/needs assessment → reconstruction
                                                   ↓
                                           BUILD BACK BETTER

PHYSICAL RISK ↓ is different from FINANCIAL BURDEN shifted
```

Disaster finance asks how money will arrive, to whom, under what trigger and for which phase. **Ex-ante** arrangements are made before an event: mitigation budgets, reserves, contingent credit, insurance, reinsurance, catastrophe bonds and pre-agreed social-protection finance. **Ex-post** finance is mobilised afterward through relief funds, supplementary grants, budget reallocation, borrowing, donations or reconstruction loans.

Risk reduction lowers expected physical or social loss. Risk retention accepts loss and finances it from budget or reserves. Risk transfer shifts defined financial loss to another party for a premium or return. None is universally superior: frequent small losses are usually retained or reduced, while rare severe layers may justify transfer.

Speed and purpose matter. Immediate relief liquidity cannot substitute for long-term reconstruction finance; insurance payout cannot repair poor land use; a sanctioned mitigation project is not an avoided-loss outcome.

### Four-question screen

For each rupee ask **before/after event, retained/transferred, trigger, eligible use, arrival time and risk outcome**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Ex-ante arrangements are made before disaster.
2. Ex-post finance is mobilised after impact.
3. Risk reduction changes expected harm.
4. Risk retention finances loss from own resources.
5. Risk transfer shifts defined financial burden.
6. Liquidity speed differs across instruments.
7. Relief and reconstruction need different finance.
8. A payout does not reduce physical exposure.
9. Sanction is not resilience performance.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Classify a drainage retrofit, emergency grant, reserve fund, indemnity policy and reconstruction loan by timing and risk function.

**Model score/word ceiling:** 10 marks | 98 words | maximum 150 words

**Model answer:** A drainage retrofit is ex-ante risk reduction because it seeks to lower expected flooding. An emergency grant is ex-post fiscal support for relief or continuity. A reserve is ex-ante retention: money is set aside before an event but spent afterward. An indemnity policy is ex-ante contractual transfer with ex-post payment based on assessed loss. A reconstruction loan is mainly ex-post financing and may deepen debt unless terms and BBB conditions are suitable. The classification depends on trigger and purpose: an after-event payment is not automatically risk transfer, and pre-arranged finance does not necessarily reduce the hazard or exposure.

**Adjacent rubric:** Five classifications 5 + timing/function logic 3 + qualification 2 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Any payment received after a disaster is risk transfer, while any pre-disaster expenditure is mitigation.

**Transition:** Lesson 2 applies these distinctions to India’s statutory response and mitigation funds.

### 🖼️ India’s Four-Fund Statutory Architecture — visual
```text
DISASTER MANAGEMENT ACT
s.46 NDRF (national response)   s.47 NDMF (national mitigation)
s.48 SDRF (State response)     s.48 SDMF (State mitigation)

EVENT-TRIGGERED                 PLAN/PROJECT-TRIGGERED
SDRF first → severe need → NDRF ex-ante risk-reduction approval

FUND ≠ FORCE: NDRF/SDRF names can denote institutions in other contexts
```

The Disaster Management Act distinguishes national and State response and mitigation funds. SDRF finances immediate relief at State level; for a disaster of severe nature, national assistance supplements State capacity after assessment rather than automatically replacing it. NDRF here means the **fund**, not the response force. NDMF and SDMF finance ex-ante mitigation.

The current position must be dated. Section 47(1) for NDMF commenced on 5 February 2021, so the mitigation architecture now exists; the stale claim that India has no mitigation fund is wrong. State Disaster Mitigation Funds and National Disaster Mitigation Fund windows were operationalised through the Fifteenth Finance Commission period and continued under the Sixteenth.

Response-fund eligibility is norm-bound. MHA’s current official page lists 14 nationally notified disasters, including heatwave and lightning. States may use up to 10% of annual SDRF allocation for State-notified local disasters outside the national list, subject to prescribed process.

### Status ladder

State **Act section → commencement → Finance Commission window → guidelines → allocation → sanction/release → use → outcome**. Never jump stages.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Section 46 concerns the National Disaster Response Fund.
2. Section 47 concerns the National Disaster Mitigation Fund.
3. Section 48 covers State and district funds.
4. SDRF is for immediate relief.
5. National assistance supplements severe-disaster needs.
6. NDMF and SDMF are ex-ante mitigation windows.
7. Section 47(1) commenced in February 2021.
8. The current national list contains 14 disasters.
9. The local-disaster window is capped at 10% of annual SDRF allocation.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Correct the statement: “India still has only relief funds, and every disaster automatically receives NDRF money.”

**Model score/word ceiling:** 15 marks | 102 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** India has a four-fund architecture: SDRF and NDRF for response, and SDMF and NDMF for mitigation. Section 47(1) commenced in 2021 and the Finance Commission framework operationalised mitigation windows, so their non-existence is outdated. SDRF provides immediate State-level relief under norms. National assistance through NDRF is supplementary for severe disasters after assessment; it is not automatic for every event. Eligibility follows the notified-disaster and expenditure framework, while a State may use up to 10% of annual SDRF allocation for properly notified local disasters outside the national list. Fund existence or release still does not prove adequate mitigation, full recovery or reduced loss.

**Adjacent rubric:** Four-fund correction 5 + trigger/escalation 4 + eligibility/local window 3 + outcome qualification 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** The National Disaster Response Fund is an unconditional entitlement that bypasses State funds whenever a disaster occurs.

### 🖼️ Sixteenth Finance Commission, Grants and Fiscal Layering — visual

The Sixteenth Finance Commission award period is 2026–31. Its State disaster-risk-management envelope is ₹2,04,401 crore: **SDRF ₹1,63,521 crore and SDMF ₹40,880 crore**, retaining an 80:20 response–mitigation split. The combined national NDRF–NDMF pool is ₹79,406 crore, producing an all-four-fund envelope of ₹2,83,807 crore. MHA describes 75% for NDRF and a maximum 25% for NDMF within that national pool; this is an allocation rule, not project expenditure.

Centre–State contributions to SDRF remain 75:25 for non-NEH States and 90:10 for North-Eastern and Himalayan States. Inter-State allocation uses 70% past expenditure and 30% Disaster Risk Index. FC16 permits flexibility within SDRF between relief and recovery but not transfer from SDRF to SDMF; preparedness and capacity-building draw on mitigation windows.

A fiscal layer should match predictable budgets and reserves to frequent losses, contingent credit to liquidity gaps and transfer to rare severe loss. Grants protect fiscal space but need clear eligibility and accountability.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Assess whether an 80:20 response–mitigation split by itself constitutes a complete fiscal-risk strategy.

**Model score/word ceiling:** 15 marks | 104 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** The split establishes predictable response and mitigation windows and corrects the older relief-only architecture. Yet it is only one layer. Mitigation remains the smaller window and is approval-dependent, while frequent losses still require departmental budgets and maintenance. Liquidity after medium shocks may need reserves or contingent credit; rare severe losses may justify insurance, reinsurance or capital-market transfer. Recovery can exceed immediate-relief norms and requires PDNA-based grants or borrowing. The 80:20 split also says nothing about implementation quality, data, equity or outcomes. A complete strategy therefore combines risk reduction, retained reserves, transfer, scalable social protection and transparent recovery finance, matched to loss frequency and severity.

**Adjacent rubric:** Value of split 3 + limitations 5 + layered instruments 5 + conclusion 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Once response and mitigation percentages are fixed, no additional liquidity, transfer or recovery arrangements are needed.

### 🖼️ Damage, Loss and Post-Disaster Needs Assessment — visual
```text
EVENT
  ↓ rapid assessment: life safety + immediate relief
  ↓ sector assessment

LOSS = disrupted flows, income, production and services

```

Rapid assessment estimates immediate life-saving needs; it should not be mistaken for a complete recovery budget. A Post-Disaster Needs Assessment distinguishes physical damage, changes in economic and service flows, human impacts and recovery needs. India’s official PDNA manuals and SOP adapt a structured sector-and-cross-cutting method.

Needs are not damage multiplied by a factor. Recovery must include debris, temporary service, livelihood restart, social protection, environmental restoration, safer standards, relocation where justified, institutional capacity and monitoring. Baselines should account for informal assets, unpaid care, tenancy and pre-existing service deficits without turning recovery into an unlimited development wish list.

A financing strategy maps needs by time and responsible owner: insurance and reserves for early liquidity; grants for public goods and vulnerable groups; credit where repayment is viable; and capital budgets for reconstruction. Public assumptions and revision prevent inflated or politically selective estimates.

### PDNA equation

Record **pre-disaster baseline + damage + flow loss + human impact + recovery need + BBB increment − confirmed finance = gap**.

3. Damage concerns physical assets.

5. Needs include risk reduction and capacity.
6. Needs are not a fixed multiple of damage.
7. Informal and unpaid losses require attention.
8. Confirmed finance should be separated from need.
9. Assumptions and revisions should be public.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) A State estimates reconstruction finance by multiplying verified asset damage by 1.2. Explain why this is inadequate and propose a needs method.

**Model score/word ceiling:** 15 marks | 101 words | maximum 250 words

**Model answer:** A multiplier misses service interruption, income and supply-chain loss, temporary facilities, debris, livelihood recovery, environmental harm, vulnerable groups and institutional capacity. It may also reproduce unsafe location and design. The State should establish a pre-disaster baseline; assess physical damage, economic and service flows and human impacts sector by sector; identify immediate, medium and long-term needs; and add a transparent BBB increment for safer standards, relocation or redundancy. Informal assets, tenants and unpaid care require specific methods. Existing insurance, departmental budgets, grants and credit should be mapped separately to reveal the financing gap. Assumptions should be published and revised as evidence improves.

### 🖼️ Reserve Funds, Contingency Budgets and Contingent Credit — visual
```text
SPEED / COST TRADE-OFF
budget contingency → reserve fund → contingent credit → reconstruction loan
fast, retained         earmarked       pre-agreed draw      negotiated later
```

Annual contingency provisions cover small predictable needs but can be exhausted. A reserve fund accumulates earmarked resources, increasing speed and reducing dependence on emergency reallocation; idle balances carry opportunity cost and need replenishment rules.

Contingent credit is negotiated before disaster and drawn after agreed conditions or triggers. It supplies rapid liquidity for medium layers that are too large for reserves but too frequent or costly to insure. It remains debt and should be assessed for repayment, currency, pricing, policy conditions and moral hazard. Ordinary post-disaster borrowing is slower and may arrive after critical response windows.

Design needs a target size based on probabilistic loss and fiscal capacity, authorised uses, investment and audit rules, drawdown authority, replenishment and links to procurement and social-protection delivery. Multiple instruments should not all depend on the same declaration bottleneck.

SDRF provides the statutory response layer under norms. Pre-arranged contingent credit can bridge a medium-to-severe liquidity gap and support eligible recovery once reserve and SDRF capacity tighten. The State should estimate target sizes from loss distributions, define draw authority and uses, link procurement and social-protection delivery and publish replenishment. It should test debt affordability and avoid a single declaration trigger for every source. Only the residual rare loss above these layers should then be considered for transfer.

### 🖼️ Insurance, Reinsurance and Parametric Products — visual
```text
INDEMNITY                         PARAMETRIC
verified actual loss → adjust → pay trigger index crossed → formula pay
slower; closer to loss             faster; basis risk
        ↓                                  ↓
INSURER retains layer → REINSURANCE / CAPITAL transfers tail

PREMIUM buys financial protection; it does not reduce hazard
```

Indemnity insurance pays assessed covered loss, subject to exclusions, deductibles, limits and claims verification. It can align payment with loss but requires asset records, adjusters and time. Reinsurance transfers part of an insurer’s portfolio risk, protecting solvency and expanding underwriting capacity.

Parametric insurance pays a preset amount when an independently measured index—rainfall, wind speed, earthquake intensity or another parameter—crosses a threshold. Speed and objective triggers are advantages; payment can differ from actual loss. Products may combine parametric early liquidity with later indemnity cover.

Pricing reflects expected loss, uncertainty, expenses, capital and market conditions. Subsidy can improve access but should be transparent and paired with risk reduction. Contract design must specify insured interest, trigger, geography, data source, exclusions, payout use and grievance. Calling every indexed public transfer “insurance” is incorrect unless risk is contractually transferred for consideration.

Compare **loss basis, trigger, verification, speed, basis risk, counterparty, premium, coverage limit and user comprehension**.

4. Parametric payout follows an index trigger.
5. Parametric payment can be rapid.
6. Basis risk is its central limitation.
7. Premium does not reduce physical risk.

Emergency liquidity needs speed; a parametric layer tied to an authoritative cyclone measure can release a preset sum for debris, shelters or continuity without waiting for full adjustment. The trigger should correlate with local loss, use reliable data and be tested for basis risk. A layered contract can combine both: parametric first liquidity and indemnity for verified asset loss, backed by reinsurance. Neither replaces safer siting, codes or maintenance.

### 🖼️ Basis Risk, Affordability, Moral Hazard and Data — visual
```text
PRODUCT FAILURE QUADRANT
INDEX misses loss → BASIS RISK       premium too high → EXCLUSION
cover changes behaviour → MORAL HAZARD

REPAIR: calibration + disclosure + subsidy design + deductibles
        + loss prevention + audit + grievance + data governance
```

Basis risk is the mismatch between indexed payout and actual loss: a harmed user may receive little, or an unharmed user may receive payment. Spatial variation, threshold design, data outage and changing climate can worsen it. Back-testing, denser observations, layered triggers and transparent disclosure reduce but do not eliminate the mismatch.

Affordability affects take-up, especially for low-income households, small farmers and MSMEs. Subsidy can correct access and public-good failures but may conceal price, reward poor design or create unstable fiscal liabilities. Moral hazard arises when protection weakens incentives to reduce avoidable risk; deductibles, co-payments, standards and inspections can align behaviour, but excessive cost sharing defeats protection.

Reliable exposure, asset, hazard and loss data underpin pricing and claims. Privacy, consent, interoperability, audit trails and appeal matter. Historic data must be adjusted cautiously for changing exposure and climate rather than treated as stationary truth.

### Failure-mode audit

Ask **who receives no payout despite loss, who cannot buy, whose behaviour changes, whose data are missing, and who can challenge an error**.

4. Affordability shapes coverage and take-up.
5. Subsidies create fiscal and design trade-offs.
6. Moral hazard concerns changed incentives.
7. Deductibles can align incentives but exclude users.
8. Exposure and loss data determine pricing.
9. Privacy and grievance are financial-protection safeguards.

The insurer and government should audit the gauge and contract, compare farm and remote-sensing evidence and provide an accessible dispute route without falsely converting every complaint into indemnity cover. Future design can use denser or blended indices, crop-stage weights, multiple triggers and transparent back-testing. Farmers need plain-language disclosure of residual basis risk. A limited safety-net layer may protect catastrophic mismatch, while agronomic risk reduction and extension continue.

### 🖼️ Sovereign, Subnational Risk Pools and Public–Private Architecture — visual
```text
HOUSEHOLD/FIRM POLICIES ─┐
STATE RESERVES/CREDIT ───┼→ pooled portfolio → insurer/reinsurer/capital
SOVEREIGN BUDGET/FUNDS ──┘

PUBLIC: rules, data, subsidy, last-resort support, consumer protection

SHARED: prevention incentives, delivery, audit, grievance
```

Sovereigns protect budgets, essential services and social obligations; subnational governments finance local assets and relief; households and firms protect property, income and continuity. A risk pool aggregates exposures across members or locations so uncorrelated losses diversify, but a national-scale correlated catastrophe still requires reinsurance, capital or sovereign backstop.

Public–private architecture allocates roles. Government can supply hazard data, legal rules, targeted subsidy, consumer protection, social registries and last-resort support. Insurers price, underwrite, service claims and mobilise reinsurance. Public guarantees must be explicit and capped; otherwise contingent liabilities remain hidden.

Cross-subsidy can support solidarity but should be transparent. A pool is not diversification if every member shares the same hazard at the same time.

```text
basis risk | debt | leakage | safeguards | maintenance | risk reduction
```

Evaluation separates financial readiness from financial resilience. Readiness asks whether instruments were legally valid, funded, triggered and delivered on time. Resilience asks whether money protected welfare, restored services and reduced future risk.

A baseline should include fiscal exposure, coverage gaps, service downtime, household coping, debt and asset condition. Instrument metrics include premium or carry cost, trigger reliability, payout ratio, claims time, basis risk, credit terms and contingent liability. Recovery metrics include beneficiary reach, procurement, safeguard compliance, livelihood restoration, maintenance and future-risk performance.

Counterfactuals are difficult because disasters differ. Use comparable jurisdictions, phased adoption, scenario exercises and contribution analysis. Audit moral hazard, risk transfer to vulnerable groups and whether BBB premiums financed real safety. Evaluation must revise layers, contracts, norms and mitigation priorities.

### Evaluation verdict

Rate **relevance, readiness, liquidity, protection, fiscal sustainability, equity, risk reduction and learning**, then state uncertainty.

4. Premium and reserve carry costs should be counted.
5. Basis risk is an outcome metric.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark practice

**Question:** Explain the logic of fiscal risk layering in disaster management. **(10 marks; answer in no more than 150 words.)**

**Model answer (108 words):** Disaster finance should match instruments to loss frequency, timing and purpose. Frequent small losses are best reduced and retained through maintenance, annual budgets and reserves. SDRF supports norm-based immediate State relief, while national assistance supplements severe needs. Contingent credit can bridge medium liquidity gaps but creates debt. Insurance, reinsurance or catastrophe bonds may transfer rare severe loss when data, scale and pricing justify them. SDMF and NDMF finance ex-ante mitigation rather than relief. Every layer needs a defined trigger, arrival time, eligible use and accountable owner. A portfolio is successful only when it protects essential services and vulnerable people while preserving fiscal sustainability and incentives for risk reduction.

**Adjacent rubric:** Layering thesis 3 + instrument matching 4 + governance/outcome 3 = **10 marks**

## Original 15-mark practice

**Question:** Assess India’s current response–mitigation fund architecture. **(15 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (124 words):** India now has response and mitigation funds at national and State levels, so the claim of a relief-only architecture is outdated. Under the Sixteenth Finance Commission for 2026–31, State allocations comprise SDRF ₹1,63,521 crore and SDMF ₹40,880 crore, retaining an 80:20 split. The combined national NDRF–NDMF pool is ₹79,406 crore; MHA describes 75% for response and a maximum 25% for mitigation. This provides predictable windows, sharing rules and risk-based allocation. Yet mitigation remains smaller and approval-dependent, SDRF cannot be transferred to SDMF, and relief norms do not finance complete recovery. A stronger system should combine departmental prevention, reserves, contingent credit, selective transfer, PDNA-based recovery and transparent outcome monitoring. Finance should be judged by timely protection and lower future loss, not corpus or expenditure alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Current architecture/figures 5 + strengths 3 + structural gaps 4 + reform/outcome conclusion 3 = **15 marks**

## Original 20-mark practice

**Question:** Discuss how recovery finance can operationalise Build Back Better rather than reproduce pre-disaster risk. **(20 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (146 words):** Build Back Better requires finance to change the conditions that produced loss. A PDNA should establish damage, flow loss, human impact and recovery needs, including informal livelihoods and non-economic harm. The recovery framework must sequence immediate services, temporary arrangements, livelihood restoration and permanent reconstruction and map each need to confirmed grants, insurance, reserves, credit or capital budgets.

Options should compare repair, retrofit, relocation and system redesign using future hazard, service continuity, lifecycle cost, maintenance and equity. Grants are appropriate for public goods and vulnerable groups; debt should be limited to affordable uses; insurance covers only contracted loss. Procurement readiness, land, skills and safeguards determine whether funds become safe assets. Dashboards should connect sanction, release, expenditure, output, beneficiaries and residual gaps. Independent review should test whether reconstruction reduced exposure and downtime without displacement or maladaptation. Recovery speed matters, but reproducing unsafe location or institutions is not recovery.

**Adjacent rubric:** PDNA/sequence 4 + financing fit 5 + BBB option analysis 5 + safeguards/accountability 4 + conclusion 2 = **20 marks**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core financial logic

- Ex-ante finance is arranged before impact; ex-post finance is mobilised afterward.
- Risk reduction lowers harm; retention finances own loss; transfer shifts contracted financial loss.
- Match instruments to frequency, severity, timing and purpose.
- Relief liquidity, mitigation investment and reconstruction finance are distinct.

## 2. Statutory India architecture

- Section 46: National Disaster Response Fund; section 47: National Disaster Mitigation Fund.
- Section 48 covers State response and mitigation funds and district funds.
- SDRF finances immediate relief; NDRF supplements severe needs beyond State capacity under rules.
- NDMF and SDMF finance ex-ante mitigation; section 47(1) commenced in February 2021.
- MHA’s current framework lists 14 national disasters, including heatwave and lightning; up to 10% of annual SDRF can address properly notified local disasters outside that list.

## 3. FC16 current figures, 2026–31

| Item | Verified current figure/rule |
|---|---:|
| SDRF | ₹1,63,521 crore |
| SDMF | ₹40,880 crore |
| State total | ₹2,04,401 crore |
| National NDRF–NDMF pool | ₹79,406 crore |
| Four-fund total | ₹2,83,807 crore |
| State response–mitigation split | 80:20 |
| Inter-State weighting | 70% expenditure; 30% Disaster Risk Index |
| Centre–State SDRF share | 75:25 non-NEH; 90:10 NEH |

- MHA describes 75% NDRF and maximum 25% NDMF within the national pool; do not infer project expenditure.
- Flexibility exists within SDRF between relief and recovery, but not from SDRF to SDMF.

## 4. Assessment and liquidity

- Rapid assessment identifies immediate needs; PDNA estimates damage, flow loss, human impact and recovery needs.
- A financing gap subtracts confirmed usable finance, not announcements, from costed needs.
- Annual contingencies and reserves cover retained layers; reserves need draw and replenishment rules.
- Contingent credit provides rapid pre-arranged liquidity but creates debt.
- Cash arrival time and eligible use matter as much as total volume.

## 5. Transfer products

- Indemnity insurance pays assessed covered loss after adjustment.
- Reinsurance protects an insurer or pool against portfolio loss.
- Parametric products pay a formula when an index crosses a threshold.
- Basis risk is mismatch between actual loss and indexed payout.
- Catastrophe bonds place investor principal at risk after a defined trigger; they are not infrastructure bonds.
- Premium, exclusions, deductibles, data and grievance determine real protection.

## 6. Users and architecture

- Sovereigns protect budgets and obligations; States and cities protect public assets and services.
- Households need shelter, income and accessible protection; low-income groups need grants/social protection.
- MSMEs need asset, interruption and working-capital solutions plus utility continuity.
- Agriculture combines insurance with advisories, credit, markets and livelihood measures.
- Pools require diverse exposure, capital, reinsurance, transparent cross-subsidy and disclosed public guarantees.

## 7. Recovery and BBB

- Immediate relief is narrower than full recovery and reconstruction.
- PDNA should sequence temporary service, livelihoods, repair and permanent reconstruction.
- Sendai Priority 4 embeds Build Back Better in recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction.
- Compare repair, retrofit, relocation and system redesign through future risk, service, lifecycle cost and equity.
- Finance the genuine BBB increment, not prestige or size.
- Procurement, land, skills, safeguards and maintenance determine implementation.

## 8. Current Indian mitigation and accountability

- UFRMP Phase II: ₹2,444.42 crore for 11 cities under NDMF.
- National GLOF Risk Mitigation Programme: ₹150 crore for four Himalayan States.
- Assam wetland restoration/rejuvenation: ₹519.04 crore for 24 wetlands.
- These are approval/outlay facts, not completion or avoided-loss findings.
- The Gazette text of the 2025 Amendment Act defines the disaster database to include “fund allocation detail” and “expenditure”; operational publication should not be assumed.
- Track authority → sanction → release → utilisation → functioning capability → maintained outcome.
