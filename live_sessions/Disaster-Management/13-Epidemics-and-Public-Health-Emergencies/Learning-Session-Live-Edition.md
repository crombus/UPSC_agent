### 🖼️ Classification grid

| Label | Core test | Scale implication | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| outbreak | cases above expected in a defined setting | may be local | treating every cluster as nationwide |
| epidemic | excess disease across a community/region | wider coordinated control | equating it with high fatality |
| pandemic | sustained international spread | cross-border coordination | using it as a severity label |
| public-health emergency | urgency, consequence, uncertainty and system pressure | exceptional coordination | relying on case count alone |

*Spread, severity and governance urgency are separate axes; the correct label determines surveillance, authority and communication.*

An **outbreak** is occurrence of disease above what is normally expected in a defined place, group and period; it may be local. An **epidemic** denotes excess occurrence across a community or region. A **pandemic** describes sustained international or worldwide spread of a new disease; it is a geographic-spread category, not automatically a measure of lethality. Endemic means expected continuing presence in an area.

A public-health emergency arises when health consequences, uncertainty, speed, scale or cross-sector disruption require exceptional coordination. The hazard may be infectious disease, contamination or another health threat. Case counts alone do not determine emergency status; severity, transmission, health-system burden, inequity and international implications matter.

The canonical biological-disaster definition includes natural and deliberate events. This session teaches natural outbreaks and epidemic management. Suspected deliberate release requires public-health detection but transfers security, forensic and CBRN command depth to Topic 12.

### Classification decision

Classify an event through **observed count versus baseline → geographic spread → severity/system pressure → coordination threshold → deliberate-release indicators**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. An outbreak is excess disease in a defined setting.
2. An epidemic covers broader community or regional excess.
3. A pandemic describes wide international spread.
4. Pandemic does not mean the highest fatality rate.
5. Endemic describes expected continuing presence.
6. Emergency status includes urgency and system pressure.
7. Severity and inequity matter alongside case numbers.
8. Natural outbreak management belongs to this topic.
9. Deliberate biological release is cross-linked to Topic 12.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why should pandemic, epidemic and public-health emergency not be used as synonyms?

**Model answer:** The terms answer different questions. An outbreak or epidemic compares observed disease with an expected baseline in a defined setting; a pandemic describes sustained international spread. A public-health emergency is a governance judgment based on urgency, severity, uncertainty, health-system pressure and coordination needs. A geographically widespread disease may be relatively mild, while a local outbreak with high fatality or laboratory danger can require emergency action. Precise terms determine surveillance, legal authority, communication and resource escalation without exaggerating either spread or severity.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definitions 4 + emergency distinction 3 + operational significance 3 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** A pandemic is simply any epidemic with a high case-fatality ratio.

**Transition:** Clear categories establish what is happening; Lesson 2 shows how transmission and severity measures guide action without becoming deterministic forecasts.

### 🖼️ Chain–metric intervention map

```text
AGENT → SOURCE → EXIT → TRANSMISSION → ENTRY → SUSCEPTIBLE HOST
  │       │        │          │          │           │
assay   sanitation isolation ventilation/PPE prophylaxis vaccine

R0 = transmission potential under stated susceptible conditions
Rt = transmission under current immunity, behaviour and control
BURDEN = cases + severity + hospital pressure + indirect disruption
```

*The chain identifies where to intervene; the metrics show trajectory and consequence without mechanically prescribing policy.*

### Metric-to-action briefing

Match each metric to a decision: **Rt for trajectory, attack rate for bounded exposure, hospital occupancy for surge, excess mortality for broader impact, test positivity for surveillance context**.

The chain of infection links an agent, reservoir or source, portal of exit, transmission mode, portal of entry and susceptible host. Intervention breaks one or more links: safe water and sanitation, vector control, infection prevention, isolation, ventilation, vaccination or prophylaxis.

The basic reproduction number **R0** is a model-based average number of secondary infections generated by a typical infectious person in a wholly susceptible population under stated conditions. The effective reproduction number **Rt** changes with immunity, behaviour, season and control. Values above one suggest growth under model assumptions; they are not fixed biological labels and can differ by setting.

Incidence counts new cases; prevalence counts existing cases. Attack rate describes the proportion affected in a defined outbreak population. Case-fatality ratio uses detected cases and is sensitive to testing and time lag. Hospitalisation, excess mortality and service burden complement case counts.


### REVISION NOTES

1. The chain of infection identifies intervention points.
2. R0 assumes a largely susceptible setting and stated conditions.
3. Rt changes with immunity, behaviour and controls.
4. A reproduction estimate is not a guaranteed case count.
5. Incidence counts new cases over time.
6. Prevalence counts existing cases.
7. Attack rate suits a defined exposed population.
8. Case-fatality ratios depend on detection and time lag.
9. Hospital burden complements transmission measures.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Explain why R0 alone cannot determine an epidemic response.

**Model answer:** R0 is a model-dependent average for transmission in a largely susceptible population under specified conditions. Response decisions occur under changing immunity, behaviour, variants, season and interventions, better reflected by Rt and direct observations. R0 also does not show severity, geographic concentration, vulnerable groups, hospital capacity, detection bias or essential-service disruption. Authorities should combine transmission estimates with incidence, test context, hospitalisation, mortality, laboratory and field evidence and consequence. The metric informs scenarios; it does not prescribe a universal restriction.

**Adjacent rubric:** R0 meaning 3 + limitations 4 + complementary evidence/action 3 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** R0 is a permanent property of a pathogen that directly specifies the required legal restriction.

**Transition:** Metrics become useful only through surveillance; Lesson 3 traces signals from community and laboratory to verified outbreak action.

### 🖼️ Surveillance-to-action swimlane

| Level | Signal task | Verification task | Action/feedback task |
|---|---|---|---|
| community/facility | report syndrome or unusual event | clinical history and line list | precautions and referral |
| district unit/RRT | compare baseline; remove duplicates | person–place–time, field investigation, sample | local control and reporter feedback |
| State unit | detect cross-district pattern | coordinate laboratories/resources | escalation and inter-district support |
| central/NCDC | national analysis and standards | specialist/reference support | technical guidance and cross-border interface |

```text
signal → verify → describe → sample → decide → deliver → feed back → close/escalate
```

*Surveillance succeeds only when information returns as verified action, not when it merely moves upward.*

Surveillance is continuous, systematic collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination for action. Indicator-based surveillance follows defined conditions and reporting formats; event-based surveillance captures unusual reports from clinicians, communities, media or other sectors. Neither is complete alone.

NCDC’s Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme operates decentralised surveillance units at district, State and central levels and uses suspected, presumptive and laboratory-confirmed reporting streams. Rapid Response Teams investigate signals. Weekly outbreak reports make the action chain visible, but reporting volume is not disease incidence by itself: completeness, definitions and verification matter.

Laboratory confirmation improves specificity, yet outbreak action may begin on strong epidemiological evidence before every result returns. A line list, epidemic curve, person–place–time description, exposure hypothesis and active case search guide testing and control. Data must flow back to local reporters so surveillance is not extraction without learning.

### Signal audit

Audit a signal through **who noticed → case definition → duplicate check → laboratory plan → threshold → responsible action → feedback and closure**.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Surveillance exists to support action.
2. Indicator-based and event-based systems are complementary.
3. IDSP has district, State and central surveillance units.
4. S, P and L streams represent different evidence levels.
5. Rapid Response Teams investigate outbreak signals.
6. Reporting completeness affects interpretation.
7. Laboratory confirmation is important but may lag urgent action.
8. Person–place–time analysis guides hypotheses.
9. Local feedback sustains reporting quality.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Design an early-action protocol after a district detects an unusual fever cluster.

**Model answer:** The district should verify the signal, establish a sensitive working case definition, line-list cases and describe person, place and time. Active case search and event-based information should determine whether the cluster exceeds baseline and identify common exposures. Appropriate samples must reach a biosafe laboratory with chain and quality controls. The Rapid Response Team should assess severity, transmission route, vulnerable groups and health-facility burden, while beginning proportionate measures such as infection prevention, safe water, vector control or isolation before all results arrive. State and central surveillance units receive timely updates. Risk communication should state uncertainty, and the district should document actions, revise the case definition and close or escalate the event with feedback to reporters.

**Adjacent rubric:** Verification/description 4 + investigation/lab 4 + proportionate action 4 + reporting/communication 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Surveillance succeeds when data reach a national dashboard, even if local reporters receive no action or feedback.

**Transition:** Domestic surveillance connects to cross-border obligations; Lesson 4 distinguishes PHEIC, pandemic emergency and the unfinished Pandemic Agreement.

### 🖼️ International legal-status ladder

| Instrument/status | Current position by 3 Oct 2026 | Operational meaning |
|---|---|---|
| IHR (2005), amended 2024 | amendments in force from 19 Sep 2025 | binding notification/coordination duties; pandemic-emergency tier |
| WHO Pandemic Agreement | adopted 20 May 2025 | agreed treaty text, but not yet open for signature |
| PABS annex | negotiations advanced at IGWG8, concluded 18 Sep 2026; not finalised | completion is required before signature pathway opens |
| future entry into force | sixty ratifications after signature opens | not a current binding obligation |

*Adoption, annex completion, signature, ratification and entry into force are distinct legal events.*

### Status-verification rule

Use a legal-status ladder: **negotiated → adopted → open for signature → signed → ratified → in force**. Never collapse these stages.

The International Health Regulations (2005) are the binding framework for preventing and responding to international disease spread while avoiding unnecessary interference with traffic and trade. States assess and notify events through national IHR arrangements; WHO’s Director-General determines a Public Health Emergency of International Concern using expert advice.

Amendments adopted in 2024 entered into force on **19 September 2025** and introduced a **pandemic emergency** alert for a qualifying communicable-disease threat with wide or high-risk international spread requiring coordinated action. It is a higher alert within the IHR architecture, not a synonym for every PHEIC. WHO does not acquire power to impose a national lockdown.

The WHO Pandemic Agreement was adopted on **20 May 2025**. ✅ **Latest verified status:** WHO reported on **18 September 2026** that Member States had concluded IGWG8 and were still advancing negotiations on the proposed Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing annex; WHO stated that **finalising** the annex would pave the way for countries to sign and consider ratification.[^WHOPABSSEP] Therefore, as of **3 October 2026**, the annex was not finalised and the Agreement was not yet open for signature or in force. Sixty ratifications are ultimately required after the complete package opens for signature.


### REVISION NOTES

1. IHR governs international health-event cooperation.
2. PHEIC is an IHR emergency determination.
3. The 2024 amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025.
4. The amendments created a pandemic-emergency tier.
5. Pandemic emergency is not identical to every PHEIC.
6. WHO cannot directly impose national lockdowns.
7. The Pandemic Agreement was adopted on 20 May 2025.
8. IGWG8 concluded on 18 September 2026 with the PABS annex still under negotiation.
9. The Agreement is not yet open for signature or in force.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Distinguish the amended IHR from the WHO Pandemic Agreement in current legal status and function.

**Model answer:** The amended IHR is an existing binding regime for notification, assessment and coordinated response to international health events. Amendments in force from 19 September 2025 added a pandemic-emergency alert and strengthened implementation and equity provisions. The WHO Pandemic Agreement is a separate instrument adopted on 20 May 2025 to improve pandemic prevention, preparedness and equitable access. WHO’s 18 September 2026 IGWG8 update confirms that the PABS annex was still being negotiated; as of 3 October the package was therefore not open for signature or in force, and sixty ratifications will later be required. Thus, IHR duties operate now, while the Agreement remains an adopted but incomplete treaty framework.

**Adjacent rubric:** IHR function/status 5 + Agreement function 3 + exact current status 5 + comparison 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Adoption by the World Health Assembly automatically made the Pandemic Agreement binding on India.

**Transition:** International duties require domestic authority and capacity; Lesson 5 maps India’s State, Union, health and disaster-management interfaces.

### 🖼️ Constitutional and legal authority matrix

| Source | Exact subject/function | Legal significance | Operational relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| State List health responsibility | public health/hospitals primarily implemented by States | field health action remains decentralised | facilities, surveillance and local control |
| Concurrent List Entry 23 | “Social security and social insurance; employment and unemployment” | canonical basis cited for the DM Act’s social-protection/disaster route | nationwide welfare and whole-of-government coordination |
| Concurrent List Entry 29 | “Prevention of the extension from one State to another of infectious or contagious diseases or pests affecting men, animals or plants” | Parliament and States can legislate on interstate disease spread | common reporting, interstate containment and One Health reach |
| Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 | special epidemic measures; amended in 2020 | sector-specific law remains in force | legally bounded epidemic orders |
| DM Act, 2005 | general disaster coordination | enabled pan-India COVID coordination | Union–State mobilisation and directions |

*The architecture combines State health delivery, concurrent legislative routes and exceptional disaster coordination; none erases technical public-health judgment.*

Public health and hospitals are primarily State responsibilities, but the Seventh Schedule also supplies concurrent legislative routes. ✅ **Exact constitutional text:** Concurrent List Entry 23 is **“Social security and social insurance; employment and unemployment”**; Entry 29 is **“Prevention of the extension from one State to another of infectious or contagious diseases or pests affecting men, animals or plants.”**[^CONSTITUTION] The canonical owner identifies Entry 23 as the legal basis used for the general DM Act route and Entry 29 as the disease-specific interstate route. Because Concurrent List subjects permit both Parliament and State Legislatures to legislate, national interoperability can coexist with State field responsibility. Entry 29 also makes the human–animal–plant scope legally visible rather than treating One Health as a slogan.

The **Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 remains in force**, amended in 2020, and empowers specified epidemic measures while adding protections relating to violence against healthcare personnel. It has not been replaced by a comprehensive public-health emergency law. During COVID-19, the Disaster Management Act, 2005 supplied nationwide coordination through NDMA/Union directions. This generic disaster-law route enabled scale but raises questions of proportionality, public-health calibration and federal fit.

Two named NDMA records should be used together: **National Disaster Management Guidelines—Management of Biological Disasters (July 2008)** and **National Disaster Management Guidelines—Medical Preparedness and Mass Casualty Management (October 2007)**.[^NDMABIO][^NDMAMED] The first supplies biological-hazard prevention, surveillance and coordination architecture; the second supports emergency medical planning, triage, pre-hospital/hospital coordination and surge for mass casualties. Operationally, an epidemic plan must connect disease control to ambulance, referral, hospital networking and continuity rather than assume surveillance alone saves patients. A suspected deliberate release still requires NCDC/public-health detection plus MHA/security and forensic coordination; operational CBRN depth remains Topic 12.

### Authority-to-action test

Map every proposed measure through **legal authority → technical decision-maker → implementing level → review/appeal → sunset or de-escalation trigger**.

1. Health is primarily a State responsibility.
2. Concurrent List Entry 23 is “Social security and social insurance; employment and unemployment.”
3. Concurrent List Entry 29 covers prevention of interstate extension of infectious or contagious diseases or pests affecting men, animals or plants.
4. Both Parliament and State Legislatures can legislate on Concurrent List subjects.
5. MoHFW provides national health leadership.
6. NCDC is the current institution; NICD is obsolete nomenclature.
7. IDSP supports decentralised surveillance.
8. The Epidemic Diseases Act remains in force.
9. The Act was amended in 2020.
10. The DM Act enabled nationwide COVID coordination.
11. Generic disaster powers require health calibration.
12. Deliberate biological release crosses to MHA and Topic 12.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Assess the strengths and risks of using the Disaster Management Act for a pandemic.

**Model answer:** The DM Act enables rapid nationwide coordination, common directions, logistics and whole-of-government mobilisation across Ministries and States. During a fast, uncertain pandemic this can overcome fragmented mandates. However, it is a generic disaster statute rather than a detailed public-health code. Uniform restrictions may outlast local epidemiological need, blur technical and enforcement roles, weaken federal adaptation and insufficiently specify data, compensation, review and rights safeguards. Its use should therefore be guided by MoHFW/NCDC evidence, graded State and district implementation, transparent reasons, time limits, legislative oversight and continuity support. Long-term reform should clarify purpose-built public-health emergency powers without discarding disaster coordination capacity.

**Adjacent rubric:** Coordination strengths 4 + legal/design risks 5 + safeguards 4 + reform conclusion 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Because health is a State subject, the Union has no lawful role in an inter-State epidemic.

**Transition:** Legal power cannot substitute for prevention; Lesson 6 links human, animal and environmental surveillance and AMR risk.

### 🖼️ One Health trigger triangle

```text
             HUMAN HEALTH
          cases • hospitals • AMR
             /           \
     shared signal       shared response
           /               \
ANIMAL HEALTH ---------- ENVIRONMENT
livestock/wildlife       water • vectors • waste

JOINT TRIGGER: unusual human cluster + animal illness/death or shared exposure

```

*One Health becomes operational when sectors share a trigger, investigation and decision—not when they merely attend the same meeting.*

### Joint-trigger design

For a zoonotic signal, open a joint table: **species/location/time → human exposure → laboratory ownership → immediate controls → compensation/livelihood effect → shared message**.

One Health recognises that human, domestic-animal, wildlife and environmental health are interconnected. Zoonotic preparedness requires shared event reporting, joint outbreak investigation, compatible laboratories and coordinated risk communication across health, animal husbandry, wildlife, food and environment authorities. Avian influenza illustrates why a human-only surveillance chain is late.

AMR is a slow-moving public-health emergency risk: antimicrobial use creates selection pressure, while poor infection prevention, sanitation, diagnostics and stewardship enable resistant organisms to spread. India’s **National Action Plan on AMR 2025–2029** uses a One Health approach. AMR differs from an acute epidemic but can magnify mortality, treatment failure and health-system cost during outbreaks.

One Health does not mean merging all agencies. It requires shared triggers, specimen/data protocols and exercises while preserving technical accountability. Community livelihoods, farmers and animal-health workers need communication that avoids stigma and destructive blanket culling.


### REVISION NOTES

1. One Health connects human, animal and environmental systems.
2. Many emerging infections are zoonotic.
3. Joint surveillance can detect spillover earlier.
4. Veterinary and wildlife laboratories need defined interfaces.
5. AMR is driven by selection and transmission.
6. IPC and WASH are AMR controls.
7. Diagnostics support appropriate antimicrobial use.
8. India has a NAP-AMR 2025–2029.
9. One Health coordination does not erase agency accountability.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Develop a One Health response to suspected avian-influenza spillover in a poultry district.

**Model answer:** Human and animal-health teams should share a case and event definition, map poultry mortality and exposed workers, collect properly routed specimens and conduct joint epidemiological investigation. Health facilities need syndromic surveillance, IPC, clinical referral and contact follow-up; veterinary authorities manage flock measures, farm biosecurity and movement controls under applicable law. Wildlife and environmental information may identify additional pathways. A single risk message should explain uncertainty, safe reporting and food or occupational precautions without stigmatising farmers. Compensation and livelihood support improve reporting and compliance. The review should assess data speed, laboratory turnaround, cross-sector decisions and antimicrobial stewardship rather than create one undifferentiated command.

**Adjacent rubric:** Joint surveillance/investigation 5 + human/animal controls 4 + communication/livelihood safeguards 3 + review/accountability 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** One Health means that any one participating department may direct all other technical sectors.

**Transition:** Prevention must be converted into readiness; Lesson 7 sizes plans, stockpiles and logistics against scenarios rather than headline quantities.

### 🖼️ Usable-stock equation

| Stock component | Failure if absent |
|---|---|
| correct item and quality | ineffective or unsafe product |
| unexpired quantity with rotation | nominal stock, unusable at crisis time |
| cold chain/storage and power | degraded medicine, vaccine or reagent |
| trained user and protocol | inventory cannot become care |
| transport and last-mile allocation | central abundance, local shortage |
| replenishment and consumption data | early exhaustion or hidden wastage |

**Usable stock = item × quality × storage × trained delivery × distribution × replenishment**

*If any multiplier approaches zero, the headline unit count overstates preparedness.*

Preparedness starts with scenarios: likely route, case growth, severity, geographic spread, duration and health-system baseline. Capability targets follow—surveillance staff, laboratory throughput, beds, oxygen, referral transport, infection-control supplies, medicines, vaccines where available, and risk-communication capacity.

A stockpile is a managed service, not a warehouse photograph. Items require quality standards, expiry rotation, cold chain, maintenance, trained users, distribution plans, buffer location and replenishment contracts. Central reserves can equalise shocks; decentralised caches shorten delivery. Vendor diversification and visibility across public and private inventories reduce single-source failure.

Scarcity rules must be prepared before crisis. Allocation should follow transparent clinical and public-health criteria, not political visibility. Exercises should test procurement delay, border disruption, a contaminated batch, power loss and simultaneous district demand.

### Logistics stress test

Calculate **days of cover = usable stock ÷ credible daily burn rate**, then stress-test lead time, wastage and surge. Nominal units without consumption assumptions mislead.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Preparedness is scenario-based.
2. Capability targets precede procurement quantities.
3. Stockpiles require expiry rotation.
4. Cold-chain and maintenance are operational requirements.
5. Trained users are part of equipment readiness.
6. Central and decentralised reserves serve different purposes.
7. Vendor diversification reduces supply failure.
8. Allocation rules should be pre-agreed.
9. Exercises must test logistics disruption.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) How should India design health-emergency stockpiles without repeating over-purchase and shortage cycles?

**Model answer:** MoHFW and States should derive stocks from transparent scenarios covering disease route, severity, duration, regional spread, baseline capacity and supply lead time. Inventories should include appropriate PPE, diagnostics, medicines, oxygen-related consumables and vaccines where available, but every item needs quality assurance, trained users, maintenance, expiry rotation and cold-chain support. A layered system should combine strategic national reserves with State and district caches and diversified framework contracts. Digital visibility can track usable—not nominal—stock and consumption. Pre-agreed equitable allocation, emergency procurement safeguards, exercises and post-use replenishment are essential. Independent audit should examine availability at point of care, wastage and delivery time rather than procurement value alone.

**Adjacent rubric:** Scenario/capability logic 4 + lifecycle logistics 5 + layered allocation 3 + audit/outcome 3 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** A larger central warehouse automatically guarantees timely district availability and correct clinical use.

```text
LISTEN to questions/rumours
        ↓

        ↓
```

Trust depends on fairness. Advice to isolate is ineffective where workers lose wages or families lack space, food and care. 2. Local conditions justify calibrated implementation.
3. States cannot withhold cross-border outbreak information.
4. Central coordination should remain technically reasoned.
5. Common procurement can improve equity.
6. Restrictions need legality and evidence.
7. Support and remedy increase compliance.
8. Tightening and relaxation need published indicators.
9. Emergency measures should not become permanent by default.

### Concept check

**Question:** (20 marks) Reconcile Union coordination, State responsibility and civil liberties during a multi-State epidemic.

**Model answer:** The Union should set common case definitions, reporting and laboratory standards, product quality, interstate information rules, IHR coordination and equitable resource support. States retain operational leadership over health facilities, field control and locally suitable measures; districts calibrate by clusters, exposure and capacity. A standing coordination mechanism should publish evidence, minimum requirements, adaptation space and a rapid dispute route. Restrictions must be lawful, necessary, proportionate, least restrictive, time-bound and reviewable, with food, income, care and grievance support. Privacy and non-discrimination apply to surveillance and digital tools. Transparent indicators should govern escalation and relaxation. Thus, federalism supplies both national interoperability and local intelligence, while rights safeguards improve rather than obstruct effective public health.

**Adjacent rubric:** Union functions 5 + State/district functions 4 + coordination design 4 + rights/support 5 + synthesis 2 = **20 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Civil-liberty safeguards are obstacles that should be suspended whenever national coordination is invoked.

### 🖼️ Evaluation scorecard

| Results rung | Example measure | False success to reject |
|---|---|---|
| capability | trained RRT, quality lab, staffed bed, usable stock | facility or unit count |
| reliability/speed | reporting completeness, signal-to-action, turnaround | fast but inaccurate decision |
| delivery quality | IPC, supported isolation, communication understanding | intervention announced |
| outcome/continuity | severe illness, excess mortality, routine-care disruption | case count alone |
| equity/governance | disaggregated access, privacy, grievance, legal sunset | aggregate reach |
| learning | exercise/AAR finding closed | report published without correction |

*Preparedness is credible when resources repeatedly become safe, equitable action and measurable recovery.*

### Dashboard rejection rule

Reject any dashboard that lacks at least one indicator for **field action, essential-service continuity, equity, worker safety and corrective closure**.

Evaluation begins with a results chain, not counts of laboratories, apps or stockpiled units. Capability indicators include reporting completeness, laboratory quality, trained RRT availability, usable stock and staffed beds. Reliability and speed include signal-to-verification, sample turnaround, decision and delivery times.

Use and quality indicators test whether warnings, isolation support, vaccination, IPC and risk communication reached intended groups. Outcomes include severe illness, excess mortality, worker infection, disruption and recovery, adjusted for pathogen and baseline differences. Essential-service continuity and indirect harm belong beside outbreak control.

Equity audits compare access and outcomes by geography, gender, disability, migrant or livelihood status. Trust, grievance resolution, privacy incidents and legal de-escalation are measurable governance outcomes. After-action review should publish misses, false alarms and corrective closure. Exercises and comparable districts support learning where major emergencies are infrequent.


### REVISION NOTES

1. Inputs and outputs are not outcomes.
2. Laboratory count does not prove quality or turnaround.
3. Stock must be usable at point of care.
4. Signal-to-action time measures system integration.
5. Excess mortality captures broader impact.
6. Essential-service disruption is an outcome.
7. Equity analysis requires disaggregation.
8. Privacy and grievance are governance indicators.
9. Corrective actions need verified closure.

### Concept check

**Question:** (20 marks) Develop an evaluation framework for India’s epidemic preparedness and response system.

**Model answer:** Map resources—IDSP reporting, laboratories, RRTs, workforce, stockpiles and coordination—to verified action, proportionate control, care continuity and equitable outcomes. Capability indicators should cover reporting completeness, lab quality, trained team availability, usable stock and staffed surge capacity. Measure signal-to-verification, sample turnaround, decision, dispatch and treatment time. Quality indicators include IPC, worker infection, communication understanding, supported isolation and vaccination reach. Outcomes include severe illness, excess mortality, essential-service disruption, livelihood harm and recovery, adjusted for pathogen and baseline. Disaggregate access and outcomes for remote, migrant, low-income and disabled groups. Audit privacy, grievances, legal review and emergency-power sunset. Exercises, after-action reviews and corrective-action closure should complement rare-event outcome comparison.

## Check 1

**Prompt (10 marks):** Distinguish outbreak, epidemic, pandemic and public-health emergency.

**Responsive model:** Outbreak and epidemic compare observed disease with an expected baseline at different spatial scales; pandemic describes sustained international spread; public-health emergency is a governance judgment based on urgency, consequence, uncertainty and coordination need. ## Check 2

**Prompt (10 marks):** Explain why Rt and hospital burden should accompany R0.

**Responsive model:** R0 estimates transmission in stated susceptible conditions; Rt changes with immunity, behaviour and control. Hospital burden shows consequence and system capacity. **Adjacent scoring logic:** R0/Rt distinction 4 + burden relevance 3 + decision qualification 3 = **10 marks**

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark practice

**Question:** Why must epidemic surveillance be judged by action rather than data volume? **(10 marks; answer in no more than 150 words.)**

**Model answer (106 words):** Large reporting volumes can coexist with incomplete coverage, duplicate alerts, delayed verification or no local response. Surveillance creates value when a signal is checked against baseline, converted into a case definition and line list, linked to appropriate laboratory testing and followed by proportionate control. IDSP’s district, State and central structure therefore needs feedback, Rapid Response Team action and closure records, not only dashboards. Performance should measure reporting completeness, signal-to-verification time, laboratory turnaround, decision and delivery time and whether the intervention reduced exposure or protected care. Community and event-based reports also need acknowledgment. Data volume is an input; timely, accurate and equitable action is the public-health outcome.

**Adjacent rubric:** Data-volume limitation 3 + action chain 4 + performance metrics 3 = **10 marks**

## Original 15-mark practice

**Question:** Examine preparedness as a health-system and social-capability problem, not merely a stockpile problem. **(15 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (133 words):** Stockpiles matter only when based on scenarios and supported by quality, rotation, cold chain, distribution, trained users and replenishment. Epidemic preparedness also requires surveillance, laboratory networks, RRTs, IPC, protected health workers, surge beds with oxygen and staff, referral and resilient data systems. Routine maternal, tuberculosis, dialysis, immunisation and chronic care need protected pathways. Social capability is equally important: risk communication, trusted community institutions, food, wage, shelter and medicine support make isolation or quarantine feasible. One Health interfaces detect zoonotic risk, while public and private facilities require common standards. Exercises should test supply disruption, staff illness, misinformation and simultaneous district demand. Evaluation must cover signal-to-action time, usable stock at point of care, worker safety, service continuity, equity and corrective learning. Preparedness is therefore a maintained network of institutions and trust, not an inventory photograph.

**Adjacent rubric:** Stockpile qualification 4 + health-system capability 5 + social/One Health capability 4 + evaluation conclusion 2 = **15 marks**

## Original 20-mark practice

**Question:** India’s epidemic governance needs both stronger legal clarity and stronger local public-health capacity. Analyse. **(20 marks; answer in no more than 250 words.)**

**Model answer (170 words):** India combines State responsibility for health, Union technical leadership through MoHFW/NCDC, the continuing Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 and exceptional nationwide coordination under the Disaster Management Act. COVID-19 showed the value of rapid common directions and resource mobilisation, but also the risks of using generic disaster powers for a crisis requiring epidemiological calibration, rights safeguards, review and service continuity. A purpose-built framework should clarify surveillance and data duties, graded measures, Union–State coordination, compensation, privacy, review and sunset without weakening emergency logistics.

Law alone cannot supply district epidemiologists, laboratories, RRTs, IPC, protected staff, oxygen, referral, risk communication or trusted community support. IDSP signals must reach field action; One Health interfaces must connect animal and human events; stockpiles must be usable locally. Migrants and informal workers need portable support so control measures are feasible.

National minimum standards and interstate information should coexist with locally adapted action. Accountability should publish signal-to-verification time, essential-service disruption, worker safety, equity, excess mortality and corrective closure. Legal clarity creates legitimate authority; local capacity converts it into proportionate protection.

**Adjacent rubric:** Existing architecture 4 + legal strengths/gaps 5 + local capacity 5 + federal/equity design 4 + conclusion 2 = **20 marks**

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Concepts and measures

- Outbreak/epidemic compares observed disease with expected baseline; pandemic describes sustained international spread.
- Public-health emergency adds urgency, severity, uncertainty, system pressure and exceptional coordination need.
- Chain of infection: agent, source/reservoir, exit, mode, entry and susceptible host.
- R0 is model-based under susceptible conditions; Rt changes with immunity, behaviour and controls.
- Use incidence, prevalence, attack rate, hospitalisation, excess mortality and service burden with case counts.

## 2. Surveillance and early action

- Surveillance is continuous collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination for action.
- Indicator-based and event-based surveillance are complementary.
- IDSP links district, State and central units; S, P and L streams represent suspected, presumptive and laboratory evidence.
- Rapid Response Teams verify signals, line-list cases, describe person–place–time and initiate control.
- Laboratory confirmation improves specificity, but urgent action need not await every result.

## 3. Global framework

- IHR is binding international law for health-event notification and coordinated response.
- Amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025 and added a pandemic-emergency alert.
- WHO does not directly impose national lockdowns.
- Pandemic Agreement adopted 20 May 2025; WHO’s 18 September 2026 IGWG8 update confirms the PABS annex remained under negotiation, so as of 3 October it was not open for signature or in force.
- Distinguish adoption, signature, ratification and entry into force.

## 4. Indian architecture

- Health is primarily a State responsibility; MoHFW/DGHS/NCDC lead national policy and technical coordination.
- Concurrent List Entry 23 is “Social security and social insurance; employment and unemployment”; the canonical owner identifies it with the DM Act’s general coordination route.
- Concurrent List Entry 29 is “Prevention of the extension from one State to another of infectious or contagious diseases or pests affecting men, animals or plants”; it is the direct interstate disease-spread route.
- Concurrent List subjects permit both Parliament and State Legislatures to legislate, while implementation capacity remains substantially State/local.
- Use NCDC, not the historical NICD name.
- Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 remains in force and was amended in 2020.
- DM Act, 2005 enabled nationwide COVID coordination but is a generic disaster framework.
- NDMA’s **Management of Biological Disasters (July 2008)** and **Medical Preparedness and Mass Casualty Management (October 2007)** guidelines jointly connect disease control with triage, emergency medical services, hospital coordination and surge.
- Deliberate release requires public-health detection plus security/CBRN coordination owned in depth by Topic 12.

## 5. One Health and AMR

- One Health connects human, animal, wildlife and environmental surveillance and response.
- Joint triggers, laboratories and messages are essential for zoonotic spillover.
- India’s NAP-AMR 2025–2029 uses a One Health approach.
- AMR control combines stewardship, diagnostics, IPC, WASH, surveillance and cross-sector action.

## 6. Preparedness and surge

- Plan from scenarios, not procurement targets.
- Stockpiles need quality, expiry rotation, cold chain, trained users, distribution and replenishment.
- Laboratory surge requires validated assays, biosafety, quality assurance and sample transport.
- Clinical surge combines space, staff, supplies and systems.
- Protect healthcare workers and maintain maternal, TB, dialysis, immunisation and chronic services.

## 7. Communication, containment and ethics

- Risk communication states facts, uncertainty, feasible action, support and next update.
- Isolation applies to infected/suspected persons; quarantine applies to exposed persons.
- Controls must be lawful, necessary, proportionate, least restrictive, supported, time-bound and reviewable.
- Digital systems need purpose limitation, privacy, security, offline access, grievance and sunset.
- Vaccination requires equitable prioritisation, cold chain, safety surveillance and confidence.

## 8. Equity, continuity and recovery

- Migrants and informal workers need portable food, cash, shelter, transport and wage support.
- Continuity plans protect water, sanitation, power, transport, supply chains and essential workers.
- Recovery addresses deferred care, long-term symptoms, mental health, bereavement, education and livelihoods.
- After-action reviews need frontline/community evidence, funded corrective owners and deadlines.
- Resilience maintains essential functions, adapts and learns; it is not zero disruption.

## 9. Advanced evaluation

- Evaluate capability, reliability, speed, use, safety, equity, continuity, outcome and legal de-escalation.
- Tests, apps, laboratories and stock units are inputs or outputs, not proof of resilience.
- Disaggregate outcomes by geography, gender, disability, migration and income.
- Track privacy incidents, grievances, indirect mortality and corrective closure.
