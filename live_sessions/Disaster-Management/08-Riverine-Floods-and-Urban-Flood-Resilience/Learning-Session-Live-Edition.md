### 🖼️ Flood Hydrology, Hazard and Risk — visual

```text
PRECIPITATION → interception / infiltration / storage
                         ↓ excess
                     RUNOFF → CHANNEL FLOW
                                   ↓
HAZARD × EXPOSURE × VULNERABILITY ÷ CAPACITY = FLOOD RISK
```

A flood occurs when water exceeds the carrying, storage or drainage capacity of a river, floodplain or urban system and inundates normally dry land. The hydrograph links rainfall or upstream inflow to discharge over time: antecedent soil moisture, catchment size and slope, infiltration, storage, channel capacity and travel time shape the peak and duration.

**Riverine flooding** usually develops as catchment runoff raises river stage and spreads onto the floodplain. **Pluvial flooding** arises when intense rain exceeds local infiltration or drainage, even away from a river. Flash flooding is rapid and short-lead-time. Coastal inundation from cyclone surge is cross-linked to Topic 07 rather than retaught here.

Risk is not “high water” alone. Settlement on floodplains, unsafe housing, livelihoods, critical systems, warning access and recovery capacity decide loss. A floodplain also performs natural storage, sediment and ecological functions; eliminating every inundation is neither feasible nor desirable.

### Hydrograph reading method

Read flood causation as **input → storage/infiltration → runoff synchronisation → channel/drain capacity → exposed people/assets → protective capacity**. This gives a causal answer instead of a rainfall list.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Flood is inundation when water exceeds channel, storage or drainage capacity.
2. Discharge is flow volume per unit time; stage is water level at a gauge.
3. A hydrograph shows discharge or stage through time.
4. Antecedent soil moisture changes how much rainfall becomes runoff.
5. Catchment slope, size and storage influence peak and travel time.
6. Riverine and pluvial flooding are distinct processes.
7. Flash floods have rapid onset and short action windows.
8. Floodplains store water and support ecological functions.
9. Risk combines hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Distinguish riverine, pluvial and flash flooding and explain why rainfall alone does not determine disaster loss.

**Model answer:** Riverine flooding develops when catchment runoff raises river stage beyond channel capacity and water occupies the floodplain. Pluvial flooding occurs when intense rain exceeds local infiltration or drainage, including away from rivers. Flash flooding is defined by rapid onset and short lead time and may arise from concentrated rain or sudden flow. Rainfall alone does not determine loss: antecedent moisture, storage, slope, channel or drain capacity, exposure, building and livelihood vulnerability, warning access and response capacity shape consequences. Management must therefore connect hydrology with land use and preparedness.

**Adjacent rubric:** Definitions 4 + hydrological modifiers 3 + risk factors 3 = **10 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Every flood in a city is an urban pluvial flood, and every river rise is a flash flood.

**Transition:** The hydrological vocabulary now supports Lesson 2’s India-specific river drivers and regional contrasts.

### 🖼️ Riverine Drivers, Sediment and Indian Regions — visual

```text
HEAVY CATCHMENT RAIN / SNOWMELT / SATURATED SOIL
        + erosion, landslide blockage, sediment/aggradation
        + channel migration and constrained drainage
        → rising stage → overbank flow → prolonged inundation
```

Riverine floods result from interacting meteorological, catchment and channel processes. Heavy or persistent rainfall, saturated soils and synchronised tributary peaks increase inflow. Erosion and landslides supply sediment; aggradation can reduce effective channel capacity; meandering and channel migration alter exposure. Roads, railways, canals and bridges may obstruct natural drainage when cross-drainage is inadequate.

India’s **Brahmaputra region** combines intense monsoon rain, fragile-hill erosion, sediment and channel instability. The **Ganga plain** combines large tributary systems, low-gradient floodplains and extensive encroachment. North-West flood problems often involve drainage congestion, while central and Deccan rivers are generally less extensive but include flood-prone east-coast basins and deltas.

These are broad regional patterns, not deterministic labels. Urbanisation, embankments, reservoirs and changing rainfall can alter local risk. Detailed geomorphology belongs to Geography; disaster management asks how the processes change exposure, warning and intervention.

### Regional comparison frame

| Region | Dominant flood concern | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Brahmaputra | rain, sediment, erosion, unstable channels | basin data, erosion-sensitive land use |
| Ganga plain | tributary synchronisation, low-gradient floodplain occupation | zoning, storage, cross-State coordination |
| North-West | drainage congestion and local river risk | drainage/cross-drainage maintenance |
| East-coast basins/deltas | intense rain, river and tidal interaction | compound planning; cyclone boundary |

### REVISION NOTES

1. Persistent catchment rain and saturated soil increase river inflow.
2. Tributary synchronisation can sharpen the downstream peak.
3. Sediment aggradation may reduce effective channel capacity.
4. Landslides can obstruct rivers and create rapid downstream risk.
5. Road and rail embankments need adequate cross-drainage.
6. Brahmaputra risk includes sediment, erosion and channel instability.
7. Ganga risk includes low-gradient floodplains and dense occupation.
8. Regional labels do not replace local basin evidence.
9. Disaster answers link physical drivers to exposure and management.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Why do riverine-flood strategies need basin-specific diagnosis rather than a uniform national template?

**Model answer:** Basins differ in rainfall regime, slope, sediment, channel stability, tributary timing, floodplain width, drainage obstruction, settlement and institutional boundaries. The Brahmaputra combines intense monsoon inflow, erosion, sediment and unstable channels; the Ganga plain adds low gradients, tributary synchronisation and dense floodplain occupation; some North-West problems are dominated by drainage congestion; east-coast basins may face river–tidal interaction. Therefore forecasting networks, zoning, embankment policy, catchment treatment, evacuation thresholds and inter-State coordination must fit basin processes. A national framework can set standards, but local hydro-meteorological evidence and exposure determine action.

**Adjacent rubric:** Basin differences 6 + Indian applications 4 + policy implications 4 + qualification 1 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** All Indian basins experience identical flood causes and require the same structural works.

**Transition:** Basin diagnosis explains where water moves; Lesson 3 asks why society repeatedly places vulnerable uses in its path.

### 🖼️ Floodplains, Exposure and Risk-Sensitive Land Use — visual

```text
RIVER CHANNEL | ACTIVE FLOODPLAIN | HIGHER TERRACE
frequent flow      storage/conveyance      lower-frequency exposure
       X dense housing / hospitals / landfill / blocked flow path
       → risk transfer upstream, downstream and across groups
```

A floodplain is part of the river system, not vacant land awaiting construction. It stores and conveys floodwater, supports wetlands and groundwater interaction, and changes with erosion and sediment. **Floodplain zoning** maps expected inundation and regulates uses by risk: essential and high-occupancy facilities require the strictest siting; compatible open space, agriculture or seasonal uses may remain where appropriate.

Zoning is politically difficult because urban land and housing demand, informal tenure and legacy settlements are real constraints. Sudden eviction can transfer risk to poorer households. A credible approach combines current maps, disclosure, development controls, no-new-risk rules, safe incremental upgrading, voluntary and compensated relocation where unavoidable, and protection of evacuation routes and critical facilities.

Flood-proofing—raised platforms, elevated water points, protected utilities or upper-floor refuge—reduces residual risk but does not legitimise every location. Maps are scenarios, not guarantees; development control and public access determine their value.

### Land-use decision ladder

**Avoid new exposure → restrict high-consequence uses → preserve conveyance/storage → flood-proof compatible uses → upgrade or relocate existing high-risk settlements with rights safeguards.**

### REVISION NOTES

1. Floodplains are storage and conveyance spaces within the river system.
2. Zoning regulates use by probability, depth, velocity and consequence.
3. Hospitals and emergency facilities require conservative siting.
4. No-new-risk controls are easier than retrofitting dense exposure later.
5. Flood-proofing reduces residual damage but does not erase hazard.
6. Encroachment can transfer water and risk elsewhere.
7. Current maps and public disclosure support enforcement.
8. Informal residents need participation, tenure and compensation safeguards.
9. Evacuation routes and open storage areas must remain functional.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Critically examine floodplain zoning as a risk-reduction instrument in India.

**Model answer:** Floodplain zoning can prevent new exposure, preserve river conveyance and storage, guide siting of critical facilities and support insurance, warning and evacuation. Its effectiveness depends on current inundation maps, legal incorporation into development plans, public disclosure and enforcement across jurisdictions. Constraints include uncertain maps, legacy development, land scarcity, informal tenure and political pressure. Blanket eviction may transfer risk to low-income households. Reform should prioritise no-new-risk rules, protect high-consequence sites and wetlands, flood-proof compatible uses, upgrade existing settlements and use participatory, compensated relocation only where residual risk is intolerable. Zoning is therefore a governance process, not merely a coloured map.

**Adjacent rubric:** Benefits 4 + implementation constraints 4 + equity 3 + reforms/verdict 4 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Drawing a floodplain map automatically removes existing exposure and guarantees enforcement.

**Transition:** Land-use reduction works over years; Lesson 4 addresses the operational hours-to-days window through forecasts and thresholds.

### 🖼️ CWC Forecasting, Thresholds and Warning-to-Action — visual

```text
IMD RAINFALL + CWC GAUGE/DISCHARGE + RESERVOIR/UPSTREAM DATA
              → MODEL / FORECAST → SITE-SPECIFIC THRESHOLDS
WARNING (≈1 m below Danger, normally) → DANGER → HFL
      YELLOW / ABOVE NORMAL → ORANGE / SEVERE → RED / EXTREME
```

CWC field formations observe hydro-meteorological data while IMD supplies rainfall information; upstream levels, discharge, reservoir inflow and model guidance support forecasts. CWC’s direct **April 2026 SOP** lists **350 forecasting stations: 200 level-forecast and 150 inflow-forecast stations**.

CWC uses site-specific **Warning Level, Danger Level and Highest Flood Level (HFL)**. Warning Level is normally about one metre below Danger Level but is fixed locally; HFL is the highest previously recorded level at that site. Operational categories associate Warning-to-Danger with Above Normal/Yellow, Danger-to-HFL with Severe/Orange, and above HFL with Extreme/Red. These are gauge thresholds, not one national elevation.

A forecast is useful only if translated into action: likely area/depth and timing, affected routes and groups, evacuation or asset-protection trigger, responsible authority and update frequency. False-alarm concern cannot justify waiting until water crosses Danger Level when evacuation time is longer than the remaining lead time.

### Threshold-to-action card

| Information | Decision example |
|---|---|
| rising toward Warning | inspect embankments, alert institutions, ready shelters |
| forecast crossing Danger | evacuate mapped pockets, close routes, protect utilities |
| approaching/exceeding HFL | expand exclusion, mutual aid, continuity mode |
| falling stage | retain caution for drainage, contamination and damaged structures |

### REVISION NOTES

1. CWC and IMD data serve different but connected forecast functions.
2. The April 2026 SOP lists 350 forecast stations.
3. The split is 200 level-forecast and 150 inflow-forecast stations.
4. Warning, Danger and HFL are site-specific gauge thresholds.
5. Warning is normally about one metre below Danger, not universally.
6. HFL is the highest previously recorded level at the site.
7. Yellow, Orange and Red communicate escalating severity/action.
8. Forecast lead time must be compared with evacuation time.
9. Warnings need area, impact, instruction, owner and update time.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Explain how CWC river-stage thresholds should be converted into local protective action.

**Model answer:** CWC’s Warning, Danger and Highest Flood Level thresholds are fixed for each gauge; Warning is normally about one metre below Danger, while HFL is the highest recorded level there. Authorities should link forecasts, not only observed crossing, to pre-agreed actions. A rise toward Warning triggers readiness, embankment checks, institutional alerts and shelter preparation. Forecast crossing of Danger triggers evacuation of mapped pockets, route closure and utility protection. Approaching or exceeding HFL requires expanded exclusion, mutual aid and critical-service continuity. Messages must state area, likely timing and depth, action and update. Triggers should account for travel and evacuation time so action does not wait for certainty.

**Adjacent rubric:** Threshold meaning 4 + staged actions 6 + message/lead-time logic 4 + qualification 1 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Warning Level is a universal national height and action should begin only after observed Danger crossing.

**Transition:** Forecasting identifies expected inflow; Lesson 5 examines how reservoir operators and downstream authorities coordinate within that evidence window.

### 🖼️ Reservoir Operations and Inter-Agency Coordination — visual
```text
FORECAST INFLOW → RULE CURVE / CURRENT STORAGE / DAM SAFETY LIMIT
       → HOLD, PRE-RELEASE OR CONTROLLED RELEASE DECISION
       → DOWNSTREAM INUNDATION FORECAST → WARNING → STAGGERED ACTION
Dam structural integrity and failure analysis → dedicated dam-safety topic
```

Reservoirs can moderate some flood peaks when storage is available and operations are coordinated, but they cannot abolish extreme basin flood risk. Decisions depend on current storage, forecast inflow, rule curves, spillway and structural limits, downstream channel condition, concurrent tributary flow and uncertainty. Prolonged conservation storage may reduce later flood cushion; abrupt releases can compound downstream exposure.

Coordination must connect dam operators, CWC, IMD, State water-resources departments, EOCs, district administrations and downstream communities. Release schedules need scenario-based downstream inundation, travel time, clear warning and staged evacuation or asset protection. Operators should document why a decision was reasonable with the information then available.

This lesson owns **flood-operation coordination**, not dam design, surveillance, structural failure or the 2023 dam-failure PYQ, which belong to the dedicated dam-safety topic.

### Release-decision test

Ask five questions: **What inflow range? What usable storage? What downstream tributary/tide condition? What release path and travel time? Who warns and acts?** A discharge number without downstream consequence is incomplete.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Reservoirs can attenuate peaks only when usable storage exists.
2. Rule curves balance conservation, flood cushion and safety.
3. Forecast inflow carries uncertainty and should use scenarios.
4. Concurrent tributary peaks can worsen downstream consequences.
5. Pre-release may create space but requires justified timing and warning.
6. Abrupt release can transfer risk downstream.
7. Operators, forecasters, EOCs and districts need a common protocol.
8. Downstream travel time must inform warning and evacuation.
9. Dam structural safety and failure analysis remain a separate owner.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) How should reservoir-operation decisions be integrated with downstream flood-risk management?

**Model answer:** Operators should combine forecast inflow ranges with current storage, rule curves, spillway and safety constraints, concurrent tributary flows and downstream channel capacity. Alternatives—holding, staged pre-release or controlled release—must be tested through downstream inundation and travel-time scenarios. CWC, IMD, State water agencies, EOCs and districts need a shared operating picture and pre-agreed communication protocol. Warnings should state release timing, expected affected areas and protective action before water arrives. Decisions and assumptions should be logged for review. Reservoirs reduce selected peaks but cannot eliminate extreme basin risk, and operational coordination must not be confused with structural dam-safety assessment.

**Adjacent rubric:** Decision inputs 5 + downstream modelling 4 + coordination/warning 4 + limits/boundary 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Every reservoir necessarily prevents floods, or every downstream flood proves operator negligence.

**Transition:** Reservoirs are one structural tool; Lesson 6 compares them with embankments, diversions, catchment treatment and natural storage.

### 🖼️ Embankments, Diversions, Catchment and Nature-Based Measures — visual
```text
STRUCTURAL: embankment | diversion | drainage/channel works | reservoir
NATURE-BASED: wetland | floodplain storage | afforestation | soil retention
             ↓ combine with
ZONING + FORECAST + MAINTENANCE + SAFE FAILURE PLANNING
```

Embankments can reduce frequent inundation in protected areas but may constrict conveyance, raise water levels, impede local drainage, encourage settlement behind them and shift risk downstream. Breach or overtopping creates concentrated danger. Channel improvement or desilting may be useful at diagnosed bottlenecks, but indiscriminate dredging is not a basin strategy and can disturb morphology.

Diversion channels, drainage improvement and reservoirs have context-specific roles. Catchment treatment—soil and water conservation, slope stabilisation, vegetation and erosion control—can slow runoff and sediment delivery but cannot “stop” exceptional rainfall.

Nature-based measures preserve wetlands, reconnect floodplains, restore riparian buffers and create room for rivers. They provide storage and ecological benefits but require land, maintenance and realistic performance expectations. A portfolio combines structural protection, natural storage, zoning, warning and preparedness; source material itself records that Damodar-basin dams could not fully control floods.

### Structural-measure audit

For every structure identify **design event, maintenance owner, residual risk, failure mode, induced development and transferred risk**. Then pair it with a non-structural or ecosystem measure.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Embankments can reduce frequent inundation but retain residual risk.
2. Confinement may increase stage or transfer risk.
3. Settlement behind protection can create a levee effect.
4. Breach and overtopping need warning and evacuation plans.
5. Dredging should target diagnosed constraints, not serve as a slogan.
6. Catchment treatment can reduce runoff speed and sediment delivery.
7. Wetlands and floodplains provide storage and conveyance.
8. Nature-based measures need space, maintenance and performance limits.
9. A diversified portfolio is safer than single-structure dependence.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) Critically assess embankments and nature-based measures in riverine-flood management.

**Model answer:** Embankments can protect developed areas from frequent floods and support evacuation time, but they may constrict flow, impede local drainage, transfer risk, encourage dense settlement and create severe breach or overtopping consequences. They require inspection, maintenance and residual-risk communication. Nature-based measures—wetland and floodplain restoration, riparian buffers and catchment soil conservation—store or slow water and provide ecological benefits, but need land, basin continuity and realistic limits during extremes. Neither is universally superior. Risk-informed management combines selective structures with natural storage, zoning, forecasting, safe-failure planning and preparedness, while auditing who receives protection and who receives transferred water.

**Adjacent rubric:** Embankment benefits 3 + limits 4 + nature-based role/limits 4 + integrated verdict 4 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** An embankment removes flood risk, while a wetland can absorb any flood without limits.

**Transition:** The basin portfolio now shifts to the city, where sealed surfaces and drainage produce a faster, distinct runoff system.

### 🖼️ Urban Flooding as Distinct Catchment Hydrology — visual
```text
RURAL: rain → infiltration/storage → slower, lower runoff
URBAN: rain → roofs/roads → rapid connected runoff
                     + lost storage + drain bottleneck
                     → peak 1.8–8× | volume up to 6× (owner evidence)
```

Urban flooding is not merely river flooding located in a city. Impervious roofs and roads reduce infiltration, storm-drain networks synchronise runoff, natural channels and wetlands lose storage, and dense exposure converts minutes of delay into severe disruption. The canonical evidence states that developed catchments can produce flood peaks **1.8–8 times** and volumes **up to six times** those of comparable natural catchments; these are source-period technical factors, not universal ratios for every city.

Drivers include intense rainfall, blocked or undersized drains, disconnected inlets, floodplain/wetland occupation, altered watercourses, upstream inflow or releases and, in coastal cities, tidal impedance. Climate change can alter rainfall risk, but attributing an individual flood requires event-specific science; governance-created exposure and drainage failure must not be hidden behind the climate label.

A ward-scale diagnosis should reconstruct the actual flow path: where rainfall becomes runoff, which inlets receive it, where conveyance or storage is lost, how river or tide levels affect the outfall, and which people or services occupy the resulting inundation zone. This prevents a single-cause diagnosis and supports targeted maintenance, storage, land-use and continuity decisions.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Urban flooding is hydrologically and institutionally distinct from riverine flooding.
2. Imperviousness reduces infiltration and accelerates runoff.
3. Connected drains can synchronise and sharpen the peak.
4. Owner evidence gives 1.8–8× peaks and up to 6× volumes.
5. Blocked inlets and solid waste reduce effective drainage.
6. Wetland and waterway loss removes storage and flow paths.
7. Tidal impedance can delay discharge in coastal cities.
8. Event-specific climate attribution should not be assumed.
9. Ward diagnosis should trace runoff, inlet, storage, outfall and exposed-service failures.

### Concept check

**Question:** (15 marks) A neighbourhood floods repeatedly during moderate rain even though a new trunk drain has spare design capacity. Diagnose the likely ward-scale failures and propose a verification plan.

**Model answer:** Spare trunk capacity does not prove that runoff can reach or leave it. Investigators should map roof and road flow paths; inspect blocked, disconnected or poorly graded inlets and secondary drains; test whether encroachments or utility crossings constrict links; and compare drain levels with river or tidal outfall levels. Lost ponds, wetlands or open ground may have removed detention, while road embankments can redirect water toward low-lying homes and facilities. Verification should combine timed rainfall, inlet and drain-level observations, topographic survey, maintenance records and resident reports. Short trials can clear selected inlets or restore a storage pocket and compare depth and drainage time. The remedy should follow the demonstrated bottleneck rather than automatically enlarging the trunk drain.

**Adjacent rubric:** Network diagnosis 5 + storage/outfall interaction 3 + verification evidence 5 + targeted-remedy conclusion 2 = **15 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** Rated spare capacity in the trunk drain proves that the entire ward drainage network is functioning.

**Transition:** The ward diagnosis identifies where conveyance or storage fails; Lesson 8 examines drains, wetlands, encroachment and solid waste in detail.

A sponge-city approach manages rainfall near where it falls through infiltration, detention, storage, reuse and safe conveyance. **Blue infrastructure** includes rivers, lakes, wetlands and detention ponds; **green infrastructure** includes rain gardens, bioswales, vegetated roofs, permeable landscapes and urban forests. 5. Parks and streets can provide planned exceedance storage.
6. Blue-green systems complement rather than abolish grey drainage.

Test forecast-to-warning, evacuation, hospital, power, water and pump performance. Compare drains, storage, wetland restoration, zoning and continuity options by avoided loss, downtime, equity, co-benefits and lifecycle maintenance. Publish assumptions, thresholds and responsible owners, then retest after implementation or the next flood.

**Adjacent rubric:** Decision/events 4 + coupled risk model 6 + validation 3 + response test 3 + appraisal/accountability 4 = **20 marks**

**Misconception to avoid:** The most technically complex model is automatically the most useful scenario.

**Transition:** Scenario outputs require institutional evaluation; Lesson 16 separates sanctioned finance and asset counts from verified resilience outcomes.

Evaluation separates money and infrastructure from hydraulic and social performance. Drain length, forecast count, pumps purchased or wetland area notified are inputs/outputs. Performance asks whether storage, conveyance, warning, evacuation and service continuity work under a design or actual event. Outcomes include reduced depth/duration, avoided exposure, restored services, health, livelihoods and distribution.

The **Urban Flood Risk Management Programme (UFRMP)** is a current mitigation-finance anchor. The direct PIB approval record, **PRID 2173811**, dated **1 October 2025**, states that Phase 2 covers **11 cities** with a total outlay of **₹2,444.42 crore** from the NDMF and a **90% Centre–10% State** cost-sharing pattern. Approval is not evidence of completed assets or reduced loss.

Evaluation should publish baseline and post-project risk, model assumptions, maintenance readiness, procurement quality, social safeguards and independent event performance. It must detect maladaptation: barriers transferring water, pumps without power, wetlands “restored” without connectivity, or protection inducing denser exposure.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10-marker
**Question:** Explain why flood forecasting is an institutional action chain rather than a technical product. **(≤150 words.)**
**Model answer (107 words):** A forecast estimates future river stage, inflow or inundation from rainfall, gauges, upstream conditions and models; it does not itself protect people. CWC and IMD information must reach State and district EOCs, reservoir operators, ULBs, utilities and communities in time. Site-specific Warning, Danger and HFL thresholds require pre-agreed actions: inspections and readiness, route closure and evacuation, utility protection and mutual aid. Messages need affected area, timing, likely impact, instruction and update. Lead time must exceed decision, communication and travel time. Redundant channels, accessible formats, local verification and drills convert information into action. Performance should therefore be measured by receipt, decision and protective action, not forecasts issued alone.
**Adjacent rubric:** Forecast role 2 + institutional chain 3 + triggers/messages 3 + performance verdict 2 = **10 marks**

## 15-marker
**Question:** Discuss an integrated strategy for riverine and urban flood resilience in India. **(≤250 words.)**
**Model answer (144 words):** Riverine resilience begins with basin diagnosis—rainfall, tributary timing, sediment, channel capacity, floodplain storage and upstream operations—supported by CWC/IMD data and site-specific thresholds. Floodplain zoning should prevent new high-consequence exposure while protecting rights in legacy settlements. Reservoirs, embankments, diversions and catchment treatment require residual-risk, maintenance and downstream-transfer analysis; wetlands and floodplains provide complementary storage. Urban resilience needs catchment-based drainage from inlet to outfall, solid-waste control, restored lakes and waterways, distributed blue-green detention and planned exceedance routes. Warnings must trigger inclusive evacuation, safe shelters, public-health measures and livelihood protection. Power, water, sewage, transport, hospitals and communications require dependency-based continuity plans. Basin and inter-State coordination should coexist with named ULB and district accountability. Recovery must rebuild safer and include tenants and informal workers; insurance can complement, not replace, mitigation and social protection. Success is lower depth, exposure and downtime across groups, not kilometres of drains or embankments.
**Adjacent rubric:** Basin/riverine 4 + urban measures 4 + warning/systems 3 + governance/recovery 3 + verdict 1 = **15 marks**

## 20-marker
**Question:** Critically evaluate India’s shift from flood control toward catchment-to-city flood resilience. **(≤250 words.)**
**Model answer (175 words):** Flood control traditionally emphasised reservoirs, embankments, channel works and drainage. These can reduce frequent loss but have limits: storage may be unavailable, embankments transfer or concentrate risk, drainage fails at blocked links or high outfalls, and protection can induce denser exposure. Resilience instead links basin hydrology, floodplain land use, forecasting, natural storage, urban drainage, critical systems, inclusive action and recovery. India has useful instruments: CWC’s forecast network and site thresholds, NDMA’s urban-flood guidance, MoHUA’s drainage manual, ULB responsibilities, EOCs/IRS and NDMF-financed UFRMP. Yet fragmented ownership, weak maintenance, floodplain encroachment, wetland loss, informal-settlement vulnerability and forecast-to-action delay remain binding. Reservoir coordination must use downstream scenarios without substituting for dam-safety governance. Reform should combine no-new-risk zoning, connected blue-green and grey systems, interoperable basin data, consequence-based triggers, critical-service continuity, equitable relocation or upgrading and event-based audits. UFRMP sanctions are inputs, not evidence of reduced risk. Evaluation should track depth, duration, exposed population, health, livelihoods and restoration time by social group. The shift is credible only when verified outcomes replace asset counts and structural protection does not transfer risk.
**Adjacent rubric:** Control critique 5 + resilience architecture 5 + Indian institutions/gaps 4 + reforms 4 + verdict 2 = **20 marks**
