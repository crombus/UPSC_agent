# Nature and Significance of Political Theory — Live Session Edition

This session teaches the opening topic of Political Theory as one continuous argument about what kind of knowledge politics admits of. It begins where the subject itself begins — with the question of what makes a matter *political* rather than merely social or private — and then asks what the word *theory* adds to it. From there it builds the three-way classification of statements that governs the whole subject (empirical, logical and evaluative), states the positivist case for throwing the third type out, and gives the reply that keeps it in. It then teaches the two components that survive that argument: political science, with its four-step method and its behavioural turn, and political philosophy, with its critical, justificatory and concept-clarifying functions. It shows why the two are complementary rather than rival, separates political theory from ideology at the point where candidates most often confuse them, and sets out the four heads under which the subject's significance is defended. The second half teaches the decline-and-revival debate in its proper shape: Easton's 1953 indictment, Cobban's 1953 diagnosis, Lipset's 1960 claim that the search was over, Easton's own 1969 reversal, and the normative counter-attack of Strauss, Germino and Marcuse, ending in the post-1970s settlement. It closes with the three things a candidate actually has to do under exam conditions — apply the lens without straying outside the subject, build an answer whose shape follows the directive word, and handle a quotation or a dated claim without inventing provenance.

What this topic does **not** own is as important as what it does. It carries no constitutional articles, no institutional mandates, no case law and no scheme detail; those belong to Polity, Governance and the applied papers. It supplies the conceptual vocabulary — fact and value, explanation and justification, theory and ideology, means and ends — that those papers then anchor in Indian material. Keeping that boundary is itself an examinable skill, and Lesson 16 teaches it directly.

Start with the difficulty rather than the definition. A family decides who controls the household income. A school decides what its students may read. A temple committee decides who may enter. A company decides whom to promote. Each involves authority, each involves a decision binding on people who did not make it, and each can be described using the vocabulary of power. If "political" simply meant "involving power", all four would be political and the word would do no work at all.

The subject's first move is therefore a restriction, and it is worth seeing the shape of it before the wording.

### 1. The source formulation

The chapter opens by observing that we live in society, that society contains many institutions — family, school, religious organisation, polity — and that each involves organisation and the use of authority. **Authority** is then given a working sense: a relationship of command and obedience in which the command is generally regarded as reasonable, so that mild protests do not upset it (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.15). Notice that authority is not peculiar to the state. A parent has it; a teacher has it. So authority alone cannot mark off the political.

What marks it off is **scope**. When an organisation is designed to regulate the *whole* community, it takes the character of a **polity**: rules are made and decisions taken for the whole community, and authority is exercised over each member (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.15). The term **political** then refers to something that is *public*, as distinguished from private or from something applicable to a limited number of persons.

The classic statement of this is **Sheldon S. Wolin's**, in *Politics and Vision* (1960), quoted in the chapter:

> "Of all the authoritative institutions in society, the political arrangement has been singled out as uniquely concerned with what is 'common' to the whole community. Certain functions, such as national defense, internal order, the dispensing of justice, and economic regulation, have been declared the primary responsibility of political institutions, largely on the grounds that the interests and ends served by these functions were beneficial to all of the members of the community." (Wolin, *Politics and Vision*, 1960, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.15)

Read the quotation twice, because it contains two distinct claims and candidates usually take only the first. The first is about **subject-matter**: the political is what is common. The second is about **justification**: those functions were *declared* the responsibility of political institutions *on the ground* that their benefits reach all members. Even the opening definition, in other words, already contains a value-judgment about whose interests count. That observation is the seed of everything Lesson 3 will argue.

### 2. Where the vocabulary comes from, and why it narrowed

"Polity", "politics" and "political" all derive from the Greek **polis**, the city-state (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16). Greek city-states were small communities separated by forests, mountains and seas, each with a compact social life in which institutions and activities were knitted together; activities aimed at securing the **good life** for the community were regarded as part of politics. Because the *polis* was so central, **Aristotle** could describe man as by nature a political animal, and could say that one who lived outside the state, or did not need one, was either a beast or an angel (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16).

Modern usage is narrower. Today we draw a distinction between public and private spheres and confine "politics" to the public one. The chapter's own examples are precise and worth memorising as a pair of lists:

| In the sphere of politics | Not properly in the sphere of politics |
|---|---|
| Decisions of cabinet and parliament | The object of our faith and worship |
| Election campaigns and other activities of political parties | The content of our education |
| People's movements seeking change in law and public policy | Our art and culture |

— with one qualification attached to the right-hand column: those matters stay outside politics **until some regulation of them is required to maintain public order and safety** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16).

### 3. The qualification is the examinable part

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The right-hand column is not a list of things that can never be political. It is a list of things that are not political *by default*. The moment a public-order or safety justification is offered, the matter crosses the line — and the interesting question becomes who gets to decide that the justification applies. A candidate who writes "religion is private, therefore not political" has stated half the rule and inverted its force; a candidate who writes "religion becomes political precisely when regulation for public order is claimed to be necessary, and that claim is itself contestable" has stated the whole rule and opened an analytical line.

### 4. The limit of the definition

Every definition buys clarity at a price, and this one is no exception. Wolin's criterion — what is common to the whole community — gives a clean line between the public sphere and private life, but it under-counts forms of power that operate *inside* what it classifies as private: authority within the household, within community institutions, within informal hierarchies. Later traditions, feminist theory above all, argue that this is precisely where some of the most consequential power lies, and that calling it "non-political" protects it from scrutiny.

That argument is not resolved here, and it is not owned here either; the feminist reworking of the public/private divide belongs to the dedicated topic on feminism, sex and gender. What belongs here is the honest statement of the cost: ⚠️ the public/common criterion is defensible as a way of bounding a subject, and it is vulnerable as a way of bounding *power*. Saying so in an answer converts a definition into an argument.

### 5. How this is used in an exam answer

The opening definition is rarely the whole question, but it is almost always the first move. Three uses:

- **As a boundary device.** When a stem invites you to call everything political, the Wolin criterion lets you say why a particular dispute is or is not in the subject's field — and to say it with a named source rather than an assertion.
- **As a conflict-generator.** The public/private line is where most disputes about the scope of state action begin. Naming the line is how you set up the later liberty, rights and state-role arguments.
- **As a built-in qualification.** The "until regulation is required" clause gives you a ready-made, source-grounded qualification sentence for almost any over-stated proposition about what politics should leave alone.

Revision notes:
- **Authority** = a relationship of command and obedience where the command is generally regarded as reasonable; mild protests do not upset it (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.15).
- Authority is common to many institutions, so authority alone does not define the political.
- **Polity** = an organisation that regulates the *whole* community: rules made and decisions taken for all, authority exercised over each member.
- **Political** = that which is *public* or *common*, as against private or applicable to a limited number of persons.
- **Wolin, *Politics and Vision* (1960):** the political arrangement is uniquely concerned with what is "common" to the whole community — defence, internal order, dispensing of justice, economic regulation — declared so because their benefits reach all members.
- Wolin's sentence contains a subject-matter claim *and* a justification claim; the justification claim already contains a value-judgment.
- *Polis* → polity/politics/political; in the Greek city-state, activities aimed at the **good life** counted as politics; Aristotle's "political animal"; the man outside the state is beast or angel.
- Modern narrowing: cabinet and parliament, election campaigns, party activity and law-reform movements are political; faith and worship, content of education, art and culture are not — **until regulation is needed for public order and safety**.
- The qualification, not the list, is where the marks are.
- ⚠️ Cost of the definition: it under-counts power exercised inside the sphere it labels private. Name the cost; do not pretend the definition is free.

### Concept check

**Question:** A state government requires all private schools to teach a prescribed civics syllabus. A candidate writes: "Education is a private matter, so this is not a political question." Using only this lesson's apparatus, say what is wrong with that sentence and rewrite it.

**Model answer:** The sentence applies the public/private distinction as if it were absolute, when the source states it conditionally: the content of education is not *by default* in the sphere of politics, but it enters that sphere once regulation is required to maintain public order and safety (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16). A rewrite: "The content of education sits outside the sphere of politics by default, which is why a prescribing authority must justify its entry; the question therefore becomes whether a public-order or common-interest ground genuinely applies, and Wolin's criterion — that political institutions take charge of what is common to the whole community — is the test that justification has to meet." That version keeps the distinction, identifies who bears the burden, and generates the argument the stem actually wants.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the public/private distinction as a permanent partition of subject-matter. It is a default allocation with a stated override condition. Candidates who memorise the two lists and forget the override produce answers that cannot handle any contested case — which is the only kind of case examiners set.

Three sentences, one event, three different ways of being right or wrong. That is the whole of this lesson, and nearly the whole of the dispute that follows.

### 1. "Theory" means systematic knowledge

The chapter's move is deliberately unglamorous. The term **theory** stands for *systematic knowledge*. Therefore **political theory** denotes *a systematic knowledge of political phenomena* (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16).

Two things follow immediately, and both are examinable.

First, "systematic" is doing real work. A collection of opinions about politics is not theory; a set of claims organised so that they support, qualify and test one another is. ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this is why "political theory" is not a synonym for "political thought". Political thought is the historical body of what has been said; political theory is the organised knowledge-claim built from and against it.

Second, the definition is deliberately silent about *method*. It does not say "scientific knowledge" and it does not say "philosophical reflection". It says systematic knowledge of political phenomena, and then asks a second question: **what type of knowledge do we require about political phenomena?** That second question is where the classification enters.

### 2. The three statement-types

Broadly speaking, political theory is concerned with three types of statement (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16):

| Type | Basis | Example (the book's own, where one is printed) | How a dispute about it is settled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Empirical statement** | Observation, through sense-experience alone | None printed; any claim about what is the case | Look again, measure again, check the observation |
| **Logical statement** | Reasoning | "two plus two is four" | Check the inference; the conclusion follows or it does not |
| **Evaluative statement** | Value-judgment | "men are born free and equal" | Contested — and that contest is the subject of Lesson 3 |

Learn the two printed examples exactly as the book gives them. They are small, and they are the fastest way to show an examiner that you have the classification from the source rather than from a coaching paraphrase.

### 3. The verification claim — and exactly how far it goes

Here is the sentence that sets up the entire decline debate: **political science relies only on empirical and logical statements** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16). The argument offered for that restriction is that correct observation and correct reasoning by different persons would lead to similar conclusions; hence empirical and logical statements are *capable of verification*.

Against this, it is **alleged** — the chapter's own verb — that evaluative statements rest on individual or group preferences which differ from person to person and group to group; that there is no reliable method of determining what is right or wrong, good or bad; and that one cannot scientifically discover the purpose of the universe or of human life (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16).

Note the asymmetry carefully, because it is the single most common place where a candidate loses the argument before making it:

```text
   CLAIMED FOR (1) AND (2)        ALLEGED ABOUT (3)
   ---------------------------    -------------------------------
   intersubjective convergence     irreducible divergence
   capable of verification         no reliable method of settlement
   therefore: knowledge            therefore: preference, not knowledge
                                           |
                                           v
                        IF ACCEPTED: political theory shrinks to political science
```

The move from "cannot be verified by sense-experience" to "is not knowledge" is not itself an empirical finding. It is a philosophical premise about what counts as knowledge. ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** that is the hinge. Lesson 3 attacks it directly, and an answer that identifies the hinge before attacking it reads as analysis rather than assertion.

### 4. Why the classification is worth more than its length suggests

A candidate who has this classification can do four things quickly:

- **Diagnose a stem.** "Why do voter turnout rates differ across states?" is empirical. "Does a right imply a corresponding obligation?" is largely logical. "Should the state subsidise this?" is evaluative. Many stems mix all three and award marks for noticing.
- **Explain disagreement.** Two people arguing about a policy may be disagreeing about what happened, about what follows from what, or about what matters. Saying which converts a flat "both sides" paragraph into an analysis.
- **Spot a smuggled value.** A claim presented as purely descriptive often carries an evaluative term inside it — "efficient", "disruptive", "developed". Naming that is a reliable source of analytical marks.
- **Set up the whole topic.** Everything from Lesson 3 to Lesson 13 is a fight over whether type (3) belongs.

### 5. A caution about the classification itself

⚠️ **Analytical inference, flagged as such.** The three types are a clean analytical device, not a clean division of real sentences. Most interesting political propositions are mixed: "preventive detention is being used excessively" contains an empirical claim about frequency and an evaluative standard embedded in "excessively". Treating the classification as a sorting machine for whole sentences produces false precision. Treat it instead as a way of asking, of any claim, *what would settle this?* — and expect many claims to need more than one kind of settlement.

Revision notes:
- **Theory** = systematic knowledge. **Political theory** = systematic knowledge of political phenomena (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16).
- "Systematic" distinguishes theory from a collection of opinions; ⚠️ it also distinguishes political *theory* from political *thought* as a historical body of writing.
- The definition names no method; it then asks what *type* of knowledge is required.
- Three statement-types: **empirical** (observation through sense-experience alone), **logical** (reasoning; "two plus two is four"), **evaluative** (value-judgment; "men are born free and equal").
- **Political science relies only on empirical and logical statements** — the stated ground being that correct observation and correct reasoning converge, so such statements are capable of verification.
- The case against evaluative statements, as the chapter reports it: they reflect individual or group preferences, there is no reliable method of settling right and wrong, and the purpose of the universe or of human life cannot be scientifically discovered.
- The hinge is the inference *unverifiable → not knowledge*; that inference is itself philosophical, not empirical.
- Uses of the classification: diagnosing a stem, explaining a disagreement, spotting a smuggled value, framing the decline debate.
- ⚠️ Limit: real political propositions are usually mixed; use the classification to ask "what would settle this?", not to sort sentences into boxes.

### Concept check

**Question:** Classify this sentence and say what is dangerous about accepting it as it stands: "The scheme has been a success, since disbursement reached ninety per cent of enrolled beneficiaries."

**Model answer:** It presents itself as a single empirical statement, but it is two claims fused. The reported figure is empirical, settled by checking the records. "Success" is evaluative: it asserts that disbursement to enrolled beneficiaries is the right standard of achievement. The danger is that the evaluative half travels free on the credibility of the empirical half — the reader accepts the standard because the number is checkable. Separating them reopens the real questions: who was enrolled and who was not, and whether reaching the enrolled is what the scheme was for. ⚠️ Note also that this is exactly the mixed case warned about above; the classification earns its keep by exposing the fusion, not by assigning the sentence to one box.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat "empirical" as a synonym for "true" and "evaluative" as a synonym for "opinion". Empirical statements can be false, and the chapter's whole argument in the next lesson is that evaluative statements have a logical structure and can be reasoned about. The three types differ in *what would settle them*, not in their respectability.

### 1. The restriction, stated fairly

Exponents of **logical positivism** argue that evaluative statements have no empirical content and no logical structure; they are expressions of subjective reflection or emotional preference. In the same spirit, champions of scientific method for the study of politics insist on a **value-free** or **value-neutral** approach (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.16-17).

The chapter later names the full set of parties pressing this restriction, and the enumeration is worth having exactly: advocates of **positivism**, **neo-positivism (logical positivism)** and **behaviouralism** wish to confine political theory to the sphere of political science, arguing that evaluative statements rest on individual or group preferences differing between persons and groups, that there is no reliable method of determining what is right or wrong, good or bad, and that one cannot scientifically discover the purpose of the universe or of human life — so questions of value-judgment should be dropped from the purview of political theory altogether (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.23).

⚠️ **Why the three-party enumeration matters.** It prevents the answer from treating the restriction as one school's eccentricity. Positivism is a general epistemological position, logical positivism its twentieth-century logical refinement, and behaviouralism the programme that applied the demand inside political studies — three layers of the same demand, which is why the reply has to engage the demand rather than any one of its carriers. It also sets up Lesson 11, where Cobban will list the logical positivists among the *causes* of decline, and Lesson 13, where Strauss and Germino attack positivism by name.

State this at full strength in an answer before replying to it. The restriction is not stupidity; it is a disciplined response to a real problem. If one person says capital punishment is just and another says it is barbaric, and there is no agreed procedure for settling the matter, then a subject that admits such claims risks becoming a contest of assertions. Demanding verification is a way of protecting the subject from that.

### 2. The reply, in four moves

The chapter's answer is compressed but structured, and it repays being unpacked move by move (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.17).

**Move 1 — Scope.** "In any case, political theory cannot be confined to the so-called scientific knowledge. It is equally concerned with determining values which come within the scope of philosophy." Note the phrase *so-called*: the restriction is being denied the right to monopolise the word "knowledge".

**Move 2 — Structure.** "We cannot accept the view that values are based on individual or group preferences. On the contrary, values do have a sound logical structure unless we mistake them for biased statements." This is the direct contradiction of P2. The claim is not that values are empirically verifiable; it is that they are *reasoned*, and that confusing a value with a bias is what makes them look arbitrary.

**Move 3 — Procedure.** Upholders of different values can be invited to have a dialogue, to have an opportunity to understand each other's point of view, to convince each other, and probably to agree on certain universal principles by which to judge the validity of values. ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this supplies the missing item in P1. The positivist says there is *no reliable method*; the reply offers a method — a procedural, dialogical one rather than an observational one. Whether that method is reliable *enough* is exactly where a critical answer can push back, and Lesson 13's residual returns to it.

**Move 4 — Consequence.** Determination of values is the basis of a sound public policy or decision. If we abdicate this responsibility, it may fall into irresponsible hands, with disastrous consequences. Hence political theory must comprehend both political science and political philosophy.

Move 4 is a different *kind* of argument from Moves 2 and 3, and saying so is worth a mark. Moves 2 and 3 are epistemological: they say values *can* be reasoned about. Move 4 is practical: it says the work of determining values will be done by someone whatever we decide, so a discipline that refuses the task does not abolish it — it vacates it.

### 3. The strongest objection, and the reply to the reply

⚠️ **Objection.** Move 4 proves too much. From "the task will be done badly if we do not do it" it does not follow that the task is *knowledge*. An astrologer could argue identically: if trained people refuse to cast horoscopes, untrained people will. The consequence of abandonment says nothing about the epistemic status of what is abandoned.

⚠️ **Reply.** The objection would land if Move 4 stood alone, but it does not. Moves 2 and 3 have already asserted a logical structure and a procedure of reasoned convergence; Move 4 then supplies the reason for *urgency*, not the reason for *validity*. The defensible reading is therefore: validity from structure and dialogue, urgency from consequence. ⚠️ **Residual:** the reply does not establish that dialogue will in fact converge, and the chapter's own word — "probably" — concedes as much. A graded conclusion should keep that residual visible rather than smoothing it away.

### 4. A provenance caution about the source's own box

The chapter carries a boxed definition of **Logical Positivism**, which it describes as a school founded by the German sociologist Max Weber (1864-1920) and the members of the Vienna Circle (Moritz Schlick, Ludwig Wittgenstein, A. J. Ayer and others) in the early decades of the twentieth century, holding that reliable and valid knowledge in any field is obtainable only by empirical method, so that questions of value lie beyond scientific knowledge (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.17).

⚠️ **Flagged discrepancy.** The substantive doctrine in that box is standard and can be used. The founding attribution should be handled with care. The usual account places the Vienna Circle around Schlick's seminar in the 1920s, with Carnap, Neurath, Waismann and Hahn among its members, and treats the early Wittgenstein as a major influence on the Circle rather than a member of it; Weber's relevance to the value-freedom debate is as a methodologist of value-neutrality in social science, not as a founder of logical positivism. This session states the discrepancy openly rather than reproducing or silently correcting the box.

⚠️ **Why this matters pedagogically, not just bibliographically.** This topic will spend five lessons teaching you to test provenance. It would be incoherent to exempt the source that teaches it. The safe exam practice is: use the box's *doctrine* ("valid knowledge only by empirical method; value-questions outside its scope"), and avoid asserting a founding roster you have not checked. If you must attribute, attribute the doctrine to logical positivism as a school, not to a named founder.

### 5. The trap this lesson defuses

❌ **Trap:** "Political theory is normative; political science is empirical; the two are separate subjects."
✅ **Correction:** the chapter's conclusion is the opposite. Political theory is the *inclusive* term; political science and political philosophy are its two components. The sentence to keep is: political theory must comprehend both political science and political philosophy (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.17).

❌ **Trap:** "Evaluative statements are irrational."
✅ **Correction:** the text expressly denies it — values have a sound logical structure unless mistaken for biased statements, and can be argued through dialogue.

Revision notes:
- The restriction: logical positivists hold that evaluative statements have no empirical content or logical structure and are expressions of subjective reflection or emotional preference; advocates of scientific method demand a **value-free / value-neutral** approach (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.16-17).
- State the restriction at full strength before answering it; it is a disciplined response to the genuine problem of unsettleable disputes.
- Reply, Move 1 — **scope:** political theory cannot be confined to the so-called scientific knowledge; it is equally concerned with determining values, which fall within philosophy.
- Reply, Move 2 — **structure:** values do have a sound logical structure unless we mistake them for biased statements.
- Reply, Move 3 — **procedure:** dialogue among upholders of different values; understand, convince, and *probably* agree on universal principles for judging validity.
- Reply, Move 4 — **consequence:** determination of values is the basis of sound public policy; abdication lets the task fall into irresponsible hands, with disastrous consequences.
- Moves 2-3 establish *validity*; Move 4 establishes *urgency*. Do not let Move 4 carry the epistemic weight alone.
- ⚠️ Residual: the chapter's own "probably" concedes that convergence is not guaranteed.
- Conclusion: political theory must comprehend **both** political science and political philosophy.
- ⚠️ Provenance caution: the source's Logical Positivism box names Weber and Wittgenstein among founders/members; the standard account differs. Use the doctrine, not the roster.
- ❌ Do not write that theory is normative and science empirical as if they were rival subjects; theory is the inclusive term.

### Concept check

**Question:** A stem reads: "Political theory should confine itself to what can be verified. Comment." Give the two-sentence opening that sets up a scoring answer, and say why each sentence is there.

**Model answer:** Opening: "The demand for verification is a defensible response to a real difficulty — that disputes about right and wrong appear to admit no settlement procedure — and it is the ground on which political science restricts itself to empirical and logical statements. It nevertheless fails as a boundary for political theory, because the claim that values are mere preference is itself a philosophical premise rather than an empirical finding, and because values possess a logical structure that permits reasoned dialogue, while the determination of values is in any case the basis of sound public policy and will be undertaken by someone whether or not the discipline undertakes it." The first sentence concedes the opponent's strongest ground, which is what "Comment" rewards and what prevents the answer reading as a reflex defence. The second carries the position — the epistemic reply and the practical reply, in that order, so that the practical reply is supporting urgency rather than doing the work of validity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not answer this stem by listing the uses of political philosophy. The stem contains a proposition about *verification*, and an answer that never engages the verification criterion has changed the subject, however much true material it contains.

Having kept both components inside political theory, the chapter now describes each on its own terms. Take the scientific component first, because its method is concrete and because the decline debate is an argument about this method's ambitions.

A learner's honest question at this point is: what does "studying politics scientifically" actually *do*? Not what does it promise — what are the steps?

```text
     relies on sense-experience alone
```

### 1. Reading the four steps properly

All four steps are in the source (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.18). Three features repay attention.

**Step (a) contains an exclusion, not just a procedure.** Observation "rules out supernatural or metaphysical causation (because it is beyond our sense-experience)". So the scientific component is not neutral about everything; it has already excluded a class of explanations as a condition of operating. ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this is a small but useful point against the idea that science is simply "the view from nowhere". It is a view from a stated standpoint, and the standpoint is defensible on its own terms — but it is a standpoint.

**Step (c) is the one candidates skip, and it is the load-bearing one.** Generalization alone yields correlation; explanation supplies reasons, and without reasons an observed correlation might be mere coincidence. The sentence to remember is that explanation alone makes particular events, situations or tendencies *meaningful*. In an answer, this is the line that lets you say why data-reporting is not yet political science.

**Step (d) quietly re-opens the value question.** Prescription means suggesting measures for achieving such objectives as *higher efficiency, stability, satisfaction*. Those are objectives, and objectives are chosen. ⚠️ **Analytical inference, and a high-value one:** the scientific component, taken to its own fourth step, prescribes — and prescription presupposes an end. A value-free political science that nevertheless prescribes has either smuggled in its ends or borrowed them from somewhere else. That tension is the seed of Marcuse's objection in Lesson 13, and it is available to you here, inside the description of the method itself.

### 2. The behavioural approach

The **behavioural approach** insists on studying the actual behaviour of human beings in a political situation, rather than describing the salient features of political institutions and their legal position. In this approach formal political institutions are dissolved into **systems** and **processes**, so as to focus attention on the actual behaviour of political actors, which alone is held to be capable of scientific study (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.18-19).

Two contrasts make this concrete:

| Question | Pre-behavioural framing | Behavioural framing |
|---|---|---|
| What is a legislature? | An institution with a stated composition, powers and procedures | A set of processes through which actors with interests produce authoritative outputs |
| Why did a law pass? | Because the constitutionally prescribed steps were completed | Because of the observable conduct of identifiable actors within a system |

The behavioural reframing buys explanatory traction. It also pays a price, and the price is the next lesson's subject-matter: a system described purely as processes and behaviours has no vocabulary left for asking whether its outputs are *justified*.

### 3. Post-behaviouralism, in one sentence

The post-behavioural approach insists on making the achievements of political science **subservient to human values and ends**. Hence it heralds a revival of concern with *values* **without compromising scientific method for the study of *facts***. In short, it calls for the application of political science to overcome the prevailing crises in various spheres of human life (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19).

That is the whole formula, and it should be memorised in the book's own shape, because the most frequent error on this topic is to report post-behaviouralism as a rejection of science. It is not. It subordinates science's achievements to values and ends; it does not abandon scientific method for facts.

Lessons 10 to 12 reconstruct how this position came to be held, and by whom. Here it is enough to have the formula in place, because without it the behavioural approach looks like a dead end rather than a stage.

### 4. Where this lands in an answer

- A stem asking whether political science can *predict* should be answered through steps (b) and (c): generalization gives correlation, explanation gives reasons, and prediction is only as strong as the explanation behind it.
- A stem asking whether political science can be value-free should be answered through step (d): the method's own fourth step is prescriptive, and prescription requires an objective.
- A stem on behaviouralism should use the dissolution of institutions into systems and processes as the defining move, not a vague reference to "studying behaviour".

Revision notes:
- Four steps of political science (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.18): **(a) observation** — sense-experience alone, ruling out supernatural or metaphysical causation; **(b) generalization** — regularities, relation and correlation, by **inductive** (particular → general) or **deductive** (general → particular) method, expressed as a general rule, preferably quantitative, capable of verification by experimentation; **(c) explanation** — reasons for the rule, because an unexplained correlation might be coincidence, and explanation alone makes events meaningful; **(d) prediction and prescription** — outcomes anticipated and measures suggested for higher efficiency, stability, satisfaction.
- ⚠️ Step (a) *excludes* a class of causes; the method has a standpoint.
- ⚠️ Step (d) *prescribes*; prescription presupposes chosen ends — a value-freedom problem generated from inside the method.
- **Behavioural approach:** study the actual behaviour of human beings in a political situation rather than describing institutions and their legal position; formal institutions are dissolved into **systems** and **processes**; actual behaviour alone is held capable of scientific study.
- **Post-behavioural approach:** makes the achievements of political science subservient to human values and ends; revives concern with **values** *without compromising scientific method for the study of facts*; applies political science to overcome prevailing crises.
- The most common error: reporting post-behaviouralism as anti-science. It is not.

### Concept check

**Question:** Using only this lesson, explain why the sentence "political science is value-free because it merely describes" is self-undermining on the chapter's own account of the method.

**Model answer:** It is self-undermining because description is only the second of four steps. The method as set out proceeds through observation and generalization to explanation and then to **prediction and prescription**, where measures are suggested for achieving objectives such as higher efficiency, stability or satisfaction (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.18). Prescription of means towards an objective presupposes that the objective has been selected, and selection among objectives is a value-judgment. So the discipline that claims value-freedom on the ground that it "merely describes" either stops at step (b) — in which case it has not explained anything and has not reached the prescriptive claims made for it — or it completes its own method and thereby takes a position on ends. The honest formulation is narrower: the *observational and generalizing* steps can be conducted without evaluative premises; the prescriptive step cannot.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse "rules out supernatural or metaphysical causation" with "has no presuppositions". Excluding a class of explanations is itself a methodological commitment, defensible but not invisible. An answer that treats scientific method as assumption-free has already conceded Strauss's and Marcuse's ground without noticing.

### 1. Why "ideal versus real" under-describes the subject

It is *sometimes alleged* that political science deals with the real while political philosophy deals with the ideal — that science inquires into what men and women actually do in a political situation while philosophy tries to determine what they ought to do in keeping with the ultimate good or purpose of human life. The chapter's verdict: this view does not define the scope of political philosophy adequately (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19).

**D. D. Raphael**, in *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976), supplies the correction, and the chapter quotes him:

> "It is true that some of the classical political philosophers have set out ideal forms of society, but… this has not been their central concern. Even in Plato, the purpose of depicting an ideal society is to criticize existing society and to promote understanding of general social concepts such as justice." (Raphael, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19)

Read the Plato example carefully, because it inverts the usual picture. The ideal society is not the destination; it is the *instrument*. It is constructed in order to criticise the existing society and to make a general concept — justice — intelligible. Political philosophy may properly be recognised, the chapter concludes, by its **critical** function.

### 2. Explanation versus justification

Raphael's second formulation is the single most portable sentence in this entire topic, and the chapter quotes it directly:

> "Philosophy differs from science in that science seeks explanation while philosophy seeks justification." (Raphael, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19)

The term **justification** is then given a precise sense: the attempt to give *rational grounds* either for accepting or for rejecting beliefs which we normally take for granted without thinking of any grounds for them.

Three things make this distinction so useful under exam conditions:

- It is **symmetrical**. Justification covers grounds for *rejecting* as well as accepting. A candidate who treats political philosophy as the business of defending things has halved it.
- It is **diagnostic**. Given any political question, ask: is the demand here for *why it happens* or for *on what grounds it is right*? That single question sorts most stems.
- It targets **the taken-for-granted**. The characteristic philosophical move is not to answer a disputed question but to ask for the grounds of an undisputed one.

| Question | Demand | Belongs to |
|---|---|---|
| Why do riots occur? | Causes, regularities, mechanisms | Political science |
| What is a good society? | Grounds for holding an arrangement worth having | Political philosophy |
| Does turnout rise with competitiveness? | Correlation and its explanation | Political science |
| Should an elected majority be able to do this? | Rational grounds for a limit on authority | Political philosophy |

⚠️ **Limit of the distinction, stated honestly.** Many real questions straddle it. "Does democracy increase legitimacy?" requires an empirical measure of acceptance *and* a philosophical account of what makes acceptance rightful. The clean split is a sorting heuristic, not a fact about questions; saying so in an answer is a qualification examiners reward rather than penalise.

### 3. Clarification of concepts, and its three operations

The other closely related function is **clarification of concepts**. Raphael's point, as the chapter reports it, is that concepts such as society, authority, social class, justice, liberty and democracy are not only highly general but also **vague**. Clarification involves three related purposes (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19):

| Operation | What it does | The source's own example |
|---|---|---|
| **Analysis** | Specifies a concept's elements, often by way of definition | Defining sovereignty as supreme legal authority |
| **Synthesis** | Shows the logical relationship between two concepts | Showing that the concept of a right involves that of an obligation |
| **Improvement** | Recommends a definition or use that will assist clarity or coherence | Recommending that sovereignty be applied only to the legal authority of a state, and not to its coercive power |

Learn the three examples as given. They are compact, they are from the source, and the third is unusually instructive: **improvement is a recommendation, which means that concept-clarification is not purely descriptive either.** To recommend that "sovereignty" be confined to legal authority and withheld from coercive power is to make a substantive move about what the word should let us say. ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** this is why concept-clarification is not a neutral preliminary to political argument; it is political argument conducted at the level of vocabulary. Lesson 9 shows the practical stakes of that.

### 4. The worked example: freedom

The chapter's illustration of the clarifying function is the concept of freedom, and it is worth running the three operations on it explicitly, because an answer that does so demonstrates the method rather than naming it:

- **Analysis:** does "freedom" mean absence of interference by others, or the presence of conditions for self-development? The two specifications pick out different elements.
- **Synthesis:** how does freedom relate to equality — does securing one constrain the other, and under what account of each?
- **Improvement:** should we recommend a usage that keeps the two senses distinct, so that a claim about one is not quietly transferred to the other?

That exercise settles nothing about policy. It makes it impossible to equivocate — and preventing equivocation is a real political achievement, which is exactly Lesson 9's argument.

### 5. Why the function survives the objection that values cannot be ascertained

The chapter concedes the hardest point directly: political philosophy deals with the needs, objectives and goals of human life, which *cannot be scientifically ascertained*. But these can always be discussed by right-thinking people, argued on the basis of available data and reasoning, and some acceptable point may be reached at the current level of our social consciousness. Most arguments can be picked up for further scrutiny from the long tradition of political thought; new arguments can be introduced; conclusions can be drawn from the expanding horizons of our knowledge. Hence the search for values and the critical review of our position is an **ongoing process**, which justifies the continuing pursuit of political philosophy (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.19-20).

And the historical note attached to it: political philosophy itself arose from critical reflection on political activity that existed long before philosophy did. It exemplified the Socratic function of **speaking truth to power** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the qualification that makes this a strong rather than a pious conclusion:** notice what the concession costs. "At the current level of our social consciousness" and "some acceptable point" are deliberately provisional. The defence of political philosophy offered here is not that it reaches final truths; it is that the inquiry is cumulative, self-correcting and unavoidable. An answer that claims more than this for philosophy over-reaches; an answer that claims less has not understood why the subject continues.

Revision notes:
- The "science = real, philosophy = ideal" picture is *inadequate*, not simply false; the chapter rejects it as a definition of scope (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19).
- **Raphael, *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976):** classical philosophers did set out ideal forms, but that was not their central concern; even in Plato the ideal society serves to **criticize existing society** and to promote understanding of general concepts such as justice.
- Political philosophy is properly recognised by its **critical function**.
- **"Science seeks explanation while philosophy seeks justification"** — justification meaning rational grounds for **accepting or rejecting** beliefs normally taken for granted.
- The distinction is symmetrical, diagnostic, and aimed at the taken-for-granted; ⚠️ its limit is that many real questions straddle it.
- **Clarification of concepts:** general but vague concepts — society, authority, social class, justice, liberty, democracy.
- Three operations: **analysis** (specify elements; sovereignty as supreme legal authority), **synthesis** (logical relations; a right involves an obligation), **improvement** (recommend a usage; confine sovereignty to legal authority, not coercive power).
- ⚠️ Improvement is a *recommendation*, so clarification is itself a substantive move, not a neutral preliminary.
- Freedom as the worked case: analysis (non-interference vs self-development), synthesis (relation to equality), improvement (keep the senses distinct to block equivocation).
- Concession and defence: values cannot be *scientifically ascertained*, but they can be discussed, argued from data and reasoning, and brought to an acceptable point at the current level of social consciousness; the search is an **ongoing process**.
- Political philosophy arose from critical reflection on pre-existing political activity, exemplifying the Socratic function of **speaking truth to power**.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Political philosophy is prescriptive because it tells us what ought to be done, while political science is descriptive." Identify the precise inadequacy in that sentence, and supply the correction in the source's own terms.

**Model answer:** The inadequacy is that it reproduces exactly the "ideal versus real" framing the chapter declares inadequate, and it misplaces the centre of political philosophy. Political philosophy is properly recognised by its **critical** function, and its characteristic activity is **justification** — supplying rational grounds for accepting *or rejecting* beliefs we normally take for granted — together with the **clarification of concepts** through analysis, synthesis and improvement (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19). The sentence also misdescribes political science, whose own fourth methodological step is prediction *and prescription*. A corrected version: "Political science seeks explanation of political reality and, at its prescriptive step, suggests means to stated objectives; political philosophy seeks justification — grounds for accepting or rejecting beliefs — and clarifies the vague general concepts in which political argument is conducted."

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not reduce concept-clarification to the writing of definitions. Its third operation, improvement, *recommends* a usage, which is a substantive intervention in the argument and not a neutral tidying of language. Treating clarification as a preliminary chore is what makes candidates skip the function that Lesson 9 shows to be among the subject's most consequential.

### 1. Catlin: the division, and what each half is for

**George Catlin**, writing in the *Political Quarterly* (March 1957), is quoted for the structural claim: "the theory (of politics) itself is divided into political science and political philosophy" (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.17).

Catlin then characterises each side. Pleading for combining the study of political science with sociology, he asserts that "it is the supreme virtue of the fusion of sociology and political science that it could enable us to be sharp-eyed for the phenomena of **control** in its many forms, over all the processes of the whole social field." And defining the scope of political philosophy: "Our concern here… is with the **kingdom of ends or final values**… So soon as a man begins to ask, 'What is for the national good?' or 'What is the good society?', he is asking questions in philosophy" (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.17).

The chapter's own gloss is the compact version worth carrying into an answer: political science deals with the **facts** of political life — what is the real situation, and which laws govern our actual behaviour — while political philosophy is concerned with **values**, that is, what is good for us.

Note Catlin's test. He does not classify by subject-matter; he classifies by the *question being asked*. The moment someone asks "what is for the national good?" or "what is the good society?", the inquiry has moved into philosophy regardless of what it is about. That makes the division portable: it applies to a question about taxation as readily as to a question about war.

### 2. Hacker: the double role and the two-way dependence

**Andrew Hacker**, in *Political Theory: Philosophy, Ideology, Science* (1961), accepts the two components but adds the claim that makes separation impossible (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.17-18):

> "Every political scientist plays a double role. He is part scientist and part philosopher… no theorist can make a lasting contribution to human knowledge unless he works in the realms of both science and philosophy. The scientific parts of a theory can only achieve coherence and significance if the writer has a preconceived idea or the goals of political life." (Hacker, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.17-18)

⚠️ **Text note.** The last clause is reproduced exactly as it stands in the local copy, which prints "a preconceived idea **or** the goals of political life". Read in context — and consistent with the sentence that follows about the goals a theorist must hold — this is almost certainly a scanning artefact for "idea **of** the goals". The artefact is flagged rather than silently corrected, because silently amending a quotation is the one thing this topic's own provenance rules forbid.

And, on each role separately:

> "The theorist whose pursuit is political science is interested in describing and explaining the realities of political behaviour. He attempts to draw up generalized propositions about the actual relations between states and citizens and about the role of power in society."

> "The theorist whose interest is in writing political philosophy, on the other hand, is concerned with prescribing the goals which citizens, states and societies ought to pursue. His aim is to generalize about right conduct in the political life and about the legitimate uses of power."

Then the return direction, which is the half candidates forget:

> "the philosophical parts of a theory must be informed by a profound understanding of the facts of political life. The best political philosophers have always been well aware of the existing realities; they have given intense and systematic study to the needs and capabilities of man and society." (Hacker, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.18)

### 3. Why this is an argument and not a slogan

Set out the dependence as a two-column claim, because that is how it earns marks:

| Direction | Claim | Why it holds | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Philosophy → science | The scientific parts achieve coherence and significance only if the writer has a preconceived idea of the goals of political life | Without a view of what matters, a researcher has no principle for selecting which of indefinitely many observable regularities is worth explaining | The picture of a theorist who simply "follows the data" |
| Science → philosophy | The philosophical parts must be informed by a profound understanding of the facts of political life | A prescription addressed to beings whose needs and capabilities are misdescribed cannot bind them; the best philosophers studied existing realities intensely | The picture of a philosopher reasoning from first principles about an imaginary population |

⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the first row is the stronger and more interesting claim, because it attacks value-freedom at the point of **selection** rather than at the point of conclusion. Even a researcher who draws no evaluative conclusions has had to decide what was worth counting. That decision is not delivered by the data.

### 4. The objection, and the limit of Hacker's thesis

⚠️ **Objection.** Granting that selection requires a purpose does not show that the purpose must be a *political-philosophical* goal. Curiosity, tractability or funding can all drive selection, and none of them is a view about the good life.

⚠️ **Reply.** The objection narrows the claim without defeating it. Where selection is driven by curiosity or tractability, the resulting body of knowledge is still shaped by a judgment about significance, and someone must eventually say why the findings matter for collective life — at which point the philosophical component re-enters, whether or not the original researcher supplied it. ⚠️ **Residual:** Hacker's thesis therefore establishes that the *enterprise* cannot be purely scientific; it does not establish that every individual study must contain a philosophical component. Stating that residual is the difference between asserting integration and arguing it.

A second limit worth naming: Hacker's formula tells you that both halves are necessary; it does not tell you **how much weight** each should carry in any concrete dispute. ⚠️ That is the unresolved remainder which the decline debate in Lessons 10 to 13 is, in effect, an extended quarrel about.

### 5. The comparison table to carry into the exam

| Axis | Political science | Political philosophy | Political theory as a whole |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic concern | Facts of political life; actual behaviour | Values; what is good for us; the kingdom of ends | Systematic knowledge of political phenomena, including both |
| Characteristic question | What is the real situation, and which regularities govern behaviour? | What is for the national good? What is the good society? | Which means fit which ends in public life? |
| Method | Observation, generalization, explanation, prediction and prescription | Critical evaluation, justification, analysis, synthesis and improvement of concepts | Empirical discipline joined to normative reasoning |
| Characteristic product | Generalized propositions about relations between states and citizens and the role of power | Generalizations about right conduct and the legitimate uses of power | Description, criticism and reconstruction together |
| Failure mode if isolated | Value-blind or selectively descriptive; coherence and significance unattainable without a view of goals | Poorly grounded or merely rhetorical; prescriptions addressed to a misdescribed world | — |

⚠️ The final row is where a 20-mark answer finds its structure: each component has a *characteristic pathology*, and integration is argued as the remedy for both, not as a slogan about balance.

Revision notes:
- **Catlin (*Political Quarterly*, March 1957):** "the theory (of politics) itself is divided into political science and political philosophy."
- Catlin on science: fusing sociology with political science makes us sharp-eyed for the phenomena of **control** in its many forms across the whole social field.
- Catlin on philosophy: the concern is the **kingdom of ends or final values**; asking "What is for the national good?" or "What is the good society?" *is* asking a philosophical question.
- Catlin classifies by the **question asked**, not by subject-matter — which is what makes his test portable.
- **Hacker (*Political Theory: Philosophy, Ideology, Science*, 1961):** every political scientist plays a **double role**, part scientist and part philosopher; no theorist makes a lasting contribution without working in both realms.
- Direction 1: the **scientific** parts achieve coherence and significance only if the writer has a preconceived idea of the **goals** of political life.
- Direction 2: the **philosophical** parts must be informed by a profound understanding of the **facts** of political life; the best philosophers studied existing realities intensely.
- ⚠️ The first direction attacks value-freedom at the point of **selection**, which is the strongest available form of the attack.
- ⚠️ Residual: integration is established for the enterprise, not for every individual study; and the thesis fixes necessity, not **weighting**.
- Each component has a characteristic pathology in isolation: science becomes value-blind, philosophy becomes ungrounded.

### Concept check

**Question:** "A truly scientific political science would simply follow the evidence wherever it leads, without any prior commitment." Using Hacker, state the strongest reply and then state honestly what the reply does not prove.

**Model answer:** The reply is that evidence does not come pre-sorted into what is worth explaining. Hacker's claim is that the scientific parts of a theory can achieve coherence and significance only if the writer already has a preconceived idea of the goals of political life (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.17-18); since any political field contains indefinitely many observable regularities, a researcher must decide which ones matter, and that judgment of significance is not supplied by the data. "Following the evidence" therefore describes what happens *after* selection, not the whole of inquiry. What the reply does not prove is that the selecting judgment must itself be a view about the good life: curiosity, tractability or external funding can also drive selection. The defensible conclusion is accordingly that the *enterprise* of political science cannot be purely scientific, since someone must eventually say why its findings matter for collective life — not that every individual study contains a philosophical premise.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not reduce Hacker to the single sentence "part scientist, part philosopher". Quoted alone, it reads as a pleasant compromise. Its force comes from the two directions of dependence that follow it — goals making scientific work coherent, and facts making philosophical work binding — and an answer that cites only the slogan has given the examiner a label instead of an argument.

Notice that both branches of the ideology column are ideological. The critic of the existing order is as much an interested party as its defender, on this account. That symmetry is the part candidates most often get wrong.

### 1. The source's statement

The chapter attributes the warning to **Hacker**: political theory must be distinguished from **ideology**. A theory — whether it takes the character of science or of philosophy — must be **dispassionate and disinterested**. When a theorist has no personal interest in any political arrangement, Hacker writes, "his vision of reality and his image of the good life will not be clouded, nor will his theory be special pleading… The intention of ideology is to justify a particular system of power in society. The ideologue is an interested party: his interest may be to defend things as they are or to criticize the status quo in the hope that a new distribution of power will come into being" (Hacker, *Political Theory: Philosophy, Ideology, Science*, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20).

The boxed definition in the same chapter fixes the term: **ideology** is a set of ideas and arguments used to defend an existing or a proposed distribution of power in society; these ideas are accepted to be true by their upholders **without inquiring into their validity** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20).

### 2. Two distinct marks, and why both are needed

The definition actually contains two criteria, and an answer that separates them is more precise than one that runs them together:

| Mark | What it says | Test question |
|---|---|---|
| **Function** | The ideas are *used to defend* an existing or proposed distribution of power | What work is this set of ideas doing? |
| **Epistemic attitude** | They are accepted as true *without inquiring into their validity* | Is the holder willing to test them? |

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The two marks can come apart, and noticing that is a genuine analytical gain. A theorist may hold a position that happens to support an existing distribution of power while remaining fully willing to test it; that is theory with a conclusion someone finds congenial, not ideology. Conversely, a person may hold a radical position dogmatically; that is ideology despite its oppositional content. Ideology is therefore not defined by **which side** the conclusions fall on. The defining features are the justificatory *function* and the refusal of *validity-testing*.

### 3. What goes wrong when theory is clouded

The chapter specifies the damage separately for each component (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20):

- **Political philosophy** clouded by ideology is reduced to a **rationalization** for current or future political and social arrangements. Its aim — a disinterested search for the principles of the good state and the good society — is replaced by advocacy with a philosophical vocabulary.
- **Political science** clouded by ideology is reduced to **partial or selective depiction**, resulting in a distorted description or explanation of political and social reality. Note that the failure mode here is not fabrication; it is *selection*. That connects directly to Lesson 6: if selection among observable regularities requires a judgment of significance, then an interested judgment of significance distorts a science that never states a false sentence.

The chapter's own illustrations are symmetrical pairs, and they are safe to reproduce because they name no contemporary actor: upholders of capitalism regard private property as most conducive to justice and social progress, while upholders of socialism regard social ownership of the means of production as the most suitable method of achieving those same ends; upholders of nationalism place national pride and national interest above other goals, while supporters of internationalism tend to disregard national boundaries in determining the index of human progress (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20).

The conclusion: all ideologies are biased towards partisan ends, and any such bias obstructs the search for truth. The chapter's closing sentence on the point is exact and worth carrying whole: "Scholarly detachment is, therefore, the keynote of political theory in real sense of the term." (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20).

### 4. The hardest objection — and the reply that Lesson 13 will complete

⚠️ **Objection.** The ideal of disinterestedness is self-serving. Every theorist occupies a social position; claiming detachment is itself a rhetorical move that insulates a position from scrutiny. On this view the theory/ideology distinction is a device by which the dominant perspective describes itself as neutral and its challengers as ideological.

⚠️ **Reply.** The objection has real force against a *naive* reading, in which detachment means having no standpoint. It has much less force against the reading the chapter supports, where the operative mark is not the absence of a standpoint but the willingness to subject claims to validity-testing. One can occupy a position and still invite refutation; the ideologue, on the definition given, declines to inquire into validity at all.

⚠️ **Residual, stated rather than hidden.** The reply concedes something important: detachment so understood is a *disposition*, and dispositions are not externally verifiable. We can test whether a claim has been subjected to scrutiny; we cannot read a theorist's willingness directly. This is precisely the gap that Germino will exploit from one direction — *detachment is not ethical neutrality* — and Marcuse from another, when he argues that a social science confident of its own neutrality can become an instrument of the status quo. Flagging that forward connection here is what makes Lesson 13 land as a development rather than an interruption.

### 5. Boundary note

The full treatment of ideology — its two senses, false consciousness, totalitarianism and the end-of-ideology thesis — belongs to the next topic in this subject's sequence, which owns it. What this lesson owns is only the **distinction**: what makes a body of political ideas theory rather than ideology. Resist importing the end-of-ideology debate here; it is a different argument, and Lesson 11's treatment of Lipset is the only point at which the two legitimately touch.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two writers reach opposite conclusions about land redistribution. One defends the existing distribution; the other demands its reversal. A candidate concludes that the first is ideological and the second is theoretical. On the chapter's criteria, what has gone wrong?

So both writers could be ideological, both could be theoretical, or the labels could fall either way. ### 1. Control of social life — the argument and its three analogies

Scientific analysis of political life enables us to understand and solve the problems of our social life. The chapter makes the case through three parallels (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.21):

| Established science | What it enables | Political science's parallel claim |
|---|---|---|
| Geology | Understanding the causes of earthquakes; insights for preventing the havoc they cause | Understanding the causes of conflict and violence in society; insights for preventing their outburst |
| Physics | Generating electricity from thermal and water resources | Securing the development of society from our human resources |
| Medical science | Controlling and curing diseases of the human body | Finding remedies for political instability and various types of social crises |

The structure of each analogy is the same: knowledge of causes yields capacity for intervention. That is a genuine and defensible claim, and it is the strongest case available for the scientific component's practical value.

### 2. The limits of the analogies — which is where the marks are

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the most valuable move in this lesson.** The three analogies are not equally strong, and an answer that says so demonstrates judgment rather than recall.

- The **geology** parallel is the most apt, because geology predicts and mitigates without being able to prevent; it promises understanding and damage-limitation, not control. If political science claims only this, the claim is modest and defensible.
- The **physics** parallel is the weakest, because generating electricity from a river is a problem with a determinate engineering solution, whereas the chapter's parallel claim — that the knowledge of political science "enables us to secure development of society from our human resources" (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.21) — names a goal whose content is contested. Here the analogy quietly imports an *end* into a claim about *means*, which is the very move Lesson 4 identified at step (d).
- The **medical** parallel carries a hidden normative premise: medicine knows what health is. Transposed to politics, it requires that we already know what a healthy polity is, and that is exactly the philosophical question the scientific component was said not to answer.

So the first head of significance, examined closely, does not stand alone. ⚠️ It establishes that causal knowledge of political life is practically valuable, and it simultaneously shows why causal knowledge cannot be the whole of the subject: every one of the three analogies presupposes an agreed objective — fewer earthquakes, more electricity, better health — and in politics the objective is itself disputed. That is a conclusion drawn from the chapter's own material, and it is the kind of internal analysis a "critically examine" stem rewards.

### 3. Social criticism and reconstruction

The second head belongs primarily to the philosophical component. Political philosophy is primarily concerned with right and wrong, good and evil in social life. When we find something wrong in our society and polity, we look for **logical grounds** for criticising it and speculate about the creation of a good society (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.21).

The chapter then names a roll of philosophers who have pointed to prevailing ills and offered their own schemes of social reconstruction: **Plato, Aristotle, St. Augustine, Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Hegel, Marx, Mill and Macpherson**.

Four of them are given specific contributions, and these four are the ones to carry into an answer because they convert a list into evidence (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.21):

| Thinker | Contribution as the chapter states it |
|---|---|
| **Plato** | Brilliantly exposed the *modus operandi* of selfish and cunning politicians in a democracy |
| **Machiavelli** | Vividly described the character of selfish and greedy people |
| **Marx** | Analysed the sources of conflict between the owners and non-owners of property |
| **Macpherson** | Pointed to the intricacies of the power structure in contemporary society which obstructs the way to the creative freedom of the individual |

### 4. The qualification that makes this head defensible

The chapter attaches a limitation of its own, and it is unusually candid: **we cannot accept any of these proposals as the final truth.** But they give us ample insights into the possible ills of social life and their remedies, and we can draw our own scheme of social reconstruction on the basis of these insights (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.21).

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** This reframes the entire tradition. Its value is **diagnostic rather than prescriptive**: Plato is useful not because his ideal city should be built but because his account of demagogic politics is penetrating; Marx is useful not because his end-state must be accepted but because his analysis of propertied conflict is illuminating. A candidate who uses the tradition this way can cite freely without appearing to endorse, which is a practical advantage in a GS answer where endorsement of any thinker's programme is rarely wanted.

Note also that this is the chapter's own answer to the objection it will later record from Easton — that political theory lived parasitically on century-old ideas. The reply implicit here is that the classics are being used as a stock of diagnoses, not as a stock of blueprints.

### 5. How to deploy this head without over-claiming

- Use the four named contributions, not the eleven-name list. A list demonstrates memory; a contribution demonstrates use.
- Always attach the qualification ("none of these proposals is the final truth; they supply insights from which we construct our own"). It costs one clause and converts a reverent paragraph into a judgment.
- When a stem invites you to say political theory is impractical, answer with the first head *and* its limits: causal understanding genuinely enables intervention, but every analogy for that claim presupposes an agreed objective, which is why the second head is required.

### 1. Why the problem is specific to political language

**Wolin** supplies the diagnosis, quoted in the chapter:

> "there is the widespread tendency to utilize the same words and notions in describing non-political phenomena that we do in talking about political matters. In contrast to the restricted technical usages of mathematics and the natural sciences, phrases like 'the authority of the father', 'the authority of the church', or 'the authority of Parliament' are evidence of the parallel usages prevailing in social and political discussions." (Wolin, *Politics and Vision*, 1960, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.22)

So when we use terms of common parlance in political discourse, it becomes very important to determine their technical meaning. Compounding this, terms such as authority, social class, liberty, equality, justice and democracy are applied by different schools of thought to indicate different ideas, and political philosophy tries to determine a precise meaning acceptable to upholders of different ideologies (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.22).

Connect this back to Lesson 1. The *political* was defined by contrast with other spheres — but its core vocabulary is shared with those spheres. That is precisely why the boundary has to be policed conceptually rather than assumed.

### 2. What agreement on meaning does and does not buy

The chapter is careful here, and the care is examinable. Agreement on the meaning of the terms of political discourse **does not necessarily mean** that the parties come to accept each other's viewpoint. But it certainly **paves the way for their dialogue**. The example given: if a liberal and a socialist accept the same meaning of "freedom" or "equality", they are likely to appreciate each other's viewpoint (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.22).

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** This is a modest and therefore credible claim. Shared meaning converts a verbal dispute into a substantive one. Two people who use "equality" differently may be agreeing while appearing to disagree, or disagreeing while appearing to agree; once the term is fixed, the real disagreement becomes visible and can be argued. Clarification does not resolve conflict — it *locates* it. Writing that sentence in an answer is worth more than a paragraph praising conceptual precision.

### 3. The political edge: demagogues, autocrats, and the dated example

As long as precise meanings are not determined, some people may apply terms cleverly enough to **conceal a weak point** in their argument. Some selfish leaders and demagogues may use these terms to **mislead people by creating an emotional appeal and evading reason**, and autocrats may apply them to **legitimize oppressive regimes** — as the chapter's illustration has it, as **Mussolini (1883-1945)** did in Italy (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.22).

📰 **Dating discipline, applied here rather than deferred.** The Mussolini reference is the source's own historical illustration, and it is used here exactly as the source uses it: as a completed historical case, not as a live comparison and not as a template for naming any contemporary government, party or leader. This topic's boundary rules out such naming, and Lesson 16 explains why the rule is methodological rather than merely cautious.

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The three failure modes are distinct and should be kept distinct in an answer: concealment is a logical fault, emotional appeal is a rhetorical one, and legitimation is an institutional one. Vague language enables all three, but a candidate who names which of the three is operating in a given case is performing analysis rather than denunciation.

### 4. The constructive side: not reinventing the wheel

A precise and widely accepted definition also enables each thinker to build an argument on a sound footing, and the chapter's image is memorable: as every innovative mechanic need not invent a wheel to assemble a new machine, so every new thinker need not devise new terminology to present a point of view (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.22).

⚠️ This has a direct exam application. A candidate who redefines standard terms in an answer forfeits the accumulated work the terms carry and forces the examiner to reconstruct the argument from scratch. Using the discipline's established vocabulary precisely is not conformism; it is how an argument gets to start from the current state of the subject rather than from zero.

### 5. Encouragement to mutual respect and toleration

The fourth head: the tradition of political theory encourages a dignified debate between upholders of different points of view. Most political philosophers from ancient times to the present have dwelt on common problems and given new insights. **Hacker** is quoted:

> "Political theory is a never-ending conversation among theorists. And while the greatest of the debates are never resolved, the criticisms which the writers make of each other are always most vivid and illuminating…. Politics is, after all, the most democratic of sciences. The final judgements concerning political reality and the good life are the responsibility of all who undertake the study of theory." (Hacker, *Political Theory: Philosophy, Ideology, Science*, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.22)

Following that tradition inspires us to understand one another's viewpoint, to identify the strengths and weaknesses of our own thought, to convince others and be convinced when truth is discovered. In short, political theory generates **mutual respect and toleration** and prompts us to resolve our differences peacefully (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.22-23).

⚠️ **The honest reading of Hacker's sentence.** "The greatest of the debates are never resolved" is not a complaint; it is the point. A subject whose central debates were resolved would have no further need of the conversation. The claim is that the *criticisms* are illuminating even when the disputes are not settled — which is a defensible account of what a humanities discipline produces, and a far better answer to "what is political theory for?" than a promise of eventual consensus.

### 6. The three functions — the chapter's conclusion

The chapter then consolidates. Broadly speaking, political theory consists of political science and political philosophy; taken together, these two branches perform three functions recognised as the functions of political theory (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.23):

| Function | Carried mainly by | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Description** | Political science | It relies mainly on the empirical method — knowledge based on practical experience, supposed to be most reliable |
| **Criticism** | Political philosophy | It is concerned with value-judgment |
| **Reconstruction** | Political philosophy | Same ground: evaluating what is wrong implies proposing what should replace it |

Map the four heads onto the three functions and the architecture of the whole chapter becomes visible: control of social life is description put to work; social criticism and reconstruction are the second and third functions by name; clarification of concepts and the encouragement of toleration are the conditions under which criticism and reconstruction can proceed at all.

### 7. Held's warning, and why it closes this block

The chapter records the renewed emphasis on values since post-behaviouralism (1969) and the revival of political philosophy in the 1970s and 1980s, noting that value-judgment serves as an essential guide to social policy, that indifference to value-judgment will leave society in the dark, and that emerging concerns with **environmentalism, feminism, human rights and social justice for the subaltern groups** call for exploring new horizons of value-judgment. If political theory relinquishes this function, it may be grabbed by some less competent agency. **David Held**, in *Political Theory Today* (1991, Editor's Introduction), is quoted:

> "Taken as a whole, the tasks of political theory are unquestionably demanding. In the absence of their systematic pursuit, there is always the danger that politics will be left to the ignorant and self-interested, or to those simply with a 'will to power'." (Held, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.23)

⚠️ **Limit, stated honestly.** Held's sentence is itself a value-judgment. It cannot serve as a neutral, empirical demonstration that political theory is necessary; it is an argument *within* the normative enterprise it defends. That does not weaken it — Lesson 3 established that evaluative claims can be reasoned — but a candidate who presents Held as proof rather than as argument has walked into the circularity an examiner is looking for.

The chapter closes the block with a claim about scale rather than about method: all the functions of political theory have now become very important and urgent in a world where **most of our problems are assuming global dimensions and are being recognised as the problems of humanity as such** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.23).

📰 **Date-bounding this sentence.** "Now" and "present-day" there mean the position as of the Fifth Edition (2009); the claim is the book's reading of its own moment, not a dated statement about any later year. Used with that qualifier it is still strong, because it supplies the reason the four heads matter beyond a single polity: once a problem is recognised as humanity's, the question of which values should govern the response cannot be settled by any one society's existing consensus — which is exactly what Lipset's thesis in Lesson 11 assumes can be done.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does the chapter treat the clarification of concepts as a *political* achievement rather than a merely linguistic one? Answer in the source's own terms and add the limit.

**Model answer:** Because unclarified political terms can be put to three political uses: concealing a weak point in an argument, misleading people by creating an emotional appeal that evades reason, and legitimizing oppressive regimes (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.22). Clarification removes the equivocation on which each of those depends, and it does so under conditions that make equivocation unusually easy, since political vocabulary is shared with non-political spheres — Wolin's parallel usages of "authority" — and the same term is used by rival schools to mean different things. The positive claim is correspondingly modest: shared meaning does not produce shared viewpoints, but it converts a verbal dispute into a substantive one and so paves the way for dialogue. The limit is that clarification is not neutral: its third operation recommends a usage, so deciding what "sovereignty" or "freedom" shall mean is itself a contested move, and a clarification can entrench a position as easily as it can expose one.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the fourth head collapse into a pleasantry about tolerance. The claim is specific — that a tradition of sustained mutual criticism produces illuminating objections even where it produces no settled conclusions, and that final judgements rest with everyone who studies theory. "Political theory makes people tolerant" is neither what the text says nor defensible as stated.

### 1. Reading the six charges properly

All six are in the source, and they are of three different kinds. Separating the kinds is what turns a recitation into an analysis (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.24).

| Kind of charge | Which charges | What it would take to refute it |
|---|---|---|
| **Methodological** | 1, 2 | Show that traditional theory did rest on observation of political reality, or that classical study is compatible with scientific foundations |
| **Historical/sociological** | 3, 4 | Show that the tradition was not merely a product of past turmoil, or point to an outstanding political philosopher after Mill and Marx |
| **Practical/institutional** | 5, 6 | Show that the discipline did possess usable tools, or that it was not in fact sidelined in wartime decision-making |

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** Charges 5 and 6 are the ones most candidates ignore, and they are the most concrete. They do not say traditional theory was intellectually worthless; they say it left the discipline *unequipped* — unable to explain the two great political formations of the century and unable to contribute when governments were actually deciding. That is a professional grievance as much as an epistemological one, and recognising it explains why the remedy proposed was institutional (get closer to the other social sciences, take a place in decision-making) and not merely philosophical.

Charge 4 deserves a note of its own. The claim that there has been no outstanding political philosopher after Marx and Mill is a claim about a period, made in 1953, by a participant in the dispute. ⚠️ It is a *dated judgment*, not a timeless fact; the chapter itself will later record a list of writers whose work was taken to constitute a revival. An answer that reports Charge 4 without that framing has handed the examiner an easy counter-example.

### 2. The remedy, and the specific shape of the value clause

Easton appealed for building up a **behavioural political science**, closer to other social sciences, so as to take its due place in the decision-making process (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.24).

The accompanying claim about values is precise, and precision matters because this is where Easton is most often misreported. He suggested that while traditional political theory was primarily concerned with **evolving suitable values for society**, modern political science **need not make efforts in this direction**. His ground: values represent individual or group preferences *relative to the social conditions in which these are developed*, and contemporary society would evolve its own value system from its own experience and insight. Political scientists should focus on building **causal theory** to explain political behaviour.

The chapter boxes the key term: **causal theory** is the theory that explains the relation between cause and effect — it inquires into the cause of what happens, and anticipates what will happen if a certain cause is present (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.24).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the most useful distinction in this lesson.** Easton's position is *not* that values do not matter. It is that the task of evolving them is **not the political scientist's job**, because society will perform it autonomously. That is a claim about the **division of intellectual labour**, not a claim that value-questions are meaningless. Logical positivism says evaluative statements lack content; Easton says they are someone else's business. The two are often run together, and keeping them apart is worth a mark in any question that mentions both.

### 3. What the critique gets right

The chapter does not deny the problem, and nor should an answer. Easton's criticism bites hardest where traditional theory merely repeats classic texts without explaining contemporary political behaviour. A tradition that could not say why fascism arose, or why communist regimes persisted, had a genuine explanatory deficit, and "we have read Plato carefully" is not a response to it.

⚠️ Conceding this is strategically important. A "critically examine" answer that treats Easton as obviously wrong has nothing left to explain the subsequent fifteen years of behavioural dominance, nor the fact that the chief prosecutor's own later position preserved scientific method rather than abandoning it.

### 4. What it gets wrong, in advance

Two weaknesses can be flagged now and developed in Lessons 12 and 13:

- **The remedy over-corrects the diagnosis.** The diagnosis is that theory lacked causal tools; the remedy suspends the quest for values. ⚠️ But lacking tools for explanation is not a reason to abandon justification; those are different functions, as Lesson 5's Raphael distinction established. A discipline could acquire causal tools *and* retain the normative function.
- **The value clause has an empirical premise it does not test.** "Contemporary society would evolve its own value system from its own experience and insight" is itself an empirical prediction about societies — exactly the sort of claim Easton demands be supported by observation. ⚠️ It is offered without that support, and the crises of the 1960s are precisely what led him to withdraw it.

### 5. Exam use

A stem such as "Political theory declined in the mid-twentieth century. Examine." requires Easton's charges stated as charges, sorted into their three kinds, and then tested. A stem that names behaviouralism requires Charge 5 and the remedy, because the professional-equipment argument is what explains the behavioural turn's appeal. In both cases, naming the 1953 work and the sorted charges is what distinguishes a reconstructed argument from a remembered label.

### Concept check

**Question:** Distinguish Easton's 1953 position on values from the logical-positivist position taught in Lesson 3, and say why the distinction changes how each must be answered.

**Model answer:** The logical-positivist position is epistemological: evaluative statements have no empirical content and no logical structure, so they are expressions of subjective reflection or emotional preference and cannot be objects of knowledge at all (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.16-17). He argued that one mode of theorising lacked observation, tools and relevance, and proposed a reconstruction of the discipline. The flat death-notice is a different claim made by a different writer, and Lesson 11 handles it separately.

```text
                     that it cannot be analysed by any partial or narrow theory
                                          |
                     PLUS: LOGICAL POSITIVISTS who concentrated on facts to the
                     exclusion of values were ALSO responsible for the decline
                                          |
                                          v
                     BUT: "all was not yet lost" -- political science has to
                     answer questions that the methodology of social sciences
                     may not be able to answer; it MUST EVOLVE CRITERIA OF
                     JUDGMENT which will revive the relevance of political science
```

Three features of Cobban's argument are worth naming explicitly in an answer:

- **It is symmetrical.** Decline under capitalism *and* communism. This blocks the lazy reading in which the decline thesis is a complaint about one ideological bloc.
- **It blames the positivists too.** Cobban treats the exclusion of values from inquiry as one of the *causes* of decline. ⚠️ That places him on the opposite side from Easton even though both are classed under "decline" — Easton diagnoses too much value-talk and too little observation; Cobban diagnoses, among other things, too little value-talk. Noticing this is the single sharpest point available in this lesson.
- **It ends constructively.** "All was not yet lost": political science must evolve criteria of judgment to revive its relevance, because there are questions social-science methodology cannot answer. Cobban's decline thesis therefore already contains the revival's central demand.

### 2. Lipset: the search, not the subject

**Seymour Martin Lipset**, in *Political Man: The Social Bases of Politics* (1960), argued that the values of contemporary society had already been decided: in the United States the age-old search for the **good society** had come to an end because it had already been achieved. The prevailing form of democracy in that country was, in the sentence the chapter quotes, "**the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation**." Thus Lipset too questioned the continued relevance of political theory in those days (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.25).

Notice what Lipset is and is not saying.

- He is **not** saying political theory lacks method, as Easton does.
- He is **not** saying systems have no use for it, as Cobban does.
- He is saying the **normative search has been completed** — in one country, at one time. The claim is therefore *contextual*, and the chapter's own phrase "in those days" marks it as such.

📰 **Dating discipline, applied.** Lipset's claim is a mid-twentieth-century contextual thesis about Western, and specifically United States, democracy as it then appeared to him. It is not a timeless factual conclusion about all societies, and it must never be reported as one. An answer that writes "Lipset showed that democracy is the good society" has converted a dated, situated argument into a universal claim, which is both a factual error and precisely the failure this topic exists to prevent.

⚠️ **The connection to the next topic, and its limit.** Lipset's thesis sits inside the broader end-of-ideology context that the following topic in this subject owns. Here, use it only for what it contributes to the decline debate: a claim that theory is redundant because its question has been answered. Do not import the end-of-ideology debate's machinery into this lesson.

### 3. Laslett: the death notice, flagged as an addition

⚠️ **Provenance flag, stated before the content.** The following does **not** appear in this topic's source chapter and carries no page citation from it. It is included because the decline debate is routinely taught with it and because its omission leaves a visible gap; its wording was verified independently in this pass, and no page, edition or further claim is asserted.

**Peter Laslett**, in the editorial Introduction to the first series of *Philosophy, Politics and Society* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1956), wrote: "For the moment, anyway, political philosophy is dead."

Three observations make this usable rather than decorative:

- The **qualifier is in the sentence**. "For the moment, anyway" is not a timeless death certificate; it is a diagnosis of a situation, offered by an editor who was in the act of publishing a volume of political philosophy. Quoting the sentence without its opening clause changes its meaning, which is itself a useful demonstration of why verified wording matters.
- It is a **report, not an argument**. Unlike Easton and Cobban, Laslett offers no reconstruction of the discipline's failures and no remedy. Its evidential weight is as testimony about how the field looked to a well-placed observer, not as a demonstration that the field had failed.
- It supplies the **sharpest contrast** with the revival. A field pronounced dead in 1956 and described as "in ascendancy" by 1967 is the clearest possible illustration of the difference between the decline of a mode and the disappearance of a subject.

### 4. The decisive move: three claims, three scopes

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the thesis sentence for most questions on this debate.** Easton, Cobban, Lipset and Laslett are making claims of different scope:

```text
   EASTON   -> a MODE of theorising has failed (speculative, classicist, normative)
               remedy: replace the mode
   COBBAN   -> theory's SIGNIFICANCE has collapsed in both system-types
               remedy: evolve criteria of judgment
   LIPSET   -> the normative SEARCH is over, in one society, at one time
               remedy: none needed, by hypothesis
   LASLETT  -> the field is, FOR THE MOMENT, dead
               remedy: none offered
```

None of the four says that systematic knowledge of political phenomena had ceased to be possible or necessary. Running them together into a flat chronology — "political theory ended in the 1950s and revived in the 1970s" — is the standard error on this topic, and it is an error with three separate faults: it merges claims of different scope, it ignores that Cobban's own conclusion demands revival, and it misdates the turn, since the reversal begins inside behaviouralism itself in 1969.

Revision notes:
- **Cobban, "The Decline of Political Theory", *Political Science Quarterly* (1953):** theory lost significance in **capitalist as well as communist** systems.
- Capitalist side: inspired by **libertarian democracy**, yet with **no political theorist of democracy**; overwhelming role of **bureaucracy**; creation of a huge **military machine**; theory had practically no role in sustaining the system.
- Communist side: a **new form of party organization** and the **rule of a small oligarchy**; theory took a back seat.
- Cobban on his predecessors: **Hegel** was concerned with the **territorial state**, **Marx** with the **proletariat class**; each sought what was predestined within his own frame of reference; contemporary politics is too large in scale for any partial or narrow theory.
- Cobban also blames **logical positivists** who concentrated on facts to the exclusion of values. ⚠️ On this point he stands opposite Easton while sharing the "decline" label.
- Cobban's conclusion: **all was not yet lost** — political science must answer questions social-science methodology cannot, and must **evolve criteria of judgment**.
- **Lipset, *Political Man: The Social Bases of Politics* (1960):** the values of contemporary society had already been decided; in the United States the search for the **good society** had ended because it had been achieved; the prevailing form of democracy there was "**the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation**."
- 📰 Lipset's claim is **contextual and dated** — one country, one period. Never report it as a timeless conclusion.
- ⚠️ **Laslett, Introduction to *Philosophy, Politics and Society*, First Series (Oxford: Blackwell, 1956)** — outside the source chapter, verified independently in this pass: "For the moment, anyway, political philosophy is dead." Note the qualifier; note that it is testimony rather than argument.
- **The decisive distinction:** Easton attacks a *mode*; Cobban reports collapsed *significance*; Lipset declares the *search* complete in one place; Laslett records a *moment*. None asserts that systematic knowledge of politics had become impossible.
- ❌ Never write the flat chronology "theory died in the 1950s and revived in the 1970s".

### Concept check

**Question:** Both Easton (1953) and Cobban (1953) are classed under "the decline of political theory". Identify the point on which they are in direct opposition, and explain why that opposition matters for an answer.

**Model answer:** They are in direct opposition on the role of values in the decline. Easton's diagnosis is that traditional theory was too speculative and too concerned with evolving values, and too little equipped with causal tools; his remedy is a behavioural political science that need not pursue values at all (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.24). Cobban, by contrast, lists the **logical positivists who sought to concentrate on facts to the exclusion of values** among the causes of the decline, and concludes that political science must **evolve criteria of judgment** to recover its relevance (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.25). So one writer treats value-concern as part of the disease and the other treats its absence as part of the disease. This matters because an answer that groups them under a single "decline school" cannot explain the revival at all: Cobban's remedy is already the revival's demand, which is why the debate is better described as a dispute about the proper balance between explanation and justification than as a quarrel between modern and old writers.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat Lipset as a general theorist of the end of political theory. His claim is that one society had, in his judgment at that time, already achieved the good society, so the normative search was over there. Detaching it from its country and its period turns a dated situated argument into a universal assertion — exactly the error this topic trains you to avoid.

This is the pivot of the whole topic. One sentence of it, properly understood, answers most questions that can be set on the decline debate.

**Easton changed his view after one and a half decades.** In his presidential address to the American Political Science Association in 1969 he launched his **post-behavioural revolution**. In fact, Easton was trying to convert political science from a **pure science** into an **applied science**. He insisted that scientific investigation should enable contemporary society to tide over the prevailing crisis. This also involved a **renewed concern with values**, which had been sought to be excluded in the earlier behavioural approach (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.24-25).

### 1. What exactly changed, and what did not

Set it out as a three-column audit, because candidates routinely assume more changed than did:

| Element | 1953 position | 1969 position | Changed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scientific method for the study of facts | Essential | Retained | **No** |
| Causal explanation of behaviour | The discipline's task | Still valid | **No** |
| Concern with values | Not the political scientist's job | Revived | **Yes** |
| Relation to society's crises | Relevance sought through decision-making access | Science made subservient to human values and ends; applied to crises | **Yes** |
| Status of the discipline | Pure science | Applied science | **Yes** |

The first two rows are the ones to say out loud in an answer. Post-behaviouralism **heralds revival of concern with 'values' without compromising scientific method for the study of 'facts'** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19). It is a correction of behaviouralism's *scope*, not a repudiation of its *method*.

### 2. Why the reversal is evidentially powerful

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The 1969 turn is not merely one more opinion in the debate. It has a special evidential weight for three reasons:

- **Source credibility.** It comes from the author of the most influential statement of the behavioural case. A concession from the strongest advocate of a position counts for more than an objection from its opponents.
- **It is internal.** The revival therefore cannot be described as traditionalists refusing to accept progress. The behavioural programme generated the correction from inside itself.
- **It is a scope correction, not a defeat.** Because method is retained, the 1969 turn does not vindicate the claim that science is useless — which is why an answer should use it against *both* over-claims, the one that says theory died and the one that says science is worthless.

### 3. The decisive distinction, stated for use

⚠️ The move that unlocks any unfamiliar question on this topic is this: Easton (1953), Cobban and Lipset describe the decline of one particular **mode** of political theorising — classical, speculative, historicist and explicitly normative — not the disappearance of political theory as an enterprise. Conflating the two is the single most common trap on this topic.

Three consequences follow, and each is directly usable:

- A question asking whether political theory declined should be answered **yes, in a qualified sense**: a mode lost credibility. Answering a flat "no" ignores a real episode; answering a flat "yes" concedes far too much.
- A question asking whether the revival was a rejection of science should be answered **no**, and Easton's own formula is the evidence.
- A question asking *when* the revival began should be answered **1969, from inside behaviouralism**, with the 1970s and 1980s as the period of consolidation — which is how the chapter itself dates the renewed emphasis on values (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.23).

### 4. The objection worth taking seriously

⚠️ **Objection.** The 1969 turn might be read as institutional rather than intellectual: a discipline adjusting its public posture during a period of social crisis, not revising its epistemology. On that reading, post-behaviouralism is behaviouralism plus relevance rhetoric, and the fundamental commitment to causal explanation is untouched.

⚠️ **Reply.** The reading has some force, and it should be conceded rather than brushed aside — the explicit motivation *was* the prevailing crisis, and the method *was* retained. But it does not survive the specific content of the turn. Converting the discipline from a pure to an applied science, and making the achievements of political science **subservient to human values and ends**, is a change in what the discipline is *for*, and a discipline's purpose is not a merely presentational matter. ⚠️ **Residual:** the objection does establish that 1969 settles less than it appears to. It restores values to the agenda without specifying how value-questions are to be adjudicated — which is exactly the gap that Strauss, Germino and Marcuse move into, and exactly why Lesson 13 is necessary rather than merely additional.

### 5. The sentence to carry into the exam

⚠️ A defensible one-line verdict for an unfamiliar question: political theory did not die; a narrow, ahistorical, facts-only variant of it lost credibility, and the discipline recovered by re-admitting values without discarding evidence.

Revision notes:
- **Easton changed his view after one and a half decades**; in his **presidential address to the American Political Science Association in 1969** he launched the **post-behavioural revolution** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.24-25).
- The content: scientific investigation should enable contemporary society to **tide over the prevailing crisis**; political science moves from **pure science** to **applied science**; there is a **renewed concern with values** that the earlier behavioural approach had sought to exclude.
- The chapter's compact formula: post-behaviouralism makes the achievements of political science **subservient to human values and ends** and heralds **revival of concern with values without compromising scientific method for the study of facts** (PDF p.19).
- What did **not** change: scientific method for facts; causal explanation of behaviour.
- What **did** change: the place of values; the discipline's relation to social crisis; its status as pure or applied.
- ⚠️ The turn is evidentially powerful because it is a concession by the strongest advocate, it is internal to behaviouralism, and it corrects scope rather than conceding defeat.
- ⚠️ **The decisive distinction:** Easton (1953), Cobban and Lipset describe the decline of a **mode** — classical, speculative, historicist, explicitly normative — not the disappearance of political theory. Conflating them is the commonest trap.
- Dating: the reversal begins in **1969, inside behaviouralism**; consolidation runs through the **1970s and 1980s**.
- ⚠️ Objection: the turn may be institutional rather than epistemological. Reply: changing what a discipline is *for* is not presentational. Residual: 1969 restores values without saying how value-disputes are to be adjudicated.
- One-line verdict: theory did not die; a facts-only variant lost credibility, and the discipline recovered by re-admitting values without discarding evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** "Post-behaviouralism rejected the scientific study of politics." State why this is false, and then state the strongest *true* claim in its neighbourhood.

**Model answer:** It is false because the post-behavioural approach insists on making the achievements of political science subservient to human values and ends and so heralds a revival of concern with values **without compromising scientific method for the study of facts** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19); Easton's own 1969 move was to convert political science from a pure into an applied science so that scientific investigation could help society tide over the prevailing crisis (PDF pp.24-25). Method is retained throughout; what is corrected is scope and purpose. The strongest true claim nearby is this: post-behaviouralism rejected the *sufficiency* of a value-excluding political science — the claim that causal explanation alone constitutes the discipline's task — while affirming the necessity of scientific method for the study of facts. Stated that way the sentence is defensible, and it also explains why the position could be adopted by the leading behaviouralist rather than only by his critics.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not date the revival to the 1970s as though it arrived from outside. The chapter records renewed emphasis on values **since the advent of post-behaviouralism (1969)** and the consequent revival of political philosophy in the 1970s and 1980s. Getting the sequence right is what allows you to say that the correction came from inside the behavioural camp — the most valuable single observation available on this topic.

### 1. Strauss: a science that cannot rank

**Leo Strauss**, in his paper "What is Political Philosophy?" published in the *Journal of Politics* (1957) and in "An Epilogue" to *Essays on the Scientific Study of Politics* (edited by Herbert J. Storing, 1962), argued that the new science of politics was in fact a **symptom** of the alleged decline of political theory. By adopting a positivist approach it had ignored the challenge of normative issues. Empirical theory of politics asserts the **equal importance of all social values**; it denies that certain things are intrinsically high while others are intrinsically low. Thus, in the chapter's rendering of Strauss's charge, it **obliterates the distinction between men and brutes**, as if it destroys the identity of clean water by mixing it with dirty water (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.25-26).

⚠️ Note the structure of the charge, because it is more interesting than "Strauss disliked behaviouralism". The claim is not that political scientists personally hold bad values. It is that a method committed to treating all values as equally weighted *cannot represent* the ordering that political life actually contains. A discipline with no apparatus for ranking cannot say that justice stands above tyranny; it can only record that some people prefer one and some the other.

The chapter also boxes the target: **positivism** is the view that relies on scientific method as the only source of true knowledge, rejecting superstition, religion and metaphysics as pre-scientific forms of thought, and holding that all knowledge is ultimately based on sense-experience, so that empirical method must be adopted for any genuine inquiry in the social as well as the physical sciences (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.26).

⚠️ **The reply Strauss has to face.** The argument risks over-claiming: from "empirical method cannot by itself rank values" it does not follow that empirical work on politics is worthless. The chapter does not endorse that stronger conclusion, and nor should an answer. The defensible version is that positivism is inadequate *as an account of the whole subject*, which leaves the scientific component intact in its own domain. ⚠️ **Residual:** Strauss's objection shows that a ranking is needed; it does not by itself supply the procedure by which a ranking is to be established and defended. That gap is the one Lesson 3's dialogue argument tries to fill, with its own acknowledged "probably".

### 2. Germino: the revival as a recovery of identity

**Dante Germino**, in *Beyond Ideology: The Revival of Political Theory* (1967), identified two major causes of decline across most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: the rise of **positivism**, which led to the craze for science; and the prevalence of **political ideologies**, culminating in Marxism (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.26).

But, he argued, political theory was now again in the ascendant. The chapter records two lists, and the second is the more useful because of its range:

| List | Writers named |
|---|---|
| In *Beyond Ideology* (1967) | Michael Oakeshott, Hannah Arendt, Bertrand de Jouvenel, Leo Strauss, Eric Voegelin |
| Expanded in a subsequent paper (1975) | John Rawls, C. B. Macpherson, Christian Bay, Robert Nozick, Herbert Marcuse, Jürgen Habermas, Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Walzer |

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and a strong answer-point.** Both lists bear on Easton's fourth charge — that no outstanding political philosopher had appeared after Marx and Mill — but they bear on it differently, and the difference is worth stating precisely. The 1967 list contests the charge *at its own moment*, since Oakeshott, Arendt, de Jouvenel, Strauss and Voegelin were already writing when Easton made it in 1953. The 1975 list shows that the charge did not survive the following two decades, since Rawls, Nozick, Macpherson, Habermas, MacIntyre and Walzer largely published after 1953 — so it refutes the claim's continuing force rather than its accuracy about 1953. ⚠️ Note also that two names in these lists are normalised: the local copy prints de Jouvenel's surname with a final "a" and renders Alasdair MacIntyre as "Alasdaire Maclntyre", both apparently scanning variants, so the standard forms are used throughout this session rather than the variants. Observing that the chapter supplies, in its own pages, the material that answers one of the charges it reports is the kind of internal cross-reading that separates a strong answer from a competent one.

Germino's positive proposal is definitional. To understand political theory's new role it is imperative to **identify it with political philosophy**, understood as a **critical study of the principles of right order in human social existence**, involving inquiry into right and wrong. It is **neither** reductionist behavioural science, where everything is reduced to sense-experience, **nor** opinionated ideology, which accepts some principles as true without inquiring into their validity. It comprehends both the knowledge of facts and the insight with which that knowledge is comprehended (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.26).

Three further Germino positions, each usable:

- Political philosophy deals with **perennial problems** confronting man in his social existence.
- **Detachment is not ethical neutrality.** A political philosopher cannot remain indifferent to the political struggle of his times, as a behaviouralist would claim. This is the precise answer to the worry raised at the end of Lesson 7: detachment as the keynote of theory means freedom from interested special pleading, not indifference to the outcome.
- Political philosophy **cannot grow alongside positivism**, which abstains from a critical examination of any social situation; the gulf between the traditionalist and behaviouralist components is **so wide that they cannot be 'reunited'**, and any theory separated from the perennial concerns of political philosophy will prove irrelevant.

Germino's complaint is then sharpened: behavioural political theory has often implicitly or uncritically **endorsed the policies and practices of the established order** instead of performing the Socratic function of **speaking truth to power**; and full recovery of critical political theory cannot be achieved within the **positivist universe of discourse** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.26-27).

⚠️ **A tension worth naming, because examiners reward it.** Germino says the two components **cannot be reunited**; the chapter's own conclusion says that since the 1970s the dispute has **largely subsided** and that the two play **complementary** roles. These are not the same position. The honest reading is that Germino states the strongest separatist case within the revival, and that the chapter does not adopt it. An answer that reports the chapter's settlement as if everyone in the revival accepted it has flattened a real disagreement.

### 3. Marcuse: when measurement becomes control

**Herbert Marcuse** (1898-1979) pointed to the risk involved in the demand for a scientific study of society and politics. When the language of social science attempts to conform to the language of natural science, it tends to **lend support to the status quo**. Scientific terminology is then defined in terms of operations and behaviour capable of observation and measurement, and this leaves **no scope for a critical vision** in the scientific language (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.27).

The illustration is exact, and it should be reproduced rather than paraphrased loosely: when people's participation is estimated on the basis of the number of voters who turn up at elections, **we do not question whether the prevailing electoral system conforms to the spirit of democracy**. When this method of study is adopted, social science no longer remains an instrument of social inquiry; it becomes an **instrument of social control**.

⚠️ **Why this is the most portable of the three objections.** Strauss's objection requires you to accept that values have an intrinsic ordering. Germino's requires a definitional commitment about what political theory is. Marcuse's requires neither: it is a claim about what happens when a concept is replaced by its measurable proxy. Any indicator you can name — participation measured by turnout, welfare measured by disbursement, development measured by output — carries the same structure. Note the mechanism precisely: the proxy does not merely approximate the concept, it **displaces the question** the concept was there to ask.

⚠️ **The reply Marcuse must face.** Without measurement there is no evidence, and without evidence criticism becomes assertion. The defensible position is therefore not to abandon indicators but to keep the concept and its proxy distinct, and to treat the gap between them as an open question rather than a settled equivalence. ⚠️ **Residual:** Marcuse's argument identifies a risk inherent in operationalisation; it does not give a criterion for deciding when a given proxy has become a displacement rather than an approximation. That judgment remains to be made case by case.

### 4. The post-1970s settlement

Since the 1970s, the chapter records, the dispute between political science and political philosophy has **largely subsided**, and it names the traffic in both directions (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.27):

| Movement | Named instance |
|---|---|
| Behaviouralism admitting values | **David Easton** showed a renewed concern with values in his post-behavioural approach |
| Philosophy admitting empirical method | **Karl Popper** (1902-94), an eminent exponent of scientific method, proceeded to draw conclusions regarding social values |
| Philosophy admitting empirical method | **John Rawls** (1921-2002) adopted empirical method for arriving at his principles of justice |
| Philosophical critique of empirical theory | **C. B. Macpherson** (1911-87) attacked the empirical theory of democracy propounded by **Joseph Schumpeter** (1883-1950) and **Robert Dahl**, advancing his own radical theory of democracy |
| Critical theory using empirical insight | **Herbert Marcuse** and **Jürgen Habermas** showed strong empirical insight in their critical analysis of contemporary capitalism |

And the closing formula, which is the sentence to end almost any answer on this topic with:

> It is now held that political science, like other social and natural sciences, enables us to **strengthen our means** but we will have to resort to political philosophy to **determine our ends**. Means and ends are **interdependent**; hence political science and political philosophy play complementary roles in our social life. (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.27)

⚠️ **Read the last clause carefully, because it is doing real work.** "Means and ends are interdependent" is stronger than "both are useful". It denies that ends can be fixed in ignorance of what is achievable, and it denies that means can be selected without reference to what they are for. That is why the settlement is an integration rather than a division of labour — and it is why the answer to "should political theory be scientific or philosophical?" is not "both" but "the question misdescribes the relation".

⚠️ **How to characterise the settled position in one phrase.** The chapter's own position is closer to a **critical empiricism** than to either crude positivism or free-floating moralism: evidence is required, and evidence is not self-interpreting. That phrase is an analytical characterisation rather than the source's own label, and it should be offered as such — but it is the most economical way to state in a conclusion what the settlement actually commits you to.

⚠️ **One standing caution about the three critics.** Strauss, Germino and Marcuse are best used as correctives to scientism, not as licence for evidence-free assertion. Each of them attacks a claim about what evidence *can settle*; none of them attacks the requirement that claims be evidenced. An answer that cites Marcuse to avoid engaging with data has borrowed his authority for the opposite of his argument, since his own complaint is that a particular way of handling data suppresses a question — which presupposes that the data matter.

### 5. The two-level reading of the whole debate

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the structure for a 20-mark answer.** The debate runs on two levels at once:

- a **methodological dispute** about what counts as valid evidence — Easton (1953) against Strauss and Germino; and
- a **civilizational dispute** about whether politics can ever avoid argument about ends — Strauss, Germino and Marcuse against Lipset's thesis that the good society had already been found.

The chapter's closing formula settles the first in favour of integration. It does not concede the second: theory can be scientifically disciplined *and* normatively serious at the same time. An answer that distinguishes the two levels and says which one the settlement resolves is doing something most answers on this topic never attempt.

Revision notes:
- **Strauss**, "What is Political Philosophy?" in the *Journal of Politics* (1957) and "An Epilogue" to *Essays on the Scientific Study of Politics* (ed. Herbert J. Storing, 1962): the new science of politics is a **symptom** of the alleged decline; positivism ignored the challenge of normative issues; empirical theory asserts the **equal importance of all social values** and denies that some things are intrinsically high and others low — obliterating the distinction between men and brutes, like destroying clean water by mixing it with dirty water.
- **Positivism (boxed):** scientific method as the only source of true knowledge; rejects superstition, religion and metaphysics as pre-scientific; all knowledge ultimately based on sense-experience; empirical method required in social as well as physical sciences.
- ⚠️ Strauss's limit: inadequacy of positivism as an account of the whole subject does not make empirical work worthless; and the objection shows a ranking is needed without supplying the procedure for establishing one.
- **Germino, *Beyond Ideology: The Revival of Political Theory* (1967):** two causes of decline — the rise of **positivism** and the craze for science, and the prevalence of **political ideologies culminating in Marxism**.
- Germino's 1967 revivalists: **Oakeshott, Arendt, Bertrand de Jouvenel, Strauss, Voegelin**; expanded in a 1975 paper to **Rawls, Macpherson, Christian Bay, Nozick, Marcuse, Habermas, MacIntyre, Walzer**.
- ⚠️ The 1967 list answers Easton's charge at its own moment; the 1975 list shows the charge did not remain true, since most of those writers published after 1953.
- Germino's proposal: identify political theory **with political philosophy** = critical study of the principles of **right order** in human social existence; **neither** reductionist behavioural science **nor** opinionated ideology; comprehends both knowledge of facts and the insight with which it is comprehended.
- Germino: political philosophy deals with **perennial problems**; **detachment is not ethical neutrality**; philosophy cannot grow alongside positivism; the gulf between traditionalist and behaviouralist components is **so wide they cannot be 'reunited'**; behavioural theory has uncritically endorsed the established order instead of **speaking truth to power**; full recovery is impossible within the **positivist universe of discourse**.
- ⚠️ Germino's "cannot be reunited" is in tension with the chapter's own settlement that the two are complementary. Name the tension; do not flatten it.
- **Marcuse (1898-1979):** when social-science language conforms to natural-science language it tends to **support the status quo**; operational definitions leave **no scope for a critical vision**; participation measured by turnout means we never ask whether the electoral system conforms to the **spirit of democracy**; social science becomes an instrument of **social control** rather than of inquiry.
- ⚠️ Marcuse's mechanism: the proxy **displaces the question** the concept was there to ask. His argument identifies the risk without supplying a criterion for when a proxy has crossed the line.
- **Post-1970s settlement:** Easton's renewed concern with values; **Popper** (1902-94) drawing conclusions about social values; **Rawls** (1921-2002) adopting empirical method for his principles of justice; **Macpherson** (1911-87) attacking the empirical theory of democracy of **Schumpeter** (1883-1950) and **Dahl**; **Marcuse and Habermas** bringing empirical insight to the critique of contemporary capitalism.
- **Closing formula:** political science strengthens our **means**; political philosophy determines our **ends**; means and ends are **interdependent**; the two play complementary roles.
- ⚠️ The settled position is best characterised as a **critical empiricism** — evidence is required, and evidence is not self-interpreting — rather than as crude positivism or free-floating moralism. Offer the phrase as analysis, not as the source's own label.
- ⚠️ Strauss, Germino and Marcuse are correctives to scientism, not licence for evidence-free assertion; each attacks what evidence can *settle*, none attacks the requirement to be evidenced.
- ⚠️ Two-level reading: a **methodological** dispute about valid evidence, settled in favour of integration; and a **civilizational** dispute about whether politics can avoid argument about ends, which the settlement does not concede.

### Concept check

**Question:** A stem asserts: "The revival of political theory was a rejection of the scientific study of politics. Critically examine." Give the finding your answer should reach, with the two pieces of evidence that establish it and the one concession it must make.

**Model answer:** The finding is that the claim fails as stated, and holds only in a much weaker form: what the revival rejected was the **sufficiency** of a value-excluding political science, not scientific method itself. Two pieces of evidence establish it. First, the chapter's own account of the post-1970s settlement records traffic in both directions — Popper drawing conclusions about social values, Rawls adopting empirical method for his principles of justice, Macpherson attacking Schumpeter's and Dahl's empirical theory of democracy with a rival theory of his own, and Marcuse and Habermas bringing empirical insight to their critique of contemporary capitalism (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.27). Second, the revival's most influential single move came from inside behaviouralism, since Easton's 1969 post-behavioural turn restored concern with values **without compromising scientific method for the study of facts** (PDF p.19). The concession the answer must make is Germino's: he held that the gulf between the traditionalist and behaviouralist components was so wide that they could not be 'reunited' and that full recovery was impossible within the positivist universe of discourse (PDF pp.26-27). So the strong separatist reading is genuinely present in the revival; it is simply not the position the chapter's settlement adopts, and the graded verdict should say exactly that.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present Strauss, Germino and Marcuse as three statements of one objection. Strauss attacks the inability of a value-neutral method to rank values; Germino attacks the identity of a theory that is neither philosophy nor ideology; Marcuse attacks the social function of a measured social science. Collapsing them loses two-thirds of the available content and makes the revival look like a mood rather than an argument.

### 1. Test case one: a preventive-detention power

⚠️ The case is stated generically on purpose; no statute, article or judgment is named here.

**The scientific question.** A behavioural approach can study compliance with the power, the pattern of opposition to it, its institutional effects, the frequency and duration of its use, and the characteristics of those subjected to it. All of that is empirical work and all of it is genuinely informative.

**The philosophical question.** None of that work answers whether liberty is being unjustifiably restricted. That question asks for **grounds**, which is Raphael's justification rather than explanation.

**What the pairing buys in an answer.** The two questions are not rivals; the second cannot be answered responsibly without the first. A justification of a restriction has to be a justification of *this* restriction, with its actual scope and effects. ⚠️ So the answer-shape is: establish the empirical picture, identify the standard being invoked, and test whether the empirical picture satisfies that standard. That is Hacker's two-way dependence used operationally rather than quoted.

### 2. Test case two: do elections deepen democracy?

**The scientific question.** Science can measure turnout, competitiveness, participation rates and the regularity of alternation.

**The philosophical question.** Philosophy must judge whether those patterns amount to meaningful freedom, equality and legitimacy.

**The trap, and this is Marcuse's point in operational form.** If participation is *defined* as turnout, the second question disappears — not because it has been answered but because the vocabulary no longer contains it. Marcuse's exact formulation is that we then do not question whether the prevailing electoral system conforms to the spirit of democracy (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.27).

| Concept | Common proxy | What the proxy cannot see |
|---|---|---|
| Participation | Voter turnout | Whether the available choices were meaningful |
| Political equality | One person, one vote | Whether unequal resources shape which options exist |
| Legitimacy | Absence of visible protest | Whether acquiescence is consent or resignation |

⚠️ The table is an inference drawn from Marcuse's mechanism, not a list from the source. Its use in an answer is to show *how* a measured concept can lose the question it was standing for; it should be presented as analysis, not as a finding.

### 3. Test case three: efficiency as a justification

**The claim.** A policy is defended on the ground that it is efficient.

**What science supplies.** Behavioural and administrative data may well show the claimed output — faster processing, wider coverage, lower unit cost.

**What philosophy must still ask.** Whether efficiency without justice or consent is politically defensible. Efficiency is a relation between means and a given end; it cannot validate the end. A policy can be efficient at doing something that should not be done.

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the reason this case is the most examinable of the three.** "Efficient" is an evaluative term masquerading as a descriptive one — precisely the smuggled value identified in Lesson 2. The analytical move is to ask: efficient *at what*, and *for whom*? Once the end is named, it becomes assessable; while it remains implicit, the measurement does its evaluating silently.

This is also the point at which the whole topic becomes usable in applied papers: when a governance debate is framed only in output terms — coverage numbers, disbursement speed, processing time — the explanation/justification distinction supplies the missing question, namely whether the underlying distribution of burdens and benefits is fair and whether the affected population's dignity and voice are respected.

### 4. The necessary-versus-sufficient check

Before concluding any answer in this subject, test whether the condition named in the question is merely **necessary**, actually **sufficient**, **both**, or **neither**. It is the cheapest reliable source of analytical marks in Political Theory, and it is directly connected to this topic because most over-claims in the decline debate are condition errors.

| Proposition from this topic | Necessary? | Sufficient? | What else is required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Empirical verification for political knowledge | Not even necessary for the whole subject — only for its scientific component | No | A procedure for reasoning about values |
| Causal tools for a relevant political science | Necessary | No | A view of the goals that makes findings significant (Hacker) |
| Measuring turnout to assess participation | Arguably necessary as evidence | **No** | A standard of meaningful choice the measure does not contain (Marcuse) |
| Restoring values to the agenda (1969) | Necessary for the revival | **No** | A procedure for adjudicating value-disputes — the residual Lesson 12 flagged |

⚠️ The fourth row is the one to use when a stem asks whether the post-behavioural turn resolved the debate. It did something necessary and did not do something sufficient, and saying exactly that is a graded verdict rather than a hedge.

### 5. The boundary rule, stated as a working practice

This topic supplies a **lens**, not a stock of facts. The specific, independently verified Indian material — constitutional provisions, institutional mandates, judgments, scheme data — belongs to the papers that own it, and must be drawn from your own verified preparation rather than improvised inside a theory answer.

Three practical consequences:

- **Do** write: "the explanation/justification distinction shows what an output-based defence leaves unanswered" and then attach *your own verified example*.
- **Do not** write a theory answer that names a provision or a judgment you have not verified. An unverified anchor converts a strong conceptual answer into a factual error.
- **Do** use generic or clearly dated illustrations where no verified example is available. The three test cases above are deliberately generic for that reason, and they remain fully usable.

Revision notes:
- The application move: **split** the claim into empirical and evaluative parts; ask the **science question** (what is the real situation, which regularities govern behaviour) and the **philosophy question** (on what grounds is this right); run the **conditions test**; name the **proxy risk**; insert your own verified example.
- **Preventive-detention case:** behavioural study of compliance, opposition and institutional effects is informative but does not answer whether liberty is unjustifiably restricted; the justification must be of *this* restriction, so it depends on the empirical picture — Hacker's two-way dependence in operation.
- **Elections case:** science measures turnout, competitiveness and participation; philosophy judges whether those patterns amount to meaningful freedom, equality and legitimacy. If participation is *defined* as turnout, the question vanishes from the vocabulary.
- ⚠️ Proxy table (inference, not source): participation/turnout; political equality/one person one vote; legitimacy/absence of protest — each proxy loses a question.
- **Efficiency case:** efficiency is a relation between means and a *given* end and cannot validate the end; "efficient" is an evaluative term in descriptive clothing. Ask: efficient at what, and for whom?
- Applied use: where a governance debate is framed only in output terms, the explanation/justification distinction supplies the missing questions of fair distribution of burdens and benefits, and of dignity and voice.
- **Necessary/sufficient check:** empirical verification is not even necessary for the whole subject; causal tools are necessary but not sufficient without a view of goals; turnout is evidence but not a standard; restoring values in 1969 was necessary but not sufficient without a procedure for adjudicating value-disputes.
- **Boundary rule:** this topic supplies the lens; verified Indian constitutional, institutional and policy facts come from the papers that own them. Never improvise a provision inside a theory answer.

### Concept check

**Question:** A stem reads: "Digitisation has improved governance, as shown by faster service delivery." Apply the five-step application move and say what the answer's analytical core should be.

**Model answer:** Step 1 splits the claim: "service delivery has become faster" is empirical and checkable; "governance has improved" is evaluative, since "improved" asserts a standard. Step 2 asks the two questions — what the real situation is, including who gained speed and who did not, and on what grounds speed counts as improvement in governance. Step 3 runs the conditions test: faster delivery is plausibly *necessary* for better governance on most accounts, and clearly not *sufficient*, since a faster process can also be a less accountable or less contestable one. Step 4 names the proxy risk in Marcuse's form: if the quality of governance is operationalised as processing time, then questions about discretion, redress and exclusion disappear from the vocabulary rather than being answered, and the measurement begins to do the evaluating. Step 5 is where a candidate inserts an independently verified example of their own, drawn from the paper that owns the facts. The analytical core is therefore the gap between the measured proxy and the concept: the answer's thesis is that speed is a genuine but partial indicator, and that an answer claiming improvement in governance owes a standard that speed alone cannot supply.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use the application move as a licence to avoid facts. The point is not that empirical claims are suspect; it is that an empirical claim settles the empirical question only. An answer that uses the fact/value distinction to dodge the evidence has inverted the lesson — Hacker's second direction of dependence requires the philosophical part to be informed by a profound understanding of the facts.

### 1. The qualified thesis

Open with a thesis sentence that already contains the position. Never open with a definition-dump and never with a story.

⚠️ **The formula for this topic:** "Political theory is neither pure science nor pure philosophy; it is the systematic study of political life that uses science to explain political reality and philosophy to judge its ends — a balance whose exact weighting has itself been a historically contested question, as the decline and revival debate shows."

Adapt the final clause to the directive. The clause is what makes the sentence a *qualified* thesis rather than a slogan: it concedes in advance that the balance is contested, which pre-empts the obvious objection and signals that the decline debate is coming.

### 2. The directive decides the shape

| Directive | What it demands on this topic | Verdict required | Commonest failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Define** | Genus and differentia in one sentence, then one contrast pair — theory against ideology, or science against philosophy | A boundary line, not a verdict | Writing a descriptive paragraph instead of a bounded definition |
| **Explain** | Premises → mechanism → conclusion, with one worked illustration and a statement of what the account rules out | No | Listing features with no causal chain |
| **Discuss** | Both components reconstructed fairly, the exact point of disagreement named, then a verdict on integration | **Yes** | Describing only one component, or refusing to conclude |
| **Examine** | Restate the claim as testable, supply supporting evidence, then the strongest counter-claim — Easton 1953 and Lipset — and a finding | **Yes** | Narrating the definition without testing the stem's claim |
| **Critically examine / analyse** | Everything in "Examine", plus an attack on the internal logic — necessary versus sufficient, a hidden premise, scope over-reach — and a reply to it | **Yes** | Treating "critically" as licence to attack one side only |
| **Evaluate** | Name the criterion first — coherence, explanatory power, adequacy to political experience — score each position against it, then a graded verdict | **Yes, graded** | Judging with no criterion made explicit |
| **Elucidate** | Explain through a paired illustration: riot causation against the good-society question | No | Skipping the pairing that makes the distinction concrete |
| **Comment** | Decode the proposition, state a position early, give two supports and one qualification | **Yes, early** | A neutral summary with no position |

### 3. The reusable evidence units

An evidence unit in this subject is a **reconstructed argument attached to a named source**. A named thinker without a mechanism is name-dropping; a mechanism without a named source is unattributed assertion. Both halves are required. Six units are available on this topic, and each carries its own limitation — which is what lets you deploy it without over-claiming:

| # | Claim | Named source | What it lets the answer do | Its limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Politics is defined by what is common to the whole community | **Wolin**, *Politics and Vision* (1960) | Justify why not every dispute is political; bound the subject | Under-counts informal power inside what it labels private |
| 2 | Every political scientist is part scientist, part philosopher | **Hacker** (1961) | Ground the integration thesis and refuse a one-sided answer | Fixes necessity, not the weighting between the two in a concrete dispute |
| 3 | Science seeks explanation, philosophy seeks justification | **Raphael** (1976) | Classify any political question quickly; decode a directive | Many real questions straddle both and resist a clean split |
| 4 | Without systematic theory, politics is left to the ignorant, the self-interested, or those with a will to power | **Held**, *Political Theory Today* (1991) | Supply a reason the subject matters, for an opening or a close | Itself a value-judgment; cannot serve as neutral proof |
| 5 | Traditional theory declined because it was speculative and lacked causal tools | **Easton** (1953) | Explain the mid-century behavioural turn | Easton's own 1969 correction shows the proposed remedy was incomplete |
| 6 | A value-free political science cannot even rank tyranny below justice | **Strauss** (1957/1962) | State the sharpest case against value-neutrality | Risks implying all empirical work is worthless — a conclusion the source does not endorse |

### 4. Objection-and-reply chains

Carry two ready-made chains; a 20-mark answer should deploy both, and at least one should attack the *logic* rather than the conclusion.

**Chain 1 — Objection:** evaluative statements cannot be empirically verified, so they are not knowledge; political theory should be confined to political science. **Reply:** values have a sound logical structure unless mistaken for biased statements, and can be argued through dialogue aimed at principles for judging their validity; excluding them leaves public policy with no standard by which to judge outcomes as fair or unjust, and the task will then fall into irresponsible hands (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.16-17, 20). **Residual:** the dialogue argument promises convergence only "probably".

**Chain 2 — Objection:** the decline and revival debate shows political theory is now settled science, with no further room for philosophy. **Reply:** Easton's own 1969 turn and the chapter's closing formula present political science and political philosophy as continuing, complementary, mutually testing enterprises since the 1970s, not a victory for either side (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.27). **Residual:** Germino's position — that the two components cannot be 'reunited' — remains a live minority view inside the revival.

**Chain 3 — Objection:** ideology and political theory are the same thing, since both discuss liberty, equality and the state. **Reply:** theory is dispassionate and disinterested and tests claims for validity; ideology is interested and justificatory towards a particular distribution of power, and is accepted without inquiry into validity (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20). **Residual:** disinterestedness is a disposition and cannot be externally verified.

### Concept check

**Question:** A 15-mark question reads: "Examine the view that political theory must include both political science and political philosophy." Set out the answer's skeleton — not the prose — and justify each choice against the directive.

**Model answer:** Skeleton: (1) qualified thesis asserting integration while conceding that the weighting is contested; (2) restate the stem as a testable proposition — that an account of political phenomena confined to empirical and logical statements is incomplete; (3) supporting evidence unit: Raphael's explanation/justification distinction, showing that "why do riots occur?" and "what is a good society?" are different demands arising from the same political life; (4) second unit, supporting from the other side: Hacker's double role, with the two directions of dependence — goals making scientific work coherent, facts making philosophical work binding; (5) strongest counter-evidence: Easton's 1953 charge that traditional theory was speculative, lacked causal tools and had no relevance, together with his claim that modern political science need not evolve values; (6) objection → reply chain on verification, with the residual that dialogue promises convergence only "probably"; (7) finding: the claim holds, and it holds *because* the counter-evidence was itself withdrawn from inside behaviouralism in 1969, with the means-and-ends formula as the closing line. Each choice is dictated by "Examine": the directive requires a claim restated as testable, evidence on both sides, and an explicit finding on whether the claim holds, holds partly or fails — which is why step (5) is not optional and step (7) must be a finding rather than a summary.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the six evidence units as a checklist to be exhausted. At 15 marks, two well-reconstructed units with their limitations outscore five names with no mechanisms; the unit's value lies in the reconstruction, and an answer that lists sources without showing how each reasons has supplied only the second half of what the subject's scoring atom requires.

### 1. The five-step method for a proposition-based stem

Many stems in this subject are a proposition or an attributed line rather than a question. Handle them by method, never by guessing provenance.

**Step 1 — Decode the proposition, not the personality.** Restate the line as an explicit claim with a subject, a predicate and an implied contrast. Identify the concept invoked, the rival concept it is set against, and the hidden premise that makes it work.

**Step 2 — Locate it.** Which question is it answering — what the unit of analysis is, what makes authority legitimate, what freedom is, what justice is, or what the state should do? The placement immediately supplies the rival positions to bring in.

**Step 3 — Attribute only as far as the evidence goes.** Use one of the three registers above.

**Step 4 — Test, do not celebrate.** Ask whether the proposition is true universally, true under stated conditions, or false. Supply one evidence unit for it and one against, then a graded verdict on the proposition **as stated**.

**Step 5 — Answer the directive, not the quotation.** "Comment" wants a compact position; "Critically examine" wants the proposition's internal logic attacked and defended. The quotation is the subject-matter; the directive still fixes the shape.

⚠️ An unfamiliar quotation is not a knowledge failure. Decoding it correctly and arguing the underlying proposition scores; bluffing a source loses more than admitting the limit. And never build the whole answer as exegesis of the quoted author — the stem is a doorway into a debate, and the debate carries the marks.

### 3. Dated-source discipline

The entire body of material on this topic is grounded in a single edition — the Fifth Edition (2009) of the source text. Four consequences follow, and all four are examinable habits rather than bibliographic trivia:

- **Institutional descriptions, "contemporary" examples and numerical claims in that text are book-period claims.** They describe the position as of that edition and must not be re-presented as current.
- **No page anchor travels across editions.** Every page reference in this session is to the local copy used to build it; it is not a claim about any other edition's pagination.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 to 3

**Question:** Build the chain from the definition of the political to the conclusion that political theory must contain both components, naming each step.

**Model answer:** The political is what is public or common to the whole community, because a polity is the institution that regulates the whole community and exercises authority over each member (Lesson 1). Theory means systematic knowledge, so political theory is systematic knowledge of political phenomena, and that knowledge divides into empirical statements resting on observation, logical statements resting on reasoning, and evaluative statements resting on value-judgment (Lesson 2). Political science relies on the first two, on the ground that correct observation and correct reasoning converge and are therefore capable of verification, while evaluative statements are alleged to be mere preference. The chain then turns on whether the inference from "unverifiable by sense-experience" to "not knowledge" holds. It is rejected on three grounds: values possess a sound logical structure unless mistaken for biased statements; their upholders can be brought into a dialogue aimed at principles for judging validity; and the determination of values is the basis of sound public policy, so abdicating the task delivers it to irresponsible hands (Lesson 3). Hence political theory must comprehend both political science and political philosophy.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present the conclusion as a compromise between two schools. It is the product of rejecting a specific inference, and an answer that reaches it without identifying that inference has asserted the conclusion rather than earning it.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 4 to 7

**Question:** Three failures are possible here: a political science without philosophy, a political philosophy without science, and a theory clouded by ideology. State the characteristic symptom of each and the source-grounded reason it occurs.

**Model answer:** A political science without philosophy becomes value-blind and selectively descriptive. The reason is Hacker's: the scientific parts of a theory achieve coherence and significance only if the writer has a preconceived idea of the goals of political life, so a discipline with no view of goals has no principle for selecting which observable regularities matter — and its own fourth methodological step, prescription, presupposes objectives it cannot supply. A political philosophy without science becomes poorly grounded or merely rhetorical, because the philosophical parts of a theory must be informed by a profound understanding of the facts of political life; a prescription addressed to beings whose needs and capabilities are misdescribed cannot bind them. A theory clouded by ideology fails differently again: the philosophical component is reduced to rationalization for current or future arrangements, and the scientific component to partial or selective depiction producing distorted description — note that the scientific failure is one of selection rather than fabrication, which is why it can occur without a single false sentence being written. The common structural point is that each failure is a failure of the *whole*, which is why the remedy in each case is integration under conditions of scholarly detachment.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat ideology as a third discipline alongside science and philosophy. It is a condition that either component can fall into, defined by justificatory function and refusal of validity-testing, not by subject-matter.

### Cumulative check C — Lessons 8 to 9

**Question:** "Political theory is significant because it tells governments what to do." Correct this using the four heads and the three functions.

**Model answer:** The sentence collapses four distinct claims into one, and the one it keeps is the least defensible. The first head is control of social life: scientific analysis enables us to understand the causes of conflict and violence and gives insights for preventing their outburst — the analogy being geology and earthquakes, which supports anticipation and damage-limitation rather than control, and which in any case presupposes an agreed objective. The second is social criticism and reconstruction: when something is wrong we seek logical grounds for criticising it, drawing on a tradition from Plato to Macpherson whose proposals cannot be accepted as final truth but which supply insights from which we draw our own scheme. The third is clarification of concepts, which removes the equivocation on which concealment, demagogic emotional appeal and the legitimation of oppressive regimes depend. The fourth is the encouragement of mutual respect and toleration through a never-ending conversation whose greatest debates are never resolved but whose criticisms are illuminating. Mapped onto the three functions — description by political science, criticism and reconstruction by political philosophy — the corrected sentence reads: political theory is significant because it describes political reality, supplies grounds for criticising it, proposes reconstructions, keeps the vocabulary of public argument honest, and sustains a tradition of reasoned disagreement. Telling governments what to do is, at most, a downstream consequence of the second function and never its definition.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let "clarification of concepts" sound like the weakest head. It is the one with the sharpest political edge, because the source's own illustration of unclear language is its use by demagogues to evade reason and by autocrats to legitimize oppressive regimes.

### Cumulative check D — Lessons 10 to 13

**Question:** Write the four-sentence account of the decline-and-revival debate that would open a 20-mark answer, and then name the two things that account deliberately refuses to say.

**Model answer:** In 1953 Easton charged traditional political theory with speculation, classicism, historical parochialism, exhaustion after Marx and Mill, a failure to acquire tools capable of explaining fascism and communism, and professional irrelevance during the Second World War, proposing a behavioural political science that need not evolve values. In the same year Cobban described a collapse of theory's significance under capitalist and communist systems alike, blaming among other causes the logical positivists who concentrated on facts to the exclusion of values, and concluded that all was not yet lost because political science must evolve criteria of judgment; in 1960 Lipset argued that in the United States the search for the good society had ended because the prevailing form of democracy was the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation. Easton himself reversed in 1969, launching the post-behavioural revolution, converting political science from a pure to an applied science and reviving concern with values without compromising scientific method for the study of facts. Against the scientistic programme, Strauss argued that a positivist political science cannot rank values at all, Germino identified revival with the recovery of political philosophy as the critical study of right order and insisted that detachment is not ethical neutrality, and Marcuse warned that a social science conforming to natural-science language converts inquiry into social control. The account refuses to say, first, that political theory disappeared — what declined was a mode, speculative, classicist and explicitly normative — and second, that the revival rejected science, since the decisive turn came from inside behaviouralism and retained scientific method throughout.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not place the revival's beginning in the 1970s. The renewed emphasis on values dates from the advent of post-behaviouralism in 1969, with the 1970s and 1980s as the period of consolidation; getting the sequence right is what licenses the claim that the correction was internal.

### Cumulative check E — Lessons 14 to 16

**Question:** You have two minutes before writing a 20-mark answer on this topic. List the checks you run, in order, and say what each prevents.

**Model answer:** First, fix the directive and write the thesis sentence that already contains the position — this prevents a definition-dump opening and guarantees that the answer has a destination. Second, choose the evidence units, two to four of them, each with its reconstruction and its limitation; this prevents name-dropping, which scores as such, and ensures each named source arrives with a mechanism. Third, select the objection-and-reply chains, at least one attacking the logic rather than the conclusion; this prevents a one-sided answer under a "critically" directive. Fourth, run the conditions test on the stem's central claim — necessary, sufficient, both or neither; this prevents an ungraded conclusion. Fifth, run the register check on every attribution: verified quotations in quotation marks, reliable attributions paraphrased, uncertain ones flagged in the sentence that uses them; this prevents a fabricated citation. Sixth, run the date check, so that no book-period claim is written as current; this prevents converting a situated argument such as Lipset's into a universal law. Seventh, run the boundary check, so that no provision, judgment or figure is asserted that this topic does not own and that has not been independently verified; this prevents a strong conceptual answer being spoiled by one unverified fact.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the register, date and boundary checks as optional polish. They are the checks that protect against the only errors in this topic that an examiner can identify with certainty, which makes them the highest-value seconds in the answer.

**Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish political theory from ideology. *(Define/Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Political theory is the systematic knowledge of political phenomena; ideology is a set of ideas and arguments used to defend an existing or proposed distribution of power, accepted as true without inquiry into validity. The distinction is not of subject-matter — both discuss liberty, equality and the state — but of interest and epistemic attitude. Theory, whether scientific or philosophical, must be dispassionate and disinterested; a theorist with no personal interest in a political arrangement does not engage in special pleading. The ideologue is an interested party, and the symmetry matters: that interest may lie in defending things as they are or in criticising the status quo in hope of a new distribution of power. So ideology is not identified by which side its conclusions favour. Scholarly detachment is accordingly the keynote of theory. ⚠️ Its residual difficulty is that detachment is a disposition and cannot be externally verified.

**Scoring note for this answer:** the marks here concentrate in three places — stating *both* marks of ideology rather than only the justificatory one, stating the symmetry explicitly so that the distinction is not reduced to a political preference, and conceding the verifiability residual in the final clause. A definition stem at 10 marks needs a boundary line, not a verdict, which is why the answer ends on a limitation rather than a judgment.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** Explain why political science alone cannot exhaust political theory. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Political science proceeds by observation ruling out supernatural and metaphysical causation, generalization by inductive or deductive method, explanation supplying reasons without which a correlation might be coincidence, and prediction and prescription. Three features show why it cannot exhaust the subject. First, selection: a political field contains indefinitely many observable regularities, and Hacker's point is that the scientific parts of a theory achieve coherence and significance only if the writer already holds a view of the goals of political life. Second, prescription: the method's own fourth step suggests measures for objectives such as efficiency, stability and satisfaction, and objectives are chosen, not observed. Third, subject-matter: political life generates factual questions such as why riots occur and value questions such as what a good society is, simultaneously, and a method restricted to the first leaves the second unanswered rather than answered negatively. Hence theory must comprehend both components.

**Scoring note for this answer:** an "Explain" directive forbids a list, so each of the three features is stated as a reason with its mechanism attached. The distinctive move, and the one that separates this from a textbook answer, is the second: deriving the limitation from inside the method's own fourth step rather than importing it from the critics.

### 15-mark practice (~200-250 words)

**Practice question 3 [15]:** Examine the claim that the mid-twentieth-century debate establishes the decline of political theory. *(Examine — answer in 200 to 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Restated as a testable proposition, the claim is that political theory ceased to be a viable enterprise in the mid-twentieth century. Supporting evidence is real. Easton in 1953 charged traditional theory with speculation devoid of acute observation, bondage to classics and the history of ideas, origin in the turmoil of past ages, exhaustion after Marx and Mill, failure to acquire tools capable of explaining fascism and communism, and exclusion from wartime decision-making, proposing a behavioural political science focused on causal theory. Cobban the same year reported lost significance under capitalist systems, with their libertarian-democratic inspiration, bureaucratic dominance and military machine, and under communist systems, with their new party organization and oligarchic rule. Lipset in 1960 held that in the United States the search for the good society had ended because the prevailing democracy was the closest approximation to that society in operation.

The counter-evidence defeats the proposition as stated. Cobban's own conclusion was that all was not yet lost and that political science must evolve criteria of judgment — a demand for revival, not an epitaph. Easton reversed in 1969, converting political science from a pure to an applied science and reviving concern with values without compromising scientific method for facts. Germino by 1967 was already describing political theory as again in the ascendant.

The finding: the claim holds only for a mode — speculative, classicist, explicitly normative — and fails for the enterprise. ⚠️ What genuinely declined was a way of doing theory; what recovered was theory with evidence re-admitted.

**Scoring note for this answer:** "Examine" is satisfied only if the claim is restated as testable, evidenced on both sides, and closed with a finding; the structure above does each in turn and the finding is a *partial* holding rather than a yes or a no. Note also that two of the three supporting witnesses are turned into counter-evidence by their own later or concluding positions, which is the most economical way to carry a 15-mark examination.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** "Clarification of concepts is a merely linguistic exercise with no political consequence." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in 200 to 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition assumes that fixing meanings is preliminary to politics rather than part of it. Three considerations defeat that assumption. First, political vocabulary is uniquely exposed: Wolin notes the widespread tendency to use the same words for non-political phenomena as for political ones — the authority of the father, of the church, of Parliament — in contrast to the restricted technical usages of mathematics and natural science, so the terms of political argument arrive already ambiguous. Second, the ambiguity is exploitable in three distinct ways: concealing a weak point in an argument, misleading people by creating emotional appeal and evading reason, and legitimizing oppressive regimes. Third, clarification is not itself neutral, since its third operation — improvement — *recommends* a usage, as in confining sovereignty to the legal authority of a state rather than its coercive power; recommending what a political term shall mean is a substantive intervention.

Attacking the proposition's internal logic: it treats "linguistic" and "political" as exclusive, but on the account above a political effect is produced precisely *through* language, so the disjunction fails.

⚠️ A qualification is owed. Agreement on meaning does not produce agreement on viewpoint; it paves the way for dialogue, so that a liberal and a socialist sharing a meaning of freedom are likelier to appreciate each other's position. The verdict: the proposition fails, because clarification locates disagreement rather than dissolving it — and locating disagreement is a political achievement, not a verbal one.

**Scoring note for this answer:** the "critically" element is discharged in a named move — exposing the false disjunction between linguistic and political — rather than by adding hostile adjectives. The qualification paragraph then prevents the opposite over-claim, that clarification resolves conflict, which is the error an examiner expects after a strong defence.

### 20-mark practice (~250-300 words)

**Practice question 5 [20]:** Evaluate the view that political theory should be value-free. *(Evaluate — answer in 250 to 300 words.)*

**Model answer:** Evaluation requires a criterion. The one adopted here is adequacy to political experience: a conception of theory is judged by whether it accounts for the questions political life generates.

The value-free view has genuine force. Correct observation and reasoning converge across observers, so empirical and logical statements are capable of verification, whereas evaluative statements are alleged to reflect preferences with no reliable method of settlement. Easton added a professional argument: political scientists lagged behind economists and sociologists, lacked tools to explain fascism and communism, and were ignored in wartime decision-making; the remedy was causal theory, values being left to society.

Against the criterion, the view fails at three points. Selection: the scientific parts of a theory achieve coherence and significance only if the writer already holds a view of the goals of political life, so value-freedom fails before conclusions are drawn. Prescription: the method's fourth step suggests measures for objectives such as efficiency and stability, and objectives are chosen. Ranking: Strauss objects that an empirical theory asserting the equal importance of all social values cannot register that some things are intrinsically high and others low.

Two concessions are owed. Strauss over-reaches if read as denying the worth of empirical work, which this settlement nowhere endorses; and the integration thesis fixes necessity without fixing weighting in any concrete dispute.

⚠️ Graded verdict: value-freedom holds for the observational and generalizing operations of political science, where it is a discipline rather than a dogma; it fails as an account of political theory as a whole. Science strengthens our means, philosophy determines our ends, and because means and ends are interdependent the two are complementary rather than separable. The rival would win only if a society could be shown to settle its values without reasoned inquiry — the empirical premise Easton assumed and then withdrew.

**Scoring note for this answer:** "Evaluate" is the one directive that is failed by an unnamed criterion, so the criterion is declared in the first line and every subsequent judgment is referred back to it. The final sentence does the work that distinguishes a graded verdict from a hedge: it states the condition under which the rejected position would win, which is also where the 1953-to-1969 reversal is doing its evidential work.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** "The decline of political theory was the decline of a mode, not the disappearance of a subject." Critically analyse. *(Critically analyse — answer in 250 to 300 words.)*

**Model answer:** Decompose the proposition into two claims — that something genuinely declined, and that what declined was a way of theorising rather than the enterprise.

The first claim is supported. Easton's 1953 indictment is specific and of three kinds: methodological, in charging speculation and classicism; historical, in locating traditional theory in the turmoil of past ages and finding no outstanding philosopher after Marx and Mill; and practical, in the failure to acquire tools for fascism and communism and in exclusion from wartime decision-making. Cobban independently recorded lost significance under both system-types. Something had plainly gone wrong.

The second claim requires the sources to be separated rather than grouped. Easton attacks a mode and proposes to replace it. Cobban reports collapsed significance yet concludes that all was not yet lost and that criteria of judgment must be evolved. Lipset claims the normative search is complete — in one country, at one time. These are three scopes, not one thesis.

The decisive evidence is internal. Easton's 1969 post-behavioural revolution converted political science from a pure to an applied science and revived concern with values without compromising scientific method for facts; Germino had by 1967 listed Oakeshott, Arendt, de Jouvenel, Strauss and Voegelin as evidence of ascendancy, expanding the list in 1975 to include Rawls, Nozick, Macpherson, Habermas, MacIntyre and Walzer — which is itself a refutation of Easton's fourth charge.

Attacking the logic: the proposition risks being unfalsifiable, since any apparent disappearance can be redescribed as the decline of a mode. The reply is that the distinction is testable — if theory had disappeared, the 1975 list could not exist.

⚠️ Verdict: the proposition holds, with one qualification. Germino's view that the two components cannot be 'reunited' shows that the revival contained a separatist strand the eventual settlement did not adopt.

**Scoring note for this answer:** this model is built to demonstrate the hardest single move available on the topic — turning the sources' *differences of scope* into the argument rather than treating them as background. The unfalsifiability objection is included because a "critically analyse" directive rewards an attack on the proposition's own logic, and because the reply to it is concrete rather than rhetorical.

# REMEDIATION

Each entry below names an error observed to recur on this topic, states why it is wrong in source terms, and gives the repaired formulation.

### R1 — Treating the public/private distinction as absolute

**Error:** "Religion, education and culture are private, therefore not political."
**Why it fails:** the distinction is a default allocation with a stated override — those matters stay outside politics *until some regulation of them is required to maintain public order and safety* (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.16).
**Repair:** "They lie outside politics by default, which places the burden on any authority seeking to regulate them; the examinable question is whether the public-order justification genuinely applies."

### R2 — Equating political theory with normative theory

**Error:** "Political theory is normative; political science is empirical."
**Why it fails:** political theory is the inclusive term. It must comprehend both political science and political philosophy (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.17).
**Repair:** "Political theory is systematic knowledge of political phenomena; political science and political philosophy are its two components."

### R3 — Calling evaluative statements irrational

**Error:** "Values are subjective, so they cannot be reasoned about."
**Why it fails:** the text denies exactly this — values have a sound logical structure unless mistaken for biased statements, and their upholders can be brought into dialogue aimed at principles for judging validity (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.17).
**Repair:** "Evaluative statements are not settled by observation, but they possess a logical structure and admit of reasoned dialogue; the chapter's own 'probably' marks the limit of that claim."

### R4 — Reducing political philosophy to ideal-state design

**Error:** "Political philosophy imagines ideal societies."
**Why it fails:** some classical philosophers did set out ideal forms, but that was not their central concern; even in Plato the ideal society serves to criticise existing society and to clarify a general concept such as justice. Philosophy is recognised by its critical function (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19).
**Repair:** "Political philosophy is recognised by its critical function: justification — grounds for accepting or rejecting beliefs we take for granted — and the clarification of concepts."

### R5 — Quoting Hacker as a slogan

**Error:** "Hacker said every political scientist is part scientist and part philosopher."
**Why it fails:** quoted alone it is a pleasantry. Its force lies in the two directions of dependence — goals make scientific work coherent and significant; facts make philosophical work binding (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.17-18).
**Repair:** state the slogan, then at least one direction of dependence with its mechanism.

### R6 — Identifying ideology by the side it favours

**Error:** "Defending the status quo is ideological; criticising it is theoretical."
**Why it fails:** the ideologue's interest may be to defend things as they are **or** to criticise the status quo in hope of a new distribution of power (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20).
**Repair:** apply the two marks — justificatory function, and acceptance without inquiry into validity — to each writer separately.

### R7 — Reporting post-behaviouralism as anti-science

**Error:** "Post-behaviouralism rejected the scientific study of politics."
**Why it fails:** it revives concern with values **without compromising scientific method for the study of facts** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.19).
**Repair:** "It rejected the sufficiency of a value-excluding political science while retaining scientific method for facts."

### R8 — The flat chronology

**Error:** "Political theory died in the 1950s and was revived in the 1970s."
**Why it fails:** three distinct claims of different scope are merged; Cobban's own conclusion already demands revival; and the reversal begins in 1969 from inside behaviouralism, with the 1970s and 1980s as consolidation (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.23-25).
**Repair:** "A mode of theorising lost credibility across the 1950s; the correction began inside behaviouralism in 1969 and consolidated through the 1970s and 1980s."

### R9 — Universalising Lipset

**Error:** "Lipset showed that democracy is the good society."
**Why it fails:** the claim is that in the United States the age-old search had come to an end because the prevailing form of democracy there was "the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation" — one country, one period, and the chapter marks it with "in those days" (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.25).
**Repair:** state the country, the period and the comparative form of the claim.

### R10 — Merging Strauss, Germino and Marcuse

**Error:** treating the three as one objection to behaviouralism.
**Why it fails:** Strauss attacks the inability of a value-neutral method to rank values; Germino attacks the identity of a theory that is neither philosophy nor ideology and insists that detachment is not ethical neutrality; Marcuse attacks the social function of a measured social science (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.25-27).
**Repair:** name the target of each separately; the three are cumulative, not repetitive.

### R11 — Reporting the settlement as unanimous

**Error:** "By the 1970s everyone agreed the two components were complementary."
**Why it fails:** Germino held that the gulf was so wide that the components could not be 'reunited' and that full recovery was impossible within the positivist universe of discourse (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF pp.26-27), while the chapter's own conclusion records a largely subsided dispute and complementary roles (PDF p.27).
**Repair:** report the settlement as the chapter's position and name Germino's separatism as a live dissent.

### R12 — Importing unverified facts into a theory answer

**Error:** naming a provision, judgment, figure or scheme to "ground" a conceptual answer.
**Why it fails:** this topic supplies a lens, not a stock of facts, and an unverified anchor converts a strong conceptual answer into a detectable factual error.
**Repair:** use the generic test cases, mark the slot where your own verified example belongs, and insert only material you have independently verified from the paper that owns it.

### R13 — Fabricating provenance under pressure

**Error:** supplying a book, year or page for a quotation you half-remember.
**Why it fails:** the cost of admitting a limit is one clause; the cost of a fabricated citation is the credibility of the whole answer, and in this subject it contradicts the discipline the topic teaches.
**Repair:** use the three registers — verified, reliable-but-paraphrased, or openly uncertain — and argue the doctrine.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Bounding the political

- **Authority:** command-and-obedience relationship where the command is generally regarded as reasonable; mild protests do not upset it. Present in many institutions, so not itself the mark of the political.
- **Polity:** an organisation regulating the **whole** community — rules and decisions for all, authority over each member.
- **Political = public/common**, as against private or applicable to a limited number of persons.
- **Wolin, *Politics and Vision* (1960):** the political arrangement is uniquely concerned with what is "common" to the whole community — national defense, internal order, dispensing of justice, economic regulation — declared so because their benefits reach all members. Contains a subject-matter claim *and* a justification claim.
- *Polis* → polity/politics/political. Greek city-states: compact communities whose activities aimed at the **good life** counted as politics. Aristotle: man is by nature a political animal; one outside the state is beast or angel.
- Modern narrowing — political: cabinet and parliament decisions, election campaigns, party activity, movements for change in law and public policy. Not political by default: object of faith and worship, content of education, art and culture — **until regulation is required for public order and safety**.
- ⚠️ Cost of the criterion: under-counts power inside the sphere it labels private.

## 2. Theory, and the three statement-types

- **Theory = systematic knowledge; political theory = systematic knowledge of political phenomena.**
- **Empirical** (observation through sense-experience alone) · **Logical** (reasoning; "two plus two is four") · **Evaluative** (value-judgment; "men are born free and equal").
- **Political science relies only on empirical and logical statements**; ground — correct observation and reasoning converge, so such statements are capable of verification.
- Allegation against evaluative statements: individual or group preferences, no reliable method for right and wrong, the purpose of the universe or human life not scientifically discoverable.
- ⚠️ The hinge is the inference *unverifiable → not knowledge*, which is philosophical, not empirical.
- ⚠️ Real propositions are usually mixed; use the classification to ask "what would settle this?"

## 3. The positivist restriction and the reply

- Logical positivists: evaluative statements have no empirical content or logical structure; they express subjective reflection or emotional preference. Champions of scientific method demand a **value-free / value-neutral** approach.
- The full enumeration of the parties pressing the restriction: advocates of **positivism**, **neo-positivism (logical positivism)** and **behaviouralism** wish to confine political theory to the sphere of political science, so that value-judgment is dropped from its purview altogether (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.23). ⚠️ Three layers of one demand — a general epistemology, its logical refinement, and the programme that applied it inside political studies.
- Reply, four moves: **scope** (theory cannot be confined to the so-called scientific knowledge) · **structure** (values have a sound logical structure unless mistaken for biased statements) · **procedure** (dialogue: understand, convince, *probably* agree on universal principles for judging validity) · **consequence** (determination of values is the basis of sound public policy; abdication lets it fall into irresponsible hands, with disastrous consequences).
- Validity comes from structure and procedure; urgency from consequence. Do not let consequence carry the epistemic weight.
- **Conclusion: political theory must comprehend both political science and political philosophy.**
- ⚠️ Provenance caution: the source's Logical Positivism box names Weber and Wittgenstein among founders/members; the standard account places the Circle around Schlick with Carnap, Neurath, Waismann and Hahn, treats the early Wittgenstein as an influence rather than a member, and treats Weber as a methodologist of value-neutrality. Use the doctrine; do not assert the roster.

## 4. Political science: method and turns

- **(a) Observation** — sense-experience alone; rules out supernatural or metaphysical causation.
- **(b) Generalization** — regularities, relation and correlation; **inductive** (particular → general) or **deductive** (general → particular); expressed as a general rule, preferably quantitative, capable of verification by experimentation.
- **(c) Explanation** — reasons for the rule; without them a correlation might be coincidence; explanation alone makes events, situations and tendencies meaningful.
- **(d) Prediction and prescription** — outcomes anticipated; measures suggested for higher efficiency, stability, satisfaction.
- **Behavioural approach:** study actual behaviour rather than describing institutions and their legal position; formal institutions dissolved into **systems** and **processes**.
- **Post-behavioural approach:** makes the achievements of political science **subservient to human values and ends**; revives concern with values **without compromising scientific method for the study of facts**; applies science to prevailing crises.
- ⚠️ Step (a) excludes a class of causes; step (d) prescribes and so presupposes chosen ends.

## 5. Political philosophy: critical, justificatory, clarifying

- The "science = real, philosophy = ideal" account is **inadequate** as a definition of scope.
- **Raphael, *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976):** classical philosophers did set out ideal forms, but that was not their central concern; even in Plato the ideal society criticises existing society and promotes understanding of general concepts such as justice.
- **"Science seeks explanation while philosophy seeks justification."** Justification = rational grounds for **accepting or rejecting** beliefs normally taken for granted.
- **Clarification of concepts:** society, authority, social class, justice, liberty, democracy are general *and vague*. Three operations — **analysis** (sovereignty as supreme legal authority) · **synthesis** (a right involves an obligation) · **improvement** (confine sovereignty to legal authority, not coercive power).
- ⚠️ Improvement recommends a usage, so clarification is substantive, not neutral.
- Concession and defence: goals of human life cannot be *scientifically ascertained*, but can be discussed, argued from data and reasoning, and brought to an acceptable point at the current level of social consciousness; the search is an **ongoing process**. Philosophy arose from critical reflection on pre-existing political activity — the Socratic function of **speaking truth to power**.

## 6. The integration thesis

- **Catlin (*Political Quarterly*, March 1957):** "the theory (of politics) itself is divided into political science and political philosophy." Science: fusing sociology and political science makes us sharp-eyed for **control** in its many forms across the whole social field. Philosophy: the **kingdom of ends or final values** — asking "What is for the national good?" or "What is the good society?" *is* asking philosophy. Classification is by the **question asked**.
- **Hacker (*Political Theory: Philosophy, Ideology, Science*, 1961):** every political scientist plays a **double role**, part scientist and part philosopher; no lasting contribution without both realms.
  - Science → philosophy direction: the philosophical parts must be informed by a profound understanding of the **facts** of political life; the best philosophers studied existing realities intensely.
  - Philosophy → science direction: the scientific parts achieve coherence and significance only if the writer has a preconceived idea of the **goals** of political life.
- ⚠️ The second direction attacks value-freedom at the point of **selection** — the strongest form of the attack.
- ⚠️ Residuals: integration holds for the enterprise, not necessarily for each individual study; and necessity is fixed, weighting is not.

## 7. Theory against ideology

- **Hacker:** theory, whether science or philosophy, must be **dispassionate and disinterested**; with no personal interest in a political arrangement, the theorist's vision of reality and image of the good life are not clouded and the theory is not **special pleading**. The **intention of ideology is to justify a particular system of power**; the ideologue is an **interested party**, whether defending things as they are or criticising the status quo in hope of a new distribution of power.
- **Ideology (boxed):** ideas and arguments used to defend an existing or proposed distribution of power, accepted as true **without inquiring into their validity**.
- Two marks: **justificatory function** + **refusal of validity-testing**. ⚠️ They can come apart; the side favoured is not the test.
- Clouded philosophy → **rationalization**; clouded science → **partial or selective depiction** and distorted description. The scientific failure is **selection**, not fabrication.
- Illustrations: capitalism/private property vs socialism/social ownership; nationalism/national interest vs internationalism/disregard of boundaries. All ideologies are biased towards partisan ends.
- **"Scholarly detachment is, therefore, the keynote of political theory in real sense of the term."** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.20) ⚠️ Residual: detachment is a disposition and cannot be externally verified.

## 8. The four heads of significance and the three functions

- **Control of social life:** geology/earthquakes → causes of conflict and violence; physics/electricity → development of society from human resources; medical science/disease → remedies for political instability and social crises. ⚠️ The three analogies are unequal, and all presuppose an agreed objective.
- **Social criticism and reconstruction:** seek **logical grounds** for criticism and speculate about a good society. Roll: Plato, Aristotle, St. Augustine, Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Hegel, Marx, Mill, Macpherson. Specific contributions — **Plato** on the *modus operandi* of selfish and cunning politicians in a democracy; **Machiavelli** on selfish and greedy people; **Marx** on conflict between owners and non-owners of property; **Macpherson** on the power structure obstructing creative freedom. ⚠️ **No proposal is final truth**; the tradition's value is diagnostic.
- **Clarification of concepts:** Wolin on **parallel usages** — authority of the father, of the church, of Parliament — against the restricted technical usages of mathematics and natural science. Unclarified language permits **concealing a weak point**, **misleading by emotional appeal that evades reason**, and **legitimizing oppressive regimes** (dated illustration: Mussolini, 1883-1945, in Italy). Agreement on meaning ≠ agreement on viewpoint, but it **paves the way for dialogue**; the mechanic need not reinvent the wheel.
- **Mutual respect and toleration:** Hacker — political theory is a **never-ending conversation among theorists**; the greatest debates are never resolved but the criticisms are vivid and illuminating; politics is the **most democratic of sciences**; final judgements rest with all who study theory.
- **Three functions: description** (science, empirical method) · **criticism** and **reconstruction** (philosophy, value-judgment).
- Renewed emphasis on values since post-behaviouralism (1969) and the revival of the 1970s-80s; new horizons named — **environmentalism, feminism, human rights, social justice for subaltern groups**. **Held, *Political Theory Today* (1991):** without systematic pursuit of theory's tasks, politics may be left to the ignorant and self-interested, or to those with a "will to power". ⚠️ Itself a value-judgment.
- 📰 Reason of scale: the functions have become urgent because **most of our problems are assuming global dimensions and are being recognised as the problems of humanity as such** (Gauba, Ch.1, PDF p.23) — book-period "now" (2009), not a later claim.

## 9. The decline claims

- **Easton, *The Political System: An Inquiry into the State of Political Science* (1953)** — six charges: speculation devoid of acute observation; bondage to classics and the history of political ideas; product of the turmoil of past ages (Greece pre-Plato, Italy 15th c., England 16th-17th c., France 18th c.); no outstanding political philosopher after **Marx (1818-83)** and **J. S. Mill (1806-73)**; failure to acquire tools to explain the rise and continuance of **fascism** and **communism** while economists and sociologists advanced; exclusion from **Second World War (1939-45)** decision-making. Remedy: **behavioural political science**, closer to other social sciences. Value clause: modern political science need not evolve values — they are preferences relative to social conditions, and society will evolve its own system; focus on **causal theory**.
- **Causal theory (boxed):** explains the relation between cause and effect; inquires into the cause of what happens and anticipates what will happen if a cause is present.
- ⚠️ Easton's value clause is a **division-of-labour** claim, not the positivist claim that values are meaningless.
- **Cobban, "The Decline of Political Theory", *Political Science Quarterly* (1953):** loss of significance under **capitalist** systems (libertarian-democratic inspiration yet **no political theorist of democracy**; overwhelming **bureaucracy**; huge **military machine**) and **communist** systems (**new form of party organization**; **rule of a small oligarchy**). **Hegel** confined to the **territorial state**, **Marx** to the **proletariat class**, each seeking what was predestined within his frame; contemporary politics too large in scale for any partial theory. **Logical positivists** who excluded values also responsible. Conclusion: **all was not yet lost** — political science must **evolve criteria of judgment**.
- **Lipset, *Political Man: The Social Bases of Politics* (1960):** values already decided; in the United States the search for the **good society** had ended; the prevailing democracy there was "**the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation**." 📰 Contextual and dated.
- ⚠️ **Laslett, Introduction to *Philosophy, Politics and Society*, First Series (Oxford: Blackwell, 1956)** — outside the source chapter, verified independently: "For the moment, anyway, political philosophy is dead." Note the qualifier; testimony, not argument.

## 10. The reversal and the revival

- **Easton 1969, APSA presidential address:** the **post-behavioural revolution**; political science from **pure** to **applied** science; investigation should help society **tide over the prevailing crisis**; **renewed concern with values** excluded by the earlier approach. Method for facts retained.
- ⚠️ **Decline of a mode, not disappearance of theory** — the decisive distinction. The revival begins **inside behaviouralism in 1969**; consolidation runs through the **1970s and 1980s**.
- **Strauss**, "What is Political Philosophy?" (*Journal of Politics*, 1957) and "An Epilogue" to *Essays on the Scientific Study of Politics* (ed. Storing, 1962): the new science is a **symptom** of decline; positivism ignores normative issues; empirical theory asserts the **equal importance of all social values** and denies that some things are intrinsically high — obliterating the distinction between men and brutes, like clean water mixed with dirty.
- **Positivism (boxed):** scientific method as the only source of true knowledge; rejects superstition, religion and metaphysics as pre-scientific; all knowledge based on sense-experience.
- **Germino, *Beyond Ideology: The Revival of Political Theory* (1967):** causes of decline — **positivism** and the craze for science; **political ideologies culminating in Marxism**. Ascendancy in **Oakeshott, Arendt, Bertrand de Jouvenel, Strauss, Voegelin**; expanded 1975 to **Rawls, Macpherson, Christian Bay, Nozick, Marcuse, Habermas, MacIntyre, Walzer**. Identify theory **with philosophy** = critical study of the principles of **right order**; neither reductionist behavioural science nor opinionated ideology; **perennial problems**; **detachment is not ethical neutrality**; the components **cannot be 'reunited'**; behavioural theory uncritically endorsed the established order instead of **speaking truth to power**; no full recovery within the **positivist universe of discourse**.
- **Marcuse (1898-1979):** social-science language conforming to natural-science language **supports the status quo**; operational terminology leaves **no scope for critical vision**; participation measured by turnout means we never ask whether the electoral system conforms to the **spirit of democracy**; inquiry becomes **social control**.
- **Post-1970s settlement:** Easton's renewed concern with values; **Popper** (1902-94) drawing conclusions on social values; **Rawls** (1921-2002) adopting empirical method for his principles of justice; **Macpherson** (1911-87) attacking the empirical theory of democracy of **Schumpeter** (1883-1950) and **Dahl**; **Marcuse and Habermas** applying empirical insight to the critique of contemporary capitalism.
- **Closing formula:** science **strengthens our means**; philosophy **determines our ends**; means and ends are **interdependent**; the two are complementary.
- ⚠️ Two levels: a **methodological** dispute, settled in favour of integration; a **civilizational** dispute about whether politics can avoid argument about ends, not conceded.

## 11. Application discipline

- Five-step move: **split** empirical from evaluative → ask the **science** and **philosophy** questions → run the **conditions test** → name the **proxy risk** → insert **your own verified example**.
- Test cases: **preventive detention** (compliance and effects studied; justification of the restriction still owed) · **elections and democracy** (turnout measured; meaningfulness judged) · **efficiency** (a relation between means and a *given* end; cannot validate the end).
- **Necessary/sufficient:** empirical verification is not necessary for the whole subject; causal tools are necessary but not sufficient; turnout is evidence but not a standard; restoring values in 1969 was necessary but not sufficient without a procedure for adjudicating value-disputes.
- **Boundary:** the lens is owned here; constitutional, institutional, delivery and applied-case facts are delegated.

## 12. Answer architecture

- **Qualified thesis:** neither pure science nor pure philosophy — the systematic study of political life using science to explain reality and philosophy to judge ends, with the weighting historically contested.
- **Directive fixes shape:** Discuss, Examine, Critically examine, Evaluate and Comment all demand a verdict; Evaluate demands the criterion first; Define demands a boundary line.
- **Spine:** premises (public/common sphere; three statement-types; positivist restriction) → mechanism (factual and value questions arise together; science explains, philosophy justifies and clarifies) → conclusion (integration; four heads of significance).
- **Evidence unit:** claim → reconstructed doctrine → named source → significance → objection/limit → reply/verdict. Six available — Wolin, Hacker, Raphael, Held, Easton 1953, Strauss — with Germino and Marcuse in reserve.
- **Three objection-reply chains:** verification · settled-science · theory-equals-ideology. Each carries a residual.
- **Scaling:** 1 unit at 10 marks, 2 at 15, 3-4 at 20; one compressed chain, one full, two full. Scale by **depth**, not names. Graded verdict always.

## 13. Provenance and dating

- **Three registers:** verified attribution and wording → quote exactly; reliable attribution, unverified wording → paraphrase without quotation marks; uncertain attribution → say so, then argue the doctrine.
- **Five-step proposition method:** decode the claim → locate it among the subject's master questions → attribute only as far as the evidence goes → test for universal truth, conditional truth or falsity → answer the **directive**, not the quotation.
- **Dated-source discipline:** the material rests on the Fifth Edition (2009); institutional descriptions, "contemporary" examples and numbers are **book-period**; page anchors do not travel across editions; historical illustrations stay historical; post-2009 claims require independent dating and sourcing.
- **Ownership:** supplementary conceptual support; **no owned General Studies previous-year questions**, and none may be manufactured. Verified Philosophy Optional papers are a **separate application source** only.
- Final checks before submission: **register · date · boundary**.
