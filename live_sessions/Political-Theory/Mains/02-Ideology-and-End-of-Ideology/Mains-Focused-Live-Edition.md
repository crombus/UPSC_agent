# Ideology and End of Ideology — Mains-Focused Live Edition

This topic supplies four portable analytical moves for GS-II, GS-IV and Essay answers: classify an ideology by how ideas are held and used; separate a doctrine's social origin from its validity; identify the point at which commitment becomes closure; and distinguish the disappearance of conflict from its relocation into technical language.

## Lesson 1 — The two senses, the six dimensions, and the formal test

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

An examiner writes "Evaluate Marxism as a political ideology." One question has to be settled first: what has the stem asserted by calling it an ideology? If "ideology" means "a strong political belief", the classification is empty and the answer can only summarise Marxism. The subject's first move is therefore to make the word do precise work — and it does so twice, in two opposite directions.

```text
                        'IDEOLOGY' -- applied in TWO CONTEXTS
                                     |
        +----------------------------+----------------------------+
        |                                                         |
   (a) A SET OF IDEAS                                   (b) A CRITICAL STUDY OF IDEAS
   treated as settled by a group,                       investigates their formation,
   party or nation rather than kept                     the sources of distortion,
   open to scrutiny; used to defend                     and methods of correction
   or attack a social, economic or
   political order
        |                                                         |
   held as FAITH rather than tested                         the INSTRUMENT used
   validity treated as beyond verification                  to examine the object
        |
   different groups -> different ideologies -> differences INEVITABLE
        -> LOVE-HATE RELATIONSHIP, not conducive to SCIENTIFIC TEMPER
        |
   the chapter's ten examples: liberalism | capitalism | socialism | Marxism |
   communism | anarchism | fascism | imperialism | nationalism | internationalism
```

The two senses perform different jobs. The first names the **object** being classified; the second names an **instrument of criticism** that studies how such ideas arise, become distorted and may be corrected. This is a functional contrast, not a claim that criticism can occur only from outside every ideology.

### 1. The definition, and the clause that carries it

In the first sense, ideology means ideas that a group, party or nation treats as settled rather than continually testing, and then deploys to defend or condemn a social, economic or political order (PDF p.28). Two features matter. The first concerns the **mode of acceptance**: evidence is not allowed to keep the claims provisional. The second concerns **function**: the claims support or challenge an arrangement of collective life.

Gauba then describes the first sense as faith-held rather than scientifically tested: adherents regard verification as unnecessary, rival groups develop opposed commitments, and the resulting attraction-repulsion pattern weakens a scientific temper (PDF p.28).

⚠️ Read "a matter of faith" as a description of *how* a claim is held, not as a verdict that it is false. The same chapter later concludes that the ideologies of oppressed classes **cannot be set aside as 'false consciousness'** (PDF p.36). An answer that slides from "held as faith" to "therefore untrue" has contradicted its own source within three pages.

### 2. The second sense, and why the topic needs it

In the second sense, ideology is an inquiry into the formation of ideas, the processes that warp understanding, and the means by which those distortions may be corrected (PDF p.28). Gauba later connects this sense with critical examination of political theories, including identification of their strengths and weaknesses (PDF p.30). It therefore supplies a method for assessing the idea-sets named by the first sense.

### 3. The six dimensions: one table, six axes

| Axis | Set of ideas | Science of ideas |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-matter | Best form of society and government | How ideas are formed and distorted |
| Epistemic mode | A matter of faith | A matter of critical examination |
| Temper of mind | Closed mind | Open mind |
| Stance | Interested search for a better society | Disinterested search for a better society |
| Institutional role | Instrument of politics | Instrument of political theory |
| Relation to authority | Demands subordination to authority | Allows the individual to question authority |

The printed diagram presents six paired axes (PDF p.32). ⚠️ Treat them as related indicators, not as a deductive chain. A refusal to permit questioning is strong evidence of ideological closure, but it does not logically prove every other feature in the column; each axis should be tested on the facts.

### 4. The test is formal, and that is the point

Nothing in the definition turns on content. Constitutionalism, secularism, developmentalism, market reform and environmentalism are all capable of being held either way. The source's own list contains doctrines usually approved and doctrines usually condemned side by side — liberalism and fascism, internationalism and imperialism (PDF p.28) — which is only coherent if the category is formal.

⚠️ The analytical payoff: "ideological" is never established by quoting a position's content. It is established by showing **how the position is held** (is its validity treated as open to examination?) and **what the position is made to do** (is it being used to justify or denounce an arrangement?). That is a two-question test, and it can be run on an opponent's view and on one's own.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** Whenever a stem contrasts an "ideological" position with a "pragmatic" or "evidence-based" one, refuse the contrast until the test is run on both sides. A technically-framed proposal that is not open to being falsified by evidence, and that functions to justify an existing distribution of authority, satisfies both limbs of the definition however undoctrinal its vocabulary.
- **GS-IV.** The six dimensions give a disposition-level vocabulary a public servant's conduct can be scored against: whether a decision is held open to revision, whether criticism is permitted, whether subordination is demanded or questioning allowed. ⚠️ This is a conceptual lens; the service rules, codes and case material belong to Ethics and must come from your own verified preparation.
- **Essay.** On conviction, dogma, tolerance or the examined life, the first-paragraph move is to define the opposition precisely: not belief against doubt, but belief held beyond examination against belief held open to it.

**Answer framework — the classification paragraph.** State the two-limb test in one sentence; apply each limb to the specific body of ideas in the stem, naming the evidence that would settle it; return a verdict that may be split — the doctrine may satisfy the function limb and not the mode limb, or satisfy both only for a part of its claims.

Revision notes:

- **Sense (a):** ideas treated as settled by a group and used to defend or condemn a social, economic or political order.
- **Sense (b):** critical inquiry into idea formation, distortion and correction.
- Sense (a) is faith-held rather than evidence-tested; adherents place validity beyond verification.
- Rival commitments can produce an attraction-repulsion pattern hostile to scientific temper.
- The chapter's ten examples: liberalism, capitalism, socialism, Marxism, communism, anarchism, fascism, imperialism, nationalism, internationalism.
- The relation between the senses is **object and instrument**, which is why one word carries both.
- Six dimensions: subject-matter · faith/critical examination · closed/open mind · interested/disinterested · instrument of politics/of theory · subordination to authority/right to question it.
- The dimensions are connected indicators, not logical entailments; test each separately.
- ⚠️ The test is **formal**: how held, and what work it is made to do. Content never settles it.
- ⚠️ "Faith" describes a mode of acceptance, not a truth-value; the chapter later refuses to treat all ideology as false consciousness.
- **Move:** two-limb test → evidence that would settle each limb → split verdict.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Liberalism and socialism are ideologies, but constitutionalism is not, because constitutionalism is reasonable." What is wrong, and what is the repaired claim?

**Model answer:** The reasoning tests content when the definition tests form. An ideology is a set of ideas accepted as true by a group without further examination and invoked to justify or denounce an arrangement; reasonableness is not among the criteria, which is why the source's own list pairs liberalism with fascism. The repaired claim applies the two limbs: constitutionalism functions as an ideology wherever it is invoked to justify or denounce an arrangement and its validity is treated as beyond examination, and functions as political theory wherever its claims are held open to criticism and revision.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the classification as an insult. Calling a doctrine an ideology in this sense records how it is held and used; it neither refutes it nor exempts the classifier's own position from the same test.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "An ideology is identified by what it asserts, not by how it is held." Comment. *(Comment — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The proposition reverses the test. In Gauba's first sense, ideology concerns ideas treated as settled and used to defend or condemn a collective arrangement. The definition therefore asks about their mode of acceptance and political function, not whether their doctrine is attractive. The mixed list offered in the chapter — including liberalism, socialism, Marxism, anarchism, fascism, imperialism and nationalism — supports that formal reading. The six dimensions reinforce it by contrasting faith with examination, closure with openness, partisan investment with disinterested inquiry, and obedience with questioning. ⚠️ Content still affects probability: a doctrine claiming final truth may more readily be held beyond criticism. That is a tendency, however, not the classificatory rule. The statement is therefore wrong as a definition, though it notices a practical correlation.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks: 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1):** 3 for both limbs — settled acceptance and political use — since one limb cannot exceed 7; 2 for a first-sentence position; 2 for using the mixed doctrine list as evidence of a formal criterion; 1 for applying the six axes without claiming entailment; 1 for the conceded tendency; 1 for a graded close. Deduct 2 if ideology is equated with falsehood.

A religious community's beliefs about the afterlife are not political; its demand that the state fund its schools is. Nothing changed in the beliefs, only in what they were asked to do. This lesson fixes that threshold, then shows why the *same* mechanism that moves people to die for a country moves people to destroy their neighbours.

### 1. The four questions as comparison axes

A group uses ideology to identify its preferred government, justify authority and specify how rulers should be chosen. Gauba frames this through four questions: who rules, how selection occurs, which principles guide government, and which institutions should be preserved or replaced to realise those principles (PDF p.28).

⚠️ Use the questions as **comparison axes**, without claiming that the printed order establishes a logical dependency. Two doctrines may both endorse elections yet disagree about rightful authority or institutional design. Running all four axes prevents an institutional comparison from concealing a deeper normative disagreement.

### 2. The threshold is a threshold of use

Gauba places an ideology inside politics when it defends an existing order or seeks limited or radical change (PDF p.28). The important point is **use**: apply the test to defenders and challengers, while qualifying mixed or ambiguous programmes.

Political ideology may legitimise a ruling class or generate revolutionary impetus; Gauba consequently treats it as evidence of manipulative capacity in either dominant classes or social movements (PDF pp.28-29).

⚠️ Two cautions on that sentence, both of which separate a careful answer from a careless one. The verb is **signifies**, not *is*: the sentence says what ideology makes visible, not what it secretly amounts to. And **both** holders are named — a dominant class *and* a social movement — so the sentence is not a charge against rulers. Quoting the phrase as an indictment of power misreports it.

### 3. Action-orientation, and the indifference of the mechanism

Ideology is action-oriented: it gives adherents a cause and can move them toward struggle and sacrifice (PDF p.29). Gauba contrasts nationalism, which may inspire sacrifice for national freedom, with communalism, which can foster hatred and violence against innocent members of another community.

⚠️ The pair is not a ranking of a good ideology against a bad one. It demonstrates that **one mechanism** — cause, demand to fight, demand to sacrifice — is indifferent to the moral quality of the cause it carries. Nothing in the mechanism filters the cause. That is precisely why the chapter needs a *separate* apparatus, taught from Lesson 3, to assess the cause itself.

A second contrast concerns the ends served. Some ideals protect vested interests, while others challenge irrational conventions and support reform: Gauba contrasts imperialism's facilitation of colonial exploitation with environmentalism's concern for pollution and resource depletion (PDF p.29).

📰 The Fifth Edition (2009) links one obscurantist form of fundamentalism with worldwide terrorism; retain the date rather than turning it into a timeless description. Its box defines obscurantism as a policy or tendency deliberately making matters obscure so that people cannot know the truth (PDF p.29). The useful point is institutional: settled practices of concealment prevent examination.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** On any stem about competing visions of governance, use the four questions as axes rather than narrating positions in turn; the comparison then has a structure and a point of maximum divergence. On any stem about a movement or a reform, apply the threshold: which of defend, limited change or radical change is actually being pursued, and does the doctrine's own account match its practice?
- **GS-IV.** The action-orientation paragraph is a precise GS-IV instrument. Commitment to a cause is a virtue in a public servant and the engine of mobilisation in a mob; what differs is not the intensity but whether the cause and the means remain open to examination. ⚠️ The structure is the contribution here; the case material and codes belong to Ethics.
- **Essay.** The nationalism-and-communalism pair is among the strongest available body-sections: one mechanism, two outcomes, and the conclusion that the filter must come from outside the mechanism. The imperialism-and-environmentalism pair supplies the complementary point that ideals divide by whether they serve vested interests or challenge irrational convention.

**Answer framework — the threshold-and-direction paragraph.** Name the idea-set; identify which of the three uses it is being put to; say whether it is lending legitimacy or carrying an urge to change; then state what it signifies, naming both possible holders; close by separating the *mechanism* from the *cause*, since the mechanism never evaluates the cause.

Revision notes:

- **Four questions:** who should rule · how rulers should be selected · on what principles government should operate · which institutions should be maintained or replaced. Use them as comparison axes; do not claim a necessary order.
- Ideology also determines the **best form of government**, the **basis of the right to rule** and the **procedure of selection of rulers**.
- **Threshold:** enters politics when used to defend an existing order or seek limited or radical change. The trigger is **use**; mixed cases may need a qualified verdict.
- Then it **lends legitimacy to the ruling class** or carries an **urge for revolution**.
- It reveals manipulative capacity in either a dominant class or a social movement; do not reduce the claim to rulers alone.
- **Action-oriented:** presents a **cause** → induces adherents to **fight** → and to make **sacrifices** for its realisation.
- **Nationalism** — sacrifice of wealth or life for the nation's freedom; **communalism** — hatred toward another community, destruction of life and property of innocent persons. ⚠️ One mechanism, two outcomes.
- **Imperialism** — facilitating exploitation of colonial territories and their people; **environmentalism** — saving humanity from atmospheric pollution and depletion of natural resources. Ideals divide by whether they serve **vested interests** or challenge **irrational beliefs and conventions**.
- **Obscurantism:** a settled policy or tendency to obscure matters so that truth is harder to know; an institutional barrier to examination.
- 📰 The fundamentalism-and-terrorism sentence is a **2009** assessment and is dated when used.
- **Move:** use → direction → what it signifies → mechanism separated from cause.

### Concept check

**Question:** A stem asks you to assess a movement "driven by ideology rather than by reason". Where does the chapter's apparatus say the assessment must happen, and where can it not happen?

**Model answer:** It cannot happen in the mechanism. An ideology presents a cause and induces adherents to fight and sacrifice for it, and that mechanism is identical whether the cause is a nation's freedom or a community's destruction, so observing mobilisation establishes nothing about worth. The assessment happens at two separate places: whether the ideal is designed to serve vested interests or challenges irrational beliefs and conventions, and whether the claims are held open to examination or placed beyond it. The second test is symmetrical, so it must also be run on whatever is being offered as "reason".

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat intensity of commitment as the diagnostic. Intensity measures the mechanism, which the chapter shows to be indifferent; the diagnostic is whether validity is treated as something that need not be verified.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Explain the point at which a set of beliefs becomes a political ideology, and what it then signifies. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The threshold concerns use. A belief-set enters politics when deployed to defend an order or pursue limited or radical change; subject matter alone does not settle the issue. The beliefs may also address who rules, how selection occurs, what principles guide government and which institutions remain or change. Once politically deployed, they may legitimise a ruling class or propel revolutionary action. Gauba therefore associates ideology with manipulative capacity in dominant classes and social movements alike. ⚠️ The three directions are broad classifications, and mixed programmes require qualification.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks: 3 + 3 + 2 + 2):** 3 for all three broad directions, since an answer naming only defence of the status quo cannot exceed 7; 3 for naming dominant classes and social movements; 2 for identifying use rather than subject matter as the trigger; 2 for the legitimacy-or-revolution contrast. Deduct 2 if the sentence is presented as a criticism of rulers only.

### 1. Concede the origin, then separate the question

The same labels — liberalism, socialism and communism — can denote either political theory or ideology, which creates an apparent identity between them (PDF p.29). Gauba strengthens that challenge through the three concessions in the diagram and Sabine's 1937 argument that political reflection is itself part of political activity.

Gauba's reply is to distinguish a theory's **origin** from its **validity** (PDF pp.29-30). Political circumstances may explain emergence without deciding worth. Critical evaluation must therefore separate defensible insight from elements that owe their force mainly to political circumstance (PDF p.30).

⚠️ Notice what is *not* claimed. The chapter does not claim that theorists are detached from their times, or that theory floats free of politics. It claims only that showing where a claim came from leaves open whether it is true — and that separating the two is a task the subject performs, not an immunity it enjoys.

### 2. The operation, demonstrated in both directions

The two worked cases are the lesson's evidence, and their value lies in each one being **split**, not approved or dismissed whole.

### 3. The criterion: disinterestedness, and what it actually requires

Gauba contrasts politics as pursuit of partisan ends with two kinds of inquiry. Ideology passionately seeks a better society through a prescribed model; political theory pursues improvement while keeping its goals under continuing investigation and criticism (PDF p.30).

Gauba cites Andrew Hacker's *Political Theory: Philosophy, Ideology, Science* (1961) for a demanding ideal of dispassionate inquiry: arrangements should be supported or opposed only after their rightness is examined. Ideology, by contrast, defends an existing system or attacks it to establish the superiority of an alternative (PDF p.30).

⚠️ Two readings must be kept apart. "Disinterested" here means **having no stake in a particular distribution of power**; it does not mean having no views about what is right. And the criterion describes an **activity** — holding goals under constant investigation, examining rightness before favouring or opposing — which admits of degree and whose failures are identifiable, as the Aristotle and Machiavelli cases demonstrate. The objection "nobody is absolutely impartial" therefore misses: the criterion is a standard of practice, not a psychological claim.

### 4. The failure mode is selection, not fabrication

Gauba identifies **selection** as the practical failure mode. Ideological loading narrows attention to chosen portions of social and political reality, thereby warping both description and explanation; political theory instead aspires to impartial observation (PDF p.30).

⚠️ Distortion by **selection** requires no false statement. A report in which every fact is accurate can still be ideological, because the ideological work was done when the frame was drawn. The diagnostic question is therefore not "is anything here untrue?" but **"what was chosen for examination, and what was left outside the frame?"** That question is usable on a committee report, an index, an impact assessment, a manifesto and an examination answer alike.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** On expert bodies, commissions, regulatory impact assessments and evaluation reports, the selection test is the governing move: accuracy of the contents does not establish impartiality of the frame. On stems contrasting "neutral administration" with "political direction", the origin/validity distinction prevents the lazy conclusion that an administratively-generated proposal is thereby non-ideological.
- **GS-IV.** Disinterestedness as *no stake in a particular distribution of power*, combined with the activity-reading of the criterion, is the precise formulation of administrative impartiality: it does not require an official to have no convictions, only that convictions be held open to examination and that no outcome be favoured because of what it does for the official or for a favoured group.
- **Essay.** The move "concede the origin, separate the validity" is a complete body-section wherever a title invites the genetic fallacy — that a value, institution or right is discredited by the interests present at its birth. Aristotle supplies the paired illustration in a single sentence each way.

**Answer framework — the concede-then-separate paragraph.** State the origin thesis at its strongest; concede it; identify the validity question it leaves open; assess which claims remain defensible and which are weakened by circumstance-bound interest; close with theory as a revisable practice rather than personal detachment.

### 1. Tracy and Bacon: the second sense was a research programme

Gauba presents **Destutt de Tracy (1754-1836)** as developing ideology into a science of idea formation. In that empirical programme, the physical environment stimulates ideas, knowledge begins in sense-experience, supernatural explanation is excluded, and science may improve social and political conditions (PDF pp.30-31).

The Bacon comparison adds disciplined observation and experience as safeguards against the false impressions he called **idols**. Gauba uses Bacon and Tracy together to connect scientific method with the criticism of distorted knowledge (PDF p.31). The short boxed Bacon line is: **"All colours will agree in the dark." (1625)**.

⚠️ **Dating control:** Tracy publicly introduced *idéologie* in **1796**; *Éléments d'idéologie* appeared across **1801-15**. The exact verification URL and access date are recorded in the source ledger.

### 2. The renaming, and the two senses of "interested"

In contemporary usage, ideology commonly denotes ideas adopted to mobilise a group toward predetermined goals. The critical study of ideas travels under other names: **sociology of knowledge**, associated here with Mannheim, and **critical theory**, associated with the Frankfurt School. Gauba traces systematic analysis of ideological distortion through Marx, Lukács and Mannheim (PDF p.31).

The first box asks how social background determines, conditions or distorts knowledge and connects the term with Mannheim's *Ideology and Utopia* (1929). The second treats existing society as falling short of a rational form, so institutions and conduct are assessed by their deviation from that standard; Gauba links this approach with the Frankfurt School founded in 1923. Its emancipatory purpose is contrasted with pursuit of a narrow predetermined goal (PDF p.31).

⚠️ That last sentence is the lesson's sharpest analytical gift, because it forces a disambiguation. In Lesson 3 "interested" meant **having a stake in a particular distribution of power**, and was the mark of ideology. Here "interested" means **having a purpose** — emancipation. Having a purpose is not having a stake. An answer that misses this will either declare critical theory an ideology, contradicting the source, or declare that purposes are disqualifying, which would disqualify the subject itself.

### 3. Marx: a structural mechanism, not a conspiracy

Gauba reconstructs Marx's ideology critique through ***The German Ideology* (1845-46)** and the 1859 Preface to ***A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*** (PDF p.32). The architecture is Marx's: people enter material relations not chosen at will; together those relations form the economic foundation on which legal and political structures arise, with corresponding forms of consciousness. Material social existence therefore shapes consciousness. ✅ The explicit expression **"false consciousness"** is documented in Engels's letter to Franz Mehring of **14 July 1893**, not treated here as a verified Marx quotation.

In Gauba's reconstruction, material needs move ahead while social consciousness trails, producing distortion that appears in ideology; a dominant class may then use that ideology to preserve authority (PDF p.32).

⚠️ Two separate claims live in that paragraph and must not be fused. The **formation** claim is structural and impersonal: relations of production are *independent of their will*, consciousness *corresponds to* the economic structure, and falsity arises from a **lag**. The **deployment** claim is additional: a dominant class *makes use of* the resulting ideology. An answer that reports only the second has turned a theory of social causation into a story about liars — and has then forfeited the explanation of why sincere, intelligent people hold the beliefs in question.

### 4. The worked case: 1789, and the shape it gives an answer

Gauba's 1789 example reads the slogan **Liberty, Equality, Fraternity** as a means of mass mobilisation whose implementation narrowed to the liberty useful to the rising entrepreneurial class, especially protection of property, rather than equivalent freedom for ordinary people (PDF p.32).

⚠️ The case is valuable because it is a **selection** case, and therefore joins directly to Lesson 3's failure mode. Nothing in the slogan was false. Of three announced values, one was realised and two were deferred, and the one realised was the one that secured the new dominant class. The analytic form is: *universal vocabulary, selective realisation, and a distribution of benefit that tracks the selection.* That form is testable anywhere a reform is announced in general terms.

### 1. Engels: the answer by asymmetry

Gauba presents Marx and Engels as treating ideology as protection for dominant-class interests. The bourgeoisie needs it to remain in power; the proletariat is said to lack a comparable vested interest, to seek the state's eventual **withering away**, and to aim at a classless order rather than permanent class rule (PDF p.33).

⚠️ The structure of this reply is worth naming because it recurs: it is an **asymmetry argument**, not an exemption. The proletariat is not said to think more clearly; it is said to have **no interest to protect**, from which the absence of a need for ideology is inferred. The argument therefore stands or falls on the claim about interest, which is exactly where the next difficulty appears.

### 2. Lenin: the term goes neutral, and why

In ***What Is to Be Done?* (1902)** Lenin no longer treats ideology as necessarily deceptive. It becomes a neutral label for the political consciousness of different classes, including the proletariat. His practical reason is that class struggle persists through the socialist phase, so scientific-socialist guidance is needed to resist bourgeois ideology (PDF p.33).

⚠️ Two concepts now travel under one word. Gauba's reconstruction of Marx is pejorative; Lenin's usage neutrally names the political consciousness of any class. Moving from **false consciousness** to proletarian ideology without marking the shift creates an equivocation.

### 3. Lukács: the charge is relocated, not withdrawn

In ***History and Class Consciousness* (1923)**, Georg Lukács makes thought dependent on social life, especially material class relations. Consciousness is class-situated, while the proletariat's deepening estrangement is said to provide a distinctive historical position from which a more universal consciousness becomes possible (PDF p.33).

For Lukács, ideology can express either bourgeois or proletarian consciousness and is not inherently pejorative; Marxism itself expresses the proletarian standpoint. Bourgeois ideology is false on a different ground: its class position is structurally limited because bourgeois persistence depends on exploitation. Gauba also records its domination of proletarian psychological consciousness and Lukács's warning that ideological conflict must not replace class struggle.

⚠️ Lukács moves the criticism from ideology as a genre to the limitations of a class standpoint. The structural dependence on exploitation gives a reason to question bourgeois claims to universality, but it does not by itself prove every proposition in bourgeois thought false.

⚠️ **Supplementary vocabulary, flagged.** *Reification* — the appearance of historically produced social relations as fixed relations between things — is standard in secondary literature on Lukács and is consistent with the structural-limitation argument. ### 1. The three grounds, and which one is fatal

In ***Ideology and Utopia* (1929)**, Karl Mannheim challenges the Marxian account on the three grounds shown above (PDF p.33).

⚠️ Grounds (a) and (c) are corrections of detail: they loosen the interest-to-thought link and add non-class determinants. Ground **(b)** is of a different order, because it is **reflexive**: it applies Marx's own premise to Marxism. That is why it cannot be answered by showing that Marxism is true — the objection is not that Marxism is false but that, on its own account, it cannot claim exemption. An answer that treats (b) as just a third criticism has missed the only one that changes the shape of the problem.

Mannheim's **sociology of knowledge** widens the Marxian framework into a general inquiry about the social conditioning of knowledge (PDF pp.33-34). No standpoint remains automatically exempt.

### 2. Ideology and utopia: a division **within** false consciousness

Mannheim divides false consciousness by direction. **Ideology** conserves the status quo and is associated with a ruling class; **utopia** mobilises change through unrealizable projections and is associated with opposition. He applies the second category to the Marxist classless ideal. Gauba's box describes utopia as a perfect-society vision and, in social science, as attractive but unrealizable principles (PDF p.34).

⚠️ The commonest error on this topic is to read utopia as the *positive* counterpart of ideology — distortion on one side, hope on the other. It is not. Both are forms of **false consciousness**; they differ in **direction**, not in epistemic standing. Only that reading makes the classless-society verdict intelligible, and that verdict is the point of the pair: it is how ground (b) is cashed out against a specific doctrine.

### 3. The problem, the cure, and the word that saves Mannheim from relativism

Mannheim's relativisation makes objective knowledge extremely difficult, not impossible (PDF p.34). His remedy is procedural: recognise partiality, learn from an opponent's view, meet openly, continue dialogue, and build shared knowledge of the historical situation together with a realistic assessment of possibilities.

⚠️ The structural point is that objectivity is **relocated to a procedure among situated knowers**, not restored to an unsituated knower. That is a position worth defending in an answer, and it is the reply to the standard examination proposition that the sociology of knowledge destroys objectivity.

### 4. The step too far, and the chapter's refusal

Mannheim identifies the synthesising intellectuals with social scientists and recommends political authority for them (PDF p.34). Gauba records four responses: the account confuses origin with validity; pushes relativism toward ideas detached from actual holders; risks absolutism by empowering experts; and should instead place social scientists in critical roles such as organisers, journalists, writers and public **conscience-keepers**.

⚠️ Note that the first criticism is the **same distinction** taught at Lesson 3, now turned against Mannheim: having shown how a belief arises, he treats that as settling what it is worth, and then converts an epistemic achievement into a title to rule. The chapter's own position is therefore not anti-expert; it is a position about **the office experts should hold** — critic rather than ruler.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** This lesson is the theoretical base for every question about expert bodies, technical regulators and evidence-based decision-making. The defensible position is the chapter's: specialised knowledge earns a **critical and advisory** office, and the claim that it earns a **governing** office is a separate claim that must be argued and is rejected here as risking absolutism. ⚠️ Institutional detail belongs to Polity and Governance.
- **GS-IV.** Two exports. The reflexive requirement — recognising one's own perception as partial and treating the opponent's view as complementing rather than merely opposing it — is a disposition, not a sentiment, and it has five identifiable steps. The **conscience-keeper** formulation gives a precise description of a public institution's critical function.
- **Essay.** On truth, objectivity, dissent or the role of intellectuals, the Mannheim arc is self-contained: a generalisation that removes every exemption, a procedure that rescues objectivity without restoring certainty, and a recommendation that its own author's critics show to be dangerous.

**Answer framework — the diagnosis-accepted-cure-refused paragraph.** Accept the diagnosis in the source's own strength, including ground (b) applied to Marxism itself; mark the word "difficult"; state the five-step procedure as a relocation of objectivity rather than an escape from situatedness; then refuse the cure on the recorded grounds, naming origin-versus-validity as the decisive one; close with the office the chapter does endorse.

Three features convert the threshold into a test. First, the regime claims possession of final truth rather than merely holding a strong conviction. Second, ideology mobilises people and resources for the declared goal. Third, policy fixed by the ruling group is insulated from criticism. Ask: *is truth monopolised, is mobilisation organised around that monopoly, and can the resulting policy be challenged?*

Arendt's account adds **terror** to ideology and examines the mobilisation of uprooted masses, so ideology alone does not explain total domination. ⚠️ Her own comparison encompasses Nazi Germany and Stalinist USSR. Gauba's narrower fascist-versus-communist teaching contrast should not be mistaken for the full scope of Arendt's work.

⚠️ Read Popper functionally. An open democracy need not be coordinated by one unquestionable doctrine; that does not mean ideologies are absent. The operational condition is effective freedom to criticise institutions and power (PDF p.35), which must be tested rather than presumed.

### Part B — The refusal

Gauba begins the conclusion by noting the false-consciousness and totalitarian critiques, then refuses to extend either critique to every ideology (PDF p.36).

| The chapter's ground | What it says | What it blocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | The preceding analyses concern dangerous forms of ideology, not every instance | Extending a conditional diagnosis to the entire category |
| **Persistence** | Group value-systems continue to travel through ideologies that motivate adherents and communicate claims | Treating the category as spent |
| **Distributional** | Ideology is not confined to dominant classes; oppressed groups also articulate ideologies that cannot simply be dismissed as distorted consciousness | Reading the Marxian critique as a verdict on every holder |
| **Functional** | A **meeting ground** for like-minded people instead of confinement to **tribe, caste, religion, region**; reflecting **changing social consciousness**; producing **strong social movements for the emancipation of various oppressed sections**; manifesting a **deep concern with the future of humanity** | Treating ideology as purely divisive |

Gauba's positive standard centres commitment to a cause rather than personal interest, bias or submission to a person, group or dynasty. Ideology coherently relates a group's view of present reality to its preferred order and communicates that standpoint to others. The world-politics illustration concerns developing countries urging advanced states toward humanist policy (PDF p.36).

⚠️ This positive definition is not a second, rival definition of the same thing. It defines ideology **at its best** — the form in which the category earns its continued existence — and its three exclusions are the operative part: **personal interest**, **bias**, and **submission to a particular person, group or dynasty**. A movement organised around a person rather than a cause fails the definition on its own terms. That is a conceptual finding, and it can be stated without naming any party.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** The three-question threshold is the disciplined way to answer stems about democratic backsliding, emergency powers, or the limits of a governing doctrine, because it asks for evidence rather than adjectives: a monopoly claim to truth, resource-mobilisation for a goal so declared, and the availability of criticism. The open-society box supplies the positive counterpart — free flow of information, policy as a reconciliation of diverse interests, and power-holders who **do not claim that they have found the truth**.
- **GS-IV.** Two high-value exports. The phrase **power-holders do not claim that they have found the truth** is a complete account of official humility, stated institutionally rather than psychologically. And the positive definition's exclusion of **submission to a particular person, group or dynasty** is a precise integrity proposition about the difference between loyalty to a cause and loyalty to a patron. ⚠️ The conduct rules and case material belong to Ethics.
- **Essay.** The structure of this lesson is itself an essay: a danger identified at its extreme, a threshold specified so the extreme is not generalised, and a refusal to condemn the category — closing with the emancipatory record and the meeting-ground function that cut across tribe, caste, religion and region.

⚠️ **Galbraith is the thesis's strongest supporter and its most useful internal problem**, which is why he repays close reading rather than a one-line mention. His evidence is comparative and therefore hard to dismiss: the same four processes in systems professing communism and capitalism. But what he describes is a **transfer of power to a stratum**, together with a conclusion — emancipation through **anti-bureaucratism rather than anti-capitalism** — which is itself a programme with a preferred distribution of power. A thesis whose strongest evidence concludes in a programme has not shown that the choosing of ends has stopped; it has shown **where the choosing moved**.

⚠️ **The End of History is a replacement, not a confirmation.** Convergence predicted **mutual approximation irrespective of doctrine**; the End of History asserts the **victory of one doctrine** as the final form of human government. The collapse of one of the two converging systems is awkward for the first claim and is the premise of the second. Treating 1989 as proof of the convergence thesis fuses two incompatible claims.

⚠️ Finally, note the chapter's own sentence about the thesis's **reception** — that it was given wide publicity **as it was suited to their mode of thought**. That is an explanation of **circulation**, not of **validity**, and it is a textbook instance of the Lesson 3 distinction. Quoting it as though it refuted Fukuyama commits the genetic fallacy the topic exists to prevent.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** Galbraith's four processes — centralization, bureaucratization, professionalization, technocratization — are the analytical vocabulary for any question about expert regulation, delegated rule-making or the insulation of decisions from contestation. The useful question is never "is this technocratic?" but "which contestable choice of ends has been re-described as a technical finding, and who can now contest it?"
- **GS-IV.** Dahrendorf's **institutional isolation** generalises into a test for compartmentalised accountability: where a conflict is confined within a specialised forum, the question is whether confinement has resolved it or merely removed it from the arena where it could be answered. Lipset's "the issues are marginal" is a warning about mistaking the current agenda for the real stakes.
- **Essay.** The sequence Milan 1955 → Bell → Dahrendorf → Lipset → Rostow → Galbraith → 1989 is a complete, dated, named body-section for essays on progress, convergence, the end of grand narratives, or the authority of expertise. ⚠️ Date every claim and say which present you are assessing; the chapter's own present is 2009.

**Answer framework — the bounded-claim-first paragraph.** State the two independent grounds; give the thesis in its **bounded** originating form with Bell's five limits intact; add two or three elaborations chosen for **different kinds of evidence** — institutional, sectoral, developmental — rather than three that repeat one; distinguish the 1989 extension as a replacement; and only then open the criticism, which is Lesson 9.

Revision notes:

- **Period:** mid-1950s and 1960s, centred on Western liberal-democratic debate.

- **Milan, 1955:** *The Future of Freedom* sought anti-Communist common ground; Shils reported it in **"Letter from Milan: The End of Ideology?"**, *Encounter*, November 1955.

Four critics are routinely compressed into one sentence — "they said it defends the status quo" — which wastes three of them. Their charges are of four different **kinds**, and only one survives the test this topic taught at Lesson 3. Knowing which, and why, is the difference between a competent answer and a decisive one.

```text
   FOUR CHARGES, FOUR KINDS
     TITMUSS    -- EVIDENTIAL: omitted monopoly concentration, social
                   disorganisation and cultural deprivation
     C. WRIGHT MILLS -- FUNCTIONAL: political complacency that enables
                   social scientists to accept or defend the status quo
     MACPHERSON -- STRUCTURAL: equitable distribution cannot be solved
                   merely within market society
     MACINTYRE  -- CONSISTENCY (Against the Self-images of the Age; 1971):
                   the thesis ignored the possibility that it expressed
                   the ideology of its own time and place
                               |
   PLUS AN INTERNAL ATTACK, built from the thesis's OWN strongest evidence:
     Galbraith's transfer of power to a bureaucratic-technocratic elite RELOCATES
     the justification of ends into technical and organizational language.
     ENDS CONTINUE TO BE CHOSEN; THEY BECOME HARDER TO CONTEST.
                               |
   WHICH CHARGE SURVIVES THE LESSON-3 REPLY (origin does not settle validity)?
     TITMUSS    -> omissions CAN BE SUPPLIED; the charge improves the thesis
                   rather than destroying it
     MILLS      -> FUNCTION does not establish FALSITY (that is the genetic fallacy)
     MACPHERSON -> contests what counts as settled; depends on a further argument
                   about market societies
     MACINTYRE  -> SURVIVES: the charge is INCONSISTENCY, not origin. A thesis that
                   classifies all doctrines and EXEMPTS ITSELF is defective on its
                   own terms, whatever its origin
```

### 1. Four charges, kept apart

Gauba records criticism by Richard Titmuss, C. Wright Mills, C.B. Macpherson and Alasdair MacIntyre, with a distinct target assigned to each (PDF p.39).

⚠️ The analytic payoff of keeping them apart is that each requires a **different** reply from a defender of the thesis. Titmuss must be answered with evidence; Mills with a demonstration that the thesis is true regardless of whom it comforts; Macpherson with an argument that equitable distribution is achievable within a market society; MacIntyre with a reflexive restatement of the thesis that does not exempt itself. Four charges, four replies — and an answer that notices this is doing analysis rather than listing.

### 2. The one that survives, and why Lesson 3 decides it

This topic's own apparatus is the arbiter. **Origin does not settle validity** (Lesson 3), so a charge resting on where a claim came from, or on whom it serves, does not refute it. That disposes of the *form* of Mills's objection taken alone: showing that a thesis functions as an ideology of political complacency for many social scientists explains its appeal without showing it false.

**MacIntyre's charge is different in kind.** It does not say that the thesis arose in a particular time and place and is therefore false; it says that the thesis **failed to entertain** the possibility that it was itself an expression of the ideology of its own time and place. That is a charge of **inconsistency**: a classification scheme that sorts every doctrine while quietly exempting the sorter is defective **on its own terms**. No genetic fallacy is involved, which is why this one survives.

⚠️ The honest completion, which converts the point into a graded finding: the defender **can** restate the thesis reflexively — admitting it is itself a situated claim and asking to be judged on its evidence. But the restatement costs the thesis its **standing-outside** force, which was the source of much of its rhetorical power. So MacIntyre's charge is not fatal; it is expensive.

### 3. The relocation finding

The strongest argument against the thesis is not any of the four. It is built from **Galbraith's own evidence** and is therefore unanswerable by disputing facts. If power in advanced industrial societies is vested in bureaucracy and technocracy rather than in capitalists, and if the techno-economic structure is shaped by the level of industrialisation rather than by professed doctrine, then decisions continue to embody contested choices about ends — but those choices are now justified in **technical and organisational** language, before audiences qualified to follow it. What has changed is the **vocabulary and venue of justification**, not the fact that ends are chosen.

⚠️ Hence the formulation worth carrying into every answer on this topic: **the evidence shows relocation, not cessation.** And the symmetrical honesty: the **bounded** claim — a rough consensus among Western intellectuals, in that period, on four issues — is untouched by any of the four charges, and substantially held for its time and place.

### 4. The chapter's own verdict, in five moves

The chapter does not endorse the critics either. Its conclusion runs (PDF p.39):

1. The debate promotes liberal-democratic supremacy in both theory and practice.
2. 📰 Gauba's 2009 setting of liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation favoured that thesis.
3. The thesis still requires close examination.
4. Socialist collapse may reflect failures of implementation rather than doctrinal refutation; Western democracy is not a complete model of justice or morality.
5. Emancipation is too complex for ready-made solutions, so relevant ideas from multiple ideologies should be selected and critically tested.

⚠️ Move 4 is the most precise sentence in the conclusion and the easiest to over-read. **"Could be"** blocks an inference — from the collapse of socialist systems to the refutation of socialism — **without asserting** the counter-fact that implementation alone was at fault. That is a model of how to deny an inference without buying a thesis, and it is reproducible in any answer that must resist an over-reading without over-claiming in reply.

⚠️ Move 5 closes the whole chapter by **reopening** the question: ideas from different ideologies are to be **drawn and examined**. Both verbs matter. Drawing without examining is eclecticism; examining without drawing is paralysis. This is the chapter's own answer to the topic's central question, and it is the natural last sentence of a 20-mark answer.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** The relocation finding is the single most transferable move here: on any question about expert-led, data-led or "depoliticised" governance, the analysis is not that expertise is bad but that a contested choice of ends has been re-described as a technical finding, and the governance question is who can still contest it and in what forum.
- **GS-IV.** The four-kinds taxonomy is a reusable discipline for criticism itself: an objection is evidential, functional, structural or one of consistency, and only the last can be pressed without alleging either error or interest. That distinction is directly relevant to how a public servant frames dissent on a file.
- **Essay.** Move 4's "could be" is an essay-level instrument for a balanced paragraph: deny the inference, decline the counter-assertion, and state what further evidence would settle it. Move 5 supplies the closing posture — draw **and** examine.

**Answer framework — the four-charges-then-graded-verdict paragraph.** Name the four charges by **kind**, not by author alone; state which survives the origin-validity reply and why; add the internal relocation argument from the thesis's own strongest evidence; then grade the verdict in two halves — what the bounded claim achieved, and where the unbounded claim fails; close with the residuals that remain open.

Revision notes:

- **Titmuss — evidential:** omitted monopoly concentration, social disorganisation and cultural deprivation within capitalism.
- **Mills — functional:** the thesis encourages political complacency and enables social scientists to accept or justify the established order.
- **Macpherson — structural:** equitable distribution cannot be secured simply by working within market society.
- **MacIntyre — consistency:** the theorists did not consider that their thesis might itself express the ideology of its historical setting.
- ⚠️ Only **MacIntyre's** charge survives the origin-validity reply, because it alleges **inconsistency**, not origin — and it survives at a price for the defender rather than as a knockout, since the thesis **can** be restated reflexively, losing its standing-outside force.
- **Internal attack:** Galbraith's transfer of power to a bureaucratic-technocratic elite **relocates** the justification of ends into technical language; ends are still chosen, and become **harder to contest**.
- **Formulation to carry:** the evidence shows **relocation, not cessation**.
- **Symmetry:** the **bounded** Milan claim is untouched by all four charges and substantially held for its period and place.
- **Verdict, moves 1-2:** the thesis favours liberal-democratic supremacy and was strengthened by Gauba's 2009 climate of liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation.
- **Verdict, move 3:** subject the thesis to close examination.
- **Verdict, move 4:** socialist collapse may reflect implementation failures; this possibility blocks automatic doctrinal refutation, while Western democracy remains morally incomplete.
- **Verdict, move 5:** emancipation has no ready-made solution; select and test ideas across traditions.

### Concept check

**Question:** Which of the four criticisms survives this topic's own origin-versus-validity reply, and what does the survival cost the defender of the thesis?

**Model answer:** MacIntyre's. Titmuss supplies omitted evidence; Mills identifies a status-quo function; Macpherson disputes what market society can achieve. Each needs a further evidential or normative argument. MacIntyre instead says the theorists did not apply their category to themselves and ignored the possibility that their claim expressed the ideology of its own setting. That is inconsistency, not a genetic inference, so separating origin from validity does not answer it. A defender may restate the thesis as a situated, evidence-tested claim, but then loses the posture of standing outside ideology.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not report the four critics as making one charge. They are evidential, functional, structural and consistency-based respectively, and each requires a different reply.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "The end-of-ideology thesis is itself an ideology." Comment. *(Comment — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The claim is fallacious if historical origin is used as proof of falsity; origin and validity remain separate. MacIntyre's version is stronger. The end-of-ideology theorists failed to ask whether their own claim expressed the ideology of its period and location. The defect is therefore inconsistent self-exemption: a framework classifies other doctrines while withholding itself from the same test. This file's two-limb method reinforces the point by asking both what political work the thesis performed and whether its own status remained open to examination. ⚠️ The defect can be repaired by restating the thesis as a situated claim judged on evidence, but that repair sacrifices much of its standing-outside authority.

### Concept check

**Question:** A GS-II stem asks whether a regulatory decision taken "on purely technical grounds" can be called ideological. What does this topic authorise you to say, and what does it forbid?

**Model answer:** It authorises the two-limb test applied to the technical framing itself: is the decision's validity treated as open to examination, and is the framing doing the work of justifying or denouncing an arrangement? **Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "A decision defended as purely technical has placed itself beyond ideology." Comment. *(Comment — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The defence proves less than it claims. Ideology is a formal category — ideas held without further examination and used to justify or denounce an arrangement — so a technical vocabulary neither qualifies nor disqualifies a claim; the test is how the claim is held and what work it is doing. The end-of-ideology literature's own strongest evidence makes the point. Galbraith found centralization, bureaucratization, professionalization and technocratization in the Russian as well as the American systems and concluded that power is vested in bureaucracy and technocracy rather than in capitalists. That relocates the justification of ends into technical and organisational language; ends continue to be chosen and become harder to contest. ⚠️ The converse error must be refused too: technical expertise is not thereby suspect, and the chapter's own position is that specialists serve best as critics and conscience-keepers rather than rulers. The decision is contestable, not exempt.

Two candidates know the same material. One summarises Marx, Mannheim and Bell; the other states a position, defends it with reconstructed arguments attached to named sources, answers the strongest objection and grades the conclusion. The second scores substantially higher on identical content. This lesson is the difference.

### 1. The qualified thesis, and why it opens the answer

⚠️ **The sentence, adaptable to any directive:** *Ideology is neither pure falsehood nor a spent historical category; it is a group-accepted, action-oriented idea-set that can carry emancipatory claims as readily as dominant-class distortion, and the task of theory is to test it — including testing the claim that ideology has ended.*

That sentence is doing three jobs at once. It **refuses two over-readings** the examiner expects you to fall into. It **states the topic's own position** rather than a compromise. And it **names the operation** — testing — that the rest of the answer will perform. Never open with a definition-dump; define inside the argument, where the definition is doing work.

### 2. The directive decides the shape

| Directive | What it demands on this topic | The characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Define / Distinguish** | A boundary line, not a verdict; both senses and the object-instrument relation | Listing features without stating what separates them |
| **Explain** | A mechanism with its steps, not a list of positions | Grounds listed with no work attached to any |
| **Discuss** | Both sides reconstructed fairly, the disagreement named, a verdict | A survey that decides nothing |
| **Examine / Critically examine** | Restate testably; evidence both ways; a finding. "Critically" adds a named attack on the reasoning | Hostile adjectives in place of an attack on the logic |
| **Evaluate** | Criterion of worth **first**, score against it, graded verdict | Judgment without a criterion |
| **Analyse** | Decompose, relate the parts, conclude | Description without decomposition |
| **Comment** | A position in sentence one, then grounds and a qualification | Balance with no position stated |

⚠️ Mark-weight is itself a cue. A 10-mark stem asks for one unit and a verdict; a 20-mark stem asks for the spine, several units, at least one objection-reply chain and a graded verdict. Writing 20-mark depth in 10-mark space loses marks for proportion.

### 3. Six evidence units — claim, name, significance, limitation

A unit is a reconstructed argument attached to a named source, carrying its own limitation. Deploying units rather than names prevents an answer reading as a roll-call.

| # | Claim | Named | Significance | Limitation to state |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **U1** | Gauba reconstructs Marx through a lag between material change and inherited consciousness, followed by dominant-class use | **Marxian structure through Gauba; "false consciousness" explicitly in Engels, 1893** | Explains naturalisation without requiring deliberate deception | Reflexive difficulty; careless use becomes conspiracy |
| **U2** | Ideology is **not necessarily** a distortion; it is a neutral concept covering the political consciousness of any class, the proletariat included | **Lenin** (1902) | Ideology need not be pejorative; the struggle is long | Blunts the critique; invites a self-exempting "scientific" claim |
| **U3** | Bourgeois ideology is false **not because ideology is false consciousness** but because the bourgeois class situation is **structurally limited** | **Lukács** (1923) | Restores the critique from structure, not motive | Still claims a privileged standpoint for one class |
| **U4** | All thought is shaped by its social background, so **Marxism itself is the ideology of a class**; false consciousness divides into ideology and utopia | **Mannheim** (1929) | Nobody is exempt; objectivity relocated to a procedure | The cure — rule by social scientists — confuses origin with validity |
| **U5** | A truth monopoly, organised mobilisation and suppressed criticism mark closure; Arendt adds terror and mass uprooting | **Popper** (1945) with **Arendt** (1951), whose totalitarian comparison spans Nazi and Stalinist cases | Threshold for ideological closure | Does not condemn mobilisation where criticism remains possible |
| **U6** | Convergence through centralization, bureaucratization, professionalization and technocratization; power vested in a **bureaucratic-technocratic** elite | **Galbraith** (1967) | The thesis's best evidence, and the relocation argument against it | Describes relocation, not cessation; his conclusion is a programme |

### 4. Three objection-and-reply chains, each with its residual

```text
 CHAIN 1  OBJECTION  'Gauba's Marx reconstruction makes ideology false consciousness,
                      so all ideology
                      should be discarded.'
          REPLY      Lenin makes the term neutral and Lukacs relocates the falsity
                      to a structurally limited class situation; the chapter itself
                      holds that oppressed classes have ideologies which CANNOT BE
                      SET ASIDE as 'false consciousness'.
          RESIDUAL   Gauba's Marx reconstruction is not withdrawn -- only the general
                      verdict drawn from it.

 CHAIN 2  OBJECTION  'The end-of-ideology thesis shows advanced societies have moved
                      beyond ideological conflict.'
          REPLY      the thesis's own best evidence (Galbraith) shows power RELOCATED
                      to a bureaucratic-technocratic elite, so ends are still chosen
                      but justified in technical language; MacIntyre adds that the
                      thesis EXEMPTED ITSELF from its own category.
          RESIDUAL   the BOUNDED Milan claim -- a rough consensus among Western
                      intellectuals on four issues -- survives all four criticisms.

 CHAIN 3  OBJECTION  'The sociology of knowledge proves objective political knowledge
                      is impossible.'
          REPLY      the chapter's word is EXTREMELY DIFFICULT, and Mannheim supplies
                      a five-step procedure -- partiality recognised, complemented by
                      the opponent's view, open-minded meeting, dialogue, synthetic
                      common knowledge; objectivity is RELOCATED TO A PROCEDURE.
          RESIDUAL   the recorded criticism stands against his CURE, not his
                      diagnosis: origin is confused with validity, and rule by social
                      scientists risks absolutism.
```

⚠️ Choose the chain that attacks the **stem's** reasoning, not the chain you find most interesting. A chain deployed against a claim the stem never made is wasted space.

### 5. Mark-scaled architecture

| Weight | Shape | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **10 marks (~150 words)** | Thesis → one unit → verdict | Qualified thesis adapted to the directive; both senses or the relevant definition; **one** evidence unit; a one-line verdict. No chain. |
| **15 marks (~250 words)** | Thesis → compressed spine → two units → one chain → verdict | Premises (what ideology is and does) → mechanism (mobilisation and distortion; the closure threshold) → conclusion; two units spanning different positions; one objection-reply chain; a graded verdict. |
| **20 marks (~250 words)** | Thesis → spine → three to four units → one or two chains → graded verdict with residuals | Directive fidelity in the thesis; units chosen for **different kinds** of claim; the decline-versus-relocation distinction as the decisive move; residuals named in the close. |

⚠️ At every weight a **graded** verdict is required: what holds, what holds only under stated conditions, and what fails. "Both views have merit" is not a verdict.

### 6. Provenance under pressure

This topic punishes fabricated citation because many marks-bearing claims are attributions. Use three registers.

- **Register 1 — verified.** Use exact wording only when independently authenticated; otherwise paraphrase and attach the thinker, work and date. The source ledger holds the verification URLs.
- **Register 2 — reliable paraphrase.** State the doctrine without quotation marks: Lenin's neutral concept, Lukács's structural limitation, Mannheim's procedure, Popper's threshold, Arendt's two elements and Galbraith's four processes.
- **Register 3 — openly uncertain.** Flag in the sentence that uses it. ⚠️ *Reification* and the *total/particular conception* and *relationism* vocabulary are secondary-literature glosses, not the chapter's words; attach no title, year or page.

⚠️ **If an unfamiliar quotation is set in the stem**, do not invent a book, edition, year or page. Decode the proposition — identify its claim and which side of the distortion-mobilisation or decline-relocation debate it echoes — then answer using that analysis, noting that the exact provenance is not independently verified.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Six checks: five consolidate a block; the sixth spans the whole arc, because one continuous argument from a formal definition to a graded verdict cannot be tested inside a single block. Each model is held within 250 words.

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 and 2

**Question:** Build the chain from the definition of ideology to the claim that it signifies manipulative power, naming each step.

**Model answer:** One: ideology in the first sense concerns ideas treated as settled and used to defend or condemn a collective order. Two: this faith-held mode weakens verification and can harden rivalry between groups. Three: compare doctrines through four axes — rightful rulers, selection, governing principles and institutional design — without claiming a necessary sequence. Four: political use may defend an order or seek limited or radical change, while mixed programmes require qualification. Five: the doctrine may legitimise rulers or mobilise a movement. Six: it therefore reveals manipulative or mobilising capacity on either side. Seven: action-orientation supplies a cause, struggle and sacrifice, which explains how similar machinery can support national freedom or communal violence.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read "manipulative power" as an accusation against rulers. It names a dominant class **and** a social movement, and "signifies" describes what ideology makes visible, not who is guilty.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 3 and 4

**Question:** One distinction does most of the work across these two lessons. Name it, and show it operating in three different places.

**Model answer:** The key distinction separates a theory's social origin from its validity. Sabine places political theorising inside political activity; Gauba concedes that point but still requires claim-by-claim criticism. The procedure separates defensible insight from elements whose force chiefly reflects political circumstance. Gauba's Aristotle and Machiavelli examples illustrate partial rejection rather than wholesale dismissal. Aristotle's defence of slavery and women's subordination is rejected, while Gauba's attributed power-and-virtue maxim is retained only as his illustration; it is not authenticated here as an Aristotle quotation. Machiavelli's amoral advice is separated from useful behavioural observation. The distinction also controls the Marxian material: unchosen material relations and lagging consciousness explain formation, but explanation of origin does not itself decide truth.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read the distinction as a claim that theory escapes its social conditions. Those are conceded in full; what is denied is that conceding them answers the separate question. It names a task, not an immunity.

### Cumulative check C — Lessons 5 and 6

**Question:** Each of these thinkers answers a difficulty created by the one before. Set out the sequence.

**Model answer:** Marx supplies the material structure through which social existence shapes consciousness; Gauba reconstructs distortion through a lag in consciousness and later dominant-class use. Engels owns the explicit phrase **false consciousness**. The reflexive difficulty is that the critique also reaches revolutionary thought. Gauba's Marx-and-Engels answer is asymmetry: the proletariat supposedly lacks an interest in permanent rule and seeks a classless, stateless future. Lenin replies that conflict persists during socialism, so ideology becomes a neutral class consciousness and the proletariat needs scientific-socialist guidance. This neutralisation weakens critique. Lukács restores it by locating bourgeois falsity in a structurally limited standpoint rather than in ideology itself, but still privileges the proletarian standpoint. Mannheim generalises social conditioning to every standpoint, including Marxism, then divides distorted consciousness into conservative ideology and change-oriented utopia.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present the four as four opinions about what ideology means. Each repairs a specific failure in the position before it, and an answer not naming the failures has reduced an argument to a list.

### Cumulative check D — Lesson 7

**Question:** Popper and Arendt tie ideology to totalitarianism, yet the chapter refuses to condemn ideology as such. State the threshold and the refusal, and show they are consistent.

**Model answer:** Popper's threshold combines a regime's claim to final truth, organised mobilisation for that claim and the suppression of criticism. Arendt adds terror and the historical mobilisation of uprooted masses; her totalitarian comparison spans Nazi Germany and Stalinist USSR. Gauba's refusal addresses the wider category. Ideologies continue to carry group values, oppressed groups articulate their own, and some build solidarities or support emancipation. The positions are therefore consistent: one identifies conditions of totalitarian closure, while the other denies that those conditions define every ideology.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use Popper's claim that open societies do not need an ideology as though none operates in one. Functionally it says such a system is not **coordinated by** a doctrine held beyond question; whether something is doing that work stays open.

### Cumulative check E — Lessons 8 and 9

**Question:** Give the four-sentence account of the end-of-ideology debate you would use to open a 20-mark answer, then name two things it deliberately refuses to say.

**Model answer:** One: reviewed in the mid-1950s and the 1960s, the thesis rested on two independent grounds — that ideology is a tool of totalitarianism with no place in open societies, and that at an advanced stage of industrial development a country's social-economic organisation is determined by its level of development rather than by its political ideology. Two: its originating statement was bounded, Bell at Milan in 1955 claiming only a **rough consensus among intellectuals in the Western World** on the welfare state, decentralised power, a mixed economy and political pluralism, and it was elaborated on different kinds of evidence — Dahrendorf's institutional isolation of industrial conflict, Bell's post-industrial sectoral trends, Lipset's reading of the issues then in contention, Rostow's five stages and Galbraith's bureaucratic-technocratic elite. Three: the 1989 and 1991 collapses produced not a confirmation but a **replacement**, since Fukuyama asserts the victory of one doctrine where convergence had predicted mutual approximation. Four: four critics press distinct charges — evidential, functional, structural and consistency — and the thesis's own strongest evidence shows **relocation**, not cessation. The account refuses to say that the bounded Milan claim failed, and refuses to say the thesis is false **because** of where it arose.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not merge the two grounds. They are independent, so refuting the totalitarianism ground leaves the convergence claim standing and vice versa.

### Cumulative check F — the whole arc, Lessons 1 to 11

**Question:** In one connected argument, move from the definition of ideology to a graded verdict on the end-of-ideology thesis, marking where each earlier lesson does work.

**Model answer:** Ideology is classified formally by settled acceptance and political use (L1). Once deployed, it can legitimise rulers or mobilise movements through causes demanding sacrifice (L2). Evaluation then separates social origin from validity and tests selective framing (L3). Marx supplies the structural account; Gauba reconstructs distortion through lag and use, while Engels owns the explicit phrase **false consciousness** (L4). Lenin neutralises ideology, Lukács relocates falsity to a limited standpoint, and Mannheim generalises social conditioning (L5-L6). Popper's closure threshold combines truth monopoly, mobilisation and suppressed criticism; Arendt adds terror and mass uprooting across Nazi and Stalinist cases. Gauba nevertheless refuses universal condemnation (L7). The end-of-ideology thesis rests on open-society and convergence arguments stated in a bounded Western setting (L8). ⚠️ Verdict: its limited historical observation may hold, but the broader claim fails where conflict has moved into technical language rather than ended (L9-L11).

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the chain become a tour of the lessons. Each citation is load-bearing: remove the step and the conclusion fails.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ Original practice prompts written for this edition. They are not previous-year questions and must not be cited as such. Every scoring note is specific to its own answer.

### 10-mark practice (about 150 words)

**Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish ideology from political theory. *(Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Liberalism, socialism and communism can name either theory or ideology, so subject matter does not separate them. The difference lies in stance and method. Ideology passionately pursues a prescribed political model and supports or attacks an order accordingly. Political theory keeps its proposed ends open to continuing criticism; Hacker's ideal judges an arrangement's rightness before endorsement or rejection. The operational failure is selective framing: an account may report accurate facts yet distort by examining only chosen parts of reality. ⚠️ Disinterested inquiry means freedom from a stake in a predetermined distribution of power, not absence of values.

**Scoring note:** a Distinguish stem needs a boundary line, not a verdict. Marks concentrate in refusing the subject-matter route at once, in the passionate-against-disinterested pair in the source's own words, and in naming selection as the failure mode — the only operationally testable part, and the one most answers omit.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** Explain why the chapter refuses to treat all ideology as false consciousness. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Gauba refuses universal condemnation on four grounds. **Scope:** false-consciousness and totalitarian analyses describe dangerous forms, not every ideology. **Persistence:** groups continue to express values and claims ideologically. **Distribution:** oppressed groups also construct ideologies, so dominant-class analysis cannot dismiss the category in advance. **Function:** ideology can build solidarities beyond tribe, caste, religion or region, reflect changing consciousness and support emancipatory movements. ⚠️ The Marxian critique is not withdrawn; what fails is its conversion into an automatic verdict on every ideology.

**Scoring note:** an Explain directive forbids a bare list, so each ground carries the work it performs and the four are deliberately heterogeneous. The closing qualification stops the answer reading as a reversal of the preceding doctrine — the error this stem is designed to catch.

### 15-mark practice (within 250 words)

**Practice question 3 [15]:** Examine the claim that political theory and political ideology cannot be distinguished. *(Examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Restated testably, the claim denies any separating criterion. Its strongest support should come first. Liberalism, socialism and communism name both theories and ideologies. Theory can recommend action, political pressure can explain its emergence, and theoretical conflict may mirror political conflict. Sabine's 1937 Preface goes further by placing reflection on political ends, means, constraints and duties within political activity itself.

The counter-case concedes those origins but asks separately whether particular claims remain defensible. Critical evaluation distinguishes durable insight from elements chiefly sustained by political circumstance. Gauba's worked cases demonstrate partial rather than wholesale judgment: Aristotle's defence of slavery is rejected, while a general maxim Gauba attributes to him is retained only as Gauba's illustration; it is not authenticated here as an Aristotle quotation.

⚠️ Finding: the claim holds about origin and fails about validity. The criterion is an **activity** — submitting one's own claims to examination — so it admits of degree and cannot be settled by inspecting a label.

**Scoring note:** "Examine" is discharged by testable restatement, evidence both ways and a finding. The move that earns the marks is conceding Sabine entirely: resisting him means defending an implausible claim about theorists' personal detachment instead of the defensible claim about what theory **does**.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** "The sociology of knowledge destroys the possibility of objective political knowledge." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The statement assumes that social conditioning proves unreliability. Mannheim partly supports the concern: every standpoint, including Marxism, is shaped by social background; generations as well as classes influence consciousness; and objectivity becomes extremely difficult.

Three replies defeat the absolute conclusion. First, difficulty is not impossibility. Second, Mannheim offers a procedure: acknowledge partiality, learn from an opponent, meet openly, sustain dialogue and form shared historical understanding with a realistic view of possibilities. Objectivity is relocated among situated knowers. Third, the proposition mistakes an account of origin for a verdict on validity.

Attacking the internal logic: the proposition is self-undermining, since it is itself a claim about knowledge advanced by a socially situated speaker.

⚠️ The qualification owed is that Mannheim's **cure** attracts the same charge. The recorded criticism is that he confuses origin with validity, and his recommendation that social scientists be given authority to rule converts an epistemic achievement into a title to power. Verdict: the proposition fails, and the strongest version of the worry survives against his cure rather than his diagnosis.

**Scoring note:** the "critically" element is discharged by a named move — exposing the self-undermining structure — not by hostile adjectives. Defending the diagnosis while conceding the failure of the remedy is the position the evidence supports, and it is what separates this from a partisan defence.

### 20-mark practice (within 250 words)

**Practice question 5 [20]:** Critically evaluate the end-of-ideology thesis. *(Critically evaluate — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Evaluation requires a criterion. The one adopted here is whether contested choices about ends are still being made and remain contestable.

The thesis rests on two independent claims: that ideology is a tool of totalitarianism with no place in open societies, and that at an advanced stage of industrial development a country's social-economic organisation is determined by its level of development, not by its political ideology. Its originating statement is bounded — Bell at Milan claimed a rough consensus among Western intellectuals on four issues: the welfare state, decentralised power, a mixed economy, political pluralism. Elaborations followed on different evidence: Dahrendorf's institutional isolation of industrial conflict; Bell's post-industrial trajectory towards services and technical elites; Lipset's verdict that left-right differences were no longer profound; Rostow's five stages irrespective of ideology; Galbraith's bureaucratic-technocratic elite in whom power is vested.

Against the criterion the unbounded thesis fails, and Galbraith's own evidence shows why. A shift of power to an expert stratum relocates the justification of decisions into technical language; ends continue to be chosen and become harder to contest. His conclusion that emancipation lies in anti-bureaucratism is itself a programme with a preferred distribution of power. The critics converge from four directions: Titmuss on omitted evidence, Mills on political complacency, Macpherson on distribution within a market society, MacIntyre on the thesis as an expression of its own time and place.

⚠️ Graded verdict: the bounded claim substantially held for its period and place; the unbounded claim fails. The evidence shows relocation, not cessation.

**Scoring note:** "Critically evaluate" collapses without a declared criterion, so it is stated first and every judgment refers back to it. What lifts this above a competent answer is using the thesis's **strongest** supporter as the decisive evidence against its unbounded form, and a graded close that concedes what the thesis achieved.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** "Ideology is indispensable to politics and dangerous to truth." Discuss with reference to Marx, Mannheim and Popper. *(Discuss — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition pairs two claims usually argued separately. Both hold, with qualifications, as claims of different types.

Indispensability first. Ideology supplies causes, mobilisation and sacrifice while addressing rightful rule, selection, governing principles and institutional design. It enters politics by defending an order or seeking limited or radical change; mixed programmes may combine these directions. Gauba also notes that ideologies persist as carriers of group values, including those of oppressed groups, and may support emancipation.

Danger to truth next. Marx supplies the material structure; Gauba adds the lag-and-use reconstruction, while Engels owns the explicit phrase **false consciousness**. Mannheim extends social conditioning to Marxism itself and divides distortion into conservative ideology and change-oriented utopia. Popper locates the extreme where rulers claim final truth, mobilise around it and suppress criticism.

⚠️ Verdict: both halves hold, asymmetrically. Indispensability is a claim about politics as such; danger is a claim about a **mode of holding** ideas, which admits of degree. The chapter's close follows: draw relevant ideas from different ideologies, and examine them.

**Scoring note:** a Discuss stem requires both sides reconstructed fairly, the disagreement named, and a verdict. The distinguishing move is the **asymmetry** in the close: showing the halves to be claims of different types makes accepting both coherent rather than evasive, which the three named thinkers are chosen to support.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. The two senses, exam-tight

| Term | One-line form |
|---|---|
| Ideology, sense (a) | A group-held set of ideas treated as settled and deployed to defend or condemn a collective order |
| Ideology, sense (b) | Critical study of how ideas arise, become distorted and may be corrected |
| Obscurantism | A continuing practice of concealment that obstructs access to truth |
| Sociology of knowledge | Inquiry into the social conditioning of knowledge; associated here with Mannheim's *Ideology and Utopia* (1929) |
| Critical theory | Assessment of institutions against a still-unrealised rational and emancipatory standard; Frankfurt School context |
| Utopia | An attractive perfect-order projection judged unrealizable |
| Totalitarianism | Comprehensive state direction of public and private life, with opposition and alternative aims excluded |
| Open society | Information circulates, interests are reconciled, criticism remains possible and rulers disclaim final truth |
| Positive definition | Cause-centred coherent ideas rather than personal interest, bias or submission to a person, group or dynasty |

## B. The six dimensions as separate tests

Test six axes independently: preferred order versus study of idea formation; faith versus criticism; closed versus open temper; interested versus disinterested search; political mobilisation versus theoretical inquiry; subordination versus permission to question. ⚠️ The axes may reinforce one another, but none logically entails every other row.

## C. Entry into politics, and action

Comparison axes: rightful rulers; method of selection; governing principles; institutions retained or replaced. Political use may defend an order or seek limited or radical change; mixed cases remain possible. Ideology can legitimise a ruling class or mobilise a movement. Its action sequence is cause → struggle → sacrifice. Nationalism/communalism illustrate different moral outcomes from mobilisation; imperialism/environmentalism contrast vested exploitation with reformist concern. 📰 The fundamentalism-terrorism link is retained only as Gauba's 2009 assessment.

## D. The theory/ideology criterion

Theory and ideology may share labels. Concede that theory prescribes action, arises amid political pressure and may conflict along political lines. Sabine places theorising within politics; Gauba replies that origin still does not decide validity. Evaluate claims separately, distinguishing durable insight from circumstance-bound interest. Gauba rejects Aristotle's defence of slavery and women's subordination; his further power-and-virtue maxim is not authenticated here as an Aristotle quotation. Machiavelli's amoral advice and behavioural insight are likewise separated. Hacker's ideal is inquiry that examines rightness before support or opposition. The practical failure mode is selective framing.

## E. The science of ideas, and the four interpreters

- **Tracy (1754-1836):** empirical study of idea formation; public introduction of *idéologie* in **1796**, followed by the 1801-15 publication sequence.
- **Bacon (1561-1626):** **careful and accurate observation and experience**; distortion by **false impressions or 'idols'**; boxed, **"All colours will agree in the dark." (1625)**.
- **Marx/Gauba/Engels:** Marx supplies the material-structure and social-consciousness architecture; Gauba reconstructs distortion through a lag between changing needs and inherited consciousness; Engels explicitly uses **"false consciousness"** in the 14 July 1893 Mehring letter. The 1789 example illustrates universal language followed by selective implementation favouring property.
- **Engels (1820-95) with Marx:** ideology **protects the interests of the dominant class**; the **bourgeoisie needs** it; the proletariat has **no vested interests**, works for the state to **wither away** and for a **classless society**.
- **Lenin (1870-1924)**, *What is to be Done?* **(1902):** **not necessarily a distortion**; a **neutral concept** covering **the political consciousness of different classes, including the proletarian class**; the **class struggle continues for a very long time during the socialist phase**; **scientific socialism** needed **lest they are overpowered by the bourgeois ideology**.
- **Lukács (1885-1971)**, *History and Class Consciousness* **(1923):** consciousness is class-situated; proletarian estrangement is claimed to open a route toward universality; Marxism expresses that standpoint. Bourgeois ideology is criticised because its class position depends on exploitation, not because every ideology is intrinsically false. His warning keeps ideological struggle subordinate to material class struggle. ⚠️ *Reification* is a gloss.
- **Mannheim (1893-1947)**, *Ideology and Utopia* **(1929):** economic interest relates only indirectly to styles of thought; social background also shapes Marxism; generations and other groups matter. Sociology of knowledge generalises social conditioning. Conservative ideology and change-oriented utopia are both forms of distortion. Dialogue among partial perspectives seeks realistic shared knowledge, but rule by social scientists risks absolutism; their safer office is critic and conscience-keeper. ⚠️ *Total/particular conceptions* and *relationism* are glosses.

## F. Closure, and the chapter's refusal

Popper's closure test combines a final-truth claim, mobilisation around that claim and blocked criticism. Arendt adds terror and the mobilisation of uprooted masses; her totalitarian comparison centrally spans Nazi Germany and Stalinist USSR. Gauba's refusal then restores scope: ideology also carries group values, gives oppressed groups a language of claims, builds solidarities across inherited identities and may support emancipation. The category is therefore neither innocent nor uniformly totalitarian.

## G. The end-of-ideology debate, and the verdict

**Debate spine:** two grounds — open-society criticism and industrial convergence. Milan 1955 produced Shils's report and Bell's bounded consensus claim. Dahrendorf stressed institutional separation of industrial conflict; Bell, services and technical elites; Lipset, incremental political stakes; Rostow, a common development sequence; Galbraith, bureaucratic-technocratic power. Fukuyama's 1989 thesis replaces convergence with liberal victory. **Critic ownership:** Titmuss supplies omitted monopoly, social-disorganisation and cultural-deprivation evidence; Mills identifies political complacency and status-quo justification; Macpherson disputes equitable distribution within market society; MacIntyre identifies self-exemption. **Verdict:** bounded convergence may fit its setting, but the broad thesis confuses relocation with cessation.

## H. Decision rules for the examination hall

1. Decode the directive first; Evaluate demands the criterion before any judgment, and Critically examine demands a named attack on the reasoning.
2. Open with the qualified thesis, never a definition-dump.
3. Classify with the two-limb test, applied symmetrically to both sides of the stem.
4. Concede origin, separate validity; name the segregation operation rather than asserting objectivity.
5. Deploy units, not names: claim → reconstruction → named source → use → limitation.
6. On any "has X ended" stem, run **decline versus relocation** before concluding.
7. Grade the verdict: what holds, what holds only under stated conditions, what fails — and name the residuals.
8. Date every situated claim, say which present you are assessing, and place every attribution in a register.

## I. Seven exam-ready formulations

- Ideology is identified by settled acceptance plus political use, not by attractive or unattractive content.
- Mobilising capacity belongs to ruling classes and social movements alike.
- Social origin explains emergence; validity still requires separate assessment.
- Lukács locates bourgeois distortion in a structurally limited standpoint, not in ideology as a genre.
- Mannheim applies social conditioning reflexively, including to Marxism.
- Oppressed groups' ideologies cannot be dismissed in advance by a dominant-class model.
- MacIntyre's strongest challenge is self-exemption: the end-of-ideology thesis did not test itself as a situated ideology.
