# Diverse Perspectives on Justice — Mains-Focused Live Edition

A fast-preparation edition for GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay. It teaches only what enriches a Mains answer on how social advantages should be distributed, and stops there.

**The one idea that organises everything.** Topic 19 asks *what justice is*; this topic asks *which rule distribution should follow*, and it answers that question eight times over. Each of the eight perspectives accepts that a share has to be allocated and then fixes the allocating rule somewhere different — on a fair procedure chosen in advance (Rawls), on the valid history of a holding (Nozick), on the structure of ownership that produced the holding (Marxist), on what a democratic state can sustain without coercion (democratic socialist), on the removal of coercive authority itself (anarchist), on a standing that no share repairs (feminist), on who is believed to deserve a lesser share (subaltern), and on the shared meaning of the good being distributed (communitarian). Place a stem anywhere on that range and it becomes answerable; miss it and the answer becomes a parade of eight summaries with no verdict.

**Scope.** This is **Topic 20 of the subject's twenty-three topics**, and it remains a standalone session. Depth and format drop to Mains level; the topic's syllabus share does not. Political Theory is supplementary conceptual support. The Commission prints no Political Theory paper, and **this topic owns no General Studies or Optional previous-year question** — the route count into its canonical files is zero by design, and no proxy, alias, mirror or synthetic route is created here.

**The boundary rule for this topic is object, not thinker.** Every one of the eight perspectives already has an ideology owner elsewhere, and this edition does not re-teach any of them as ideologies. Each perspective enters **only as a rule of distribution and a verdict on justice**. ➡️ What justice is, Barker's ordering, Plato, legal and political justice, the procedural/substantive debate and the need/desert floor belong to **Topic 19**. ➡️ The common good, common interest and public interest triad, the printed comparison of the three, and the Rousseau→Green genealogy belong to **Topic 21**, which prints the same four communitarian thinkers in its own chapter. ➡️ Liberty, equality and the justification of property belong to **Topic 18**. ➡️ Anarchist thinker biographies and the violence/syndicalism debate to **Topic 05**; the labour theory of value, Gramsci and hegemony as a theory to **Topic 04**; elite theory to **Topic 15**; feminist doctrine and the waves to **Topic 06**; the situated self as a theory of politics to **Topic 07**; the two liberalisms as ideologies to **Topic 03**; the perspectives applied to the *state* rather than to distribution to **Topic 13**. ❌ Punishment theory, constitutional provisions, reservation jurisprudence, succession law and welfare-scheme administration appear nowhere in this edition.

**What is deliberately left out, and why.** The labour-theory-of-value derivation — value against price, natural against market price, the 1859 quotation and the thirty-hour arithmetic — is reduced to the one justice claim it supports. The five printed functions of the socialist state, with Lenin's two pamphlets, Soviet expropriation, Mao's permanent revolution, socialism in one country and peaceful co-existence, are institutional history and survive as a single line, kept only because that record is what generates the author's own objection about bureaucratic power. The eight anarchist thinkers are one roster line each, carrying only the distributive claim each contributes. The elite-theory block is one line. The 1995 development-report leaderboard is dropped except for the single finding that carries the argument, and the report's characterisation of the most gender-biased societies is omitted entirely. **No compiled four-option question corpus appears anywhere in this edition** — practice runs as concept checks and solved Mains models. Definitions, causal logic, objections and replies, deployment, answer frameworks and required practice are kept intact.

**Three reading conventions, stated once.** Page anchors are given as `PDF p.N (printed M)` on a flat offset of **19** across Ch.20, which runs **PDF 446-473 = printed 427-454**. **Bold is not a quotation marker** — it also carries emphasis, headings and labels, and the diagrams and register notes paraphrase as well as reproduce. ⚠️ **The six axes used throughout are an exam-use synthesis imposed on the chapter for comparison, not the author's own headings**; the Advanced owner says so expressly (§12), and the point is made once here and once again at Lesson 11 rather than at every use.

### 1. The printed opening, and the claim most answers skip

The chapter opens by enlarging its own subject: the quest for justice has been a concern **since ancient times**, and in modern times **a significant part of political theory is directly or indirectly related to the problem of justice** (PDF p.446, printed 427). ⚠️ Note what that sentence licenses. It is not a claim that every political theory is a theory of justice; it is a claim that most modern political theory is *implicated* in the problem, which is exactly why a perspective can arrive at this chapter from an ideology — anarchism, feminism, Marxism — without the ideology itself being the subject here.

The eight are then named in one printed sequence, and the sequence is worth keeping because the chapter follows it: **Liberal; Libertarian; Marxist; Democratic-Socialist; Anarchist; Feminist; Subaltern; and Communitarian**. The stated purpose is comparative — acquaintance with them **will immensely help us in understanding the complexity of the problem**.

### 2. The normative premise, which is the cleanest opening line in the topic

Immediately after, in the course of introducing the liberal perspective, the chapter states the premise that governs everything after it: a good society is characterised by **a number of virtues**, and **justice is the first virtue of a good society** — **in other words, justice is a necessary but not a sufficient condition of a good society** (PDF p.446). The rider is printed in the same place and is the half that grades an answer: those who argue that justice **should not be allowed to come in the way of social advancement and progress, run the risk of causing the moral degradation of society**.

⚠️ Read the two halves as a pair, because each blocks a different error. **First virtue** blocks the trade-off answer — the one that concedes injustice for growth, order or efficiency. **Necessary but not sufficient** blocks the totalising answer — the one that treats a single distributive rule as a complete political philosophy. 🔑 That second half is also the honest explanation of why this chapter has eight sections rather than one: a theory may be right about the rule and still silent about standing, community or power, and the chapter's own verdicts on each perspective say exactly that.

### 3. The six axes, and what kind of device they are

The axes are **rule of distribution · role of the state · property regime · meaning of equality · place of community · strongest criticism**. 🔶 They come from the comparison matrices in both canonical owners, and ⚠️ the **Advanced owner states plainly (§12)** that these headings are an **exam-use synthesis imposed on the chapter for comparison**. ❌ They are not printed headings, and no answer should attribute them to the book. The point is made here and once more at Lesson 11, and nowhere else.

Why these six and not others? Because each one is the site of a printed disagreement in the chapter itself. A1 separates Rawls from Nozick (procedure against history). A2 separates democratic socialism from anarchism (sustain the state against progressively reduce it). A3 separates the Marxist from every liberal (private property as the source of class distinction against property as an ingredient of liberty). A4 separates the feminist and subaltern perspectives from the first four (equal share against equal standing). A5 is the whole communitarian complaint. A6 is where the chapter's own appraisals live, and it is the axis candidates drop — which is why their answers describe without judging.

### 4. The two standing qualifications

Every model answer in this topic closes on one of these, and they are the reason a "diverse perspectives" answer is an argument rather than a catalogue.

1. **No single perspective is both complete and sufficient.** ⚠️ This is the chapter's own position, read off its own verdicts: Rawls needs a convergence defence against four schools; Nozick ends by making welfare depend on charity rather than justice; the Marxist account misses domination through political and bureaucratic power; democratic socialism is institutionally fragile on its own two conditions; anarchism has **no concrete programme**; Walzer has a strong moral philosophy and no political one.
2. **Procedure and structure are both necessary; neither alone is enough.** ⚠️ A just rule of distribution can be defeated by the structure people enter it with — which is what the Marxist, feminist and subaltern perspectives establish — while a structural remedy that abandons procedure forfeits the liberties the first principle protects.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** On any stem about competing conceptions of welfare or equity, the opening move is the premise, not a definition: justice is **the first virtue**, so a policy defended purely by growth or administrative convenience has not yet been defended; and it is **not a sufficient condition**, so a just allocation rule does not by itself establish that the policy is good. ⚠️ GS-I 2025 Q8 on urban poverty and distributive justice is owned by Indian Society; this topic supplies only the term of art and the axes.
- **GS-IV.** The necessary-but-not-sufficient pair is the cleanest available statement of why a decision can be procedurally correct and still criticisable, and why fairness cannot be traded against outcomes. ⚠️ The ethical architecture belongs to Ethics.
- **Essay.** For prompts on inequality, development or fairness, the premise supplies a thesis with a built-in qualification, and the six axes supply the body's paragraph plan: one axis per paragraph, two rival perspectives per axis.

**Answer framework — the comparison opening, in four moves.** Name the object precisely: this is a question about a **rule of distribution**, not about the meaning of justice → state the premise, first virtue and not sufficient, so the answer has a standard and a limit → declare the axes you will compare on, and declare that they are your analytical frame rather than the source's headings → commit to a close on one of the two standing qualifications. ⚠️ The framework earns marks because each move constrains the next paragraph; an answer that opens with a definition has made no commitment and usually drifts into a list.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does this chapter need eight perspectives rather than one theory of justice, and which printed sentence licenses that plurality?

**Model answer:** Because the chapter's own premise concedes incompleteness. It states that a good society is characterised by **a number of virtues**, that **justice is the first virtue of a good society**, and — in its own gloss — that justice is therefore **a necessary but not a sufficient condition** of a good society. If justice were sufficient, a correct rule of distribution would be a complete political philosophy and rivals would be simply mistaken. Because it is only necessary, a perspective can fix the rule correctly and still be silent about something a good society needs, which is precisely how the chapter treats its eight sections: each supplies a rule and each receives a separate appraisal naming what it misses. ⚠️ The plurality is therefore substantive, not editorial. The eight are named in one printed sequence — **Liberal, Libertarian, Marxist, Democratic-Socialist, Anarchist, Feminist, Subaltern, Communitarian** — and the stated purpose is comparative, to help in **understanding the complexity of the problem**. ⚠️ The corollary for answers is the first standing qualification: no single perspective is both complete and sufficient, so a graded conclusion identifies what each rule captures and what it leaves unaddressed, rather than crowning one.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the eight as a chronological development in which each perspective corrects the last. They are eight rivals judged separately; the chapter prints an appraisal of each, including of the later ones, and the communitarian section ends with two objections of its own. Reading them as a progression produces the standard error of treating the final perspective as the chapter's conclusion.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Justice is a necessary but not a sufficient condition of a good society." Comment. *(Comment — answer in about 150 words.)*

Verdict: justice sets the floor nothing may breach, not the ceiling — no single perspective is both complete and sufficient.

### 1. The liberal perspective's object, stated before any machinery

The liberal perspective **treats liberty as the central problem of justice**. It is **particularly concerned with substantive liberty**, for which **it seeks to accommodate the principles of equality and fraternity in the broader framework of liberty**, and it is **chiefly represented by Rawls's theory of justice** (PDF p.446). ⚠️ Three things follow and all are examinable. Liberty is the problem, not the solution — which is why this is a theory of *distribution* at all. The liberty in question is **substantive**, which is what distinguishes it from the libertarian perspective at Lesson 4, where the same word means **formal** liberty. And equality and fraternity are accommodated **within** a liberty framework rather than balanced against it — so an answer that presents Rawls as trading liberty against equality has already mis-stated the structure.

The distributive object is **primary goods**, which **include rights and liberties, powers and opportunities, income and wealth, means of self-respect and so on** (PDF p.447). ⚠️ Keep **means of self-respect** on the list when reproducing it. It is the item that lets a Rawlsian answer speak to dignity and humiliation without importing recognition vocabulary that this chapter does not use.

Rawls (**1921-2002**), an American philosopher, states the theory in ***A Theory of Justice* (1971)**. ⚠️ Neither canonical owner carries the title or the dates; both are printed, and both are cheap marks in an identification-shaped stem.

### 2. Pure procedural justice — the self-description that fixes the type

🔑 The highest-value sentence on the page: **Rawls has described his theory as the theory of pure procedural justice**, which **means that once certain principles of justice are unanimously accepted, the distribution resulting from their application will be necessarily just** (PDF p.447).

⚠️ Understand what that commits him to, because the whole of Lesson 3's defence turns on it. There is **no independent test of the outcome**. The distribution is not checked afterwards against a pattern; it is just because it issued from principles chosen under conditions that nobody could rig. That is why the conditions of choice carry the entire moral weight, and why critics who attack the veil are attacking the right thing rather than quibbling about a thought experiment.

🔑 It is also the hinge back into Topic 19 without re-teaching it. ➡️ Topic 19 owns the procedural/substantive debate; this topic owns the sentence that resolves it for Rawls, and the chapter's own closing verdict at Lesson 3 completes it — Rawls **has discovered a method for making procedural justice an instrument of meeting the requirements of social justice**. One cross-reference, no recap.

### 3. The anti-utilitarian argument, which is the theory's negative half

Rawls **has severely criticized those theories of allocation which ignore moral worth of the individual for the attainment of any predetermined goals**, and **has attacked utilitarianism because in calculating the greatest happiness of the greatest number it does not care if it leads to extreme hardship to any particular individual** (PDF p.447). The printed box defines the target: **utilitarianism**, founded by **Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832)**, prescribes the **greatest happiness of the greatest number** as the goal of legislation and public policy, and — the decisive clause — in estimating the total **the share of particular individuals need not be taken into account**.

The counter-case and the verdict are printed together: one might imagine a state of affairs in which the maximum happiness is produced and distributed to a maximum number **by the enslavement of a minority**; and **you cannot compensate for the sufferings of the distressed by enhancing the joys of the prosperous**.

⚠️ Carry the owners' qualification in the same breath. 🔶 The target is the **reduction of justice to aggregate advantage**, not every utilitarian account; a sophisticated utilitarian can refuse the enslavement case on other grounds. Stating the target precisely is what keeps this from being a straw-man paragraph, and it costs one clause.

### 4. The original position and the veil — with the specification that does the work

Following **the tradition of the social contract**, Rawls envisages an **original position** by **abstracting the individuals from their particular social and economic circumstances**, placed behind a **veil of ignorance** where they deliberate **as rational agents**, **totally unaware of their wants, interests, skills and abilities as well as of the conditions which lead to discrimination and conflict in society** (PDF p.447).

🔑 The part that separates a strong answer from a competent one is what the negotiators are **not** deprived of, and the chapter prints it in full while both owners compress it. They **have an elementary knowledge of economics and psychology**; they are **endowed with a sense of justice**; each **wants to maximize his or her well-being, without being envious**; they are **self-interested but not egoists**; and they are **not prepared to take a risk or resort to gambling**. From that specification the conclusion follows rather than being asserted: in such **a state of uncertainty the rational negotiators will choose the least dangerous path**, so each **will hypothetically place himself or herself in the least advantaged position** while recommending criteria of allocation, and **hence each of them will demand greatest benefit for the least advantaged**.

⚠️ This is the passage to reproduce when a stem asks *why* the difference principle is chosen. Without the risk-aversion clause the argument is a bare assertion that people behind a veil would protect the worst off; with it, the conclusion is derived — and the libertarian objection at Lesson 3 becomes intelligible, because it attacks precisely that clause.

### 5. The principles as printed, and the correction stated in the same passage

The chapter prints **three principles of justice** accepted **in the following order** (PDF p.448): **(1) Principle of equal liberty** — the **equal right to most extensive liberty compatible with similar liberty of others**, which **postulates that nobody's liberty will be sacrificed for the sake of any other benefit**, and whose printed content is **equal right to political participation, freedom of expression, religious liberty, equality before the law**; **(2) Principle of fair equality of opportunity, particularly for acquiring offices and positions**; and **(3) Difference principle**, under which **any departure from equal distribution of the primary goods can be justified only when it could be proved to bring greatest benefit to the least advantaged**, so that a special reward for extraordinary ability and effort is just **only if it results in the greatest benefit to the least privileged**.

The efficiency rider closes the block: when those conditions are met, **the criteria of efficiency can be justly applied in a competitive economy** — in other words, the rule **to each according to his ability** may be applied **only if higher efficiency of the concerned individual results in ameliorating the condition of the least privileged**.

✅ **State the correction in the same breath, not later.** 🔶 Rawls canonically states **two** principles, the second containing fair equality of opportunity **ahead of** the difference principle; the chapter's three-step presentation is exposition, and it is faithful in ordering while differing in structure. ❌ Never present the three-step list as Rawls's own formulation, and ❌ never silently amend the chapter. 🔑 The reason this is not pedantry: **2026 Q1(a)** asks why the **two** principles are applied in lexical order, so a candidate who has only ever met a three-item list cannot answer the stem as asked.

### Concept check

**Question:** What does it add to call Rawls's theory one of *pure procedural justice*, and how does that description constrain the rest of the theory?

**Model answer:** It fixes the kind of justification the theory offers. The chapter records that **Rawls has described his theory as the theory of pure procedural justice**, meaning that **once certain principles of justice are unanimously accepted, the distribution resulting from their application will be necessarily just**. Three constraints follow. First, there is no independent outcome standard: the distribution is not audited afterwards against a pattern of shares, so a Rawlsian cannot be refuted merely by pointing at an unequal result. Second, the entire moral burden shifts onto the **conditions of choice** — which is why the chapter specifies the negotiators so carefully, as deprived of **wants, interests, skills and abilities** yet retaining **elementary knowledge of economics and psychology**, a **sense of justice**, non-envy, self-interest **but not** egoism, and an unwillingness **to take a risk or resort to gambling**. Third, it explains the output: risk-averse choosers place themselves in **the least advantaged position**, so they demand **greatest benefit for the least advantaged**. ⚠️ It is also why the chapter can later claim that Rawls made **procedural justice an instrument of meeting the requirements of social justice** — the procedure is built to deliver a substantive result rather than to be indifferent to it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the veil as a claim about real people or real deliberation. It is a device for selecting principles, and the chapter presents it as a development of **the tradition of the social contract**; objections that real persons cannot shed their identities are answered at Lesson 3 and Lesson 10, and reproducing the device without its specification clauses leaves the difference principle looking asserted rather than derived.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Explain why, on Rawls's account, rational choosers behind a veil of ignorance would not select a utilitarian rule of distribution. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** Because the rule fails the conditions of choice. Utilitarianism prescribes **the greatest happiness of the greatest number**, and — as its printed box states — in estimating the aggregate **the share of particular individuals need not be taken into account**; so it **does not care if it leads to extreme hardship to any particular individual**. Behind the veil, choosers do not know who they will be, are **not prepared to take a risk or resort to gambling**, and have a **sense of justice**; each reasons from **the least advantaged position**. A rule permitting maximum aggregate happiness achieved **by the enslavement of a minority** is precisely the gamble they refuse. ⚠️ The chapter's verdict states the principle behind the refusal: **you cannot compensate for the sufferings of the distressed by enhancing the joys of the prosperous** — aggregation is not a method of justification; procedure and structure must both hold.

### 1. Why the order is necessary, stated as the question asks it

Take the printed sequence and ask of each step what the next could not supply. **Liberty is first** because the first principle itself contains the reason: it **postulates that nobody's liberty will be sacrificed for the sake of any other benefit** (PDF p.448). Any ranking that let liberty be traded would make the whole device pointless, since negotiators who had chosen it would have exposed themselves to exactly the position they refused to gamble on.

**Opportunity precedes the difference principle** because the two do different work. The difference principle asks whether an inequality **brings greatest benefit to the least advantaged**; fair equality of opportunity asks whether the positions that produced the inequality were genuinely open, **particularly for acquiring offices and positions**. ⚠️ An arrangement can pass the first test and fail the second — the worst off are better off, but only as beneficiaries of a closed order. That is compensation, not justice, and lexical priority is what rules it out.

🔶 **Now the correction both owners insist on, and state it as part of the answer rather than as an afterthought:** improving the condition of the least advantaged is **not sufficient** to justify an inequality if equal basic liberties or fair equality of opportunity are violated. ❌ The difference principle is never a standalone licence. 🔑 This is the single commonest Rawls error in a scripted answer, and naming it is worth more than another sentence on the veil.

### 2. Chain connection — what it is, who said what, and the flag it carries

The chapter introduces chain connection to answer a question it poses itself, and the attribution matters: **why should the meritorious accommodate the interests of the least advantaged?** (PDF p.449). The device is the reply: **in order to strengthen a chain, we should start with strengthening its weakest link, and then repeat the process by identifying the weakest link on each occasion** (PDF p.448); more meritorious persons **enjoy the benefits of their merit in association with the less meritorious lot**; **a chain is no stronger than its weakest link**; and the idea **brings Rawls very close to the image of a communitarian**.

📝 **Norman Barry** is then quoted as elucidating it: a **chain connection operates between the best and the worst off and a rise in the expectations of the best off will have the effect of raising everybody else's expectations throughout the system**.

⚠️ **Flag Barry's gloss as a contested trickle-down reading, and do it in one clause.** The direction of travel in that sentence runs from the best off downward; the difference principle tests an inequality by its benefit to the **least** advantaged, which is a different claim. The chapter itself immediately attaches the empirical condition — the justifiability of **special concessions, subsidies or protection depends on empirical facts whether or not such benefits filter down ultimately to help the neediest** — so the reading is conditional even on its own page. ❌ Never state Barry's sentence as the content of the difference principle, and ❌ never let chain connection displace the two principles or their priority; 🔶 the Basic owner (§18) says it illustrates interdependence and does not replace the ordering.

📝 **Samuel Gorovitz** supplies the cleanest one-line characterisation available, and neither owner carries it: Rawls **is clearly a redistributionist in that he takes the proper function of government to include not merely the maintenance of a social order, but the achievement of distributive justice by placing the highest social value on the needs of the neediest** ('John Rawls: A Theory of Justice', in *Contemporary Political Philosophers*, ed. Anthony de Crespigny and Kenneth Minogue, **1975**).

### 3. The four schools, each with its actual charge

❌ Do not write "criticised by Marxists, libertarians and communitarians" and move on. The chapter prints the content of each charge, and the content is what can be answered.

**Collectivists** argue that he **has discovered the ground for the justification of the existing capitalist system**: he has shown that if the rich are free to accumulate, the poor are automatically benefited; even strictly enforced fair equality of opportunity will not substantially reduce existing disparities; and **a slight improvement in the condition of the most disadvantaged sections will be treated as an excuse to permit vast socio-economic inequalities** (PDF p.448).

The **identification problem** is printed next and is absent from both owners: identifying the most disadvantaged **is very difficult**, and if **income and wealth are treated the sole criteria** for identifying such sections, **how shall we compensate those who lack ability or who suffer from emotional insecurity?** (PDF p.449). 🔑 This is the printed objection with the widest modern application, because every targeting rule in public policy has to answer it.

**Marxists** contend that principles determined in a hypothetical condition behind a veil are empty: **any deliberations without the knowledge of prevailing social and economic conditions are meaningless**, and **moral systems should always be analysed in the light of class relations and the patterns of ownership of private property**.

**Libertarians** argue that **Rawls has sacrificed liberty for the sake of equality** — **why should we force the meritorious and industrious to work for the benefit of the most disadvantaged sections?** — and add the incentive charge: enterprising persons **must take risks for their advancement in life**, while **Rawls's negotiators are not prepared to take risk. How would they help in social progress?** ⚠️ Note that this attacks the specification clause taught at Lesson 2, which is why the specification had to be taught.

**Communitarians** point out that his political philosophy **does not grade any conception of good life as superior or inferior to others**, and this **ethical neutrality evades the opportunity of the pursuit of the common good**. ➡️ The doctrine behind that charge is Lesson 10's; here it is one printed sentence, and ❌ the common-good triad itself belongs to Topic 21.

### 4. The chapter's reply, which is the half that lifts an answer

**These diverse critiques seem to be based on biased interpretations of Rawls's theory of justice.** The reason given is structural: Rawls **has tried to combine different value-systems** whose tenets **are thought to be incompatible with each other**, and **any attempt to combine them must yield a complex model**. Hence the verdict: **Rawls's theory of justice represents a convergence of libertarianism, egalitarianism and communitarianism** (PDF p.449) — argued limb by limb.

**Libertarian**, because the conception of men negotiating in the original position **envisages those who are trying to maximize their self-interest**, and because the first principle **accords priority to liberty which cannot be compromised for any other benefit**. **Egalitarian**, because he concedes **equal liberty for all**, insists that social-economic inequalities are allowed only on the condition of **fair equality of opportunity**, and — the formulation worth memorising — **accepts equality as a cardinal principle, and insists that only inequalities shall be required to be justified**. **Communitarian**, through chain connection.

The close is printed in two sentences and both are usable: capitalism is retained **on some specified conditions**, but **once these conditions are fulfilled, the capitalist system is bound to assume a new humane look**; and **Rawls has discovered a method for making procedural justice an instrument of meeting the requirements of social justice** (PDF p.450).

### 5. Two objection-and-reply chains for the fifteen- and twenty-mark slot

🔶 Both are the Basic owner's answer apparatus and carry **no** page anchor.

- **Communitarian objection (Sandel) → Rawlsian reply.** *Objection:* the unencumbered self imagined behind the veil is impossible, since real persons are partly constituted by ends, roles and attachments they cannot bracket. *Reply:* the veil is a heuristic for choosing fair principles for the basic structure, not a metaphysical claim about actual selves; a thin theory of the good suffices to generate principles, after which thick community life proceeds within just constraints.
- **Libertarian objection (Nozick) → Rawlsian reply.** *Objection:* the difference principle treats natural talents as a collective asset and requires continual redistribution, violating self-ownership. *Reply:* the principles govern background institutions and the basic structure, not every individual transaction; redistributive taxation is a structural rule chosen ex ante behind the veil, not an ad hoc seizure of a particular transfer.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why must the three steps of the printed list be applied in order, and what does the chapter's convergence verdict add to a critical answer?

**Model answer:** The order encodes what may not be traded. The first principle **postulates that nobody's liberty will be sacrificed for the sake of any other benefit**, so no gain in income or opportunity can purchase a reduction in basic liberty. Fair equality of opportunity then precedes the difference principle because the two test different things: the difference principle asks whether an inequality brings **greatest benefit to the least advantaged**, while fair opportunity asks whether the positions that generated it were open **particularly for acquiring offices and positions** — an arrangement can pass the first and fail the second, which is compensation rather than inclusion. 🔶 Hence the correction both canonical owners insist on: benefit to the worst off is **not sufficient** where liberties or fair opportunity are violated. The convergence verdict then does the critical work: the four printed charges rest on **biased interpretations**, because Rawls **has tried to combine different value-systems** and **any attempt to combine them must yield a complex model** — libertarian in the self-interested negotiators and the priority of liberty, egalitarian in **equality as a cardinal principle** with **only inequalities** requiring justification, communitarian through chain connection. ⚠️ That converts a list of objections into a judgment.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use chain connection as the content of the difference principle or as a trickle-down thesis. The chapter deploys it to answer **why should the meritorious accommodate the interests of the least advantaged?**, the quoted elucidation is attributed to Norman Barry rather than to Rawls, and the page itself makes the filter-down claim depend on **empirical facts**. The difference principle continues to test inequalities by benefit to the **least** advantaged.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Examine whether an inequality that improves the position of the worst off is, for that reason alone, just. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** No — it clears the last test while the prior ones go unanswered. The difference principle permits departure from equal distribution **only when it could be proved to bring greatest benefit to the least advantaged**, and the efficiency rider confirms: **to each according to his ability** applies **only if** higher efficiency **ameliorates the condition of the least privileged**. But the principles are stated **in the following order**: the first **postulates that nobody's liberty will be sacrificed for the sake of any other benefit**, while the second requires fair equality of opportunity **particularly for acquiring offices and positions**. 🔶 Benefit at the bottom is necessary and **not sufficient**: raising the worst off while closing offices compensates instead of includes. ⚠️ The identification problem sharpens this — if **income and wealth** alone fix who is worst off, those who **lack ability** are invisible to the test. Procedure and structure must both hold.

### 1. The libertarian object, and the contrast that opens a comparison answer

The libertarian perspective **also treats liberty of the individual as its central problem**, but it **focuses on formal liberty**, insists on **minimal role of the state in economic activities**, regards **the right to property as an important ingredient of individual liberty**, and is **largely opposed to the idea of welfare state** (PDF p.450). 🔑 The single word that organises the whole comparison with Lesson 2 is **formal** against **substantive**.

The printed contrast is the cleanest opener available for any Rawls-and-Nozick stem, and neither owner carries it: whereas **Rawls sought to moderate his libertarianism by a modicum of egalitarianism and communitarianism, Nozick adhered to libertarianism in its pure form**; if Rawls is the **left liberal** or egalitarian liberal **advocating a substantially redistributive welfare state**, Nozick is the **right liberal** or libertarian **committed to a laissez-faire nightwatchman state**. Nozick (**1938-2002**), ***Anarchy, State and Utopia* (1974)** — ⚠️ again, title and dates absent from both owners.

The methodological charge is printed and is worth one sentence: Nozick **ridicules Rawls's approach which seeks to determine the principles of distribution of certain goods as if they have come to us as a gift from heaven**, insisting instead on an account of **the different modes of acquisition of goods and entitlement of different individuals to own those goods**. 🔶 This is the **patterned against anti-patterned** distinction in the chapter's own words: Rawls asks what shape the distribution should have, Nozick asks how it came about.

### 2. Two lists, not one — and why the collapse costs the argument

⚠️ Both canonical owners merge Nozick's **modes of acquisition** with his **principles of entitlement**. The chapter prints them separately (PDF pp.450-451), and the separation is the structure of the argument.

**The three modes of acquisition** name the kinds of thing a person can come to hold. **(a) Their selves** — **their bodies, brain cells, etc.** — over which **they have absolute right**, so that **an individual is free to use his limbs and brain to do whatever he likes**; the chapter adds that entitlement to self-ownership **is obvious which needs no further justification**. **(b) The natural world** — **land, water resources, minerals** — and this is **precisely the area where principles of entitlement are required to be determined according to logic**. **(c) The things people make by applying themselves to the natural world** — **agricultural and industrial products** — whose entitlement **may not be questioned**, and whose **voluntary transfer will establish others' entitlement to them**.

🔑 The payoff: only (b) and (c) raise a question of justice at all. Self-ownership is the premise, not a conclusion — which is why the later charge of "forced labour" has the force it does, and why an answer that lists six items as one set cannot explain where the argument begins.

### 3. The three principles of entitlement, with their printed limits

**(a) Initial acquisition** — **the method whereby an individual comes to appropriate some previously unowned bits of the natural world** — operates on a **first come first served basis, as long as nobody is made worse off by their doing so**, meaning it **should not result in creating scarcity for others**. The chapter attaches the anchor and then, in its own voice, the scepticism: this is **similar to the condition spelled out in John Locke's *Second Treatise of Government* (1690)** — **as long as enough and as good is left for others** — and it is **a condition which may scarcely be satisfied** (PDF p.451). ⚠️ The Basic owner names "the Lockean acquisition proviso" but never states it and never carries the scepticism; both are printed, and the scepticism is what keeps an answer from treating original appropriation as unproblematic.

**(b) Voluntary transfer** applies to all property however acquired, in a market situation, and **must be based on voluntary contract, without force or fraud**. The printed moral limit is the part that matters and is absent from both owners: **in all such transactions, an individual shall be treated as end-in-itself, and not as a means to others' ends**, **similar to the moral principle enunciated by Immanuel Kant (1724-1804)** — **hence a contract through which an individual sells himself or any other individual to slavery will be void**. 🔑 Transfer is therefore not validated by consent alone; there is an internal constraint on what may be consented to.

**(c) Rectification** is **precisely the area where the state or the international community will be justified to intervene in order to restore justice**, and Nozick **concedes that the history of the world abounds with involuntary transfers as well as unjust acquisitions of natural resources**. ✅ Keep the owners' point: rectification is a **constitutive third principle**, not an optional appendix. ❌ An answer that presents entitlement as acquisition-plus-transfer has removed the principle that most constrains existing holdings and has, in effect, blessed the status quo in Nozick's name.

### 4. The three examples as one graded series

Taught together, the three printed cases make the test legible; taught separately, they look like anecdotes. **Wilt Chamberlain** becomes a millionaire **because millions of people are willing to pay for watching him play basketball** — **it is his legitimate right**, because the transfers were voluntary. The **inventor of the cure of a dreaded disease like cancer** who **demands exorbitant charges from his patients** is also legitimate for Nozick, **because he does not make anybody worse off by treating his patients** — ⚠️ the middle case is absent from both owners and is the one that makes the standard visible. But **if there is a single source of water which is needed by all human beings, nobody has the right to take it into his control**.

🔑 The series shows that the operative test is **worse off**, not price, profit margin or resulting inequality. That is why Nozick can accept an enormous fortune and still rule out a monopoly on a necessity — and it is the distinction a critical answer must grant before it attacks.

### 5. The printed appraisal, which is half of a critical answer

Nozick **claims to discover the principles of justice for all human beings, but his bias is quite clear**. He is an **out-and-out champion of a competitive market society which favours the rich and the resourceful, and lets the weak go to the wall**, and **absolves the rich of all social responsibility, not to speak of social indebtedness**. The formulation most worth carrying: in much of the contemporary world **justice is thought to be the voice of the oppressed**, but **Nozick wants to maintain the prevailing oppression in the name of justice** (PDF p.452).

On the fiscal question the chapter is precise: he **approves of taxation only for the provision of the common services, like streets and street lights, police and defence**; when part of the taxes imposed on the rich is spent on welfare of the poor he would **term it immoral, as it is akin to forced labour**, because it **involves using abilities and efforts of one section as means to others' ends** and is therefore an **involuntary transfer**. **The lucky should have freedom to help the unlucky, if they so like** — and hence the decisive line: **Nozick makes welfare of the poor dependent on charity, not on justice.** He **is not prepared to concede that the operation of competitive market society may itself create certain conditions of injustice**. The chapter closes by contrast: Rawls's provision for regulating inequalities on condition of **giving greatest benefit to the least advantaged shows his genuine concern for justice**.

⚠️ **One printed charge needs handling, not suppression.** The claim that even rectification **is designed to legitimize the huge riches of the manipulators, and hit at the only assets of oil-producing countries** is the author's own 2009 polemic and runs against the standard reading, on which rectification is the principle most hostile to unjust existing holdings. Present it as **the chapter's criticism of Nozick**, ❌ never as the consensus critique, and ❌ never in place of rectification's constraining role.

✅ **Two guards to close on.** ❌ Nozick is not anti-justice — he advances a full theory of justice centred on entitlement rather than redistribution. ❌ And not every voluntary transfer is just, because title is **historical**: a transfer inherits the defect of the acquisition behind it. ⚠️ Name the Topic-18 split once: there the same trio justifies **property** against a function test; here it tests **holdings** against the difference principle. Same printed material, different question.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Nozick need three separate principles of entitlement, and what work does each do that the others cannot?

**Model answer:** Because a holding can be defective in three different ways, and only a historical theory can say which. **Initial acquisition** governs the move from unowned to owned: appropriation of **previously unowned bits of the natural world** on a **first come first served basis, as long as nobody is made worse off**, echoing Locke's **as long as enough and as good is left for others** — a condition the chapter itself calls one **which may scarcely be satisfied**. **Voluntary transfer** governs movement between holders: it must rest on **voluntary contract, without force or fraud**, and each party must be treated as **end-in-itself, and not as a means to others' ends**, which is why a contract selling oneself into slavery **will be void** — so consent is necessary and not sufficient. **Rectification** governs the past: since the history of the world **abounds with involuntary transfers as well as unjust acquisitions**, it is **precisely the area where the state or the international community will be justified to intervene in order to restore justice**. ⚠️ Remove rectification and the theory ratifies whatever exists; remove the proviso and acquisition becomes unconditional. The operative test throughout is whether anybody is made **worse off**, which is why the inventor's fortune passes and the sole water source does not.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not collapse the three **modes of acquisition** into the three **principles of entitlement**, and do not treat Nozick as hostile to justice. The first list says what kinds of thing are held — selves, the natural world, things people make — and establishes that self-ownership **needs no further justification**; only the second list supplies tests of title. He advances a full theory of justice in holdings, and the chapter's quarrel is with its consequences, not with its ambition.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "On the entitlement view, any transfer made without force or fraud is just." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The proposition drops two of the theory's three tests. ### 1. The case against, stated at full strength first

The chapter opens by recording that the **status of the notion of justice in the Marxist thought has remained a controversial subject** and then gives the sceptical case four distinct supports (PDF pp.452-453). Marxism **aims at replacing capitalism by socialism for which revolution is indispensable**, so **inclination toward justice would mean maintaining the existing system with minor changes**, a departure from its basic tenets; exploitation is **the natural characteristic of capitalism**, and it would be **futile to regard it as the problem of injustice and to try to find its solution within the capitalist system**, since Marx **did not seek any reform in the level of incomes and wages** but wanted to transform **the entire mode of production and property relations**. In the ***Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875)** he **rebuked those socialists who became complacent after raising the demand of 'fair distribution' within the existing system**. In the Marxian framework **morality is regarded as a part of superstructure**, so changes in the mode of production produce **corresponding changes in the criteria of right and wrong, just and unjust** — **then, how can we determine any enduring principles of justice?** And the communist society **will be free from the conditions involving scarcity and conflict; hence the state and its judicial apparatus will no longer be required**.

⚠️ Reproduce this as a block before replying. An answer that states the sceptical case properly has already demonstrated the understanding that the reply then builds on; an answer that mentions it in a subordinate clause looks like it is defending a position it has not tested.

### 2. The reply, point for point

**Should we assume on the basis of the foregoing arguments that Marxism is not concerned at all with the question of justice?** — **this assumption would not be correct** (PDF p.453). Four supports follow.

**Exploitation is named as a wrong, not described as a process:** Marx **has repeatedly dubbed workers' exploitation a theft, or even an act of robbery**. **A distributive ranking is conceded:** he **has conceded moral supremacy of one mode of distribution (viz. 'to each according to his need') over another mode of distribution (viz. 'to each according to his ability'). This shows his concern with distributive justice.** 🔑 This is the reply to the superstructure argument, and it is the sentence that answers an equity-versus-equality stem: a theory that ranks distributive rules is making a justice judgment whatever its meta-ethics. **A morally superior system is identified:** in spite of **his adherence to moral relativism** he **has shown the way to set up a social system where the ideology or 'false consciousness' emanating from the prevailing mode of production will disappear; hence it would be a morally superior system**. **And the normative commitment is explicit:** he **firmly believed in freedom and humanism**, and Marxism **attacks those conditions of dominance and dependence which are the glaring examples of social injustice**.

The qualification is printed in the same place and belongs in the answer: he **does not accept a solution which would serve as an excuse to retain capitalism with certain modifications**. ⚠️ So the reply is not that Marxism is a reformist theory of fair shares; it is that a theory can condemn a distribution as unjust while refusing to repair it inside the system that produced it.

### 3. Why the share is unjust at source — the mechanism, in one compressed block

The chapter grounds the verdict on the concept of **surplus value**: the capitalist mode of production **involves the exploitation of the working class**; **labour is the sole creator of value**, while of the four elements of production **land, capital and organization are sterile because they are capable of reproducing only what is put in them**; and the worker, selling labour power at a price fixed by his own maintenance and his family's replacement cost — the printed box is titled **Subsistence Wages** — gets back only **a part** of the value he produces (PDF pp.453-455).

❌ **Stop there.** The derivation of value against price, natural against market price, the 1859 passage and the thirty-hour illustration are **Topic 04's**, verified by cross-file count; reproducing them here converts a justice lesson into an economics lesson and costs the comparison that this topic exists to make. 🔑 What Topic 20 needs is one proposition: the injustice is located **in the structure of production**, not in the size of the share, which is precisely what distinguishes this perspective from every welfare argument in the chapter.

The remedy is given the same compression: socialization ends the private appropriation of surplus; a transitional state **retains power to suppress reaction**; and the state is then expected to **wither away** toward a classless society (PDF pp.455-456). ❌ Lenin's two pamphlets, Soviet expropriation without compensation, Mao's permanent revolution, **socialism in one country** and **peaceful co-existence** are book-period institutional history and are reduced to a single line here — they are retained only because that record is what generates the chapter's own closing objection.

### 4. The conclusion: which property, and the objection that qualifies everything

The classless society is one **no longer divided into antagonistic classes on the basis of ownership of private property**, and the distinction the chapter insists on is between **private property**, **the source of exploitation and injustice in society**, and **personal property**, **which is the source of security for the individual** (PDF p.458). Marxian communism **does not contemplate to abolish personal property which is the fruit of man's own labour, and which is the ground work of all personal freedom, activity and independence**, including **the property of the petty artisan and of the small peasant** — and **such property needs to be protected**.

Both ***Communist Manifesto* (1848)** formulations are printed and both are usable: **the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property**; and **Communism deprives no man of the power to appropriate the products of society; all that it does is to deprive him of the power to subjugate the labour of others by means of such appropriation**. The closing quotation is the one to end an answer on: in place of the old bourgeois society **we shall have an association, in which the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all** (PDF p.459).

Then the chapter's own objection, which is the qualified verdict a twenty-marker needs and which neither owner carries in full: the picture **is quite fascinating**, but **it treats the possession of private property as the only source of class distinctions. In actual practice, class distinctions may reappear on the basis of possession of political and bureaucratic power, even after the abolition of private property, giving rise to new forms of dominance and injustice. So the problem of injustice will have to be tackled at many more subtle levels.**

🔑 That is the single most transferable sentence in this lesson. It converts a Marxist answer from advocacy into analysis, it is the chapter's own, and it is the printed bridge to the subaltern perspective at Lesson 9, where subordination is shown to run on ideological as well as economic power.

### 1. The differentiating claim, and what it is not

**While Marxism seeks to bring about socialism through revolutionary method, democratic socialism prefers evolutionary or democratic method** (PDF p.459). Its supporters **pay equal importance to democracy and socialism** and hold that **the goals of democracy and socialism are not separable from each other: both stand for the amelioration of the ordinary man**. In effect it **signifies use of the democratic method for achieving the socialist goal**, and **seeks to modify Marxian socialism in some important details**.

⚠️ The contrast examiners want is **distributive and institutional**, not a contrast of temperament. Topic 20 owns exactly that: transitional dictatorship and wholesale socialization on one side, against **partial** socialization with civil liberties and an effective opposition on the other. ➡️ Socialism and communism as ideologies, their histories and their internal schools belong to Topics 04 and 05.

### 2. The five tenets, as the chapter heads them

**Partial socialization of production and distribution.** Socialism **does not require wholesale socialization of the means of production and distribution**; placing **some essential means of production and distribution under state ownership so as to ensure the supply of essential goods and services for the bulk of the population** is **a substantial achievement in the direction of socialism**. 🔑 This is the printed answer to a socialism-against-communism stem, and it is an answer about **ownership scope**, which is a distributive claim rather than a procedural one.

**Satisfaction of moral as well as material needs.** The socialist programme **should not be confined to satisfying the material needs**; it should take care of **moral, intellectual and emotional needs so as to ensure a fuller development of the personality of each individual** — hence **not only of food, clothing and shelter for the masses, but also for their education, entertainment, art and culture**.

**Freedom of thought and expression.** The printed argument is sharper than the heading suggests and is worth reproducing: **even if a society has abolished private property, put an end to economic exploitation and managed to satisfy the material needs of all individuals, such conditions will not be conducive to the fuller development of personality** without **freedom of thought and expression, freedom of religion and worship, freedom of movement, and other similar democratic freedoms** (PDF p.460). ⚠️ That is a direct argument against the sufficiency of material redistribution, made from inside the socialist tradition rather than against it.

**No form of dictatorship.** Democratic socialism **is opposed to all forms of dictatorship, even if it is a 'dictatorship of the proletariat' as expounded by the Marxian theory of socialism**, because **dictatorship of any kind leads to the suppression of personality, and is hence not conducive to human happiness**.

**Free competition for power.** Democratic structures — **free competition for power among political parties, freedom of pressure groups, parliamentary institutions with an effective role for the opposition** — are **essential for achieving the ends of socialism**. ⚠️ Note the claim's shape: competition is not a constraint reluctantly accepted alongside socialism, it is presented as a condition of achieving it.

### 3. The sources, named once

**Harold J. Laski (1893-1950)** has **sought to combine the ends of socialism with the democratic method of liberalism**, across ***Liberty in the Modern State* (1930)**, ***State in Theory and Practice* (1935)** and ***A Grammar of Politics* (1938)**. **E.F.M. Durbin**, in ***The Politics of Democratic Socialism* (1940)**, **has competently elaborated the tenets**. Among countries following the path, **the Scandinavian countries — Sweden, Norway and Denmark — are the most notable**, and **India also claims to follow the path of democratic socialism** (PDF p.460).

⚠️ Handle the India clause exactly as printed. It is a **2009 statement about a claim**, and it is reproduced or omitted, never upgraded. ❌ No Preamble, Article, amendment, scheme or policy verdict attaches to it in this edition; those belong to Polity and Social Justice.

### 4. The conclusion, which is the lesson's real payload

Democratic socialism seeks **to provide for democratic rights and civil liberties along with socio-economic rights of citizens — a difficult combination indeed!** If achieved, **it would serve as an ideal scheme for social justice**. Then the sentence the whole lesson turns on: **democracy is a matter of procedure; it does not prescribe the goals of state policy** (PDF p.460).

🔑 That is a structural limitation, not a complaint. A democratic constitution guarantees how decisions are made, not what they aim at; so social-justice goals are an outcome of politics, not a guarantee of the system. Two factors therefore decide the matter: **(a) prevalence of a strong and discerning public opinion in favour of the goals of socialism so that those committed to these goals win majority in elections and they have a strong political will to implement their programme**; and **(b) a strong resource-base for the state which enables it to provide for elaborate public services and social security without resorting to coercion and undue taxation** (PDF p.461). Meeting them needs **cultivation of a sense of duty, work culture and patriotism among citizens in general — a difficult task indeed!**

And the printed incentive objection closes it: if the leadership **resorts to heavy taxation of the relatively well-placed sections, who have improved their standards of living by dint of their talents, efforts, enterprise and frugality, it might mar the incentives and thereby impoverish the resource-base of the nation. This will erode the prospects of maintaining democratic socialism.**

⚠️ Note the shape of that objection, because it is unusually strong: it is not an external libertarian complaint about taxation, it is an internal warning that the instrument used to fund social justice can destroy the base that funds it. ❌ It is not a verdict that redistribution is wrong; it is a constraint on how fast and how heavily it can proceed.

### Concept check

**Question:** If democracy is only a matter of procedure, what secures social justice in a democratic-socialist state?

**Model answer:** Nothing in the machinery itself — which is precisely the chapter's point. **Democracy is a matter of procedure; it does not prescribe the goals of state policy**, so a democratic constitution guarantees how decisions are taken, not what they pursue; the chapter calls the combination of democratic rights, civil liberties and socio-economic rights **a difficult combination indeed**. Two substantive conditions therefore carry the weight. First, **prevalence of a strong and discerning public opinion in favour of the goals of socialism so that those committed to these goals win majority in elections**, together with **a strong political will to implement their programme** — demand must be informed and must survive an election. Second, **a strong resource-base for the state which enables it to provide for elaborate public services and social security without resorting to coercion and undue taxation** — provision must be funded without the two instruments that would destroy the regime's democratic character. ⚠️ Both rest on **a sense of duty, work culture and patriotism**, and both are threatened by the chapter's own warning that heavy taxation of the able **might mar the incentives and thereby impoverish the resource-base**. Procedure and structure must both hold.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read democratic socialism as Marxism minus revolution. It differs on ownership scope — **partial** rather than **wholesale** socialization — on political form, since it rejects **all forms of dictatorship** including that **of the proletariat**, and on the content of need, which extends to **education, entertainment, art and culture**. The method is one of several differences, not the difference.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Examine the claim that democratic socialism is institutionally fragile. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The claim is supported by the chapter's own conclusion, not only by critics. Its goals are demanding — **democratic rights and civil liberties along with socio-economic rights**, **a difficult combination indeed** — while its method withholds the means: **democracy is a matter of procedure; it does not prescribe the goals of state policy**. Delivery rests on two contingent conditions: **a strong and discerning public opinion** behind those goals plus **political will**, and **a strong resource-base** funding services **without resorting to coercion and undue taxation**. Worse, the obvious remedy is self-defeating: heavy taxation of those who rose **by dint of their talents, efforts, enterprise and frugality** **might mar the incentives and thereby impoverish the resource-base**. ⚠️ Fragility is structural, not accidental — the price of refusing dictatorship, itself a justice claim. ### 1. The premise set, and the clause that makes it a distinct theory of justice

Anarchism **holds that society should be organized without coercive power of the state**; **in its view government is intrinsically evil**; **men are benign by nature**; **society is a natural institution**; and men **are capable of organizing themselves into a just society through voluntary cooperation, but they are corrupted by the intervention of the coercive power of the state**. Therefore **justice in society can be restored only if government is abolished, either completely, or at least partly**, and anarchists **reject all forms of authority that interferes with the spontaneous actions and associations of individuals** (PDF p.461).

🔑 The decisive clause, absent from both owners, follows immediately: **even reforms, introduced by an authority 'from above' are worthless**. ⚠️ Notice what that rules out. It is not an argument that the state reforms badly or slowly; it is an argument that the *source* of a reform defeats its value, because a benefit handed down by coercive authority reproduces the dependence that is the injustice. That single sentence is what separates anarchist justice from the democratic-socialist and Rawlsian positions, both of which depend on authoritative action from above.

### 2. The caveat, which protects the answer

**Anarchism is not a consistent doctrine.** Various types of thinkers who advocate abolition of political authority **on one ground or the other** are regarded as anarchists. And the chapter distinguishes Marx explicitly: he **was also a champion of a 'stateless and classless society'**, but his thought **is not clubbed with mainstream anarchism**, because he **outlined an elaborate programme for overthrowing capitalism, and setting up a proletarian state from which a stateless and classless society would eventually emerge**, whereas **mainstream anarchists usually stand for straightaway abolition of the state**.

⚠️ Deploy the caveat early in an answer. It pre-empts the objection that the perspective is internally contradictory — the chapter concedes it — and it supplies the exact distinction a stem comparing anarchism with Marxism or with socialism needs.

### 3. The roster, as one line each

❌ No thinker biographies. The eight names are **Topic 05's**, verified by cross-file count, and here each contributes only its distributive claim. **Godwin** traces inequality to **the condition of society** and looks to small producers united by cooperation. **Proudhon** holds that **property is theft** because the right to property **enables one man to control the life of another**, and proposes **mutualism**, under which everything needed for production is available on **mutually beneficial, but non-profit-making, terms**. **Bakunin** proposes **a free federation of independent associations** with equal rights and privileges, **including the right to secession**. **Kropotkin** argues that **sociability** rather than struggle is the advantage in the struggle for life, and advocates **anarchist communism**. **Sorel** supplies anarcho-syndicalism and the general strike — ➡️ the violence and myth arguments are Topic 05's. **Tolstoy** locates the remedy in individual moral regeneration against state violence and property-based luxury. **Gandhi** holds that the state **as a coercive institution, is based on violence**, and that under universal non-violence **the strong will not oppress the weak; the rich will not exploit the poor**, so political power becomes **redundant**. **Bookchin** argues that technology has ended the **age of scarcity which was the source of all conflict**, licensing small self-sufficient communities where people **live together, work together and eat together**.

⚠️ Used this way the roster takes four lines of an answer and still supplies four distinct distributive proposals — mutualism, federation, mutual aid and post-scarcity decentralisation — which is more than a page of biography would.

### 4. The critical appraisal, which is the "critically examine" half

Anarchism is **based on a fascinating idea**, but **anarchists have no concrete programme to implement it** (PDF p.464). **Pure anarchists oppose all types of authority and want to do away with political parties, social movements and leadership because they involve some use of authority** — ⚠️ which is a self-undermining position, since it removes the instruments by which the change could be brought about. Some advocate **even terrorism, assassination of heads of states and prominent political leaders**, and the chapter answers with a question: **how can we justify inhuman acts in the name of human liberty and emancipation?**

🔑 Then the strongest objection printed anywhere in this chapter, and it is absent from both owners. **Restoration of justice in the contemporary world is a very complicated affair.** With **the immense increase of population and rising levels of consumption, natural resources are being depleted, atmospheric pollution is rising, and the need of regulation is becoming more pressing**. It is **increasingly felt that the authority of the nation-states is not sufficient to deal with this situation, and that some sort of global regulation has become necessary**. Under these conditions, **the vision of spontaneous adjustment between all individuals would turn out to be the flight of imagination**.

⚠️ Observe the structure, because it is what makes the objection decisive rather than merely sceptical: it does not claim people are too selfish to self-organise, it claims the **scale and nature of the coordination problem** have outgrown even the nation-state, so the direction of travel required is *more* coordination, not less. An answer that reproduces that argument has refuted the premise rather than doubting it.

The human-nature objection completes the appraisal: **the notion of 'benign nature' of human beings is also based on empty optimism**; human nature is **a mixed bag of good and evil tendencies**; **social institutions are set up to regulate them**, and if destroyed, **man will return to savage life**.

### 5. The positive verdict — what anarchist justice actually requires

The chapter refuses to end on refutation, and the closing passage is the only place in the chapter where anarchist *justice* is stated positively. **Anarchism has launched a severe attack on the coercive side of the existing institutions, and has highlighted the need of social reconstruction.** Therefore: **the relevance of anarchism should be sought, not in the destruction of the present civilization, but in a pattern of social reconstruction through which the need of state power should be progressively reduced. Its primary goal should be to dilute the power associated with the possession of private property so that the poor and weaker segments of society are liberated from their continuing exploitation. That is the primary condition of social justice.** In other words, the perspective should be applied to create a social order **where the few will not lead a luxurious life by cornering the fruits of others' labour, but all members of a community will live together, work together and share the fruits of their labour equitably**.

🔑 Three things make this the lesson's payload. It converts abolition into a **direction of reform** — *progressively reduced*, not removed. It identifies the target as **the power associated with property**, not property as such, which is a narrower and more defensible claim than Proudhon's. And it states a **primary condition of social justice**, which is a sentence any welfare, inequality or decentralisation essay can be built around.

### 1. The baseline, dated in the same sentence

The lesson opens on a quoted block: **women constitute half the world's population, perform nearly two thirds of its work hours, receive one tenth of the world's income, and own less than one hundredth of the world's property** — attributed in print to the **United Nations Report, 1980** (PDF p.465). The author's next sentence, **the condition remains more or less unchanged today**, is a **2009** statement.

⚠️ **Reproduce both with their dates attached and never update either.** The reason is not timidity; it is that the figures do their analytical work as a *ratio*, not as a current measurement. Work share **rises** to two thirds while income share **falls** to a tenth and property share to under a hundredth — contribution and reward move in opposite directions. That is a claim about justice, and it survives whatever the current numbers are.

### 2. The claim, located precisely

**Feminist critique of justice maintains that women are disadvantaged in comparison with men, and that this disadvantage is not warranted by their natural and biological differences. Roots of this discrimination lie in the social arrangements which have perpetrated injustice against women** (PDF p.465). ⚠️ Both halves matter. The first denies a natural justification; the second locates the cause in arrangements, which is what makes the disadvantage a matter of justice rather than of misfortune. ➡️ Feminist theory as a doctrine, its waves and the sex/gender distinction belong to **Topic 06**; this topic owns the distributive claim.

### 3. The origin account, and the formulation that carries the lesson

The chapter asks why, if injustice against women has existed since earliest times, **it has come to the forefront only in recent times**, and answers in stages (PDF pp.465-466). At early stages of social organization, biological differences **necessitated the division of labour which suited both of them**, and the system **was based on mutual care and adjustment, and did not involve any significant level of resentment**. Then **with the development of technology, sweeping changes took place in other parts of social organization, but the division of labour between men and women remained more or less unchanged**. Meanwhile **with the evolution of various forms of power**, man **acquired more and more power** while woman **largely continued to live in a subordinate position**, compensated in status: the **weaker sex**, the **fair sex** endowed with special dignity, manners such as **ladies first**, and chivalry.

🔑 Then the sentence to carry into any answer: **in spite of so much importance accorded to woman in social life, she was systematically deprived of her share in power. She was given security but not an opportunity** to learn the things that would make her as competent as man **and thereby vindicate her claim to equality**. In particular she was deprived of **the right to ownership of property, right to vote and opportunities of education and higher learning**, **although these deprivations had no logical connection with her biological status as a woman** (PDF p.466).

The anchors are printed with the 1792 baseline: when **Mary Wollstonecraft (1759-97)** published ***Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792)**, woman was **not only deprived of the right to vote, but was deemed to be unfit for education, was debarred from many occupations, and had no legal right to own property**, and **had no real right to divorce even if her husband abused her**; she **forcefully challenged the prevailing belief in female inferiority**, arguing that women **are, like men, rational individuals**. **John Stuart Mill (1806-73)**, in ***The Subjection of Women* (1869)**, **sought to demonstrate that women were in no way inferior to men in their talents, and pleaded to give them full legal and political rights**. ⚠️ Wollstonecraft as a feminist thinker is **Topic 06's**; here she is a dated anchor for the deprivation list and nothing more. ❌ No succession law, no statute, no case.

### 4. The two broad views — the printed structure that answers the stem

**Currently there are two broad views concerning equal rights for women** (PDF p.466). **(a)** One view is that **there is no difference between men and women as regards their capabilities; hence they should be governed by the same laws**. **(b)** The other is that women are **essentially different from men — biologically, culturally and socially**, and **should be given equal opportunities to develop and apply their distinctive capabilities along with equal rights**. The measures that follow from (b) are printed: exemption **from hazardous tasks, like underground mining and working in night shifts**; **maternity leave and related benefits**; **arrangements for maintenance and custody of children after divorce**; and, **in order to compensate women for their underrepresentation in important positions, reservations for women** in **the seats for higher learning, appointments, seats in legislatures**. The verdict is printed: **this view seems to be more reasonable and is widely endorsed**.

🔑 **(a) against (b) is the equality-versus-empowerment question in the chapter's own words.** View (a) is equality as sameness of rule; view (b) is equality of standing, which requires differentiated treatment to be achieved. ⚠️ Both canonical owners carry the *measures* but not the *debate structure* or the verdict — and the structure is what converts a list of provisions into an argument.

### 5. The proof that development is not empowerment

**Worldwide concern for gender justice was expressed in *Human Development Report, 1995*** issued by the UNDP, which introduced the **gender-related development index (GDI)** for **130 countries (out of a total of 174 countries included in the report)** and the **gender empowerment measure (GEM)**, defined as **the extent to which women participate in a country's economic and political life** (PDF pp.466-467).

The report's definition is quoted and is usable in an essay: moving towards gender equality **is not a technocratic goal — it is a political process**, requiring **a new way of thinking — in which the stereotyping of women and men gives way to a new philosophy that regards all people, irrespective of gender, as essential agents of change**.

🔑 The analytically decisive finding, absent from both owners: **there is no essential correlation between GDI and GEM in many cases. It means that they have given adequate attention to health care and education of women, but no adequate share in the exercise of power.** The illustration is the East Asian **economic tigers**, where **in spite of substantial improvement in the level of development of women, they have been denied a tangible share in economic and political power in an essentially male-dominated society**. The report separately records **no correlation between gender bias and a country's economic development** — Cuba ranking **72** on the Human Development Index, **47** on GDI and **16** on GEM, ⚠️ all 1995 figures — and commends socialist models, which **used social and political mobilization to achieve rapid and equal progress in education and health for women and men**.

### 1. The definition, and the share claim inside it

The subaltern perspective **is concerned with the plight of those groups in society who are more or less permanently placed in subordinate position because of various constraints inherent in the social structure** — **exploited, oppressed and marginalized groups** (PDF p.467). ⚠️ Two words carry the weight. **Permanently** distinguishes subordination from misfortune or temporary disadvantage; **inherent in the social structure** locates the cause where remedies have to act.

The distributive claim follows in the next sentence: because of **an inherent division of society into the ruling and subaltern groups, a lion's share of all benefits accruing from the total efforts of society is cornered by a tiny class variously described as ruling class, dominant class or the elite**, while **the majority consisting of various subordinate groups who put their abilities and efforts into the creation of these benefits are left with a meagre share thereof**. Hence the chapter's own assessment that **the subaltern critique of justice raises the question of social justice in most eloquent manner**.

The term's origin is given in one line and stays there: the concept **was introduced in social theory by Antonio Gramsci (1891-1937)**, imprisoned by Mussolini, in his ***Prison Notebooks***. ➡️ Gramsci as a thinker, hegemony as a developed theory and the neo-Marxist apparatus are **Topic 04's**; this lesson takes a definition and moves on.

### 2. The confrontation with elite theory — the move that makes this a justice perspective

The Marxian theory of class structure **was challenged by the elite theory in the beginning of the twentieth century**. Class theory **had envisaged the division of society broadly into two classes on the basis of ownership and non-ownership of means of production**. Elite theory, **as expounded by Vilfredo Pareto (1848-1923), Gaetano Mosca (1858-1941) and Robert Michels (1876-1936)**, advanced **an alternative division of society into elite and masses on the basis of distinctive ability or organizational capacity of one group vis-a-vis others** (PDF p.468). ➡️ Elite theory itself is **Topic 15's**; it appears here in exactly these two sentences.

🔑 Then the inference, and the clause that justifies this perspective's place in a justice chapter: **it means that if ordinary people (the masses) get a lesser share of the cake, they deserve it. This view is designed to vindicate the liberal model of distribution — 'to each according to his work', that is the market society model.**

⚠️ That is the hinge of the entire lesson. Elite theory is not merely a sociology of rule; it supplies a **desert justification** for unequal shares, and the rule it justifies is the market rule taught at Lesson 4. So the subaltern perspective is a reply to the liberal and libertarian distributive rules, from a different direction than the Marxist reply at Lesson 5: Marxism attacks the ownership structure, subaltern theory attacks the **belief about desert** that makes the outcome acceptable.

### 3. The reply, and the printed definition of hegemony

Subaltern theory **contradicts the elite theory and holds that ordinary people contribute substantially to the production of the cake and still get a marginal share thereof because of their exploitation by the ruling class**. The mechanism of acceptance is then stated: **the ruling class evolves a value-system to suit its own interests, and projects it as the one embodying universal interests**, so subaltern groups — **peasants, workers, and other subordinate groups** — **are made to believe that they are being ruled with their consent**.

The chapter then supplies a definition neither canonical owner carries, although both use the word: **hegemony signifies the ability of the ruling class to convince the subject classes that their rule represents the common interest** (PDF p.468). ⚠️ Keep it at that. ❌ The common interest and the common good as concepts, and the triad that organises them, belong to **Topic 21**; here the phrase appears only inside a definition of a mechanism of consent.

Two further printed claims complete the position: subaltern theory **believes that the subaltern groups are capable of organizing themselves and shaping human history**; and because **most of the historians are biased towards the elite and they have largely ignored the role of the subaltern groups**, it **insists on rewriting of history giving due place to the role of the subaltern groups**.

### 4. The printed table — the chapter's only one

The chapter prints a comparison headed **Distinction Between Elite and Subaltern Perspectives**, on five issues plus exponents (PDF p.469). ⚠️ Reproduced here with its printed content; it is absent from both canonical owners.

If the division is **natural and functional**, redistribution corrects nothing and may harm output; if it is **artificial and exploitative**, the existing distribution is not a measure of desert at all. ⚠️ Note that the printed subaltern cell on alterability says **through revolution**, which is the chapter's wording; the expanded modern scope in the next paragraph plainly contemplates other routes, and an answer should not over-read a single table cell.

### 5. The expanded category, and the three-part remedy

**In contemporary social analysis, the scope of subaltern theory has been considerably expanded**: any group **relegated to a subordinate position because of undeserved discrimination** — **on grounds of gender, age, vocation, class, caste, race, region, religion, language, culture** — belongs in the category. **Their energies, abilities and skills are systematically exploited, but they are deprived of their due reward.**

The remedy is printed in three parts plus a share clause, and it is the most usable line in the lesson: **justice can only be restored if their rights are protected, their contribution to society is valued and properly rewarded, and their dignity is restored. It also demands restoration of equitable shares of social benefits for the elite and subaltern groups.**

⚠️ Three distinct repairs, and they do not substitute for one another: **rights** is a legal remedy, **valued and properly rewarded contribution** is a distributive remedy, and **dignity** is a standing remedy. 🔶 That triad is what lets this perspective speak to caste, disability, language or region without importing recognition vocabulary, which is a later bridge and not this chapter's term.

**Question:** Why does the chapter set the subaltern perspective against elite theory rather than simply describing subordination?

**Model answer:** Because elite theory supplies a **justification** for the unequal share, and a justification is what a theory of justice must answer. Elite theory, **as expounded by Vilfredo Pareto, Gaetano Mosca and Robert Michels**, divides society **into elite and masses on the basis of distinctive ability or organizational capacity of one group vis-a-vis others**, from which the chapter draws the inference that **if ordinary people (the masses) get a lesser share of the cake, they deserve it** — and states plainly that this view **is designed to vindicate the liberal model of distribution — 'to each according to his work', that is the market society model**. The subaltern reply is therefore not a complaint about hardship but a denial of desert: ordinary people **contribute substantially to the production of the cake and still get a marginal share thereof because of their exploitation by the ruling class**. The acceptance of that outcome is itself explained: the ruling class **evolves a value-system to suit its own interests, and projects it as the one embodying universal interests**, so that the subordinate **are made to believe that they are being ruled with their consent** — **hegemony** being **the ability of the ruling class to convince the subject classes that their rule represents the common interest**. ⚠️ Hence the printed contrast: **natural and functional** against **artificial and exploitative**.

### 1. The complaint, defined rather than asserted

The chapter opens the perspective by contrast and gives the only printed *definition* of communitarianism in the chapter, quoting **Will Kymlicka**, ***Contemporary Political Philosophy — An Introduction* (2002)**: **liberal visions of politics do not include any independent principle of community, such as shared nationality, language, identity, culture, religion, history, or way of life** — and **communitarians believe that the value of community is not sufficiently recognized in liberal theories of justice, or in the public culture of liberal societies** (PDF p.469).

⚠️ The phrase **independent principle of community** is the precise charge. Liberal theories can value community instrumentally, as something individuals happen to want; what they lack is a principle of community that does independent normative work. That is a sharper complaint than "liberals ignore society", and it is the version an examiner can reward.

Two printed contrasts follow. Liberalism holds that an individual **should be left free to pursue his self-interest solely on the condition that his freedom does not come in the way of similar freedom of others**; communitarians hold that an individual **finds self-fulfilment by accepting the prevalent social roles, practices and situations within the community, and not in isolation from his fellow-beings** — hence the **isolated self** against the **situated self**. And, in one sentence only: **while liberalism defines the common good as the sum total of individual goods, communitarianism on the other hand, treats the common good as one entity, which is the source of good for each individual** (PDF p.470). ⚠️ One sentence is the whole allowance; ➡️ the triad belongs to Topic 21.

### 2. The right/good priority dispute

The two sides also differ **on the respective status of 'right' and 'good' in determining the principles of justice**. Liberal theory, **particularly Rawls's theory of justice, accords priority to 'right' over 'good'**: Rawls **commended justice as 'the first virtue of social institutions', as truth is the primary consideration in accepting any system of thought**. For communitarians, **the 'right' implies virtue, and when we accept the 'good', the right has already been taken care of**.

🔑 Note the relation to Lesson 1. The chapter's opening gave justice as **the first virtue of a good society**; here Rawls is quoted as commending it as **the first virtue of social institutions**. ⚠️ Keep the two formulations distinct when quoting; they are differently scoped, and the communitarian objection fastens on the institutional version.

### 3. MacIntyre: the charge, and the concession that disarms the caricature

**Alasdair MacIntyre (1929- )**, in ***After Virtue* (1981)**, is quoted: **for what constitutes the good for man is a complete human life lived at its best, and the exercise of the virtues is a necessary and central part of such a life, not a mere preparatory exercise to achieve such a life. We thus cannot characterize the good for man adequately without already having made reference to the virtues.** He **ridiculed the liberal notion of individuals as 'autonomous moral agents' operating in an atmosphere where they are disconnected from social context**, holding that individuals **flourish only within the context of socially established cooperative human activity**.

The charge is then stated in full: liberals **were committed to moral relativism, detaching themselves from 'any particular standpoint' in order to practise tolerance**, so **they could not defend any particular view of justice, nor develop a unified concept of 'the good'**, and therefore **have not been able to develop true communities or define moral obligations of the members of society to each other**. The chapter prints definitional boxes for both poles: **moral relativism** is **the view that no moral principle or value system can be treated as universally right and acceptable**, validity being established **only with reference to a particular social and historical context**; **moral absolutism** is the view that a particular principle **should be accepted as universally valid for all societies**, allowing **no freedom to any individual or group to propose or follow any alternative**.

🔑 Then the printed partial recantation, absent from both owners and the single best piece of evidence in this lesson: in ***Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988)**, MacIntyre **came to realize the pitfalls of moral absolutism, and conceded that liberal tolerance was itself a virtuous practice** (PDF p.471).

✅ That sentence is what the Advanced owner's warning — ❌ do not collapse communitarianism into authoritarian moral absolutism — has been asserting without evidence. ⚠️ Deploy it as the chapter's own correction: the leading communitarian critic of liberal neutrality conceded that the practice he attacked was itself a virtue, which converts the dispute from a clash of absolutes into a question about how much community a theory of justice must build in.

### 4. Sandel and Taylor: the attack on the person behind the veil

**Michael Sandel (1953- )**, in ***Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982)**, attacks the form of liberalism **exemplified by Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971)** by name. Rawls's **rational negotiators** represent **the disconnected and disembodied people deliberating behind the 'veil of ignorance'**; Rawls tries **to understand human beings independent of all activities, desires, ideas, roles, and pursuits that characterize human lives in actual society**; hence the printed question **Is anything left of the person when we subtract all this from his personality?**, and the verdict that the Rawlsian view of the person is **woefully impoverished** (PDF p.471).

The positive claim follows: liberal theories **have failed to recognize our 'embeddedness' in a particular time, place and culture**; **justice cannot be secured by isolated individuals seeking personal profit (as in markets and political arena), but by those who create a 'deeper commonality' through 'shared self-understanding' and mutual affection**; and, decisively, **the self is not prior to its ends; it is rather constituted by its ends, which are not chosen but discovered by the self by virtue of its being embedded in some shared social context**, so that the **unencumbered self** does not correspond with our **deepest self-understanding**. ***Democracy's Discontent* (1996)** reiterates the argument.

**Charles Taylor (1931- )**, in collected papers published in **1985**, questions **the premise of atomistic individualism which is the hallmark of liberal political theory** and attacks the concept of human beings as **autonomous choosers**, which treats them **only as a manifestation of will**. In ***Sources of the Self* (1989)** he argues that human agency is intelligible only on the premise that persons exist as **embodied individuals** engaged **both in self-interpretation and in constant interaction with others**, guided by **moral sources — secular, religious, literary and philosophical**.

🔶 The Basic owner's objection-reply chain belongs here and carries **no** page anchor. *Objection (Sandel):* the unencumbered self is impossible, since persons are partly constituted by ends they cannot bracket. *Reply:* the veil is a device for choosing principles for the basic structure, not a metaphysics of the person; a thin theory of the good generates the principles, after which thick community life proceeds inside just constraints. ➡️ The situated self as a theory of politics is **Topic 07's** and is cited, not re-taught.

### 5. Walzer: the only communitarian theory of justice in the chapter

**Michael Walzer (1935- )**, in ***Spheres of Justice* (1983)**, is distinguished from the others in the chapter's own words: while other exponents **have largely produced a communitarian critique of liberal theory of justice**, Walzer **enunciated a communitarian theory of justice, although in some respects it is akin to liberal-pluralist point of view** (PDF p.472).

His premise: **the quest for a universal theory of justice was misguided, because it was futile to look for any principle of justice outside the community — particularly its history and culture**; requirements of justice **could only be identified in the context of a particular community, its practices and institutions**.

His rule: apply **complex equality** as distinguished from **simple equality**. Simple equality is rejected twice over — it would be produced only by immense state effort, and **if the state tries to reinforce initial simple equality, it will end up as a tyrannical state** (PDF p.473). Complex equality **does not try to equalize all goods, but rather seeks to ensure that inequalities in one 'sphere' (e.g. wealth) do not permeate other spheres (e.g. health care and political power)**, because **modern society includes a number of spheres of distribution in which different goods are allocated each by its own independent criterion**. In his own quoted words: **complex equality means that no citizen's standing in one sphere or with regard to one social good can be undercut by his standing in some other sphere, with regard to some other good ... No social good x should be distributed to men and women who possess some other social good y merely because they possess y and without regard to the meaning of x.**

The four printed boundaries follow: **the spheres of politics, or health or education, should not be corrupted by the domination of money, for money properly rules in the sphere of commodities**; **the sphere of office should not (beyond a certain limited point) be contaminated by nepotism, which belongs in the sphere of kinship and love**; **the sphere of kinship and love should not be contaminated by the consideration of profit and loss which are relevant only in the market-place**; and **family organization should not be patterned after male domination which properly belongs to the sphere of military organization**. 🔑 The fourth boundary links straight back to Lesson 8 and is the one to cite when a stem joins gender to distributive justice.

The result: if boundaries are respected, **one person's pre-eminence in, say, the sphere of money may be offset by another's higher social prestige and a third's success in holding political office**, so **social pluralism may lead to a kind of equality in which no one decisively outranks anyone else** — and Walzer thereby **denies that economic status of an individual holds key to his social prestige and power**.

### 6. Both printed criticisms, because the owners carry only one

**(i) Incommensurability** — absent from both owners and the objection that makes complex equality examinable rather than decorative: **there is no reliable method to compare the value of non-economic factors like reputation, political power, education and health, etc. with the value of income and wealth. In the absence of such criteria, it is difficult to attack the disparities created by market society.** The illustration is exact: **it would be a poor consolation for a university teacher, writer, artist or scientist that the low (economic) return of his talents and effort is compensated by the high esteem in which he is held in the society!** ⚠️ The force of the objection is that complex equality can *describe* an offset without being able to *verify* one, so the scheme risks rationalising economic disadvantage instead of remedying it.

**(ii) No mechanism** — **he has not indicated the way to convince the dominant people in different spheres of social life to adopt these rules**, so his scheme **embodies a strong moral philosophy, but it does not provide for equally strong political philosophy**.

### Concept check

**Question:** How does complex equality differ from simple equality, and what are the two printed objections to it?

**Model answer:** Simple equality seeks to equalise holdings; complex equality regulates **conversion** between spheres. Walzer rejects the former twice: free-market operation would reproduce **immense inequalities**, and **if the state tries to reinforce initial simple equality, it will end up as a tyrannical state**. Complex equality instead **does not try to equalize all goods, but rather seeks to ensure that inequalities in one 'sphere' (e.g. wealth) do not permeate other spheres (e.g. health care and political power)** — in his words, **no citizen's standing in one sphere ... can be undercut by his standing in some other sphere**, and **no social good x should be distributed to men and women who possess some other social good y merely because they possess y**. Hence the four boundaries: money out of politics, health and education; nepotism out of office; profit and loss out of kinship and love; and family organization not **patterned after male domination**. The result is **social pluralism** yielding an equality in which **no one decisively outranks anyone else**. ⚠️ Two printed objections: there is **no reliable method to compare** reputation, political power, education and health with income and wealth, so it is **difficult to attack the disparities created by market society** and esteem is **poor consolation** for low economic return; and he **has not indicated the way to convince the dominant people**, leaving **a strong moral philosophy** with **not equally strong political philosophy**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat communitarianism as moral absolutism or as a rejection of justice. The chapter prints MacIntyre's own concession in *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988), where he **came to realize the pitfalls of moral absolutism, and conceded that liberal tolerance was itself a virtuous practice** — and Walzer's position is described as in some respects **akin to liberal-pluralist**. The quarrel is with the abstract self and with ethical neutrality, not with norm-governed distribution.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Explain the communitarian objection to the Rawlsian conception of the person, and assess how far it damages justice as fairness. *(Explain and assess — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The objection is Sandel's. Rawls's **rational negotiators** are **disconnected and disembodied people deliberating behind the 'veil of ignorance'**, abstracted from **all activities, desires, ideas, roles, and pursuits that characterize human lives in actual society** — hence **is anything left of the person when we subtract all this from his personality?** and the charge that the Rawlsian person is **woefully impoverished**, since **the self is not prior to its ends; it is rather constituted by its ends, which are not chosen but discovered**. 🔶 The liberal reply limits the damage: the veil selects principles for the basic structure, not a metaphysics of the self, so a thin theory of the good suffices and thick community life proceeds within just constraints. ⚠️ The objection wounds the philosophical anthropology, not the distributive rule — and MacIntyre's **1988** concession that **liberal tolerance was itself a virtuous practice** shows the critique narrowing from inside.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Why do eight perspectives on justice disagree, given that each accepts that social advantages must be distributed? *(Answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** Because they disagree about **what the distributive rule applies to**. # CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Four checks: three consolidate a block and the fourth spans the whole arc. Each model is held within 250 words.

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 to 4

**Question:** Build the chain from the chapter's opening premise to the two rival liberal rules, naming at each step the clause that carries it.

**Model answer:** One: justice is **the first virtue of a good society**, hence **a necessary but not a sufficient condition** of one — so no other good may be bought at its cost, and no distributive rule is a complete political philosophy. Two: that insufficiency is what licenses eight rivals, each appraised separately, and comparison requires stated axes — rule, state, property, equality, community, criticism — ⚠️ which are an exam-use synthesis, not printed headings. Three: the liberal rule treats liberty as the central problem and is concerned with **substantive** liberty, distributing **primary goods** including **means of self-respect**, under a theory its author calls **pure procedural justice**, so that principles once **unanimously accepted** yield a distribution that is **necessarily just**. Four: the choice is made behind a **veil of ignorance** by agents who retain **elementary knowledge of economics and psychology**, a **sense of justice**, and an unwillingness **to take a risk or resort to gambling** — hence each reasons from **the least advantaged position**. Five: the output is ordered — equal liberty, since **nobody's liberty will be sacrificed for the sake of any other benefit**; then fair opportunity for **offices and positions**; then the difference principle; 🔶 with benefit to the worst off **necessary and not sufficient**. Six: the libertarian rule attacks the object itself, replacing pattern with history — **acquisition**, **transfer**, **rectification** — on **formal** liberty and a **nightwatchman** state, and is answered in print by **charity, not on justice**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat Rawls and Nozick as opposite points on one scale of redistribution. They disagree about what a theory of justice is *for*: one evaluates the procedure that fixes shares, the other evaluates the history of each holding, which is why no amount of agreement about outcomes would reconcile them.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 5 and 6

**Question:** Trace the route from the Marxist refusal of fair shares to the democratic-socialist refusal of dictatorship, showing what each step adds that the one before could not supply.

**Model answer:** The Marxist perspective first denies that the question can be asked inside the system: justice talk would mean **maintaining the existing system with minor changes**, the **Gotha** critique rebukes socialists **complacent after raising the demand of 'fair distribution'**, morality belongs to the **superstructure**, and communist society needs no **judicial apparatus**. The chapter then shows the denial is too strong: exploitation is **a theft, or even an act of robbery**, and Marx **conceded moral supremacy** of **to each according to his need** over **to each according to his ability**, which **shows his concern with distributive justice**. The mechanism locates the wrong at source — **labour is the sole creator of value**, land, capital and organization are **sterile**, and the worker **gets back only a part** of what he produces — so the remedy is ownership, abolishing **private** while protecting **personal** property, the **fruit of man's own labour**. What that route cannot supply is a guarantee against new domination, and the chapter says so: class **may reappear** through **political and bureaucratic power**, so injustice must be tackled **at many more subtle levels**. Democratic socialism supplies exactly that guard — **partial socialization**, **moral as well as material needs**, freedom of thought even where exploitation has ended, **no form of dictatorship** including that **of the proletariat**, and **free competition for power**. ⚠️ Its own cost is then printed: **democracy is a matter of procedure; it does not prescribe the goals of state policy**, so delivery depends on public will and a resource base.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read the second as a watered-down version of the first. The refusals differ in kind — one refuses reform inside an exploitative structure, the other refuses any dictatorship even in a post-exploitative one — and the democratic-socialist objection to the proletarian dictatorship is grounded in the **suppression of personality**, not in squeamishness about method.

### Cumulative check C — Lessons 7 to 10

**Question:** The last four perspectives each say that something is missing from a distributive rule. State what each identifies, and the printed objection each faces.

**Model answer:** **Anarchist:** the missing element is that coercion itself is the injustice — **government is intrinsically evil**, men are corrupted **by the intervention** of coercive power, and so **even reforms, introduced by an authority 'from above' are worthless**. Objection: **no concrete programme**, and the regulation argument — depletion and **atmospheric pollution** mean **some sort of global regulation has become necessary**, making spontaneous adjustment **the flight of imagination**. **Feminist:** the missing element is standing, since disadvantage is **not warranted by their natural and biological differences** and **she was given security but not an opportunity**; the printed verdict endorses equal rights **plus** distinctive provision. Objection it answers: formal sameness leaves **underrepresentation** intact, proved by **no essential correlation between GDI and GEM**. **Subaltern:** the missing element is the belief that unequal shares are deserved — elite theory concludes that **if ordinary people get a lesser share of the cake, they deserve it**, a view **designed to vindicate the liberal model of distribution**, while **hegemony** is **the ability of the ruling class to convince the subject classes that their rule represents the common interest**. Remedy: rights, rewarded contribution, dignity, equitable shares. **Communitarian:** the missing element is community and meaning — liberal visions include **no independent principle of community**, and the self is **constituted by its ends, which are not chosen but discovered**; Walzer's **complex equality** supplies a rival rule. Objections: **no reliable method to compare** esteem with income, and **a strong moral philosophy** with **not equally strong political philosophy**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat these four as a single "critical" bloc opposed to the first four. Only one of them — Walzer — produces a positive rule of distribution, and the anarchist, feminist and subaltern perspectives disagree sharply with one another about whether the state is the remedy or the problem.

### Cumulative check D — the whole arc, Lessons 1 to 11

**Question:** In one connected argument, move from the chapter's premise to a graded verdict on how a distributive rule should be assessed, marking where each lesson does work.

**Model answer:** Justice is **the first virtue of a good society** and **a necessary but not a sufficient condition** of one, so a rule may be correct and incomplete (L1). The first rule evaluates a **procedure**: **primary goods** allocated by principles chosen behind a veil by risk-averse agents, yielding equal liberty, fair opportunity and the difference principle in order, with benefit to the worst off **not sufficient** on its own (L2, L3). The second evaluates **history**: **acquisition**, **transfer** and **rectification**, with the test being whether anybody is made **worse off** — and the printed reply that this leaves welfare dependent on **charity, not on justice** (L4). The third says the share is settled before distribution begins, in the ownership of production, while conceding that domination **may reappear** through **political and bureaucratic power** (L5). The fourth asks what a non-dictatorial state can sustain, and finds that **democracy is a matter of procedure** (L6). The fifth indicts coercion itself, and is answered by the need for **global regulation** (L7). The sixth shows that provision may rise while power does not — **no essential correlation between GDI and GEM** (L8). The seventh shows that unequal shares are protected by a belief in desert, sustained by **hegemony** (L9). The eighth asks whether each good keeps **its own independent criterion**, and offers **complex equality** (L10). ⚠️ Hence the assessment rule (L11): name the axis, test procedure **and** structure, and concede what the chosen rule cannot reach.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not end such an answer by selecting a winner. Every perspective in this chapter carries a printed defect, including the last, and the chapter's own opening premise explains why: a theory can be just and still incomplete. The graded close names what remains unresolved rather than crowning a rule.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ Original practice prompts written for this edition. They are **not** previous-year questions and must not be cited as such. Fifteen- and twenty-mark models are held within 250 words.

**Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish the liberal from the libertarian perspective on justice. *(Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Both **treat liberty of the individual as the central problem of justice**; they divide on which liberty and on what a theory evaluates. The liberal perspective concerns **substantive** liberty and accommodates equality and fraternity **in the broader framework of liberty**, distributing **primary goods** by principles chosen behind a veil — equal liberty, then fair opportunity, then the difference principle. The libertarian perspective focuses on **formal** liberty, insists on a **minimal role of the state in economic activities**, treats property as **an important ingredient of individual liberty**, and is **largely opposed to the idea of welfare state**. The contrast is sharper: Rawls **moderates his libertarianism by a modicum of egalitarianism and communitarianism** as a **left liberal**; Nozick **adhered to libertarianism in its pure form** as a **right liberal** of a **nightwatchman** state. ⚠️ The deeper split is the test: a **pattern** justified in advance against a **history** of valid title.

**Scoring note:** the marks concentrate on substantive against formal liberty, and on pattern against history. An answer that contrasts "more state" with "less state" has described the conclusion and missed the reasoning; the left-liberal/right-liberal formulation is the cheapest available discriminator.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** Explain what the subaltern perspective adds to a Marxist account of unjust distribution. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** It adds the ideological mechanism making unjust distribution acceptable. The Marxist account divides society by **ownership and non-ownership of means of production**, locating injustice in exploitation. The subaltern account begins from groups **permanently placed in subordinate position because of constraints inherent in the social structure**, and confronts a rival justification: elite theory divides by **distinctive ability or organizational capacity**, concluding that **if ordinary people get a lesser share of the cake, they deserve it** — a view **designed to vindicate the liberal model of distribution — 'to each according to his work'**. Reply: ordinary people **contribute substantially** yet receive a marginal share, while the ruling class projects its value-system **as the one embodying universal interests**, so the ruled **are made to believe that they are being ruled with their consent** — **hegemony**. ⚠️ It also widens the category to **gender, age, caste, race, region, religion, language and culture**, beyond ownership.

**Scoring note:** an **Explain** directive rewards the addition, not a survey of both theories. The two additions are the **desert-belief** mechanism and the **widened category**; naming only exploitation repeats Lesson 5 and earns nothing extra.

**Practice question 3 [15]:** "A just procedure is sufficient to secure a just distribution." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition has a serious defender and three serious opponents in this chapter, and the examination consists in locating what each is really claiming. **The case for it.** Rawls **described his theory as the theory of pure procedural justice**: once principles are **unanimously accepted**, the distribution resulting from their application **will be necessarily just**. The procedure is not arbitrary — agents behind the **veil of ignorance** retain a **sense of justice** and are **not prepared to take a risk or resort to gambling**, so each reasons from **the least advantaged position** — and the chapter credits the result, holding that Rawls **has discovered a method for making procedural justice an instrument of meeting the requirements of social justice**.

**The case against it.** The Marxist objection attacks the inputs: deliberation **without the knowledge of prevailing social and economic conditions** is **meaningless**, because the structure of ownership decides the share before any procedure runs. The feminist objection attacks standing: disadvantage is **not warranted by their natural and biological differences**, and **no essential correlation between GDI and GEM** shows that provision need not deliver power. The subaltern objection attacks belief: the outcome is accepted because **hegemony** persuades the subordinate that rule **represents the common interest**.

**Resolution.** 🔶 Even internally the proposition overstates: benefit to the worst off is **not sufficient** where liberty or fair opportunity fails, so the procedure carries conditions rather than replacing them. ⚠️ Verdict: a just procedure is indispensable and insufficient — procedure and structure are both necessary.

**Scoring note:** the discriminator is whether the three objections are shown to attack *different* things — inputs, standing, belief — rather than being stacked as three versions of "society is unequal". An answer that never states the proposition at its strongest, using the pure-procedural sentence, cannot reach the top band.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** Examine the claim that abolishing private property would be sufficient to end distributive injustice. *(Examine — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The claim is this chapter's Marxist conclusion, and the chapter itself refuses the word *sufficient*. **The case for it.** Private property is **the source of exploitation and injustice in society**; labour is **the sole creator of value** while land, capital and organization are **sterile**; the worker **gets back only a part** of the value he produces. Abolition targets domination, not possession: communism **does not contemplate to abolish personal property which is the fruit of man's own labour**, including **the petty artisan and of the small peasant**, and the *Communist Manifesto* (1848) states that it **deprives no man of the power to appropriate the products of society; all that it does is to deprive him of the power to subjugate the labour of others**. The goal is an **association, in which the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all**.

**The case against sufficiency.** The chapter's objection is decisive: the view **treats the possession of private property as the only source of class distinctions**, whereas distinctions **may reappear on the basis of possession of political and bureaucratic power, even after the abolition of private property**, so injustice must be tackled **at many more subtle levels**. Three perspectives corroborate: democratic socialism insists conditions without **freedom of thought and expression** are **not conducive to the fuller development of personality**; the feminist finding separates provision from power; the subaltern account shows subordination running through **ideological** as well as economic power.

⚠️ Verdict: necessary on this account, demonstrably not sufficient.

**Scoring note:** the top band requires the private/personal distinction stated before the critique, since an answer attacking "abolition of all property" has attacked a position the chapter does not hold. The corroborating perspectives must each add a *different* residual source of domination; repeating "inequality persists" three times adds nothing.

**Practice question 5 [20]:** "Diverse perspectives on justice disagree about the share; they do not disagree about justice." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition is half right, and the half it gets wrong is the one that matters. **What is true.** All eight perspectives accept that social advantages must be allocated and that an allocation can be wrong, which is why the chapter treats them as rivals rather than different subjects. The chapter also supplies a shared premise: justice is **the first virtue of a good society**, and **a necessary but not a sufficient condition** of one.

**What is false.** They disagree about the **object** the rule governs, not merely about the size of the share. Rawls evaluates a **procedure** and calls his theory one of **pure procedural justice**; Nozick evaluates a **history**, since a title is good only through **acquisition**, **transfer** or **rectification**; the Marxist account relocates the question to the mode of production, holding that justice talk inside capitalism means **maintaining the existing system with minor changes**; the anarchist indicts coercion as such, since **even reforms, introduced by an authority 'from above' are worthless**; the feminist perspective targets standing, since **she was given security but not an opportunity**; the subaltern perspective targets a belief about desert, sustained by **hegemony**; and Walzer targets **the meaning of x**, insisting that **no social good x should be distributed to men and women who possess some other social good y merely because they possess y**.

⚠️ Verdict: a disagreement about what injustice *is* cannot be reduced to a disagreement about quantity — and that is why no single perspective is both complete and sufficient.

**Scoring note:** the examination turns on conceding the proposition's true half before refuting it, and on demonstrating the refutation across at least five perspectives with the correct object attached to each. A balanced-sounding answer that merely lists eight positions has illustrated the proposition rather than examined it.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** Discuss whether a rule of distribution can be just while the structure people enter it with is unjust. *(Discuss — answer in about 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Three of this chapter's perspectives answer no, one answers yes with conditions, and the chapter's own material supports a middle position. **The yes case.** On a procedural account the distribution issuing from unanimously accepted principles is **necessarily just**, and the principles are themselves chosen under conditions designed to neutralise advantage — ignorance of **wants, interests, skills and abilities** and of **the conditions which lead to discrimination and conflict**. The structure, on this view, is what the principles correct.

**The no case.** The Marxist objection is that deliberation **without the knowledge of prevailing social and economic conditions** is **meaningless**, because exploitation is built into production. The feminist objection is that disadvantage lies **in the social arrangements**, so identical rules leave **underrepresentation** untouched — hence the endorsement of equal rights **plus** distinctive provision as **more reasonable and widely endorsed**. The subaltern objection: structure also shapes belief, since **hegemony** convinces the subordinate that rule **represents the common interest**.

**The middle.** 🔶 Even inside the procedural account, benefit to the worst off is **not sufficient** where fair opportunity fails — so the theory already concedes that entry conditions matter. Conversely, a structural remedy that abandons procedure forfeits what the first principle protects, since **nobody's liberty will be sacrificed for the sake of any other benefit**, and enforcing simple equality makes the state **a tyrannical state**.

⚠️ Verdict: no rule is just in isolation from entry conditions, and no structural correction is just without a procedure — both are necessary, and neither alone is enough.

**Scoring note:** a *discuss* directive requires both sides at strength and a relation between them, not alternation. The discriminator is the middle section: showing that the procedural account itself concedes the point through lexical priority is worth more than any number of external objections.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

Compressed revision notes for the whole topic, under topic-specific headings. ⚠️ They do not repeat the teaching introductions; they carry definitions, printed formulations, causal logic, comparisons, traps, routes and rapid-recall facts.

## A. The chapter's frame and premise

- Opening: **a significant part of political theory is directly or indirectly related to the problem of justice**; eight perspectives named in printed order — **Liberal, Libertarian, Marxist, Democratic-Socialist, Anarchist, Feminist, Subaltern, Communitarian**.
- Premise: justice is **the first virtue of a good society**, hence **a necessary but not a sufficient condition** of one; letting justice yield to **social advancement and progress** risks **moral degradation of society**.
- Six axes for comparison — rule · state · property · equality · community · strongest criticism — ⚠️ exam-use synthesis, ❌ not printed headings.
- Two standing qualifications: no perspective is both complete and sufficient; procedure and structure are both necessary.

## B. Liberal justice: object, type, device

- Liberal justice **treats liberty as the central problem**, concerned with **substantive** liberty, accommodating equality and fraternity **in the broader framework of liberty**.
- Rawls (**1921-2002**), ***A Theory of Justice* (1971)**. Object: **primary goods** — rights and liberties, powers and opportunities, income and wealth, **means of self-respect**.
- Type: **pure procedural justice** — principles once **unanimously accepted** make the resulting distribution **necessarily just**.
- Against utilitarianism (box: **Bentham 1748-1832**; shares **need not be taken into account**): the **enslavement of a minority** case; **you cannot compensate for the sufferings of the distressed by enhancing the joys of the prosperous**.
- **Original position** and **veil of ignorance**: ignorant of **wants, interests, skills and abilities**; retain **elementary knowledge of economics and psychology**, a **sense of justice**, non-envy, **self-interested but not egoists**, **not prepared to take a risk or resort to gambling** → each adopts **the least advantaged position**.

## C. The ordering, the chain, and the appraisal

- Printed three **in the following order**: equal liberty (participation, expression, religion, equality before law) — **nobody's liberty will be sacrificed for the sake of any other benefit**; **fair equality of opportunity**, **particularly for acquiring offices and positions**; **difference principle** — **greatest benefit to the least advantaged**.
- Efficiency rider: **to each according to his ability** only if higher efficiency **ameliorates the condition of the least privileged**.
- ✅ Correction: Rawls states **two** principles, fair opportunity inside and prior to the difference principle; the three-step list is exposition. 🔶 Benefit to the worst off is **not sufficient**.
- **Chain connection** answers **why should the meritorious accommodate the interests of the least advantaged?** — strengthen the **weakest link**, repeatedly; it brings Rawls **very close to the image of a communitarian**. 📝 **Barry's** gloss (rise at the top raises all) is contested; the page conditions it on **empirical facts** about filtering down. 📝 **Gorovitz (1975)**: **clearly a redistributionist**, **highest social value on the needs of the neediest**.
- Four schools: **collectivists** (ground for justifying capitalism; **a slight improvement** becomes **an excuse** for vast inequalities); **identification problem** (income and wealth alone cannot reach those who **lack ability** or suffer **emotional insecurity**); **Marxists** (deliberation without knowledge of conditions is **meaningless**); **libertarians** (liberty sacrificed; risk-averse negotiators cannot drive progress); **communitarians** (**ethical neutrality evades** the common good).
- Verdict: **biased interpretations**; a **convergence of libertarianism, egalitarianism and communitarianism**; **equality as a cardinal principle**, **only inequalities** justified; capitalism conditioned assumes **a new humane look**; **procedural justice an instrument of meeting the requirements of social justice**.

## D. Entitlement: two lists, three principles, three cases

- Libertarian justice: **formal** liberty, minimal state, property an **ingredient of individual liberty**, opposed to the **welfare state**. Nozick (**1938-2002**), ***Anarchy, State and Utopia* (1974)**.
- **Left liberal** against **right liberal**; **modicum** of egalitarianism against libertarianism **in its pure form**; the **nightwatchman** state. Rawls distributes **as if they have come to us as a gift from heaven**.
- Modes of acquisition: **selves** (absolute, **needs no further justification**); **the natural world** (**precisely the area where principles of entitlement are required**); **things people make**.
- Principles: **initial acquisition** — **as long as nobody is made worse off**; Locke's *Second Treatise* (**1690**), **enough and as good is left for others**; **a condition which may scarcely be satisfied**. **Voluntary transfer** — **without force or fraud**, **end-in-itself** after **Kant (1724-1804)**, slavery contract **void**. **Rectification** — **the state or the international community will be justified to intervene**.
- Graded series: **Chamberlain** legitimate; **cancer-cure inventor** legitimate **because he does not make anybody worse off**; **single source of water** — **nobody has the right to take it into his control**.
- Appraisal: **lets the weak go to the wall**; **absolves the rich of all social responsibility**; **the voice of the oppressed** against **maintaining the prevailing oppression in the name of justice**; tax only for **streets and street lights, police and defence**; welfare tax **akin to forced labour**; **welfare of the poor dependent on charity, not on justice**; he will not concede that market operation **may itself create certain conditions of injustice**. ⚠️ Oil-producing-countries clause = the chapter's own polemic.

## E. The Marxist question: can it speak of justice at all

- Against: replacement not reform, so justice means **maintaining the existing system with minor changes**; **Gotha (1875)** rebuke of those **complacent after raising the demand of 'fair distribution'**; morality as **superstructure**, hence no **enduring principles**; communism needs no **judicial apparatus**.
- For: exploitation as **a theft, or even an act of robbery**; conceded **moral supremacy** of **need** over **ability**, which **shows his concern with distributive justice**; the end of **false consciousness**, hence **a morally superior system**; **freedom and humanism** against **dominance and dependence**.
- Qualification: he refuses any solution that is **an excuse to retain capitalism with certain modifications**.
- Mechanism, one line: **labour is the sole creator of value**; land, capital and organization **sterile**; the worker **gets back only a part**; box **Subsistence Wages**. ❌ Derivation is Topic 04's.
- Conclusion: **private** property abolished as **the source of exploitation and injustice**; **personal** property — **the fruit of man's own labour**, the **petty artisan**, the **small peasant** — **needs to be protected**; *Manifesto* (**1848**): **Abolition of private property**, and removal only of **the power to subjugate the labour of others**; **the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all**.
- 🔑 Objection: property treated as **the only source** of class distinctions; class may reappear through **political and bureaucratic power**; injustice must be tackled at **many more subtle levels**.

## F. Democratic socialism: five tenets and the bill

- **Evolutionary or democratic method**; **the goals of democracy and socialism are not separable**.
- Tenets: **partial socialization** of **some essential means** (❌ not **wholesale**); **moral as well as material needs** — **education, entertainment, art and culture** for **a fuller development of the personality**; **freedom of thought and expression**, since even an exploitation-free, materially satisfied society is **not conducive to the fuller development of personality** without it; **no form of dictatorship**, including that **of the proletariat**, which **leads to the suppression of personality**; **free competition for power** with **an effective role for the opposition**.
- Sources: **Laski (1893-1950)** — **1930**, **1935**, **1938** — who **sought to combine the ends of socialism with the democratic method of liberalism**; **Durbin (1940)**; **Sweden, Norway, Denmark**; ⚠️ **India also claims to follow the path of democratic socialism** (a 2009 statement about a **claim**).
- Conclusion: **a difficult combination indeed**; **democracy is a matter of procedure; it does not prescribe the goals of state policy**; condition (a) **discerning public opinion** plus political will; condition (b) a **resource-base** funding services **without resorting to coercion and undue taxation**; needs **a sense of duty, work culture and patriotism**; and heavy taxation of those who rose **by dint of their talents, efforts, enterprise and frugality** **might mar the incentives and thereby impoverish the resource-base**.

## G. Anarchist justice: premises, appraisal, verdict

- Premises: organisation **without coercive power of the state**; **government is intrinsically evil**; **men are benign by nature**; **society is a natural institution**; men are **corrupted by the intervention**; abolition **completely, or at least partly**; 🔑 **even reforms, introduced by an authority 'from above' are worthless**.
- ⚠️ **Anarchism is not a consistent doctrine**; Marx excluded because he routes through a **proletarian state** while mainstream anarchists want **straightaway abolition**.
- Roster, distributive claim only: Godwin (inequality from **the condition of society**); **Proudhon** — **property is theft**, **mutualism** on **non-profit-making terms**; **Bakunin** — **free federation**, **right to secession**; **Kropotkin** — **mutual aid**, **anarchist communism**; Sorel (➡️ Topic 05); Tolstoy (moral regeneration); **Gandhi** (stateless society); **Bookchin** — **post-scarcity**, communities that **live together, work together and eat together**.
- Appraisal: **fascinating idea**, **no concrete programme**; rejection of parties, movements and leadership; terrorism — **how can we justify inhuman acts in the name of human liberty and emancipation?**; 🔑 the regulation argument — depletion, **atmospheric pollution**, **the authority of the nation-states is not sufficient**, **some sort of global regulation has become necessary**, so spontaneous adjustment is **the flight of imagination**; **empty optimism**, human nature **a mixed bag**.
- 🔑 Verdict: **not in the destruction of the present civilization, but in a pattern of social reconstruction through which the need of state power should be progressively reduced**; **dilute the power associated with the possession of private property**; **that is the primary condition of social justice**.

## H. Feminist justice: baseline, split, proof

- **United Nations Report, 1980**: **half** the population, **two thirds** of work hours, **one tenth** of income, under **one hundredth** of property. ⚠️ 1980 data; **the condition remains more or less unchanged today** is a **2009** statement.
- Claim: disadvantage **not warranted by their natural and biological differences**; roots **in the social arrangements**.
- Origin: division of labour **based on mutual care and adjustment** → technology changed everything except it → status in place of power (**weaker sex**, **fair sex**, **ladies first**, chivalry) → 🔑 **she was systematically deprived of her share in power. She was given security but not an opportunity**; lost **property, the vote, education**, with **no logical connection** to biology.
- **Wollstonecraft (1759-97)**, ***Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792)** — 1792 baseline: no vote, **unfit for education**, debarred from occupations, **no legal right to own property**, **no real right to divorce even if her husband abused her**. **J.S. Mill (1806-73)**, ***The Subjection of Women* (1869)**.
- 🔑 Two views: **(a)** no difference in capability → **same laws**; **(b)** **essentially different — biologically, culturally and socially** → equal rights **plus** opportunity for **distinctive capabilities**, hence exemption from **underground mining and night shifts**, **maternity leave**, maintenance and custody, **reservations** against **underrepresentation**. Verdict: (b) **seems to be more reasonable and is widely endorsed**.
- ***Human Development Report, 1995***: **GDI** (130 of 174 countries); **GEM** = **the extent to which women participate in a country's economic and political life**; gender equality is **not a technocratic goal — it is a political process**; socialist models commended for **rapid and equal progress in education and health**.
- 🔑 **No essential correlation between GDI and GEM** — **adequate attention to health care and education**, **but no adequate share in the exercise of power**; the East Asian **economic tigers**. Nutshell: **overall development** **as well as** an **adequate share in economic and political power**.
- ❌ Omit the report's characterisation of the most gender-biased societies; ⚠️ India's GDI rank 99 of 130 is a **1995** figure or is omitted.

## I. Subaltern justice: definition, clash, remedy

- Groups **more or less permanently placed in subordinate position because of various constraints inherent in the social structure**; **a lion's share ... cornered by a tiny class**; the majority who create the benefits get **a meagre share**. **Gramsci (1891-1937)**, ***Prison Notebooks*** — one line.
- Clash: elite theory (**Pareto, Mosca, Michels**) divides by **distinctive ability or organizational capacity** → **if ordinary people get a lesser share of the cake, they deserve it** → 🔑 **designed to vindicate the liberal model of distribution — 'to each according to his work', that is the market society model**.
- Reply: they **contribute substantially** and still get a marginal share; the ruling class projects its value-system as **embodying universal interests**; the ruled **are made to believe that they are being ruled with their consent**.
- 🔑 **Hegemony** = **the ability of the ruling class to convince the subject classes that their rule represents the common interest**.
- Printed table rows that decide answers: **natural and functional** against **artificial and exploitative**; **not alterable** against alterable **through revolution**.
- Expanded categories: **gender, age, vocation, class, caste, race, region, religion, language, culture** — **undeserved discrimination**; **systematically exploited** yet **deprived of their due reward**; plus the demand for **rewriting of history**.
- Remedy: **rights protected**, **contribution valued and properly rewarded**, **dignity restored**, and **restoration of equitable shares**.

## J. Communitarian justice: the critique and the rival rule

- **Kymlicka (2002)**: liberal visions **do not include any independent principle of community**; its value is **not sufficiently recognized in liberal theories of justice**.
- **Isolated self** against **situated self**; one sentence only on common good as **sum total of individual goods** against **one entity**; right/good — Rawls's **first virtue of social institutions** against **the 'right' implies virtue**.
- **MacIntyre**, ***After Virtue* (1981)**: the good is **a complete human life lived at its best**; **cannot characterize the good for man adequately without already having made reference to the virtues**; liberals as **autonomous moral agents** are **committed to moral relativism** and **could not defend any particular view of justice**. Boxes: **moral relativism**, **moral absolutism**. 🔑 ***Whose Justice? - Four boundaries: money out of **politics, health, education** (**money properly rules in the sphere of commodities**); **nepotism** out of office; **profit and loss** out of kinship and love; **family organization** not **patterned after male domination**. Result: **no one decisively outranks anyone else**.
- Criticisms: **no reliable method to compare** reputation, political power, education and health with income and wealth — **poor consolation for a university teacher, writer, artist or scientist**; and no way to **convince the dominant people** — **a strong moral philosophy**, **not equally strong political philosophy**.
