# Power, Authority and Legitimacy — Mains-Focused Live Edition

A fast-preparation edition for GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay. It teaches only what enriches a Mains answer on who really rules, what makes rule rightful, and what the ruled would need in order to count as powerful themselves.

**The one idea that organises everything.** Power is a capacity; authority is that capacity *plus* a belief about its rightfulness. Everything in this topic follows from that single addition. If legitimacy is present, obedience is willing and coercion is a reserve instrument; if legitimacy is absent, coercion is the operating instrument and the regime pays for it continuously. The four rival perspectives — class, elite, gender and group — are then four answers to one further question: where does the capacity actually sit, once we stop reading constitutions and start looking at society. And the last move in the chapter reverses the question altogether by asking what power the *subject* would need.

**Scope.** This is **Topic 15 of the subject's twenty-three topics**, and it remains a standalone session. Political Theory is supplementary conceptual support. The Commission prints no Political Theory paper, and every General Studies question touching these ideas belongs to another subject — Polity, Governance, Social Justice, Indian Society, Ethics or the Essay. This topic owns no General Studies previous-year question and none is manufactured here. Concepts it needs but does not own are named and deferred rather than absorbed: political obligation, the concept of law, the rule of law and punishment to Topic 14; citizenship and the welfare state to Topic 16; the full theory of patriarchy and the sex/gender distinction to Topic 06; state and civil society as such to Topic 10; base and superstructure to Topic 04; and every article, statute and judgment to Polity.

**What is deliberately left out, and why.** The full roster of power-view thinkers that opens the chapter, reduced to the two formulations that actually do work in an answer; the socialism-to-communism staging sequence, which Topic 04 owns; Gramsci's strategic advice to communist movements, which is revolutionary strategy rather than a theory of power; the named American women's organisations and the legislative aftermath of the Women's Liberation Movement; and the medieval-pluralism excursus, kept only as the clause that sets up Tocqueville. Definitions, causal logic, objections and replies, deployment, answer frameworks and required practice are kept intact.

### 1. Why power, and the two formulations worth carrying

The chapter opens by calling power **one of the central concepts of political science**, and by reporting the comparison that makes the claim memorable: the concept of power **holds the same status in the realm of political science as held by the concept of money in the realm of economics** (PDF p.301, printed 282). Two consequences are then claimed. The focus on power **emancipates the study of politics from the status of an appendage to the study of philosophy or history or law**, and it **transcends the realm of formal institutions to focus on the real motives and objectives of human beings which lie behind all political activity and institution-building**.

⚠️ That second clause is the one to carry into an answer, because it is a methodological instruction rather than a definition. It tells you that a question about who governs is not answered by reciting who holds office. The chapter states the same instruction again at the close of the appraisal: we are **no longer satisfied by learning as to 'who rules a country according to its constitution'**; we want to **identify the groups and classes which actually wield power** (PDF p.307, printed 288).

Two formulations are worth carrying verbatim, and the long roster of power-view thinkers that surrounds them is not. **H. Lasswell and A. Kaplan** (*Power and Society*, 1950) defined political science as **the study of the shaping and sharing of power** — a phrase that supplies both halves of most stems, since *shaping* covers agenda and preference while *sharing* covers distribution. And 📝 **Weber's definition of politics**, which the chapter gives as **"Politics is the struggle for power or the influencing of those in power, and embraces the struggle between states as well as between organized groups within the state"**, is printed here **as cited in G.E.G. Catlin**, *Systematic Politics* (1965) — ❌ so it must be attributed that way or the sourcing clause dropped altogether, never presented as a direct Weber citation.

### 2. The appraisal, taught as the argument it is

The chapter's **critical appraisal** is not an appendix; it is the only source-grounded route into a *critically examine* directive on the concept of power itself, and it has a definite structure — one merit pair, three defects, one repair.

The merit is stated in two clauses: the approach's **chief merit** lies in its **ability to identify the motive force operating behind politics**, and it **can be applied to an analysis of domestic as well as international politics** (PDF p.306, printed 287).

The first defect is **imprecision**, and the chapter illustrates it with an example worth reproducing because it does the work of a paragraph in a single line: power **is said to derive from sources ranging from tacitly accepted rules of politeness to the possession of spaceships**, and to manifest itself **in situations ranging from a request that the salt be passed at the dining table to a situation in which states are exchanging all-out thermonuclear blows**. The consequence is methodological rather than stylistic: **this sort of imprecision in the concept of power itself defies any classification or quantification of data for the purpose of political analysis**.

The second defect is **reductiveness**. The approach **seeks to reduce all politics to a struggle for power, and ignores other important purposes for which men have recourse to politics**; political actors **rarely, if ever, struggle for power alone**, and the theory **does not say how much power is desired or what price political actors are willing to pay for it in terms of other values**. The verdict is unusually blunt: **the thoughtless assumption that all political actors constantly seek a maximization of power at any price is obviously false** (PDF pp.306-307, printed 287-288).

The third defect is **over-extension**: **all struggle for power in different social situations cannot be treated as a subject-matter of politics**, since **competition among film-actors to win popular applause** or **between textile-manufacturers to control consumer choices** involves a struggle for power that is **hardly a manifestation of politics**.

Only the third defect is repaired. Politics is **concerned only with a struggle for power to control public affairs, that is to have access to positions of taking decisions for the allocation of public goods, services, opportunities and honours**, and **any deviation from this focus will make the power approach a futile exercise** (PDF p.307, printed 288).

⚠️ The honest summary is therefore a graded one: the repair rescues the approach's *scope* but leaves its *precision* problem intact. Saying so is worth more than either praising the approach or dismissing it.

### 3. The one-line boundary that prevents a category error

The chapter also distinguishes its own approach from the familiar constitutional one. The modern approach **should not be confused with the traditional approach which recognized a threefold division of the 'powers' of a government — legislative, executive and judicial** and described them **according to the formal constitution of a given country**; the modern approach **marks a departure** because it **implies a shift in focus from the formal organization to the informal one** (PDF p.307, printed 288).

❌ This is the single commonest category error in the topic. A separation-of-powers stem is about the *distribution of constitutional functions*; a concentration-of-power stem is about the *location of real capacity*. The words are the same and the questions are not. ➡️ The constitutional doctrine itself belongs to Polity and is not reconstructed here.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** On any stem about influence over policy, open by distinguishing the formal from the informal question in one sentence — who decides under the constitution, and who actually shapes what is decided — and say which one the stem asks. That single move converts a descriptive answer into an analytical one, and it is the chapter's own instruction rather than an imported framework.
- **GS-IV.** The "maximisation of power at any price" sentence is the cleanest available statement of why treating power as a terminal value is an error of judgment and not merely of strategy; it is more useful than any moralising opening because the chapter states it as a factual claim about how actors behave.
- **Essay.** On power, leadership or institutions, the salt-and-thermonuclear contrast gives an essay its calibrating paragraph: a concept stretched across that range needs the writer to say at what scale the essay will operate, and a writer who does so early is already better organised than one who does not.

**Answer framework — the critically-examine opening.** Name the merit the approach claims → state the defect that survives any repair → announce which of the two the answer will test → body. About forty words, and it replaces the definitional throat-clearing that usually occupies the first quarter of a page.

### 1. Why the familiar definitions are judged one-sided

The chapter collects the standard definitions and then rejects their common assumption. **Bertrand Russell** (*Power: A New Social Analysis*, 1938) defined power as **the production of intended effects**. **Robert Dahl** treats power as a kind of influence exercised **when compliance is attained by creating the prospect of severe sanctions for non-compliance**. **H. V. Wiseman** defines it as **the ability to get one's wishes carried out despite opposition**, and **Stephen L. Wasby** as **bringing about of an action by someone against the will or desire of another** (PDF p.302, printed 283).

Then comes the turn that the rest of the lesson depends on. **All these definitions give prominence to that aspect of power which is exercised by a man or a group over an 'unwilling' lot. They are, therefore, one-sided.** The reason is stated immediately: **power, to be effective and stable, often takes the character of 'authority' which also comprehends legitimacy; that is the capacity to secure willing obedience**. And then the sentence that carries the causal arrow: **use of force or coercion or sanctions may be resorted to only when legitimacy fails to work**.

⚠️ Note what has happened. The objection to Russell, Dahl, Wiseman and Wasby is not that they described something that does not exist. It is that they described the *residual* case and treated it as the *standard* case. An answer that reproduces this move has an opening paragraph that no definition dump can match.

### 2. The formula, and the image that makes it examinable

The formula is printed as a heading in the chapter and should be reproduced as it stands: **Authority = Power + Legitimacy**. The components are then unpacked. **Legitimacy of a rule or decision implies that the members of society treat that rule or decision as beneficial to society as well as to themselves. So they willingly tend to abide by it.** **Power alone involves capacity to get a decision obeyed by others against their will**, and **R. M. MacIver** (*The Web of Government*, 1965) supplies the working definition of that capacity — **the capacity in any relationship to command the service or compliance of others** (PDF p.303, printed 284).

The bridge clause is the examinable one: power **may involve use of 'force', but service or compliance of others will best be obtained when they regard the command as 'right', 'good' or 'beneficial'**. The chapter then restates the causal rule a second time — **use of force or coercion or sanctions may be resorted to only when legitimacy fails to work** — and closes with its image: **if we think of power as a naked sword, authority may be envisaged as a sword in its scabbard**; **if power is based on fear or force, legitimacy is based on respect and willing compliance**. Authority is therefore **the most effective instrument of exercising power in the sphere of politics**.

⚠️ The scabbard image is doing analytical work, not decoration. A sheathed sword is still a sword: authority does not abolish the capacity for coercion, it holds it in reserve and thereby reduces how often it must be drawn. That is exactly why the presence of consent does not by itself prove the absence of domination — a point Lesson 7 collects.

### 3. Five words that must not collapse into one

The topic punishes imprecise vocabulary more than it punishes thin content. One clean sentence each:

| Term | One-sentence definition | What it is *not* |
|---|---|---|
| **Power** | The **capacity in any relationship to command the service or compliance of others**, including against their will (PDF p.303) | Not automatically rightful |
| **Force / coercion** | Compliance secured by sanctions or compulsion, resorted to **only when legitimacy fails to work** (PDF pp.302-303) | Not the normal mode of stable rule |
| ⚠️ **Influence** | The capacity to shape conduct by persuasion, prestige or agenda-setting rather than by sanction; the chapter treats Dahl's sanction-based power as **a kind of influence**, so the two overlap rather than exclude | Not a synonym for force |
| **Legitimacy** | The quality by which members treat a rule or decision as **right**, **good** or **beneficial** to society and to themselves, so that they **willingly tend to abide by it** (PDF p.303) | Not the same as legality ➡️ Topic 14 |
| **Authority** | **Power + legitimacy** — the capacity to secure **willing obedience**, and **the most effective instrument of exercising power** in politics | Not a polite word for command |

❌ The trap worth naming once: *legitimacy* and *legality* are different tests. A validly enacted rule can fail to be regarded as right, good or beneficial, and a widely accepted practice can lack legal form. ➡️ The validity question — what makes a law a law — is Topic 14's, and is not reconstructed here.

### 4. Weber's three authorities, with both printed qualifications

**Max Weber (1864-1920)** identified **three types of authority prevalent in the modern state** (PDF p.303, printed 284).

- **Traditional authority** involves **the right to rule as established by tradition, such as hereditary or dynastic rule**.
- **Charismatic authority** **results from exceptional personal characteristics of the political leader, or his magnetic personality**. 📝 The chapter illustrated this with a twentieth-century dictator, naming Hitler. The example is historical only, is kept in the past tense, and ❌ no contemporary leader is named in its place.
- **Legal-rational authority** **emanates from the political office held by an individual, where he is appointed through the prescribed procedure, such as merit-based selection, promotion, election, rotation or nomination, and not from the personal characteristics of the individual holding an office**; it is **attached to an office which automatically extends to the individual holding that office**.

The two qualifications are what turn the typology from a list into an argument, and they are the part most answers omit. First: **Weber recognized that none of these categories existed in pure form**. Second: **legal-rational authority, which is the chief characteristic of bureaucracy, is the outstanding attribute of the modern state**.

⚠️ The first qualification is the examiner's favourite trap, because a question that asks you to classify a case is usually asking whether you will notice that it is mixed. An office-holder may draw on procedure *and* personal appeal *and* inherited prestige simultaneously, and saying which element is doing the work in the case at hand is the analysis. The second qualification tells you where to start when the case is a modern state: procedure first, personality second.

The arrows point inward for a reason. Neither economic nor ideological power is a separate kind of politics; each is a **support-base** that explains why political power holds. The downward arrow from ideology is the chapter's own bridge to the class perspective.

### 1. Political power, and the inputs that make it responsive

The chapter insists on a distinction **between the formal and informal organs** of political power (PDF p.304, printed 285). The formal organs are **legislature, executive and judiciary**, and **the obedience secured by these organs, backed by sanctions, is an important form of political power**. The chapter then specifies what that power concretely does, and the specificity is useful because it stops an answer from treating "the state" as an abstraction: executive and legislature **make laws, policies and decisions regulating the allocation of values in a society**, **impose taxes and use a vast machinery for the collection of taxes**, and **issue licences, permits, and regulate a large variety of citizens' actions**; there is **police to catch hold of offenders, courts to try the offenders and settle disputes, and prisons to punish the convicts**, and **military** for external aggression.

But the analytical point is the next one. **Informal organs of political power** take the form of **political parties in power and in opposition**, and also **a large number of pressure groups, public opinion, popular movements**. Therefore **political power is not the sole prerogative of the formal organs of the state**; those organs **are responsive to the 'inputs' received from the 'environment'**. Three cases are given: in **independent states**, public opinion, popular movements and organised interests **directly influence the decision-making**, and in democracies **the ruling party is bound to seek a fresh mandate from the people at regular intervals**; in **colonies, dependencies and dictatorships**, the struggle for independence and strong popular movements **are sooner or later able to secure not only substantial concessions but even to win their independence**; and **in the international sphere, organized groups of nations do exercise their influence on the 'authoritative allocation of values'**.

⚠️ The third case is the one worth carrying, because it is the chapter's own evidence that the power approach is not state-bound. It also makes a useful opening for any question about collective bargaining among states without importing material that belongs to International Relations.

### 2. Economic power, and the priority-fixing claim

**Economic power is the power emanating from the possession of material things, especially the major means of production and distribution**, and it is **a potent factor behind politics** (PDF p.305, printed 286). The mechanism named is not bribery but *priority-setting*: **big landlords, industrial tycoons and business magnates are able to influence public decisions regarding the fixation of priorities in economic development in a liberal democracy**.

The chapter then lists the routes. Pressure groups of the economically powerful are **stronger, more organized and more vocal**, and the asymmetry is stated as a ranking — chambers of commerce and industry strong, **workers' unions are not as strong**, peasants' unions **less strong**, and **consumers' organizations are the weakest lot**. **The major newspapers are owned by a handfull of big business houses who take full advantage of this medium to promote opinion which suits their interests.** And **big business houses extend a large amount of financial help to political parties, often clandestinely, and to the candidates seeking elections**, whose recipients **play a dual game — they pay lip-service to the interests of the masses but are secretly committed to safeguarding the interests of their financiers**.

⚠️ Two disciplines apply here. First, the ranking of organised strength is the chapter's single most direct anticipation of Lesson 7's pluralism critique: if bargaining strength is ordered in advance, "mutual bargaining" is not a neutral mechanism. Second, 📝 the chapter's own illustrations in this passage are dated book-period examples. ❌ No contemporary Indian institution, party, company or leader is named here, and the Indian register in this edition stays at the level of Gandhi's own verified formulation in Lesson 8. ➡️ Party finance, media regulation and lobbying rules belong to Polity and Governance.

### 3. Ideological power, and the sentence that makes it a mechanism

**Ideological power provides a more subtle base of political power** (PDF p.305, printed 286). Political ideology is **the ideas upheld and promoted by the ruling class in a given society regarding the 'best system of government'**, and the chapter adopts a definition worth reproducing: ideology is **a systematic set of arguments and beliefs used to justify an existing or desired social order**.

The function is then stated as a causal chain, and this is the examinable part. **An outstanding feature of political ideology is that it provides legitimacy to the ruling classes and helps them maintain their stronghold on political power. When people are made to believe that a particular system of government is the best system, they will not be inclined to challenge the authority of the ruling classes. When people have learnt to respect their laws, the need for coercion to secure their obedience would be eliminated or at any rate, minimized.**

⚠️ Set that beside Lesson 2's rule — force is used **only when legitimacy fails** — and the two sentences lock together into the single most useful causal claim in the topic: ideology lowers the frequency of coercion by manufacturing the belief that coercion would be unwarranted. That is why ideology is a *mechanism of rule* and not a decorative superstructure.

Two further features complete the account. Ideology **is always action-oriented**: it **puts forward a 'cause' for which people are prepared not only to fight but to make a lot of sacrifices**, and individuals **are prepared to fight for causes, often realistically hopeless causes, or to undergo ill-treatment and torture in the belief that some political values are superior to others**. And ideology **is often devoid of reason**: it **picks up certain convenient formulae and elevates them to the level of 'absolute truth' by exploiting people's sentiments**.

Then the sentence that opens the next lesson. **Ideological power represents the manipulative power of the dominant class which holds sway on the thinking and emotions of the people. Ideology even creates an illusion of 'consent'.** Under an ideological spell **the people express their approval to the rule of particular persons and policies**; they **are led to believe that they are being governed with their 'consent' while they actually continue to be governed according to the designs of the dominant classes** (PDF p.306, printed 287). **Marx and Engels** (*The German Ideology*, 1846) are cited for the premise — **the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas** — because **the class which owns the means of material production in any society also controls the means of mental production**.

⚠️ The phrase **illusion of 'consent'** is the hinge of the whole topic. It is the reason Lesson 4 needs Gramsci, the reason Lesson 7's third dimension is intelligible, and the reason an answer can say that observed consent is not self-validating evidence without lapsing into conspiracy. ➡️ Ideology as a concept in its own right — its end-of-ideology debate and its typology — belongs to Topic 02 and is not reconstructed here.

### 1. The class answer, stated as a causal claim

The class perspective was **developed by Marx (1818-83) and Engels (1820-95) during the mid of the nineteenth century**, and its thesis is compact: **political power is the product of economic power** (PDF p.308, printed 289). The chapter supplies two images for it — **the tree of political power grows on the roots of economic power; the edifice of political power is raised on the foundation of economic power** — and then locates economic power precisely: it is **vested in the ownership of means of social production**.

The chapter's own boxed definition of that term is worth carrying because it contains the asymmetry in one line. **Means of social production** are the **means by which material production is carried out for the larger society**, including **land, mines, forests, buildings, capital, machinery, organization and labour**; and **when society is divided into classes, all means except labour are owned by the dominant class: labour alone is retained by the dependent class**.

From this follows the historical division into **haves and have-nots** — **masters and slaves** in the ancient society, **lords and serfs** in the medieval, **capitalists and workers** in the modern capitalist society — with **class** as **the organizing category for exercising power in society**. Those who **grab ownership** organise themselves into the **dominant class** and force the rest **to do hard work on the terms dictated by the former**, reducing them to the **dependent class**; the dominant class **resorted to intense exploitation** to strengthen its position, and the dependent class **could also organize itself and launch a struggle**, which produces **class conflict or class struggle**. The *Communist Manifesto* (1848) supplies the headline: **"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."**

### 2. The step most answers skip, and why it is the examinable one

The chapter then asks why class struggle became decisive only under capitalism, and the answer is a communication argument rather than a moral one. **In the ancient slave-owning society the slaves were scattered to distant households with little communication among themselves. In the medieval feudal society, the serfs were similarly scattered to distant agricultural estates. But in the modern capitalist society the conditions of industrial production led to the concentration of workers in big cities with strong communication between themselves.** They became **not only conscious of their strength but also of their common enemy** (PDF pp.308-309, printed 289-290).

⚠️ This is the most transferable paragraph in the lesson, and it has nothing specifically Marxist about its logic. It states a general condition for collective power: shared grievance plus proximity plus communication produces organisation; grievance without the other two produces nothing. Any stem about why a dispersed group fails to convert numbers into influence can be answered on this structure, and Lesson 5's Mosca and Lesson 7's pluralism both depend on the same variable — **organisation**.

➡️ The staging of socialism and communism, the compulsory-work provision of the socialist phase and the claim that in a classless society **the use of power will become redundant** are stated in the chapter but belong to Topic 04, which owns the theory of historical development. One clause is enough here: the class perspective expects the division to be abolished rather than managed, which is exactly why the comparative table in Lesson 7 records its prospects of change as **yes** and its instrument as **socialist revolution**.

### 3. Gramsci, and the question ownership cannot answer

**Antonio Gramsci (1891-1937), an Italian Marxist**, enters the chapter as a correction rather than a supplement. He **admired the Bolshevik Revolution (1917) of Russia as a victory of the will power over economic conditions**, but **warned that this strategy would not be suitable under the conditions prevailing in Western society where the working class had come to accept the existing arrangements** (PDF pp.309-310, printed 290-291).

⚠️ That clause states the problem precisely. If power follows ownership, and ownership is unchanged, why is obedience voluntary? Ownership explains who *can* compel; it does not explain why compulsion is so rarely needed. Gramsci's answer is that **the real source of strength of the ruling classes in the West is manifested in their spiritual and cultural supremacy**, achieved by inculcating **their values system in the mind of their people through the institutions of the civil society**.

The structural move comes next. Conventional Marxist theory held that the **economic mode of production** constitutes the **base**, while **legal and political structure and various expressions of social consciousness including religion, morals, social custom and practices** constitute the **superstructure**, whose character was **determined by the prevailing character of its base** — so the superstructure **was not regarded to deserve an independent analysis**. **Gramsci did not accept this position**: the superstructure of contemporary Western society **had attained some degree of autonomy; hence its analysis was also necessary**.

He then identified **two levels of this superstructure** (PDF p.310, printed 291):

- **(a) Political Society or State**, which **resorts to coercion to maintain its domination**; **the whole organization of government comes within its purview**, and its structures are called **structures of coercion**.
- **(b) Civil Society**, which **resorts to obtaining consent of the citizens to maintain its domination**; this part is **closer to the base and it is relatively autonomous**, and its structures are called **structures of legitimation**. Gramsci **pays special importance to this part**.

The mechanism is named without abstraction: **the institutions of civil society — family, school and church — familiarize the citizens with the rules of behaviour and teach them to show natural respect to the authority of the ruling classes**, so that **even injustice involved in this rule would carry the impression of justice**. The result: the ruling classes **seem to be ruling with the consent of the people**, and **when the power is apparently exercised with the consent of its subject, it is called 'hegemony'**.

### 4. The order of resort, and the one clause that is enough on strategy

The figure's most answer-ready claim is about sequence. **Structures of legitimation within the bourgeois society usually prevent any challenge to its authority. Bourgeois society largely depends on the efficiency of these structures for its stability. It is only when civil society fails to prevent dissent that political society is required to make use of its structures of coercion, including police, courts and prisons** (PDF p.311, printed 292).

⚠️ Set that beside Lesson 2's rule — force is resorted to **only when legitimacy fails** — and the two converge from opposite traditions. One derives the sequence from the concept of authority; the other derives it from the structure of a class society. An answer that notices the convergence is making an argument, not reporting two positions. It also follows that *visible* coercion is a symptom of a legitimation failure, which is the strongest single inference this topic supports.

Gramsci's strategic conclusion is real and is kept to one sentence, because the Mains payload is hegemony rather than revolutionary method: the communist movement should **make a dent in the value system that sustains the capitalist rule** rather than confine itself to overthrowing the capitalist class, since that value system **is likely to persist through the institutions of civil society even under socialist mode of production**. 📝 The chapter also records that Gramsci **was primarily a humanist**, **opposed to any type of tyranny**, who **did not want to use revolution in order to set up a coercive state, but wanted to democratize all institutions** — a line worth one clause because it blocks the standard caricature. ➡️ The theory of base and superstructure belongs to Topic 04; state and civil society as such belong to Topic 10.

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-II.** On civil-society stems the hegemony analysis is decisive, and it must be used with care. It explains why associations situated between the individual and the state are not automatically counterweights to power: the same institutions can transmit the values that make existing arrangements appear natural. That converts an answer about the role of civil society from a list of functions into a question about which function a particular body is performing. ⚠️ The verified GS-II 2025 Q8 stem on civil-society organisations is owned by Governance, and the institutional material is the owner's.
- **GS-IV.** The **impression of justice** clause is the most precise available statement of why moral intuitions are not self-certifying: a person socialised into an arrangement will find it fair, which is why ethical reasoning needs a test that does not consist of asking whether an arrangement feels acceptable.
- **Essay.** The scattered-versus-concentrated argument gives any essay on collective action a non-obvious thesis: numbers are not strength, and what converts one into the other is communication and organisation. The verified Essay 2026 A4 prompt on asymmetry benefits directly.

**Answer framework — the consent paragraph.** State the puzzle (obedience without continuous coercion) → name the two structures and which institutions belong to each → state the order of resort, coercion only when legitimation fails → draw the inference that observed consent is compatible with domination → add the limit, that it is also compatible with justified rule, so consent is a question rather than a verdict. Five sentences, and it is the single most frequently reusable block for GS-II civil-society and legitimacy stems.

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Gramsci simply restates Marx — rule still rests on class ownership." Identify what this misses and state the corrected claim.

### 1. Pareto, and the vocabulary that must be used exactly

**Vilfredo Pareto (1848-1923), an Italian sociologist**, in **The Mind and Society (1915-19)** used **elite** to indicate **a superior social group, i.e. a group of people who show highest ability in their field of activity whatever its nature might be** — **a minority group which took all major decisions in society** (PDF p.312, printed 293). The remainder is **mass of the people, or 'masses'**, characterised by **the lack of qualities of leadership and the fear from responsibility**; they **feel that it would be safe to follow the elite**.

Within the elite he distinguished **governing elite**, **one that wields power for the time being**, from **non-governing elite**, which **constantly endeavours to replace it by showing greater ability and excellence**. The resulting competition **results in what is called 'circulation of elites'**. 📝 The chapter attaches a hard limit immediately: **in any case, masses have no chance of entering the ranks of elites**.

Beyond intelligence and talent, Pareto **also recognizes courage and cunning as the qualities of elite**: **the lions**, distinguished by **courage**, are **more suited to the maintenance of status quo under stable conditions**, while **the foxes**, distinguished by **cunning**, are **adaptive and innovative and cope better during periods of change**.

⚠️ The lions-and-foxes pair earns its place only if used as a *situational* claim rather than a personality label. Its analytical content is that the trait which secures power is relative to conditions, so a style that succeeds in stability can fail in flux — which is also the mechanism by which circulation happens.

### 2. Mosca, and the variable that changes everything

**Gaetano Mosca (1858-1941)**, in **The Ruling Class (1896)**, postulated that **the people are necessarily divided into two groups: the rulers and the ruled**, that **the ruling class controls most of the wealth, power and prestige in society**, and that **the ruled are not competent to replace it**. **Whatever form of government might be adopted, it is always this minority which exercises all power in society**, and the ruling class **tends to invoke moral and legal principles in order to win consent of the ruled** (PDF pp.312-313, printed 293-294).

The decisive difference is the basis. **While Pareto regards intelligence and talent as the outstanding qualities of elite, Mosca's ruling class was distinguished by its capacity of organization.** And an inference follows that candidates frequently invert: **of the two Italian elitists, Mosca is more democratic**, because **he believed that leadership could emerge at all levels including grass-root level**, and **each stratum of society has the potential of producing good organizers for itself**; they **need not look for outsiders for their guidance and control**. Mosca also held that **the dominance of the ruling class was essential to provide for proper organization of the unorganized majority**, and that **a constant competition between the upper and lower strata of society led to the 'circulation of elites'**.

⚠️ Organisation is the hinge of the entire topic, and it is worth saying so once. Lesson 4 explained class consciousness by concentration and communication; Mosca explains minority rule by organisation; Lesson 7's pluralism assumes that groups which organise can bargain. The same variable keeps deciding who holds power, which is why an answer that tracks *organisation* rather than *numbers* or *wealth alone* is usually ahead of the stem.

The chapter also records the normative contrast with class theory in one line: **while class theory held that the division of society into classes was based on exploitation, elite theory believed that the division of society into elite and masses was functional**; the two **compensate each other in sustaining the social organization**, the division **does not give rise to any injustice in society**, and **hence the masses need not rise in revolt**. Social change, if any, **would be confined to the 'circulation of elites'**, in which **an outmoded elite may be replaced by a more dynamic elite, but masses will have no opportunity to come to power**.

### 3. Michels, the printed contradiction, and the graded reply

**Robert Michels (1876-1936), a German sociologist**, in **Political Parties: A Sociological Study of the Oligarchical Tendencies of Modern Democracy (1911)**, propounded the **Iron Law of Oligarchy**, demonstrating that **every organization — whatever its original aims — is eventually reduced to 'oligarchy', that is the rule of the chosen few** (PDF p.313, printed 294). His premise about the governed is blunt: **majority of human beings are apathetic, indolent and slavish**, **permanently incapable of self-government**, so they **have to bank upon their leaders**; **trade unions, political parties and other organizations are the vehicles of such leadership**.

The mechanism is the usable part, because it is institutional rather than psychological. **Any organization may be set up with democratic aims, but as the organization grows in size and complexity, its management is left to the professional experts.** In due course **these experts or leaders become indispensable for the organization**; they **use their manipulative skills, including oratory, persuasion and playing upon sentiments of the people in order to perpetuate themselves in power**; they **become so prominent by virtue of their expertise and experience that it becomes very difficult to replace them at periodical re-election**; and **in exercising their undisputed power they set aside the original aims of the organization** (PDF pp.313-314, printed 294-295). The chapter's boxed definition completes it: **oligarchy** is **a form of organization in which a small group of the chosen few makes all decisions, largely in its own interest**.

📝 Now the sentence that turns three labels into a debate, printed exactly: **this 'iron law of oligarchy' rules out the possibility of 'circulation of elites' as envisaged by Pareto and Mosca. It belies all hopes of democracy in any society or organization. All forms of government are also destined to be reduced to oligarchy.**

🔶 The Basic owner offers a reconciliation — that leaders may turn over while the organisation remains oligarchic, so the two claims are distinct rather than contradictory. ⚠️ That is an analytical gloss by this topic's canonical file and is tagged as inference; ❌ it is not presented as the chapter's own statement, because the chapter says *rules out*. Carry the printed claim as the source position and the reconciliation as a reading, and say which is which.

The reply is also printed, and it supplies the graded verdict most answers lack. **Critics of Michel's iron law of oligarchy point out that this law cannot be uniformly applied to all organizations. Members of some organizations could be more vigilant, vocal and assertive than those of others.** Moreover, **some organizations by their nature could be more democratic than others. For example, political parties may offer more options to their members than trade unions.** And the conclusion is a change of status rather than a refutation: **in any case, Michel's observations could serve as a warning to those who sincerely believe in democratization of institutions.**

⚠️ *Warning* is the examinable word. It converts an exceptionless law into a standing risk with identifiable aggravating conditions — size, complexity, expert indispensability, and weak internal contestability — and those conditions are testable in a particular case. An answer that ends there has a verdict; an answer that ends at "oligarchy is inevitable" has a prophecy.

### 4. The extension, and the claim that outlives class

**Max Weber** was **inspired by this theory** and **came to reject the prevalent definition of democracy as 'government by the people'**, seeking to redefine it as **a competition for political leadership**. In his **essay on 'Socialism' (1918)** he **emphatically pointed to the relative independence of political power**: if socialism were established through a socialist revolution, **it was destined to become the dictatorship of officials rather than the dictatorship of proletariat as envisaged by Marxist theory**, and **the inequality of political power is in no way diminished or eliminated by the abolition of classes** — so **even in the so-called 'classless society' all power was bound to be concentrated in the hands of the few**. A democratic form of government **was preferable because it provided for an open competition for political leadership**, and this model **was adopted and further developed by Joseph Schumpeter** (PDF p.314, printed 295). 📝 Schumpeter is named here and nothing further is attributed to him.

⚠️ The 1918 sentence is the bridge that makes the class-to-elite sequence an argument rather than a change of subject: it asserts that abolishing the economic basis of domination leaves the political inequality intact. Whether that is true is contestable; that it is the strongest available challenge to the class perspective is not.

**C. Wright Mills (1916-62)**, in **The Power Elite (1956)**, **preferred the term 'power elite' to 'ruling class'**. The Marxian ruling class **implied that an economic class would exercise all political power**, whereas the power elite **implied a combination of several groups who exercised all power by virtue of their high status in all important spheres of social life**. Modern American society **was ruled by a set of three loosely interlocking groups who had come to occupy the pivotal positions of power: the captains of industry, military leaders and prominent politicians**. They **share their cultural and psychological orientations** and **often share their social origins**, being **linked by ties of family and friendship**. The sharpest line is about demand: **the power elites do not owe their power to fulfilling social demands. They themselves are capable of creating such demands.** They **occupy the strategic command posts within the social structure**, constitute **a self-conscious class** cooperating to strengthen each other's position, and **project their image of high moral character only with a view to commanding respect in society, but they are scarcely sensitive to their moral or social responsibility** (PDF pp.314-315, printed 295-296).

📝 Mills's account is explicitly about mid-century American society. It is used as a conceptual model, and ❌ no contemporary institution, company or office anywhere is named as an instance of it.

The three branches converge; they do not rank. That is the chapter's own instruction, and it is also what makes the perspective structural: culture supplies the symbols, the household supplies the unpaid labour, and the market supplies the segregation, so removing any one of them leaves the other two operating.

### 1. The premise, stated so that the perspective is not misread

The chapter opens the gender perspective by conceding the biological division and immediately denying that it explains the hierarchy. The division into men and women **was created by nature for sustaining the human race and to enable the human beings to build up an excellent civilization and culture, but it is society which placed men and women in dominant and dependent positions respectively**. Feminists therefore **demand a thorough-going change in this arrangement and wish to liberate women from the undue dominance of men** (PDF p.315, printed 296).

⚠️ That single contrast — nature made the difference, society made the ranking — is the premise an answer must state before anything else, because without it every later claim can be dismissed as a claim about biology. ➡️ The full theory of patriarchy and the sex/gender distinction belong to Topic 06 and are not reconstructed here; this lesson uses only what Ch.13 states about patriarchy *as a structure of power*.

### 2. Origin, definition and sites

**Friedrich Engels**, in **The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884)**, observed that **in the antiquity when the institution of marriage was not invented, lineage of a person could be reckoned only through the female line**, so **women as mothers were treated with a high degree of consideration and respect**; **the transition to monogamy, in due course, where the woman belongs exclusively to one man, implied the erosion of that respect**. In his own words: **"The overthrow of mother right was the world-historic defeat of the female sex. The man seized the reins in the house also, the woman was degraded, enthralled, the slave of the man's lust, a mere instrument for breeding children ... The first effect of the sole rule of the men that was now established is shown in ... the patriarchal family."** (PDF p.315, printed 296).

The definition then widens in three steps. **Literally, patriarchy means 'rule of the father'**; it was **originally used to describe the social organization based on the authority of male heads of households**; and **in the contemporary usage, it has come to mean male domination in general** — **a social system in which men dominate, oppress and exploit women**, comprehending **the various ways in which men exercise power over women**. The sites are named: **the social relations involving reproduction, violence, sexuality (including the use of pornography) and work culture are identified as the parts of patriarchal system** (PDF pp.315-316, printed 296-297).

⚠️ Naming the four sites is what prevents the commonest reduction of this perspective to interpersonal violence. Violence is one site among four, and three of the four are ordinary, legal and unremarkable.

### 3. The three accounts, read together

**Modern theories of patriarchy postulate that there is a fundamental division of interests between most men and most women as a result of social structuring of gender relations.** The chapter then records the range of emphases before giving the three accounts: **some writers focus on biological differences** — **men show their greater physical strength in warfare**, and **women are constrained by nature to perform an elaborate role in the reproductive function**; **others see sexuality as an instrument of control of women by men**; **still others focus on male violence as the basis of this control**; and **some feminists have asserted that men benefit from women's labour, both as unpaid house-work and as poorly paid work in the labour market** (PDF p.316, printed 297).

**Feminist sociologists have broadly given three types of account of patriarchy.**

1. **Cultural and psychoanalytic.** **Those influenced by the work of the French psychoanalysts and structuralists have argued that a society's culture is dominated by the symbols of male origin.** ⚠️ The analytical force of this account is that it needs no agent: no one decides that the symbols should be male, and the effect persists without decision — which makes it the closest thing in this chapter to Lesson 7's third dimension of power.
2. **Household.** **Patriarchy is essentially based on the household in which men dominate women economically, sexually and culturally.** The move that makes it examinable is the reading of the marriage contract: this view **treats marriage contract as a labour contract through which women exchange unpaid domestic services for their upkeep**. ⚠️ The analytical gain is that it brings an institution usually classified as private inside the analysis of power, without any claim about any particular household.
3. **Marxist-feminist.** **Patriarchy and capitalism support each other.** The mechanism is given in two halves: **within the household, men take the benefit of women's unpaid domestic labour, and thus relieve their employer — the capitalist — of the liability to pay for that labour**; and **outside the home, women are segregated to perform some specific jobs for which very low wages are paid**.

The instruction that follows is the chapter's own and must be reproduced: **in fact all these accounts focus on different aspects of women's exploitation in society and they should be read together to draw a full picture of the situation**. ❌ Ranking them, or presenting one as the correct account, contradicts the source.

Each step survives the previous answer. Show that the losers in a visible contest sometimes win and you have answered nothing at step one; show that groups are formally equal and you have answered nothing at step two; show that nobody complains and you have answered nothing at step three. That is why the chain is more valuable than any of its links.

### 1. The pluralist picture, and the anchor that gives it a history

The group perspective is distinguished from the other three at the outset. Where class, elite and gender perspectives hold that the exercise of power **divides the society into two broad categories — dominant and dependent groups**, pluralist theory **does not subscribe to this view**: power **is not concentrated in a single group, but it is dispersed amongst a wide variety of social groups**, which are **largely autonomous and almost independent centres of decision** — **organizations of workers, peasants, traders, industrialists, consumers** (PDF p.317, printed 298). These groups **cannot be classified into dominant and dependent groups**; they **have their share of power in their respective spheres of operation**. Because they are **more or less interdependent**, they **tend to balance each other's power**, and **public decisions are largely the outcome of this equilibrium**.

The chapter adds the definitional clause that matters for any civil-society question: modern pluralist society **encourages** citizens **to form autonomous groups**, and **these groups are not required to compete with the authority of the state**; they **are designed to serve as intermediate bodies between individuals and the state**, seeking **to convey the sentiments of their members to government** and **to influence the process of policy-formation and decision-making** (PDF p.318, printed 299). ⚠️ That clause is doing the heaviest lifting in any answer about associations and the state, because it distinguishes an intermediate body from a rival sovereign.

🔶 The historical anchor is **Alexis de Tocqueville (1805-59)**, whom the chapter reports as observing that **the absence of intermediary groups in France was responsible for the French Revolution (1789)**, whereas **the United States was able to maintain democracy due to the presence of such groups**, and who suggested that **in order to protect democracy these intermediary groups should be kept free from state interference**. 📝 Modern pluralists differ on that last point: they **insist that these groups should be allowed to exercise their influence on government** and that **government should also seek cooperation of these groups in the process of decision-making**. ⚠️ Tocqueville appears in the chapter but in neither canonical owner, so he is cited to the chapter and used as what he is — the premise that makes pluralism a thesis about freedom from arbitrary power rather than a mid-century description of American politics. ➡️ The medieval precursor of plural authority is one clause in the chapter and is not expanded here.

### 2. The evidence base, with every year the chapter gives

**Contemporary pluralist theory appeared in the 1950s**, particularly with **Robert Dahl and Charles Lindblom's Politics, Economics and Welfare (1953)**, **Robert Dahl's A Preface to Democratic Theory (1956)** and **Who Governs? (1961)**. It rested on the assumption that **there was a widespread distribution of political resources, and that different interests prevailed in different political disputes and at different times**. Dahl's model of democracy, **described as 'polyarchy'**, postulated that **society is controlled by a set of competing interest groups, with the government as little more than an honest broker in the middle**. Then **N. Polsby**, in **Community, Power and Political Theory (1963)**, **pointed to an unspoken notion in pluralist research that at bottom nobody dominates** (PDF p.318, printed 299).

⚠️ Polsby's phrase is the chapter's own description of an *assumption*, not of a finding — and the distinction is what the next three paragraphs exploit. A research design that only records contests can only report the contests it records.

### 3. The critique, as one chain

**Step one — what pluralism looks at.** **Critics point out that pluralism focuses only on the decision-making process. It overlooks those issues on which no decision could be taken though some of those issues might be very important.** ⚠️ The logic is a methodological one: if the evidence consists of observed decisions, an issue that is never allowed to become a decision generates no evidence, so the absence of conflict is recorded as consensus. 🔶 The discipline's label for this argument is the **second face of power**, or **non-decision-making**, associated with **Bachrach and Baratz**; the chapter states the argument **without naming them**, so the names are given as the standard label for the chapter's own claim and nothing further is attributed to them.

**Step two — who speaks for the group.** **Moreover, even if different groups bargain on equal footing, some groups may lack internal democracy. As a result, leaders or spokespersons of those groups may play a dominant role.** ⚠️ This is where the topic loops back on itself: the Michels mechanism of Lesson 5 is precisely an account of why internal democracy decays as organisations grow. So the pluralist's own units of analysis may be oligarchies, and equality *between* bargaining units tells you nothing about representation *within* them.

**Step three — what people want.** **Steven Lukes in his Power: A Radical View (1974) has pointed to a 'third-dimension of power'. It implies that powerholders can so manipulate the wants of others that their real interests are suppressed.** ⚠️ This step is the strongest claim and the weakest evidence, and saying so is part of using it well. If preferences themselves can be shaped, then neither observed defeat, nor observed absence from the agenda, nor observed contentment settles the question — which is also the standing objection to the claim, since it is hard to specify what evidence would count against it. The honest deployment is to run step three as a hypothesis and to rest the weight of an answer on step one and step two, which are in principle observable.

📝 The three-step ladder as a numbered device is a teaching reconstruction of the chapter's own sequence of criticisms, which the chapter gives in exactly this order but without numbering.

### 4. The concessions that decide the heaviest deployment

**These points of criticism have led to some modification in the pluralist theory in its later phase of development** (PDF p.319, printed 300).

**Charles Lindblom**, in **Politics and Markets (1977)**, **has conceded that there is a privileged position of business in the market-dominated polyarchies of the West; that they are controlled undemocratically by business and property**.

**Robert Dahl**, in **Dilemmas of Pluralist Democracy (1982)**, **has conceded that pluralism cannot be treated as an open competition between truly equal political forces**, and **in order to reduce the inequalities of these forces Dahl has recommended those policies which would promote redistribution of power in society**.

The chapter's own summation closes the lesson: **in a nutshell, subtle forms of domination within various groups and during their interaction should not be ignored while using group perspective for the analysis of power in society.**

⚠️ Two features make this pair the highest-yield material in the topic. First, the concessions come from the theory's two principal authors, so an answer using them is not opposing pluralism from outside but reporting its own revised position — which is both fairer and harder to rebut. Second, Dahl's remedy is *redistribution of power*, not merely regulation of conduct, which gives any answer about concentrated economic influence a conclusion with a direction.

Read the two halves side by side and note the difference in the bottom line. Arendt's unit is **the group**; Macpherson's is **the person**. They are both "power to" accounts and they are not the same account — which is the commonest error this lesson exists to prevent.

### 1. The reversal, and why it is not merely optimistic

The chapter states the conventional view fairly before leaving it: it is **largely concerned with the status of a dominant person or group (the 'agent' of power) vis-a-vis its dependent person or group (the 'subject' of power)**, in which **the agent treats himself as an end, and his subject as a means to that end**. The distinction between the two types is then named: **'power over' and 'power to'** (PDF p.319, printed 300).

Crucially, the chapter refuses to discard the first. The conventional view **cannot be set aside in any meaningful analysis of power**; the modern view **insists on including in this analysis** the power **of the subject to achieve a goal of his own choice** (PDF p.320, printed 301). And it supplies the practical translation that makes the distinction examinable: **when we insist on 'empowerment' of vulnerable sections**, we **in fact demand to equip them with 'power to' resist their oppressors and gain an opportunity and ability to utilize their capacities for self-development**.

⚠️ That sentence is the reason this lesson matters for GS answers about empowerment. It gives the word two components — resistance and self-development — so that an answer can test a measure against both instead of using "empowerment" as an unexamined good.

### 2. Arendt, and the triangle that supplies the conclusion paragraph

**Hannah Arendt (1906-75)** distinguished **violence** from **power** to reach a constructive view. **When rulers use force to fulfil their design against the wishes of the people, it may be called 'violence'**; **power essentially belongs to the people**. The analysis of power is therefore **not concerned with the question: 'Who rules whom?'** and **has nothing to do with 'command-obedience relationship'**; political institutions are **manifestations and materializations of power** (PDF p.320, printed 301).

In **On Violence (1969)** power is **not the property of an individual**; it **corresponds to the human ability not just to act but to act in concert**. Power relations are **essentially cooperative**; power **belongs to a group and remains in existence only so long as the group holds together**; it is **the quality of individuals acting and speaking together**; and while its outcome **may be retained in the shape of various political institutions**, **power itself cannot be stored or held in possession**.

The distinction from violence follows from that last property. **Power keeps the public realm together; violence threatens its existence.** The chapter inserts a distinction worth carrying: **when people voluntarily behave in such a way that they create congenial conditions for each other, they create the public realm**; **when they are forced to behave in a particular way by an external agency, they are under the state**. **Violence relies on the instruments of its application; hence it can be held in possession.** Two warnings are printed: **"Where genuine power is absent, violence may emerge to fill the gap."** and **"Violence can destroy power; it is utterly incapable of creating it."**

📝 Arendt's use of **authority** must be reported carefully, because the chapter prints two statements that pull in different directions. In her terminology **authority is linked with command-obedience relationship; it gives rise to a hierarchical order. It is based on violence; hence it corresponds to the sphere of the state**, while **power belongs to the people**, creates **an egalitarian order**, is **based on consensus and persuasion** and **corresponds to the public realm**. Yet the chapter also states that **only power but never violence can create legitimate authority** (PDF pp.320-321, printed 301-302). ⚠️ The consistent reading is that *authority* in Arendt's vocabulary names hierarchical command, while *legitimate* authority is a distinct status that only concerted action can confer — but the tension is in the source and is reported rather than smoothed over.

⚠️ The analytical pay-off is a claim no other lesson supplies: an increase in coercive capacity is not an increase in power, and may be evidence of its loss. Set beside Lesson 2's rule that force is used **only when legitimacy fails**, and Lesson 4's order of resort in which coercion follows the failure of legitimation, Arendt completes a three-source convergence. That convergence is the qualified conclusion for any coercion-versus-consent stem.

### 3. Macpherson, and three conditions that replace a slogan

**C. B. Macpherson (1911-87)**, in **Democratic Theory — Essays in Retrieval (1973)**, distinguishes **two components of power**. **Extractive power represents the familiar concept of power, viz. a person's ability to use others' capacities to serve his own purpose or 'power over' others**; it **implies the ability to extract benefits from others**. The chapter adds a historical observation that is also a defence: the Western tradition **from Machiavelli (1469-1527) to James Mill (1773-1836) has broadly focused on this concept of power**, and **this attitude was not a product of intellectual carelessness but it was a factual description of power relations in a market society** (PDF pp.321-322, printed 302-303).

**Developmental power** is **a person's ability to use his own capacities for the fulfilment of his self-appointed goals**. Its use of extractive power's opposite number is concrete: owners of land and capital **can buy and use the manual and intellectual capacities of others for their own benefit**, and **the workers and ordinary people who do not own land and capital or other means of production would be benefited only if they win developmental power for themselves**.

The capacities are enumerated, and the list is worth carrying because it refuses to reduce development to income: **capacity for rational understanding, for moral judgment and action, for aesthetic or artistic activity, for love and friendship and, of course, the capacity for materially productive labour**. The concept **treats a person as doer and creator** and **requires that he should not face any impediments or constraints while using his human capacities**.

The **three impediments to the maximization of a person's developmental power** are **(a) lack of adequate means of life; (b) lack of access to the means of labour; and (c) lack of protection against invasion by others**, and the chapter notes that the scarcity problems are **technological and material as well as cultural and ideological**. Macpherson **is quite convinced that these problems cannot be resolved within the framework of a market society based on capitalist social relations**, and suggests **a new system which would combine the protection of civil liberties with a socialist mode of production**, so that the individual can **achieve his 'creative freedom'**.

⚠️ Two notes on use. First, the three impediments convert "empowerment" from a slogan into three testable conditions, and any proposal can be scored against them: does it supply means of life, access to the means of labour, or protection against invasion? Second, the prescription is a position in a contested debate and should be presented as Macpherson's conclusion rather than as the chapter's verdict; the impediments survive disagreement with the prescription, which is what makes them the more useful half.

🔶 ⚠️ **The distinction that prevents the commonest error in this lesson.** Arendt's power is **collective and relational** — it exists while a group acts in concert and vanishes when the group disperses. Macpherson's developmental power is **individual** — a person's capacity to use his own powers for self-chosen ends. They are allies against "power over" and they have different units of analysis, so an answer that treats them as two statements of one idea loses the only comparison that is available here.

### 4. Gandhi, and the one verified Indian illustration in the chapter

**Mahatma Gandhi (1869-1948)** enters in Macpherson's company, and his **insistence on 'power to the people'** is reported through a single passage. When **Swaraj (independence + self-government)** was set as the goal of the independence movement, he wrote in **Young India (1925)**:

> **Real Swaraj will come not by the acquisition of authority by a few but by the acquisition of the capacity by all to resist authority when it is abused. In other words, Swaraj is to be obtained by educating the masses to a sense of their capacity to regulate and control authority.**

The chapter's gloss is the analytical part: **here Gandhi sought to define power from the point of view of the ruled**. It implies that **the rulers should never gain or exercise absolute power over the ruled**; if the ruled felt their rulers were **becoming too powerful**, they would **use their moral power to challenge the authority of the rulers** — standing **up in protest**, launching **social movements** and exerting **moral pressure** — and the power of the ruled would be **akin to performing the 'Socratic function of speaking truth to power'** (PDF p.323, printed 304).

⚠️ Two features make the formulation unusually useful. It defines freedom as a *distributed capacity* rather than a transfer of office, so it is immune to the objection that independence merely replaces one set of rulers with another. And the capacity it names is **to resist authority when it is abused** — a conditional that presupposes legitimate authority rather than rejecting authority as such, which is why it can sit beside Lesson 2's formula without contradiction. ➡️ The grounds of obedience and the discipline of civil disobedience are Topic 14's and are not reconstructed here.

### Concept check

**Question:** Distinguish Arendt's and Macpherson's accounts of "power to", and state what each contributes that the other cannot.

**Model answer:** They differ in their unit of analysis. For **Arendt**, power is **not the property of an individual**; it **corresponds to the human ability not just to act but to act in concert**, belongs to **a group** and **remains in existence only so long as the group holds together**, and unlike violence it **cannot be stored or held in possession** because violence **relies on the instruments of its application**. For **Macpherson**, **developmental power** is **a person's ability to use his own capacities for the fulfilment of his self-appointed goals** — **rational understanding**, **moral judgment and action**, **aesthetic or artistic activity**, **love and friendship**, **materially productive labour** — as against **extractive power**, **a person's ability to use others' capacities to serve his own purpose**. Each contributes something the other cannot. Arendt supplies the limiting principle on coercion: **violence can destroy power; it is utterly incapable of creating it**, and **only power but never violence can create legitimate authority**, so coercive capacity is not a measure of power at all. Macpherson supplies the conditions of remedy: the **three impediments** — **lack of adequate means of life**, **lack of access to the means of labour**, **lack of protection against invasion by others** — convert empowerment into three testable requirements. ⚠️ Merging them loses both, because a collective account cannot specify individual conditions and an individual account cannot explain why concerted action is the only thing that confers legitimate authority.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read Arendt as claiming that states are powerless or that institutions do not matter. She treats political institutions as **manifestations and materializations of power** — the outcome of concerted action, which persists while the power that produced it may not.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Empowerment means more than protection from power." Comment with reference to the concept of developmental power. *(Comment — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The proposition is sound and the chapter supplies its content. When we insist on **empowerment of vulnerable sections**, we demand to equip them **to resist their oppressors** *and* to **utilize their capacities for self-development** — two components, of which protection is only the first. Macpherson's **developmental power** supplies the second: **a person's ability to use his own capacities for the fulfilment of his self-appointed goals**, where the capacities include **rational understanding**, **moral judgment and action**, **aesthetic or artistic activity**, **love and friendship** and **materially productive labour**, and the person is treated as **doer and creator**. The three impediments make this testable — **lack of adequate means of life**, **lack of access to the means of labour**, **lack of protection against invasion by others** — and only the third is protective. ⚠️ Qualification: protection remains necessary, since a capacity that can be invaded at will is not secured by being possessed.

### 2. The apparatus transferred, concept by concept

**Ideological power becomes self-policing.** Lesson 3 established that ideology reduces the need for coercion by producing the belief that the order is acceptable. The mechanism associated with Foucault's discussion of panopticism works on a different variable: because the watched cannot verify whether observation is occurring at any moment, the *possibility* of being watched is internalised and conduct is adjusted in advance. ⚠️ Power becomes cheap, continuous and self-executing — it no longer requires an act of coercion for each instance of compliance. ❌ The caveat is non-negotiable and belongs in any answer that uses this: the mechanism is borrowed by analogy, since contemporary data collection is decentralised, commercial and often invisible rather than architecturally central, and Foucault's own view of power as diffuse and productive sits awkwardly with this chapter's question about where power is located.

**Economic power acquires a new input.** Lesson 3's economic power rested on **possession of material things, especially the major means of production and distribution**. Zuboff's named position is that behavioural data has become a commercial input in its own right, and that the resulting arrangement constitutes a distinctive form of private power. ⚠️ Treat it as a named position in a live scholarly debate rather than as settled fact.

**The three-step chain transfers intact.** Lesson 7's first step said pluralism **overlooks those issues on which no decision could be taken**; in this setting, an item that is never surfaced by a ranking or a default setting is functionally never on the agenda, and no one needed to decide to exclude it — which makes agenda power harder to detect, not weaker. The second step — groups that **lack internal democracy**, so that **leaders or spokespersons** dominate — applies to any association whose online presence is managed by a few. The third step, Lukes's claim that wants can be manipulated **so that their real interests are suppressed**, transfers through Pariser's vocabulary of a narrowed informational environment. ⚠️ Discipline: that third claim is the strongest and the least evidenced, so state it as a contested hypothesis — the honest formulation is that sustained personalisation **can** narrow the environment in which preferences form, and that measuring whether it does is itself disputed.

**Macpherson's contrast decides the normative question.** Data-based power is **extractive** where it converts a person's activity into another's capacity, and **developmental** where it genuinely expands the person's own capacities — information, services, association. The same technology can do either; the analytical question is which, and for whom. ⚠️ This is the move that keeps an answer from being either technophobic or promotional.

### 3. The two transferable moves

**Surveillance asymmetry.** Surveillance is rarely reciprocal: the watched cannot watch back, cannot see the criteria applied to them, and often cannot know that observation occurred. ⚠️ The asymmetry rather than the observation is what converts monitoring into a power relation, and three consequences follow. Anticipatory self-restraint — a person who believes they may be observed adjusts what they say, read and join. Loss of contestability — where criteria are opaque, the affected person cannot even formulate the objection, let alone press it. And aggregation — individually trivial observations combine into a comprehensive profile, so the defence that each item is harmless does not settle the question about the whole.

**Legitimacy-as-accountability.** Lesson 2's formula is the test. Digital power routinely satisfies the first term, **power**, and struggles with the second. Three questions carry it: on what basis is compliance secured — coercion, habit, convenience, or genuine acceptance; what would make it rightful, and is that condition met; and to whom is it answerable, by what route? ⚠️ The consent problem can be stated precisely without exaggeration: consent to terms that are unread, non-negotiable and a practical precondition of participation is formally present and substantively thin. And the accountability gap is structural — elections, legislative oversight, judicial review and a free press are all addressed to **public** power, so each route weakens where the power is private, contractual and transnational.

### 5. The objections worth conceding

- **Voluntariness.** These relationships are entered voluntarily and can be exited, so calling them power inflates the concept until it covers ordinary commerce. ⚠️ Reply, on this topic's own standard: power is the **capacity in any relationship to command the service or compliance of others**, and it does not require coercion; where a service is a practical precondition of work, speech or access, exit is nominal rather than real. ⚠️ Residual weakness to concede: how costly exit must be before "voluntary" fails is a matter of degree, and the objection retains force for genuinely optional services.
- **Unfalsifiability.** The preference-shaping claim is untestable, since any endorsement can be attributed to manufactured preferences. ⚠️ Reply: do not assert false consciousness; examine the detectable second-step mechanisms instead — what was never surfaced, what defaults were set, what was excluded from the choice architecture — which are in principle observable. ⚠️ Residual weakness: the third step stays the least evidenced rung.
- **State-centrism.** Legitimacy language was built for public power and demanding it of private firms confuses two categories. ⚠️ Reply from inside the tradition this edition already teaches: pluralism treats the state as one association among many, and its own authors conceded **a privileged position of business**. The demand is not that firms be states, but that concentrated capacity to affect basic interests attract some answerability. ⚠️ Residual weakness: *which* mechanism suits private power is an institutional question this conceptual topic leaves open. ➡️ Governance and Polity own it.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Four checks: three consolidate a block, and the fourth spans the whole arc. Each model is held within 250 words.

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 to 3

**Question:** Build the chain from the decision to make power the central concept to the claim that ideology creates an illusion of consent, naming each step and the clause that carries it.

**Model answer:** One: power is made central because the focus **transcends the realm of formal institutions to focus on the real motives and objectives** behind politics — hence the comparison with **the concept of money in the realm of economics**. Two: the approach is then bounded by its own defects, since it **lacks precision**, stretching from **a request that the salt be passed at the dining table** to **all-out thermonuclear blows**, and therefore **defies any classification or quantification of data**; and the assumption of **maximization of power at any price is obviously false**. Three: the repair restricts politics to a struggle for power **to control public affairs** — the **allocation of public goods, services, opportunities and honours**. Four: definitions that describe power over **an 'unwilling' lot** are **one-sided**, because power **to be effective and stable** takes the character of **authority**, and **authority = power + legitimacy**. Five: the causal rule follows — **use of force or coercion or sanctions may be resorted to only when legitimacy fails to work**. Six: analysis cannot stop at political power, because economic and ideological forms are its **support-bases**; ownership fixes **priorities in economic development**. Seven: ideology, **a systematic set of arguments and beliefs used to justify an existing or desired social order**, **provides legitimacy to the ruling classes**, so that once people **have learnt to respect their laws** the need for coercion is **minimized**. Eight: therefore ideology **creates an illusion of 'consent'** — the hinge the next three lessons exploit.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the illusion-of-consent claim as a conspiracy theory. The mechanism named is socialisation and justification, not deception by an identified agent, which is why the same sentence can be used without alleging that anyone intended the result.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 4 and 5

**Question:** Class and elite theory both say a minority rules. State precisely where they disagree, and what each needed to add once its first claim was made.

**Model answer:** They disagree on three things the chapter states together: elite theory treats the dominant/dependent division as **somewhat natural**, grounds it in **competence and aptitude (and not the emergence of private property)**, and **sees little prospects of a thorough-going change**, where class theory grounds it in **ownership of means of social production** and expects abolition. Each then needed an addition. Class theory needed an account of obedience, because Gramsci observed that in the West **the working class had come to accept the existing arrangements**; his answer gave the superstructure **some degree of autonomy** and split it into **Political Society** with its **structures of coercion** and **Civil Society** with its **structures of legitimation** — family, school and church — so that **even injustice involved in this rule would carry the impression of justice**, and coercion is used **only when civil society fails to prevent dissent**. Elite theory needed a limit, because Michels's claim that **every organization — whatever its original aims — is eventually reduced to 'oligarchy'** **rules out the possibility of 'circulation of elites' as envisaged by Pareto and Mosca** and **belies all hopes of democracy**; the printed reply narrows it, since the law **cannot be uniformly applied to all organizations** and the observations **could serve as a warning**. ⚠️ Weber's 1918 extension then connects the two: **inequality of political power is in no way diminished or eliminated by the abolition of classes**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the two perspectives as alternative labels for the same observation. They disagree about the basis, the justice and the reversibility of minority rule, and an answer that notices only the shared conclusion has reported the least informative part of both.

### Cumulative check C — Lessons 6 and 7

**Question:** Gender and group perspectives give opposite answers about whether society divides into dominant and dependent categories. Show how each defends its answer and where each is vulnerable.

**Model answer:** The gender perspective affirms the division. It concedes that the male/female difference **was created by nature** but insists **it is society which placed men and women in dominant and dependent positions**, producing **a fundamental division of interests ... as a result of social structuring of gender relations**, operating through **reproduction, violence, sexuality** and **work culture**, and explained by three accounts that **should be read together** — male-origin symbols, the **household** in which **marriage contract** is read as **a labour contract**, and the Marxist-feminist claim that **patriarchy and capitalism support each other**. Its evidence of scale is the **United Nations Report (1980)** quartet, and ⚠️ its vulnerability is that its own chapter's comparative table prints the basis of the division as **biological differences**, which the running text does not support. The group perspective denies the division: power is **dispersed amongst a wide variety of social groups** which **tend to balance each other's power**, so public decisions are **largely the outcome of this equilibrium** and groups are **intermediate bodies between individuals and the state**. ⚠️ Its vulnerability is threefold and sequential — it **focuses only on the decision-making process**, **some groups may lack internal democracy**, and Lukes's **third-dimension** holds that wants can be manipulated **so that their real interests are suppressed** — and the decisive concessions are its own authors': a **privileged position of business**, and pluralism as not **an open competition between truly equal political forces**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use the gender perspective's table cell and the group perspective's equilibrium claim as if both were settled source positions. Each is qualified elsewhere in the same chapter, and an answer that reports the qualification is reporting the source more accurately, not departing from it.

### Cumulative check D — the whole arc, Lessons 1 to 9

**Question:** In one connected argument, move from the concept of power to a graded verdict on when an exercise of power deserves to be called authority, marking where each lesson does work.

**Model answer:** Power is made central because it reaches **the real motives and objectives** behind politics, but **lacks precision** and must be restricted to power **to control public affairs** (L1). Capacity alone is **one-sided**: **authority = power + legitimacy**, and **force ... may be resorted to only when legitimacy fails to work**, with Weber's types never existing **in pure form** (L2). Of the three support-bases the ideological **provides legitimacy to the ruling classes** and **creates an illusion of 'consent'** (L3). Hegemony gives the mechanism: **structures of legitimation** make **even injustice** carry **the impression of justice**, and coercion follows **only when civil society fails to prevent dissent** (L4). Elite theory answers that minority rule survives redistribution: **inequality of political power is in no way diminished ... by the abolition of classes**, though the iron law is downgraded to a **warning** (L5). Gender locates the hierarchy in symbols, household and labour market, **read together** (L6). Pluralism denies any dominant group and collapses onto its authors' concessions — **privileged position of business**; not **an open competition between truly equal political forces** (L7). The reversal supplies the test: **violence ... is utterly incapable of creating** power, and empowerment means **capacity by all to resist authority when it is abused** (L8). ⚠️ Verdict: an exercise is authority where the belief in its rightfulness is not merely produced by the arrangement it justifies, where excluded issues can still be raised, and where the subject keeps capacity to contest — a test Lesson 9's site makes harder, not different.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the chain become a tour of perspectives. Each step is load-bearing: remove the illusion-of-consent claim and hegemony has no object; remove the three-step critique and consent becomes self-validating; remove the reversal and the whole topic ends with a description of domination and no standard by which to judge it.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ Original practice prompts written for this edition. They are **not** previous-year questions and must not be cited as such.

**Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish power from authority, and explain why the distinction matters for the stability of rule. *(Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Power is the **capacity in any relationship to command the service or compliance of others**, including **against their will**. Authority is **power + legitimacy**, where legitimacy means members **treat that rule or decision as beneficial to society as well as to themselves**, so they **willingly tend to abide by it**. The distinction is causal, not decorative. Compliance **will best be obtained when they regard the command as 'right', 'good' or 'beneficial'**, and **force or coercion or sanctions may be resorted to only when legitimacy fails to work** — so the coercion a regime must apply indexes the legitimacy it lacks. Hence authority is **the most effective instrument of exercising power in the sphere of politics**: power is **a naked sword**, authority **a sword in its scabbard**. ⚠️ The scabbard still contains a sword: authority holds coercive capacity in reserve rather than abolishing it.

**Scoring note:** marks concentrate in the causal direction, the efficiency inference drawn from it, and the closing qualification, which stops the answer from implying that legitimate rule has no coercive capacity at all.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** Explain why Gramsci's concept of hegemony is treated as a theory of power rather than a theory of culture. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Because it answers a question ownership cannot. Gramsci warned that revolutionary strategy was unsuited to the West **where the working class had come to accept the existing arrangements**; ownership cannot explain acceptance. His revision grants the superstructure **some degree of autonomy** and splits it: **Political Society or State**, which **resorts to coercion**, holding the **structures of coercion**; and **Civil Society**, which **resorts to obtaining consent**, holding the **structures of legitimation** — **family, school and church** — which teach citizens **to show natural respect to the authority of the ruling classes** so that **even injustice involved in this rule would carry the impression of justice**. The output: **when the power is apparently exercised with the consent of its subject, it is called 'hegemony'**. ⚠️ The sequencing claim completes it: coercion is used **only when civil society fails to prevent dissent**, so culture is an instrument of domination, not a backdrop.

**Scoring note:** an **Explain** directive forbids a survey of Gramsci. Each element is given a function, and the final sentence states the sequencing claim that makes the concept operational.

**Practice question 3 [15]:** "The pluralist account of power is not refuted by inequality; it is refuted by what it cannot observe." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Criterion: does the proposition identify the decisive objection, or a sharper version of a familiar one? Pluralism holds that power is **dispersed amongst a wide variety of social groups**, **largely autonomous and almost independent centres of decision**, which **tend to balance each other's power**, with government in Dahl's **polyarchy** as **little more than an honest broker in the middle** and Polsby's **unspoken notion** that **at bottom nobody dominates**. An objection from inequality alone is answerable within this frame, because different interests **prevailed in different political disputes and at different times**, so unequal resources need not produce consistent dominance. The observational objection is not. Pluralism **focuses only on the decision-making process** and **overlooks those issues on which no decision could be taken though some of those issues might be very important** — so the evidence base excludes what would disconfirm it. The second step compounds this: **some groups may lack internal democracy** and **leaders or spokespersons** may dominate, so observed units may not represent members. Lukes's **third-dimension** pushes furthest: powerholders **can so manipulate the wants of others that their real interests are suppressed**. ⚠️ Two qualifications are owed. The third step is least evidenced and should be carried as a hypothesis. And pluralism was revised rather than abandoned by its authors — Lindblom conceding **a privileged position of business**, Dahl that it is not **an open competition between truly equal political forces**, with **redistribution of power** as remedy. ⚠️ Verdict: the proposition holds decisively for step one, conditionally for step three.

**Scoring note:** the directive requires a criterion before a verdict. The answer states the strongest pluralist position first, distinguishes an answerable objection from an unanswerable one, and grades the verdict across the three steps instead of treating them as one.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** Examine the claim that elite theory explains the persistence of minority rule better than class theory, but justifies it less well. *(Examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Both halves are defensible, resting on different parts of the same chapter. On persistence, elite theory reaches wider. Its division is **somewhat natural** and grounded in **competence and aptitude (and not the emergence of private property)**, so it survives changes of ownership; Mosca grounds rule in **capacity of organization**, the **organised minority** over the **unorganised majority**; Michels gives it an engine, since **size and complexity** hand management to **professional experts** who become **indispensable** and **set aside the original aims**; and Weber's 1918 extension states the consequence — **the inequality of political power is in no way diminished or eliminated by the abolition of classes**, so even a **classless society** concentrates power **in the hands of the few**. Class theory cannot match that, since its mechanism is **ownership of means of social production** and its expectation is abolition. On justification, elite theory is weakest where it is most confident. It treats the division as **functional**, holding that the two **compensate each other in sustaining the social organization**, that it **does not give rise to any injustice in society**, and therefore that **the masses need not rise in revolt** — a normative conclusion drawn from a descriptive regularity, sealed by Pareto's claim that **masses have no chance of entering the ranks of elites**. ⚠️ The chapter's own correction limits the inevitability: the iron law **cannot be uniformly applied**, some organisations being **more democratic than others**, so the observations **serve as a warning**. ⚠️ Verdict: superior as diagnosis, weaker as justification.

**Scoring note:** the split between explanatory reach and normative weakness is the answer's architecture. Marks concentrate in Weber's 1918 sentence, in the functional-division clause used as evidence of the normative weakness, and in the downgrade of the iron law.

**Practice question 5 [20]:** "Consent is the strongest evidence of legitimacy and the weakest evidence of the absence of domination." Critically examine with reference to the theories of power. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Criterion: does observed consent establish rightfulness, and if not, what would? The first half is well founded. Legitimacy means members **treat that rule or decision as beneficial to society as well as to themselves**, so they **willingly tend to abide by it**; **authority = power + legitimacy**; compliance **will best be obtained** where the command is regarded as **right**, **good** or **beneficial**; and **force ... may be resorted to only when legitimacy fails to work**. Consent is therefore what legitimacy consists in, and its presence is the relevant evidence. The second half is equally well founded, on three sources. Ideology **provides legitimacy to the ruling classes** and **creates an illusion of 'consent'**, so people **are led to believe that they are being governed with their 'consent'** while governed **according to the designs of the dominant classes**. Hegemony supplies the institutional mechanism: **structures of legitimation** make **even injustice** carry **the impression of justice**. The pluralism critique removes the remaining inference, since **issues on which no decision could be taken** are invisible and Lukes holds that wants may be manipulated **so that their real interests are suppressed**. ⚠️ Two qualifications prevent collapse into scepticism: not every consent is manufactured, and the manipulation claim is least evidenced. ⚠️ Verdict: consent is necessary and non-sufficient. The usable test is procedural — whether excluded issues can be raised, whether spokespersons are accountable, and whether there remains **the capacity by all to resist authority when it is abused**.

**Scoring note:** the answer converts a paradox into a necessary-but-insufficient finding and then supplies a test, which is what a 20-mark *critically examine* rewards. The two qualifications are load-bearing, not decorative.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** "A theory of power that cannot say what the powerless need is incomplete." Discuss with reference to the shift from 'power over' to 'power to'. *(Discuss — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition states the chapter's reason for its final move. The conventional view concerns the **agent** and the **subject** of power, where **the agent treats himself as an end, and his subject as a means to that end**; the chapter then asks what follows if we **equip the subject with some power** — whether he needs **retaliation**, freedom **from subjection**, or power **to improve his condition and secure his independent development**. That view **cannot be set aside in any meaningful analysis of power**: the complaint is incompleteness, not error. Three answers follow. Arendt holds that power is **not the property of an individual** but **the human ability not just to act but to act in concert**, lasting **only so long as the group holds together**; it **cannot be stored**, while violence can, so **violence can destroy power; it is utterly incapable of creating it**, and **only power ... creates legitimate authority**. Macpherson converts it into conditions: **developmental power** is the ability **to use his own capacities for the fulfilment of his self-appointed goals**, blocked by want of **adequate means of life**, **access to the means of labour** and **protection against invasion by others**. Gandhi states it from the ruled: **Real Swaraj will come not by the acquisition of authority by a few but by the acquisition of the capacity by all to resist authority when it is abused**. ⚠️ Qualification: units differ — group for Arendt, person for Macpherson — and Macpherson's prescription is his conclusion, not an agreed finding.

**Scoring note:** the directive is *discuss*, so the answer presents three positions and relates them rather than adjudicating between them; the unit-of-analysis qualification is what prevents the standard conflation and earns the top band.

## A. The concept, and the appraisal that bounds it

- Power **holds the same status in the realm of political science as held by the concept of money in the realm of economics**; the focus **emancipates** politics from being an **appendage** to philosophy, history or law and **transcends the realm of formal institutions** to reach the **real motives and objectives** behind political activity (PDF p.301, printed 282).
- **Lasswell and Kaplan** (*Power and Society*, 1950): political science is **the study of the shaping and sharing of power**.
- 📝 Weber's definition of politics — **the struggle for power or the influencing of those in power** — is printed **as cited in G.E.G. Catlin**, *Systematic Politics* (1965).
- Merits: identifies **the motive force operating behind politics**; applies to **domestic as well as international** politics (PDF p.306).
- Defects: **lacks precision** — from **a request that the salt be passed at the dining table** to **all-out thermonuclear blows** — and therefore **defies any classification or quantification of data**; **reduces all politics to a struggle for power**, where actors **rarely, if ever, struggle for power alone** and **maximization of power at any price is obviously false**; and over-extends, since film-actors and textile-manufacturers compete for power without doing politics (PDF pp.306-307).
- The repair: politics is a struggle for power **to control public affairs** — access to positions deciding **the allocation of public goods, services, opportunities and honours**; **any deviation from this focus will make the power approach a futile exercise**.
- ⚠️ Only over-extension is repaired; imprecision and unquantifiability survive.
- The boundary: the **threefold division of the 'powers' of a government** is the **traditional approach**; this chapter's is the shift **from the formal organization to the informal one** — no longer **who rules a country according to its constitution** but which **groups and classes ... actually wield power** (PDF p.307). ➡️ Separation of powers is Polity's.

## B. Authority, legitimacy, and the direction of the arrow

- Definitions of power as compliance secured over **an 'unwilling' lot** are **one-sided**; power **to be effective and stable** takes the character of **authority**, which **comprehends legitimacy; that is the capacity to secure willing obedience** (PDF p.302).
- **MacIver** (*The Web of Government*, 1965): power is **the capacity in any relationship to command the service or compliance of others**.
- The printed formula: **Authority = Power + Legitimacy**. Legitimacy = members **treat that rule or decision as beneficial to society as well as to themselves**, so they **willingly tend to abide by it** (PDF p.303).
- **Service or compliance of others will best be obtained when they regard the command as 'right', 'good' or 'beneficial'.**
- The causal rule, printed twice: **use of force or coercion or sanctions may be resorted to only when legitimacy fails to work**.
- The image: power as **a naked sword**, authority as **a sword in its scabbard**; power rests on **fear or force**, legitimacy on **respect and willing compliance**. Authority is **the most effective instrument of exercising power in the sphere of politics**.
- ❌ Legitimacy is not legality. ➡️ Legal validity, rule of law and punishment are Topic 14's.

## C. Weber's typology, with both qualifications

- **Traditional** — **the right to rule as established by tradition, such as hereditary or dynastic rule**.
- **Charismatic** — **exceptional personal characteristics of the political leader, or his magnetic personality**. 📝 The chapter's illustration is historical and kept in the past tense.
- **Legal-rational** — **emanates from the political office**, entered **through the prescribed procedure, such as merit-based selection, promotion, election, rotation or nomination**; attached **to an office which automatically extends to the individual holding that office**.
- Qualification 1: **Weber recognized that none of these categories existed in pure form.**
- Qualification 2: legal-rational authority is **the chief characteristic of bureaucracy** and **the outstanding attribute of the modern state**.

## D. The three support-bases

- Analysis **cannot be confined to the realm of 'political power'**; economic and ideological forms are its **support-bases** (PDF p.304).
- **Political power** — **formal organs**: legislature, executive, judiciary; obedience **backed by sanctions**; laws, policies, taxes, **licences, permits**, police, courts, prisons, military. **Informal organs**: **political parties in power and in opposition**, pressure groups, **public opinion**, popular movements. Formal organs are **responsive to the 'inputs' received from the 'environment'**.
- Three cases: democracies seek **a fresh mandate ... at regular intervals**; colonies and dictatorships win **substantial concessions** or independence; **organized groups of nations** influence the **authoritative allocation of values**.
- **Economic power** — from **possession of material things, especially the major means of production and distribution**; owners influence **the fixation of priorities in economic development in a liberal democracy** (PDF p.305).
- Four routes: pressure groups **stronger, more organized and more vocal**; the organised-strength ranking from chambers of commerce down to **consumers' organizations** as **the weakest lot**; **major newspapers ... owned by a handfull of big business houses**; clandestine finance to parties and candidates, whose recipients **play a dual game**.
- 📝 The chapter's India-specific illustrations here are dated book-period examples. ❌ No contemporary institution, party, company or leader is named anywhere in this edition.

## E. Ideology, and the illusion of consent

- **Ideology** = **a systematic set of arguments and beliefs used to justify an existing or desired social order** (PDF p.305).
- Function: it **provides legitimacy to the ruling classes**; once people **are made to believe that a particular system of government is the best system, they will not be inclined to challenge the authority of the ruling classes**; and once they **have learnt to respect their laws, the need for coercion ... would be eliminated or at any rate, minimized**.
- Two features: ideology is **always action-oriented**, putting forward a **cause** for which people fight **often realistically hopeless causes**; and it is **often devoid of reason**, elevating **convenient formulae** to **absolute truth** by **exploiting people's sentiments**.
- The hinge: ideological power is the **manipulative power of the dominant class**; ideology **even creates an illusion of 'consent'**, so people **are led to believe that they are being governed with their 'consent'** while governed **according to the designs of the dominant classes** (PDF p.306).
- **Marx and Engels** (*The German Ideology*, 1846): **the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas**; the class owning the means of **material** production controls the means of **mental** production.
- ➡️ Ideology as a concept, and the end-of-ideology debate, are Topic 02's.

## F. Class: ownership, and the condition that produced consciousness

- **Marx (1818-83)** and **Engels (1820-95)**: **political power is the product of economic power**; **the tree of political power grows on the roots of economic power** (PDF p.308).
- **Means of social production** — **land, mines, forests, buildings, capital, machinery, organization and labour**; in a class-divided society **all means except labour are owned by the dominant class: labour alone is retained by the dependent class**.
- **Haves and have-nots**: masters/slaves, lords/serfs, capitalists/workers. **Class** is **the organizing category for exercising power in society**.
- *Communist Manifesto* (1848): **"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."**
- The condition: slaves **scattered to distant households**, serfs **scattered to distant agricultural estates**, workers **concentrated in big cities with strong communication** → conscious of **their strength** and of **their common enemy** (PDF pp.308-309).
- ⚠️ Generalisable rule: grievance plus proximity plus communication produces organisation; grievance alone does not.
- ➡️ The socialist and communist staging, compulsory work in the socialist phase, and the claim that **the use of power will become redundant** in a classless society are Topic 04's.

## G. Hegemony: two superstructures, two structures, one order of resort

- Gramsci (1891-1937) admired the **Bolshevik Revolution (1917)** as **a victory of the will power over economic conditions**, but warned it was unsuited to the West **where the working class had come to accept the existing arrangements** (PDF pp.309-310).
- The ruling classes' real strength lies in **their spiritual and cultural supremacy**, inculcated **through the institutions of the civil society**.
- Against classical Marxism, the superstructure **had attained some degree of autonomy; hence its analysis was also necessary**.
- **(a) Political Society or State** — **resorts to coercion**; **the whole organization of government**; **structures of coercion** (police, courts, prisons).
- **(b) Civil Society** — **resorts to obtaining consent**; **closer to the base and ... relatively autonomous**; **structures of legitimation** (family, school, church, peer groups); Gramsci **pays special importance to this part**.
- Mechanism: these institutions teach citizens **to show natural respect to the authority of the ruling classes**, so **even injustice involved in this rule would carry the impression of justice**.
- **Hegemony** = **power ... apparently exercised with the consent of its subject**; it also explains why **working-class parties have only achieved a relatively moderate degree of success** in open competition in capitalist countries (PDF p.306).
- Order of resort: **it is only when civil society fails to prevent dissent that political society is required to make use of its structures of coercion** (PDF p.311).
- 📝 Gramsci **was primarily a humanist**, **opposed to any type of tyranny**, wanting to **democratize all institutions**; the strategic advice on cultural warfare is one clause, not the payload. ➡️ Base and superstructure are Topic 04's; state and civil society as such are Topic 10's.

## H. Elite: the claim ladder, the contradiction and the reply

- Shared with class theory: a **broad division of society into dominant and dependent groups**. Different: the division is **somewhat natural**, based on **competence and aptitude (and not the emergence of private property)**, with **little prospects of a thorough-going change** (PDF p.312).
- **Pareto (1848-1923)**, *The Mind and Society* (1915-19): **elite** = **highest ability in their field of activity whatever its nature might be**, a **minority group which took all major decisions**; **masses** **lack qualities of leadership** and have **fear from responsibility**, feeling **it would be safe to follow the elite**.
- **Governing elite** wields power **for the time being**; **non-governing elite** **constantly endeavours to replace it**; their competition yields **circulation of elites**. 📝 **Masses have no chance of entering the ranks of elites.**
- **The lions** — **courage**, suited to **the maintenance of status quo under stable conditions**; **the foxes** — **cunning**, **adaptive and innovative**, coping better **during periods of change**.
- **Mosca (1858-1941)**, *The Ruling Class* (1896): people **necessarily divided into ... the rulers and the ruled**; the ruling class controls **most of the wealth, power and prestige**; **whatever form of government might be adopted, it is always this minority which exercises all power**; it **invokes moral and legal principles in order to win consent of the ruled** (PDF p.313).
- Mosca's basis is **capacity of organization**, not Pareto's intelligence and talent — the **organised minority** over an **unorganised majority**. **Of the two Italian elitists, Mosca is more democratic**: **leadership could emerge at all levels including grass-root level**, and **each stratum of society has the potential of producing good organizers for itself**.
- Elite theory's normative claim: the division is **functional**, the two **compensate each other**, it **does not give rise to any injustice in society**, and **the masses need not rise in revolt**; change is confined to **circulation of elites**, in which **an outmoded elite may be replaced by a more dynamic elite, but masses will have no opportunity to come to power**.
- **Michels (1876-1936)**, *Political Parties: A Sociological Study of the Oligarchical Tendencies of Modern Democracy* (1911): the **Iron Law of Oligarchy** — **every organization — whatever its original aims — is eventually reduced to 'oligarchy', that is the rule of the chosen few**; the **majority of human beings are apathetic, indolent and slavish**, **permanently incapable of self-government**.
- Mechanism: **size and complexity** → management **left to the professional experts** → they **become indispensable** → **oratory, persuasion and playing upon sentiments** → **very difficult to replace them at periodical re-election** → they **set aside the original aims of the organization**.
- **Oligarchy** (boxed) = **a form of organization in which a small group of the chosen few makes all decisions, largely in its own interest**.
- 📝 Printed contradiction: this **iron law of oligarchy rules out the possibility of 'circulation of elites' as envisaged by Pareto and Mosca**; it **belies all hopes of democracy in any society or organization** (PDF p.314). 🔶 ⚠️ The turnover-without-democratisation reconciliation is this topic's canonical gloss, not the chapter's statement.
- The reply: the law **cannot be uniformly applied to all organizations**; members of some are **more vigilant, vocal and assertive**; **political parties may offer more options to their members than trade unions**; so the observations **could serve as a warning to those who sincerely believe in democratization of institutions**.

## I. After class: Weber's 1918 extension and Mills's command posts

- **Weber** rejected democracy as **government by the people** and redefined it as **a competition for political leadership**. In the essay on **'Socialism' (1918)** he held that socialist revolution was **destined to become the dictatorship of officials rather than the dictatorship of proletariat**, and that **the inequality of political power is in no way diminished or eliminated by the abolition of classes** — so even in a **classless society** power concentrates **in the hands of the few**; democracy is preferable because it provides **an open competition for political leadership**. 📝 Carried forward by **Schumpeter**, named and nothing more (PDF p.314).
- **Mills (1916-62)**, *The Power Elite* (1956): **power elite** preferred to **ruling class**, because the latter implied **an economic class would exercise all political power** while the former implies **a combination of several groups** with high status **in all important spheres of social life**.
- The three: **captains of industry, military leaders and prominent politicians**, **loosely interlocking**, at **the pivotal positions of power**, sharing **cultural and psychological orientations** and **social origins**, **linked by ties of family and friendship**.
- The sharpest claim: **the power elites do not owe their power to fulfilling social demands. They themselves are capable of creating such demands.** They hold **the strategic command posts within the social structure**, are **a self-conscious class**, and **project their image of high moral character ... but they are scarcely sensitive to their moral or social responsibility** (PDF pp.314-315).
- 📝 A mid-century American model, used conceptually. ❌ No contemporary instance is named.

## J. Patriarchy: origin, definition, three accounts, scale

- The premise: the division **was created by nature for sustaining the human race**, **but it is society which placed men and women in dominant and dependent positions respectively** (PDF p.315).
- **Engels**, *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884): lineage once reckoned **through the female line**, so mothers were treated **with a high degree of consideration and respect**; monogamy eroded it. **"The overthrow of mother right was the world-historic defeat of the female sex."**
- **Patriarchy** — literally **rule of the father**; originally **the authority of male heads of households**; now **male domination in general**, **a social system in which men dominate, oppress and exploit women**.
- Sites: **reproduction, violence, sexuality (including the use of pornography) and work culture**.
- The structural claim: **a fundamental division of interests between most men and most women as a result of social structuring of gender relations** (PDF p.316).
- Account A — **a society's culture is dominated by the symbols of male origin** (French psychoanalysts and structuralists). ⚠️ Operates without an agent.
- Account B — **patriarchy is essentially based on the household in which men dominate women economically, sexually and culturally**; **marriage contract** read as **a labour contract through which women exchange unpaid domestic services for their upkeep**.
- Account C — **patriarchy and capitalism support each other**: unpaid domestic labour **relieve[s] their employer — the capitalist — of the liability to pay for that labour**, and outside the home women are **segregated to perform some specific jobs for which very low wages are paid**.
- The instruction: the accounts **should be read together to draw a full picture of the situation**. ❌ Do not rank them.
- **United Nations Report (1980)**: **"Women constitute half the world's population, perform nearly two thirds of its work hours, receive one tenth of the world's income, and own less than one hundredth of the world's property."** 📝 Dated book-period figure; always carry the year.
- **Women Liberation Movement** — emerged **in the United States since the early 1970s**, spread to Europe and beyond, demanding **equal rights and status** and a reassessment of **myths, values and beliefs** across **work and family life, social behaviour, decision-making, politics, religion and education**. 📝 The organisation roster and the legislative aftermath are omitted. ➡️ The full theory of patriarchy and the sex/gender distinction are Topic 06's.

## K. Pluralism: premise, evidence base, critique, concessions

- Against the other three perspectives, power is **not concentrated in a single group, but ... dispersed amongst a wide variety of social groups** — **organizations of workers, peasants, traders, industrialists, consumers** — which are **largely autonomous and almost independent centres of decision** and **cannot be classified into dominant and dependent groups** (PDF p.317).
- They **tend to balance each other's power**; **public decisions are largely the outcome of this equilibrium**.
- The civil-society clause: autonomous groups are **not required to compete with the authority of the state**; they are **intermediate bodies between individuals and the state** that **convey the sentiments of their members to government** and **influence the process of policy-formation and decision-making** (PDF p.318).
- 🔶 **Tocqueville (1805-59)**: the **absence of intermediary groups in France was responsible for the French Revolution (1789)**; the United States **was able to maintain democracy due the presence of such groups**; he wanted them **kept free from state interference**, while modern pluralists want them influencing government and government seeking their cooperation.
- Evidence base: **Dahl and Lindblom**, *Politics, Economics and Welfare* (**1953**); **Dahl**, *A Preface to Democratic Theory* (**1956**) and *Who Governs?* (**1961**); the assumption of **widespread distribution of political resources** and that **different interests prevailed in different political disputes and at different times**.
- **Polyarchy**: society **controlled by a set of competing interest groups, with the government as little more than an honest broker in the middle**.
- **Polsby**, *Community, Power and Political Theory* (**1963**): the **unspoken notion in pluralist research that at bottom nobody dominates**.
- Critique, step 1: pluralism **focuses only on the decision-making process** and **overlooks those issues on which no decision could be taken though some of those issues might be very important**. 🔶 The discipline's label — **non-decision-making**, the **second face**, after **Bachrach and Baratz** — is supplied for precision only; the chapter states the argument unnamed.
- Step 2: **even if different groups bargain on equal footing, some groups may lack internal democracy**, so **leaders or spokespersons ... may play a dominant role**. ⚠️ Michels, operating inside the pluralist's own unit.
- Step 3: **Lukes**, *Power: A Radical View* (**1974**) — the **third-dimension of power**: powerholders **can so manipulate the wants of others that their real interests are suppressed**. ⚠️ Strongest claim, weakest evidence.
- Concessions: **Lindblom**, *Politics and Markets* (**1977**) — a **privileged position of business in the market-dominated polyarchies of the West; that they are controlled undemocratically by business and property**. **Dahl**, *Dilemmas of Pluralist Democracy* (**1982**) — pluralism **cannot be treated as an open competition between truly equal political forces**, with policies promoting **redistribution of power in society**.
- The chapter's verdict: **subtle forms of domination within various groups and during their interaction should not be ignored** (PDF p.319).

## M. Power over to power to: Arendt, Macpherson, Gandhi

- The frame: the **agent** treats **himself as an end, and his subject as a means to that end**; the chapter asks whether the subject needs **retaliation**, freedom **from subjection**, or **independent development** (PDF p.319).
- The conventional view **cannot be set aside in any meaningful analysis of power**; the modern view adds **power (of the subject) to achieve a goal of his own choice**. **Empowerment** means equipping people **to resist their oppressors** and **to utilize their capacities for self-development** (PDF p.320).
- **Arendt (1906-75)**, *On Violence* (1969): power is **not the property of an individual**; it **corresponds to the human ability not just to act but to act in concert**; relations are **essentially cooperative**; it **belongs to a group and remains in existence only so long as the group holds together**; it **cannot be stored or held in possession**, while violence **relies on the instruments of its application; hence it can be held in possession**.
- **Power keeps the public realm together; violence threatens its existence.** The **public realm** is created when people **voluntarily behave in such a way that they create congenial conditions for each other**; they are **under the state** when **forced to behave in a particular way by an external agency**.
- The two warnings: **"Where genuine power is absent, violence may emerge to fill the gap."** · **"Violence can destroy power; it is utterly incapable of creating it."**
- **Only power but never violence can create legitimate authority**; power rests on **consensus and persuasion** and creates an **egalitarian order**. 📝 In Arendt's own terminology **authority** is tied to **command-obedience**, gives rise to **a hierarchical order**, **is based on violence** and corresponds to **the sphere of the state** — a tension the chapter prints and this edition reports rather than resolves (PDF pp.320-321).
- 📝 Her essay **'On Public Happiness' (1970)**: **no one could be called happy without his share in public happiness ... nor free without his experience in public freedom ... nor either without participating, and having a share, in public power**.
- **Macpherson (1911-87)**, *Democratic Theory — Essays in Retrieval* (1973): **extractive power** = **a person's ability to use others' capacities to serve his own purpose or 'power over' others**; the tradition **from Machiavelli (1469-1527) to James Mill (1773-1836)** focused on it, which **was not a product of intellectual carelessness but ... a factual description of power relations in a market society**.
- **Developmental power** = **a person's ability to use his own capacities for the fulfilment of his self-appointed goals**; capacities — **rational understanding**, **moral judgment and action**, **aesthetic or artistic activity**, **love and friendship**, **materially productive labour**; the person is **doer and creator** (PDF pp.321-322).
- **Three impediments**: **(a) lack of adequate means of life; (b) lack of access to the means of labour; and (c) lack of protection against invasion by others**; the scarcity problems are **technological and material as well as cultural and ideological**. ⚠️ His prescription — civil liberties combined with a socialist mode of production, yielding **creative freedom** — is his conclusion, not the chapter's verdict.
- The printed contrast: **consumer** vs **doer and creator** · **maximization of utilities** vs **maximization of powers** · **extractive** vs **developmental** · **satisfaction of material wants** vs **creative freedom**.
- **Gandhi (1869-1948)**, *Young India* (1925): **Real Swaraj will come not by the acquisition of authority by a few but by the acquisition of the capacity by all to resist authority when it is abused. In other words, Swaraj is to be obtained by educating the masses to a sense of their capacity to regulate and control authority.** Power defined **from the point of view of the ruled**; the ruled use **their moral power**; akin to the **Socratic function of speaking truth to power** (PDF p.323).
- 🔶 ⚠️ Arendt's unit is **the group**; Macpherson's is **the person**. Do not merge them.

## O. Decision rules for the examination hall

- Ask first whether the stem is about **formal decision rights** or **real capacity**. The words overlap; the questions do not.
- Any stem using *legitimacy*, *trust*, *acceptance* or *consent*: start from **authority = power + legitimacy** and state the arrow — coercion **only when legitimacy fails**.
- Any stem about a leader: classify on Weber's three, then immediately apply **none ... existed in pure form** and say which element is load-bearing.
- Any stem about influence over policy: run the three bases in order — formal rights, economic priority-fixing, ideological acceptance.
- Any stem about civil society or associations: use **intermediate bodies ... not required to compete with the authority of the state**, then ask what values the body transmits (Lesson 4) and whether it has **internal democracy** (Lesson 7, step 2).
- Any stem about elites, oligarchy or institutional capture: name the mechanism (size, complexity, indispensable experts, hard replacement), then downgrade the law to a **warning** and apply the aggravating conditions.
- Any stem about gender: concede nature, assert **society** placed the two positions, run the three accounts as three **locations**, and attach the 1980 figure with its year.
- Any stem about pluralism, lobbies or business power: state pluralism at full strength first, then the three steps, then the two insider concessions with years, then **redistribution of power** as the direction.
- Any stem about empowerment: two components — resistance and self-development — then Macpherson's **three impediments** as the test, then Gandhi's standard as the close.
- Any coercion-versus-consent conclusion: use the three-source convergence — **force only when legitimacy fails** (L2), coercion **only when civil society fails to prevent dissent** (L4), and **violence ... is utterly incapable of creating** power (L8).
- Three standing checks: **provenance** (🔶 marks owner-side vocabulary; 📝 marks printed wording or a scan note) · **dating** (the 1980 quartet, Mills's America and the charismatic illustration are book-period) · **boundary** (no article, statute, judgment, scheme, figure or named contemporary actor).
