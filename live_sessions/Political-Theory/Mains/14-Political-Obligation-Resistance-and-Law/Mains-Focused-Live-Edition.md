# Political Obligation, Resistance and Law — Mains-Focused Live Edition

A fast-preparation edition for GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay. It teaches only what enriches a Mains answer on obedience, dissent, legality and punishment, and stops there.

**The one idea that organises everything.** Political obligation is not the fact of compliance. It is the question of what, if anything, makes compliance *owed*. Every position in this topic is an answer to one sub-question — how much room does this ground leave the individual to judge the law? Force leaves none, divine right leaves none, conservative prudence leaves little, consent leaves some, the common good leaves a great deal, and the Marxist and anarchist positions invert the question altogether. Place a stem on that scale and it becomes answerable; miss the scale and the answer becomes a parade of thinkers.

**Scope.** This is **Topic 14 of the subject's twenty-three topics**, and it remains a standalone session. Political Theory is supplementary conceptual support. The Commission prints no Political Theory paper, and every General Studies question touching these ideas belongs to another subject — Ethics, Polity, Governance, Social Justice or the Essay. This topic owns no General Studies previous-year question and none is manufactured here. Concepts it needs but does not own are named and deferred rather than absorbed: the authority typology to Topic 15, the origin-of-the-state contract and Gandhi's wider critique of the machine state to Topic 13, and every article, statute and judgment to Polity.

**What is deliberately left out, and why.** The six sources of law with their commentators; the principles of natural justice; natural law's long pre-history; the historical school's wider programme; the sociological roster; the correction about French administrative law; the full twelve-argument capital-punishment inventory with its fifteen catalogued criticisms; and all jurisprudence publication years, which this edition omits as a class so that no year is misattributed. Definitions, causal logic, objections and replies, deployment, answer frameworks and required practice are kept intact.

The ladder reads downward on one axis only. Each descending rung concedes the individual more standing to ask whether this particular law deserves obedience, until rungs six and seven withdraw the question from the state altogether.

### 1. The question, and why the first two answers fail it

Political obligation is the problem of **how far, when and why an individual is obliged to obey the law and commands of political authority**, together with the duties that maintain political institutions — **payment of taxes, political participation and voting, jury service and military duty** (PDF p.280, printed 261). Note what the formulation already contains: *how far* makes obligation a matter of degree, and *why* makes it a matter of justification. A theory that cannot answer *why* has not answered the question even if it predicts obedience perfectly.

**Force majeure** means **superior strength, an irresistible compulsion or coercion**, and the doctrine makes the state's superior strength the source of obligation: the individual **has no option but to obey its laws and commands, whether he likes them or not**, so obligation is **unlimited** (PDF p.280). The objection is not that the account is factually wrong. It is that **it is not based on any moral ground**; it **simply invokes the dubious rule of 'might is right'**; it **does not allow the individual to inquire whether a law is right or wrong**; and it **does not care to secure his willing obedience** (PDF p.281, printed 262).

⚠️ The contrast that follows is the single most usable sentence in the lesson, because it supplies the criterion for every later rung: force creates a ground of obedience **akin to surrender under the threat of an armed robber rather than following the advice of a doctor in the interest of individual's own health**. Both the robber and the doctor change your behaviour. Only one of them gives you a *reason* that is yours. An answer that reproduces this contrast has defined political obligation without needing a definition.

**Divine right** holds that **the authority of the sovereign is derived from God; hence obedience to the state is as imperative as obedience to God**. Its chief exponent was **Robert Filmer (1588-1653)**; **early hints** appear **in the ancient Indian political thought**; in Europe it developed **during the ascendancy of monarchy**; in recent times it was upheld **in pre-communist Tibet and some tribal kingdoms**; and it is **seldom invoked in the modern state** (PDF p.281). It establishes obligation **on religious rather than moral ground**, and its hardest consequence is stated plainly: **if a King turns out to be a tyrant, people have to obey him as a punishment for their sin**. **James I of England (1566-1625)** justified his tyrannical rule on exactly this ground.

⚠️ Divine right and force majeure fail for the *same* structural reason, and saying so is worth more than describing either. Both **deny any right of the individual to exercise his judgment about right and wrong**. The theological wrapper changes the vocabulary of obedience, not its grammar.

📝 **One boundary note, complete as the chapter states it.** Equating divine right with **'charismatic authority'** is **not well-founded**: charismatic authority is **purely personal which may vanish with the disappearance of the person**, whereas divine right **concedes inheritance of the same authority** and is therefore **akin to 'traditional authority'**. ➡️ The authority typology itself belongs to Topic 15 and is not reconstructed here.

### 2. Prudence, and the third ground most answers never reach

The conservative rung is where obedience first acquires a defensible reason, and it is the rung candidates skip. **David Hume (1711-76)** argued that **the advantages of obedience to any type of political authority outweigh the disadvantages of having no government at all**, and upheld unconditional obligation **in view of practical utility of a government**. **Edmund Burke (1729-97)** held that politics was **a matter of 'prudence and practicability'** and that **revolution is evil, not only because it involves violence and destruction, but also because it results in misuse of power by those who manage to capture it** (PDF pp.281-282).

⚠️ Burke's second clause is the part that works in an answer, because it is not a defence of the existing order at all. It is a prediction about *successor* regimes, and it therefore binds the revolutionary as much as the ruler. Lesson 4 turns it into a test.

**Michael Oakeshott (1901-90)** supplies a third ground that rescues an answer from the consent-versus-common-good binary. Political activity is **neither a product of contract nor of any mutuality of purpose**; it is **akin to the model of informal conversation which develops by its internal logic**. Political obligation is therefore **a diffuse obligation, like an obligation arising out of friendship which does not follow any fixed pattern, nor emanates from any conditions of a contract**. In one sentence: **the conservative view of political obligation is based on legitimacy rather than on consent or morality** (PDF p.282, printed 263).

⚠️ Deploy this where a stem assumes that obligation must rest either on agreement or on moral rightness. Oakeshott denies the premise. The practical cost is also worth naming: an obligation modelled on friendship has no breach clause, which is exactly why the limited-obligation theories of Lesson 2 are needed.

### 3. The two rungs that invert the question

The **Marxist view** denies the premise rather than the conclusion. The state **is by no means the organized power of the community**; it is **the organized power of the dominant class — particularly the class owning the major means of production**, whose purpose is **not general welfare** but **helping the strong competitors to increase their wealth and power by exploiting the weak competitors as well as the dependent class**. Therefore **in a class-divided society, individual can have no political obligation toward the state**; obligation to society becomes possible only **provided it is a classless and stateless society**; until then we can think only of **worker's obligation toward the working class**, which under capitalism is an obligation **'against the state'** (PDF p.286, printed 267).

The **anarchist view** reaches a comparable conclusion by a different route and earns its own term: it **upholds negative political obligation**. **P.J. Proudhon (1809-65)** and **Peter Kropotkin (1842-1921)** argued that **all governmental authority is illegitimate because the state is indeed a coercive institution, which is suited only to a corrupt and unjust society**. The positive residue matters more than the negation: **the individual is only obliged to uphold justice**, and therefore **obliged to resist the state** and build a system of spontaneous cooperation. The chapter then links the three: **the Marxist and Gandhian views, as upholders of stateless society, come closer to the anarchist view of political obligation**.

⚠️ *Negative political obligation* is not the absence of obligation. It is an obligation that runs the other way — toward justice, and against the institution. Getting this right is what separates an analytical treatment of anarchism from a caricature of it.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Since the state can compel obedience, political obligation is simply the recognition of superior power." Identify the error and state the repaired claim.

**Model answer:** The error substitutes an explanation of compliance for a justification of obedience, which is the question actually asked — **how far, when and why** one is obliged to obey. The force-majeure doctrine does make the state's **superior strength** the source of obligation, but the chapter rejects it precisely because it is **not based on any moral ground**, **simply invokes the dubious rule of 'might is right'**, **does not allow the individual to inquire whether a law is right or wrong**, and **does not care to secure his willing obedience**. The decisive contrast is that such a ground is **akin to surrender under the threat of an armed robber rather than following the advice of a doctor in the interest of individual's own health**: both alter conduct, only one supplies the agent with a reason. Repaired claim: superior power explains why laws are obeyed, but obligation exists only where the ground offered could survive the individual's own judgment of the law — which is why consent, the common good and legitimacy are advanced as grounds and force is not.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat divine right as a milder version of force. Its defect is identical in structure — it too denies the individual any right to judge right and wrong — and reading it as merely old-fashioned loses the only point that earns marks.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Obedience secured by fear is compliance, not obligation." Comment. *(Comment — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The proposition holds. Political obligation asks **how far, when and why** obedience is owed, so a ground that predicts behaviour without justifying it answers a different question. The force-majeure doctrine supplies exactly that: the state is so powerful that the individual **has no option but to obey**, which is **not based on any moral ground** and **simply invokes the dubious rule of 'might is right'**. Two consequences follow — the individual may not **inquire whether a law is right or wrong**, and the state **does not care to secure his willing obedience**. The chapter's contrast settles it: this is obedience **akin to surrender under the threat of an armed robber rather than following the advice of a doctor**. ⚠️ One qualification is owed: fear is not irrelevant to law, since sanction is law's standing support; the claim is that sanction cannot be the *ground* of the duty it enforces.

### 1. Three contracts, one question

**Hobbes (1588-1679).** Men in the state of nature are equated with wild animals; life there was **'solitary, poor, brutish and short'** and it was **a state of war of each against all, and hence fraught with insecurity**. Men **entered into covenant to translate their urge for self-preservation into reality**, surrendering **all their natural rights to the newly created political authority — the sovereign**. The surrender **was final and irrevocable, because any departure from this position would result in the reversion to the state of nature** (PDF p.283, printed 264).

📝 The phrase is quoted exactly as the local text prints it — **'solitary, poor, brutish and short'**, without *nasty*. ⚠️ The standard wording of the English phrase is more familiar with that word included; nothing in the argument turns on it, and no claim is made here about other editions.

The crucial refinement is that Hobbes's obligation is unlimited **yet it is solely based on consent; not imposed from above** — which is why even conquest is handled by consent: **"a conqueror acquires dominion only if his vassals are understood to have tacitly promised obedience"**.

**Locke (1632-1704).** The state of nature is **not so gloomy**: man is **by nature a rational creature who is generally inclined to follow the rules of morality**, so it was a state of **'peace, good will, mutual assistance and preservation'**. Civil society was established **only to deal with the few law-breakers who could not be allowed to be the judge in their own case**, and what man surrenders is precisely **the right to be the judge in his own case** — nothing more. The state is **entrusted with the task of protection of his natural right to 'life, liberty and property'**. Therefore **government is created as a trust which can be dissolved if it fails to perform this function**, and Locke **upholds a limited political obligation and concedes individual's right to resistance and revolution**.

⚠️ The narrow surrender is the engine. Because only one right is transferred, the rest remain the citizen's own, and a trust that fails its stated purpose is dissoluble without any further premise. Answers that report Locke's conclusion without this mechanism lose the argument and keep only the slogan.

**Rousseau (1712-78).** The state of nature was **idyllic blissfulness**, ended by **the increase of population and the consequent scarcity**, which produced a conflict between **actual will** — motivated by immediate interest — and **real will**, motivated by the ultimate interest **which coincided with the interest of the community**. Men therefore placed themselves **under the direction and control of the 'general will'**, and **natural liberty was replaced by 'civil liberty'**. The result is **absolute sovereignty and unlimited political obligation**, but the obligation **is not owed to any external authority**: it is **addressed to the general will of the community of which the individual himself is a part**, embodying consent to subordinate the **'lower self'** to the **'higher self'** — hence **'man can be forced to be free'**.

### 2. The verdict, and the formula that makes it examinable

The chapter does not leave the three in balance. **Among the exponents of the social contract, Locke alone postulates a conditional consent; hence he clearly creates a limited political obligation.** Hobbes and Rousseau **postulate unconditional consent and absolute sovereignty**, and only **seem** to create unlimited obligation — since it rests on their consent and is **intended to serve their interest**, it **cannot be treated as unlimited in the real sense** (PDF p.284, printed 265).

The institutional objection is sharper than the conceptual one. Hobbes **provides for no mechanism to curb the state if the actual sovereign turns out to be tyrannical**. Rousseau's sovereign **does not stand above the community** — **theoretically this is all right**, but **in actual practice sovereignty will be exercised by a government consisting of a group of persons who might project their selfish and foolish designs as the expression of the general will**; his own remedy, small communities under direct democracy, **is not practicable in modern times**.

⚠️ Then comes the sentence that carries this lesson into GS-II: **the whole idea of consent, given once for all, is not a sound basis of political obligation. A government based on consent must be dependent on the continuous consent of a vigilant electorate, to be renewed and re-expressed at regular intervals.** This is a theory of obligation converting itself into a theory of accountability without borrowing a single constitutional provision — which is exactly why it is safe to use where this topic's boundary forbids articles and cases.

### 3. The common good, and a right of defiance inside obedience

Idealism **originally created an unconditional and unlimited obligation, but later it was modified to admit a note of caution**. **Hegel (1770-1831)** eulogised the state as **'the incarnation of divine reason'** and the **'march of God on earth'**, arguing that in obeying the state the individual **essentially follows divine reason and thereby exercises his freedom**. His structural error is named: he postulated unlimited obligation **without drawing a clear distinction between the state (which was an abstract entity) and government (which consisted of a group of fallible persons exercising actual authority)**. The consequence is stated without hedging: **it is unfortunate that fascist regimes invoked Hegel's argument to demand unconditional obedience from their citizens and led them to the path of untold disaster** (PDF p.284).

**T.H. Green (1836-82)** corrects idealism from inside. **Government cannot claim an unconditional obedience of its citizens**; the individual **owes his allegiance to society, not to the state or government**; and **the organized power of society should be recognized as political authority for the purpose of determining political obligation**. It is **society, not the state, which is the pivot of the common good**. The operative rule follows: the state **is obliged to promote the common good as conceived by its citizens**, and **individual is obliged to obey only those laws which will promote the common good**. And then the clause most answers omit — **if individuals think that they will serve the cause of the common good by defying any command of the state, their political obligation does not prevent them from such defiance** (PDF p.285, printed 266).

⚠️ Read that carefully: defiance is licensed *in service of* the common good, not as an exception to it. The permission and the duty have the same source, which is why Green's position is a conditional obligation rather than a weak one.

Green also supplies the pairing that Lesson 6 will need: **law can force the individual to perform certain acts, but these would be external acts only. No law can make them moral because morality is dependent on something freely willed.** 📝 The local text prints the summarising phrase as **'will, nor force, is the basis of the state'**; the evident sense, and the standard form of Green's formulation, is *will, not force*. It is reproduced here as printed and the reading is flagged rather than silently corrected.

**Harold J. Laski (1893-1950)** closes the division with an institutional version of the same point, **drawing a distinction between the state and government**: if a government **claims allegiance of human beings, it will have to compete with other human associations in securing their highest welfare**.

Hence the replacement: **the whole idea of consent, given once for all, is not a sound basis of political obligation**, and a government resting on consent **must be dependent on the continuous consent of a vigilant electorate, to be renewed and re-expressed at regular intervals**. ### 1. The formula, and why derivation matters

**Mahatma Gandhi (1869-1948)** is introduced as recognising **severe limits of political obligation**, and **civil disobedience implies deliberately disobeying an unjust authority and breaking an unjust law**. The decisive sentence is structural: **the duty of civil disobedience to an unjust law is the counterpart of the duty of civil obedience to a just law** (PDF pp.286-287, printed 267-268).

⚠️ That is not a rhetorical pairing. It states that both duties issue from the *same* commitment — to law as something that ought to be just. A person with no duty to obey just laws has no duty to disobey unjust ones either; he merely has preferences. This is why the first condition can require **a profound respect for law in general** without contradiction, and it is the single most efficient way to refute the charge that civil disobedience is anarchy in slow motion.

The occasions are stated narrowly: civil disobedience **may be resorted to as a protest against an unjust policy of government or in order to draw attention of the government to a demand for political reform**. ⚠️ Both are **communicative** purposes — the act is addressed to someone. That is why publicity is a condition rather than a tactic.

### 2. The evidence, with what each case proves

| Case | As the chapter states it | What it establishes that the others do not |
|---|---|---|
| **Thoreau (1817-62)** | **Originally coined** the term **'civil disobedience'**; published an essay **in 1848** explaining why he **had refused to pay taxes to the state for several years**, for which he **had to undergo imprisonment**; argued that **people must register their protest against any injustice perpetrated by their own government** | Disobedience against **one's own** government, by an individual, with the penalty actually served — the purest case of the accepted-penalty condition |
| **Conscientious objectors**, cited by **Laski** | Objectors **to military service during the period of the First World War (1914-18)**, who **had to undergo punishment for refusing to render military service as they believed in peaceful solution of all human disputes** | Refusal of a **legal duty** rather than breach of a prohibition, and refusal grounded in a general conviction rather than in the injustice of that one statute |
| **Gandhi, 1930** | Combined civil disobedience with **his principle of non-violent struggle and satyagraha (the principle of insistence on truth)**; **led the march to the seashore in 1930 to defy the ban on making salt by the Indians**, a ban **imposed by the British colonial rulers which was thought to be unjust by Gandhi and his followers** | Mass, organised, public and pre-announced breach of the very law objected to — the **direct** form, and the model for all five conditions at once |

### 3. The conditions and the disqualifiers, stated exactly

**The five conditions.** Gandhi **firmly believed that civil disobedience was based on a profound respect for law in general; only unjust law should be broken — that, too, when all methods of persuasion and petition for withdrawal of such law had been tried and had failed. The act of civil disobedience should be performed non-violently and in full public view; and penalties entailed by this act should be accepted willingly** (PDF p.287, printed 268).

Read as a test, each condition excludes a specific counterfeit: respect for law in general excludes nihilism; *only unjust law* excludes convenience; *persuasion and petition first* excludes impatience; *non-violence* excludes coercion; *full public view with penalty accepted* excludes evasion. ⚠️ The last is the strongest, because accepting the penalty is what proves the actor is addressing the public rather than escaping the law.

**The three disqualifiers.** First, **the true object of civil obedience is 'change of heart' of the authorities concerned**. 📝 The local text prints **obedience** here; in context the evident intended word is *disobedience*, and it is reproduced as printed with the reading flagged rather than silently emended. Second, it **should be resorted to only against a tyrannical regime, foreign rule or unjust government** — and the exclusion is explicit: **if a government generally maintains the citizens' rights and can be influenced through democratic means, resort to civil disobedience will not be necessary**. Third, it **should not be resorted to for pressing the demands of any particular section against the general or public interest**.

⚠️ Disqualifier B is the one that decides modern stems, and it is a *conditional*, not a prohibition. It does not say disobedience is always wrong in a democracy. It says that where rights are generally maintained and ordinary democratic influence is available, the necessity that justifies disobedience is absent — so the burden falls on the actor to show that those channels are unavailable or exhausted, which is condition 3 restated institutionally.

### 1. Realism before judgment

The conclusion opens by refusing its own earlier abstractions. Rousseau located obligation in the **'general will'**, Hegel projected **a divine image of 'the state'**, and Green suggested obligation toward the **'organized power of society' as distinguished from the state** — and then: **how do we encounter these abstract entities in real life?** The answer is deflationary and decisive: **philosophically, we may owe political obligation to any ideal object, but in actual life such obligation is always demanded by a band of government officials which must be considered with utmost caution** (PDF pp.287-288, printed 268-269).

**Laski** supplies the formulation: **"the state's claim to preeminence always means, in fact, the sovereignty of a government composed of fallible men whose intentions alone are not a sufficient justification for so vast a claim"** (*A Grammar of Politics*; 1938).

⚠️ Note what the quotation targets — **intentions alone**. It does not allege bad faith. It says that good intentions are not a sufficient warrant for an unlimited claim, which is a point about the *size* of the claim and not about the character of officials. That is why the same sentence works in a GS-IV answer about administrative humility and in a GS-II answer about accountability.

### 2. Who judges, and when judgment becomes a warrant to act

Two sentences stand in productive tension and must be used together. Government is **only an external agency**; a good government is **the product of human ingenuity**; but **the power of reasoning is still retained by human beings themselves; it cannot be transferred to the instrument created by them** — therefore **the individual himself or herself remains the best judge as to whether a law is just or not**. Immediately: **at the same time, no single individual can become wiser than all others.**

The resolution is procedural. When a law or order is thought unjust, **there should be full public debate and discussion of the issue, and the decision to oppose or resist it should be taken when public opinion on that issue has been largely crystallized and mobilized**; dissenting **sectional interests or opinions** are not ignored but analysed, **for arriving at a consensus**.

⚠️ This is the threshold most answers omit and the one that makes a conclusion defensible. Private certainty licenses *judgment*; only crystallised and mobilised opinion licenses *resistance*. An answer that says a citizen may resist whenever conscience dictates has skipped the step the chapter inserts precisely to prevent that inference.

### 3. The insufficiency test

**The failure of government to fulfil all our aspirations should not be treated as a sufficient ground of resistance, because such failure could be the outcome of paucity of resources, enormity of the problem or genuine priority of claims of others.** The named illustrations are deliberately sympathetic ones: **a government should not be resisted because it failed to provide for full employment, universal education and medical care, etc. since no government on earth is expected to solve all our problems.**

| The three defeating explanations | What an answer must then show instead |
|---|---|
| **Paucity of resources** | That the shortfall is not a capacity constraint — or that resources were available and differently applied |
| **Enormity of the problem** | That the failure is not inherent in the problem's scale |
| **Genuine priority of claims of others** | That the deprioritisation was not a defensible allocation among competing claimants |

⚠️ This is a burden-of-proof device, and it is the most transferable thing in the lesson. It converts "the policy failed, therefore resistance is justified" into "the policy failed, and here is why the failure is not explained by capacity, scale or competing claims". Every GS-II or GS-IV stem about agitation over service delivery is decided by whether the candidate performs that step.

### 4. Exhaustion, the ceiling, and the reconstruction cost

**Exhaustion.** Before **extreme measures like direct action, strike, picketing, hunger strike or courting arrest in large numbers, all constitutional means and methods should be tried fairly**. 📝 The local text prints *disect* for **direct** at this point; the reading is evident and is noted rather than relied on as wording.

⚠️ The word **fairly** is doing real work. A nominal application that was designed to fail is not a trial of constitutional means, and conversely a genuine attempt that is rebuffed satisfies the step. This is the theory-side counterpart of the constitutional-methods caution that the verified GS-IV demand at Lesson 3 puts at the centre of the Ambedkar stem.

**The ceiling.** **As Burke had warned, resistance is the medicine of the constitution, not its daily bread.** The medical metaphor carries its own dosage rule, and the chapter spells it out: **reform should be undertaken to achieve a definite objective, and the situation should be reviewed and a further programme chalked out after fulfilment of that objective. If major issues are resolved, minor issues could be left to take care of themselves.**

**Revolution.** It **is not ruled out, but it should be undertaken as a long-term programme, not as a sudden overthrow of the system.** **A. Appadorai** (*The Substance of Politics*; 1975) supplies the reason: **"While the conscientious individual who leads the resistance may often be motivated by the highest moral purpose, he must remember that he may be followed by others less conscientious who may take advantage of the opportunity to gain their selfish ends."**

⚠️ Appadorai's warning is about *succession within a movement*, and it pairs exactly with Burke's point at Lesson 1 that revolution **results in misuse of power by those who manage to capture it**. Together they make a single argument that does not depend on doubting the leaders' sincerity: a movement's moral quality is not transmitted to those who inherit it.

**The cost.** If change **is accompanied by chaos and confusion, the situation is most likely to be exploited by anti-social elements**, and destroying the old order **without leaving the foundations for the construction of a new order** is disastrous — **because, to destroy is easy, to reconstruct is difficult.**

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-IV.** This is the qualified conclusion for every dissent, protest and agitation stem, and it is a sequence, not a sentiment: realism about officials → private judgment conceded → public threshold → insufficiency test → exhaustion of constitutional means → Burke's ceiling → the reconstruction cost. Running three of these steps explicitly will outscore a page of balance.
- **GS-II.** The insufficiency test is the disciplined way to discuss agitation over service-delivery failure without either endorsing or dismissing it, and Laski's **intentions alone** line is the cleanest statement of why good faith does not validate an expansive claim of authority. ⚠️ Institutional remedies belong to Governance and Polity.
- **Essay.** On change, reform or revolution, **to destroy is easy, to reconstruct is difficult** is a closing thesis rather than a closing flourish, because the essay can then be organised around what reconstruction actually requires.

**Answer framework — the graded close.** Concede the grievance in its strongest form → apply the insufficiency test and say which of the three explanations is excluded and why → ask whether constitutional means were tried **fairly** → set the ceiling with Burke → name the residual risk with Appadorai → verdict as a condition, never as approval. Six sentences; it fits inside a 250-word answer and is the most reliably marked part of one.

### 1. Two kinds of law, three tests

**Descriptive or scientific law exists and operates in the universe, independently of our will. We can only discover it, but cannot alter it nor escape from it. No external agency is required to enforce it.** Being beyond our control, **its test is 'true' or 'false'**, and *right* or *wrong* apply to it only **in the factual sense, as alternatives of 'true' or 'false', and not to invoke any moral standards**. Its examples are the **'law of gravitation'** and the **'law of diminishing returns'**, and it **can be challenged or redefined only by a new scientific discovery and a proof supported by experimentation** (PDF p.289, printed 270).

**Prescriptive law determines what an individual should or should not do under specified conditions**; its rules are **enforced by sanctions**; it is **man-made law, adopted by human society for regulating its behaviour**, though it may rest on **long-standing custom, 'human conscience', the prevailing level of consciousness or dominant public opinion**. In political science, the social sciences and jurisprudence, **the term 'law' is throughout applied to indicate prescriptive law**.

The triple test follows, and it is where the marks are. Because prescriptive law **can be altered according to our sense of justice, its test is 'right' or 'wrong' in the moral sense. But when applied objectively, say, by lawyers and law-courts, its test is 'valid' or 'invalid'**, though **doctrines of fairness, reasonableness, due process, etc. may be invoked in its interpretation** — so that **the 'letter of law' may be supplemented by the 'spirit of law'** (PDF p.290, printed 271).

⚠️ Two different tests applied to the same object can deliver different verdicts. That is not a defect in the account; it is the thing the account exists to display, and it is the entire basis for everything the topic later says about unjust law, conscience, disobedience and the limits of the rule of law.

The chapter's own definition of **Positive Law** is worth carrying verbatim because it is compact: **the law that expresses the will of the sovereign. Usually it is duly enacted by a legislature. It is binding on all those coming within its jurisdiction, and its violation is effectively met with punishment.**

### 2. The four schools, each answering one question

| School | What it says law *is* | Which test it makes decisive | Its characteristic risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Natural law** | Positive law **draws its sanctity or authority from a higher law, 'the law of nature'**, discoverable by reason; **normative rather than positive**; it postulates **a universal system of 'justice' or 'right' as distinct from human enactments** | **Right / wrong** | Moral criticism without institutional precision |
| **Analytical / legal positivism** | **All law as positive, as direct command of a competent authority, enforceable by effective sanctions**; it **denies the status of law** to rules lacking authority to enforce them, **such as 'international law'**, and rejects natural law as **rooted in confusion between law and morality** | **Valid / invalid** | Detaching legality from legitimacy |
| **Historical** | Law traced to historical roots; **progressive societies are characterized by a 'movement from status to contract'**; **law has no fixed content** | Fit with social evolution | Treating inherited norms as self-justifying |
| **Sociological** | The state **is not the source of law**, only **an agency to impute legal value to the rules which already exist in society**; law is **not only prior to the state but also superior to the state**; **the proper function of law is 'social engineering'** | Social purpose and effect | Blurring validity into desirability |

⚠️ One clause settles the source question for a Mains answer and replaces an entire section: law pre-exists the state, and on the pluralist view associated with **R.M. MacIver (1882-1970)**, **the state does not create law but only declares it**. 📝 The local text prints the surname as *Maclver*, an evident scan artefact of the capital I.

**Natural law today.** The arc from antiquity is not needed; its endpoint is. **In the contemporary world, it is particularly invoked in the arguments concerning universal human rights** (PDF p.290). That single clause is what makes natural law usable in a modern stem without any historical excursion.

### 3. The analytical sequence as an argument, not a list

**Austin (1790-1859)** defined law as **a command of the sovereign person or body, as promulgated in a particular political society**, and argued that **'international law' was not law in the real sense because it was neither promulgated by a sovereign, nor enforced by a political superior**; at best it is **'positive morality' as distinguished from 'positive law'** (PDF p.291, printed 272).

**Kelsen** holds that **validity of law is derived from its proper promulgation as well as from its conformity to a 'basic norm'**, postulating **a hierarchy of norms** in which **the lower-level norms derive their validity from higher-level norms** up to a basic norm **to which entire legal system must conform**. Crucially, **in spite of his insistence on the normative character of law, Kelsen remains a positivist because he separates the questions of morality and moral obligation from those of legal validity and legal obligation**.

**Hart** argues that **Austin's conception of law as based on coercive order was too narrow as it was largely derived from the model of criminal law**: **civil laws and procedure** also **provide for certain private arrangements, such as contracts, marriages and wills**, and laws may **distribute benefits and regulate various organizations**. He therefore **equates law with rules which determine duties, obligations, rights and powers and also provide for the procedure of law-making and its amendment**. He **rejects Kelsen's conception of 'basic norm'** because **the standard of validity of a rule cannot exist in a vacuum**: **an act may be lawful in terms of English law, but not so in terms of French law**, so **norms of legal validity are indeed rooted in a social system, not in the legal system itself**.

🔶 One line of standard jurisprudential vocabulary sharpens Hart's position without being attributable to any sentence of this chapter: his own technical formulation distinguishes **primary rules**, which impose duties on conduct, from **secondary rules** about rules — a **rule of recognition** identifying what counts as valid law, rules of change and rules of adjudication. ⚠️ Flagged as an owner-side clarification; the chapter's phrase is **structure of rules**, and the primary/secondary vocabulary must never be quoted as its words.

**Dworkin** rejects **Hart's conception of law as a structure of rules** and introduces **'principles'**. Hart's model **cannot be applied to analysis of all aspects of a legal system, particularly in the context of disputed cases where application of rules does not give a clear answer**; in such cases **judges have to rely on certain 'principles'**, which **are by no means 'extra-legal' criteria as they are used by judges as part of law**. The famous example is the principle **that a man should not benefit from his own wrong**, **invoked to prevent a murderer benefiting from his victim's will**. The structural differences are stated precisely: **rules are precise and their applications can be specified whereas principles are not so precise and different principles may conflict with each other**; principles **can be invoked only to suggest reasons or justifications for the decision, but the decision itself cannot be derived from the application of a principle**; and unlike rules they **cannot be arranged in a hierarchy**, their validity being sought **from the realm of political theory rather than from the rationale of legal system itself**.

⚠️ Two moves, not four versions of one doctrine. Kelsen and Hart reconstruct the source of validity *within* positivism; Dworkin disputes that source-based standards exhaust the law judges actually apply. ⚠️ A further tension in the chapter is worth noticing rather than hiding: its summation groups **Kelsen**, **Hart** and **Dworkin** together as **critics of legal positivism** who point to **inadequacies of positive law**, although Kelsen was expressly called a positivist a page earlier. Treat the grouping as a loose summary of a shared complaint about Austin's model, and keep Kelsen inside positivism where the chapter's own reasoning places him.

**A law may be valid and still unjust.** Positivism makes validity turn on source and form; the triple test lets the moral verdict diverge from the legal one; natural law insists the divergence matters; Dworkin shows that even inside adjudication the valid rules run out. ⚠️ Everything Lesson 3 said about disobeying an unjust law presupposes this sentence, and everything Lesson 7 says about the rule of law's limits follows from it.

### 1. The reach of law, and the limit it cannot cross

**Technically speaking, law is the product of a prescribed procedure. It is valid only if it is properly enacted and recognized. Morality, on the other hand, is based on the sense of rightness and justice.** Ideally the two converge; **in actual practice, law and morality may take different courses** (PDF pp.295-296, printed 276-277). 📝 The local text prints *Tightness* for **rightness** at this point, an evident scan artefact.

The chapter's example of divergence is deliberately unheroic: a worker **morally committed to a trade union which has given a call for strike** where **the strike has been legally banned** must **face the consequences of acting against law** if he honours the moral commitment. ⚠️ Use this rather than a dramatic case, because it shows the clash arising from ordinary institutional life rather than from tyranny.

The reach is stated in one clause: **law is concerned with the external actions of man, and not with his inner motives**, whereas **morality springs from one's conscience which may or may not be translated into overt acts** — so that **envy, ingratitude and hypocrisy are morally wrong but these will not be legally punished until they result in such actions that are legally wrong**. The sanctions differ accordingly: **sanction behind law is force**, and **departure from law will be met with punishment; departure from morality will be followed by the prick of conscience, social disapproval and loss of reputation**.

⚠️ Then the pairing that is the strongest GS-IV move in this entire topic: **law can prevent us from certain immoral acts, but it cannot make us moral. Morality can be inculcated only through proper education and a congenial cultural environment. Again, it is morality which makes us law-abiding citizens.** The two sentences run in opposite directions and are both true — law cannot manufacture the disposition, and the disposition is what makes law workable. That is the argument for treating ethics formation and enforcement as complements rather than substitutes, and it is exactly what a question about why rules alone fail requires.

### 2. Law leading reform, and law failing

Because **law is relatively clear and consistent** while **moral standards may at times be disputed or doubtful** and **may change with time and place**, the direction of influence can run either way: **changes in law may be preceded or followed by the corresponding changes in moral consciousness**. The leading case is stated plainly — **some moral beliefs are based on superstition or outmoded thought, and law may take lead in social reform**: the abolition of **child marriage, widow-burning, untouchability or bonded labour** was **first conceived by a few reformers**, but **it created larger moral awareness when it was translated into concrete law** (PDF p.296, printed 277).

⚠️ Note the mechanism claimed. Law did not create the conviction; it **enlarged** it, by converting a reformist minority's position into a public standard. That is a modest and defensible claim, and it is more useful than the stronger claim that law changes hearts, which the previous paragraph has already denied.

Some laws are **morally neutral in their apparent form, adopted as a matter of convenience for the maintenance of public order** — **traffic laws may direct us to keep to the left or to the right** — and they are neutral **as long as they are equally applicable to all**; even then, observance **is also inspired by our moral sense, that is equal concern for the convenience and safety of all road-users**.

**The failure mode.** **Since our social life is practically regulated by law, it is imperative that laws should be adjusted with the changes in our moral consciousness. Otherwise, laws will prove to be tyrannical. Such laws will not only be frequently violated but the whole system of law will be overthrown.**

⚠️ This is a claim about systemic legitimacy, not about individual disobedience, and it belongs at the end of any answer that has argued for compliance. Law that falls out of step with moral consciousness does not merely attract breach; it erodes the standing of the system that contains it.

### 3. Four spheres in which restriction is rational

**Liberty or freedom is formally defined as 'absence of restraint'**, and law **imposes restraints**. The chapter does not resolve this by denying the tension: **it is possible that a tyrannical state may make tyrannical laws and destroy liberty of individuals. However, under democratic and welfare state purpose of law is to protect and promote liberty** (PDF pp.296-297, printed 277-278). The mechanism is the equal-liberty argument — **it is imperative that liberty of one individual does not endanger the liberty of another individual**, which **necessitates restriction on liberty of individual so as to allow equal liberty to all**, and **law is precisely the means of imposing such restriction**.

**D.D. Raphael**, in *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976), supplies the formulation the chapter quotes: **"Law restricts liberty in requiring us to do things that we might otherwise not want to do, and in requiring us to refrain from actions that we might otherwise want to do. The restrictions upon liberty imposed by the law may be intended to protect the liberty of others (or sometimes of oneself) which would be impeded by the behaviour that the law restricts, or alternatively the restrictions of law may be intended to promote other values than liberty. . . For either of these reasons the restrictions of law may be desirable, indeed essential."**

⚠️ The quotation concedes the restriction openly and then supplies two independent justifications — protecting others' liberty, and promoting **other values than liberty**. Candidates who argue only the first lose the whole welfare sphere, because provision for the poor is not justified by protecting anyone's liberty from interference.

Raphael identifies **four spheres in which restrictions imposed by law on individual's liberty can be considered as rational**:

| Sphere | What it covers, as stated | The point most answers miss |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Crime** | Acts **deemed to be a threat to the liberty and safety of other individuals (or their own safety)** are **declared offence under the law** — violation of traffic rules, **theft, causing physical injury, rape and murder**; and acts that **might harm the individual himself and his family** may also be cognizable, including **alcoholism, drug-addiction, and attempt to suicide** | The **or their own safety** clause: this sphere already contains a paternalist element, stated by the chapter itself |
| **(b) Civil disputes** | **Private rights of individuals and disputes between them**, where **'individuals' comprehends 'corporate bodies' also**; negligence attracts **damages, and not to undergo punishment**; defamation under **the law of torts**; grave matters cross over — **fraud, misappropriation and criminal breach of trust**, and **rash driving** resulting in death | The crossing point between civil and criminal is a matter of **gravity**, not of a different kind of wrong |
| **(c) Economic control** | **Minimum wages**, **dearness allowance**, **conditions of work**, compensations, labour laws; declaring **essential services** and **banning strikes** in those sectors; **quality control, weights and measures, prevention of adulteration** and consumer protection | The strike ban sits inside the *rational restriction* list, which is why a liberty-versus-regulation stem cannot be answered by assertion alone |
| **(d) Social welfare** | The welfare state has **tremendously expanded the sphere of legal regulation**: **taxation of relatively rich sections** for **schools, hospitals, cheaper transport and housing** for **the relatively poor** and for common services; entrepreneurs **encouraged or even obliged to set up industries in backward areas** | The chapter's own summary — **the law in this sphere is used as an instrument of social justice** |

The closing test is a single sentence and should be the last line of any answer in this family: **the conflicting claims of law and liberty as well as those of law and morality can be reconciled only when it can be proved that the restrictions imposed by law are based on logical or rational grounds, that is they are intended to broaden the scope of liberty or to improve the moral standards of society on the whole.**

### Deployment in GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay

- **GS-IV.** The pairing — **law can prevent us from certain immoral acts, but it cannot make us moral** and **it is morality which makes us law-abiding citizens** — is the direct answer to any stem asking why codes, rules or penalties are insufficient. State both halves; the second is what converts the observation into a recommendation, because it explains why ethics formation is not a soft supplement but the precondition of enforcement working at all.
- **GS-II.** The four spheres are a ready classification for any question about the legitimate scope of regulation, and the reconciliation test — restriction must be shown to **broaden the scope of liberty or to improve the moral standards of society on the whole** — is a stated criterion rather than an assertion of balance. ⚠️ The Indian regulatory and statutory record belongs to Polity, Governance and Social Justice.
- **Essay.** On liberty, regulation or reform, the two-directional claim about law and moral consciousness gives an essay a genuine argument: law can lead reform and can also be overthrown by failing to follow it.

**Answer framework — the restriction paragraph.** Concede the restriction in Raphael's own terms → classify it into one of the four spheres → state which of the two justifications applies, protecting others' liberty or promoting other values → apply the reconciliation test → close on the failure mode, that unadjusted law becomes tyrannical. Five steps, and it answers the whole family of law-and-liberty stems.

### Concept check

**Question:** "Stronger laws and stricter penalties are the way to make a society more ethical." Evaluate.

**Model answer:** The proposition mistakes what law can reach. **Law is concerned with the external actions of man, and not with his inner motives**, while **morality springs from one's conscience which may or may not be translated into overt acts** — which is why **envy, ingratitude and hypocrisy are morally wrong but these will not be legally punished until they result in such actions that are legally wrong**. The dependence then runs the other way — **it is morality which makes us law-abiding citizens** — so enforcement is parasitic on the disposition it cannot create, and a strategy of penalties alone weakens its own foundation. Two qualifications keep the answer from the opposite error. Law **may take lead in social reform**, as with **child marriage, widow-burning, untouchability or bonded labour**, which were **first conceived by a few reformers** and created **larger moral awareness** once **translated into concrete law**. And law that loses step with moral consciousness **will prove to be tyrannical**, being **not only frequently violated** but risking that **the whole system of law will be overthrown**. The defensible position is complementarity: enforcement for external acts, education and cultural environment for the will that makes enforcement workable.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not convert law's inability to make us moral into the claim that law is irrelevant to morality. The same passage credits law with leading reform and enlarging moral awareness; the limit is about motive, not about influence.

### 1. The conditions, and what each excludes

**Rule of law denotes a principle of governance which requires the following conditions to be fulfilled: (a) Law of the land should be properly notified so that the citizens know as to how it will effect them; (b) Laws should be general in form so that they are uniformly applicable to all the citizens; there should be no separate laws for any specific category of the people; (c) No law should be applicable with retrospective effect; and finally (d) Power of governance should be exercised strictly in accordance with the procedures, principles and restraints provided in the body of law, and not on any other basis** (PDF p.298, printed 279).

⚠️ Each condition excludes a distinct failure, and naming the failure is worth more than listing the condition: notification excludes secret law; generality excludes the special-category law; non-retrospectivity excludes punishing what was lawful when done; and condition (d) — the most comprehensive — excludes action taken on **any other basis**, which covers convenience, urgency and good intentions alike.

**The main thrust** has three limbs: **government should not be arbitrary, but should be conducted through the procedures authorized by legislation and passed in proper form; citizens should be punished for breaches of law and for nothing else; and official status should not protect a person from the operation of legal sanctions, if he has broken the law.**

⚠️ The second limb is the one candidates rarely use and the one that does the most work in a GS-IV or GS-II answer: **punished for breaches of law and for nothing else** rules out punishment for disposition, association, reputation or inconvenience. It is the rule-of-law statement of the point Lesson 6 made about law reaching external acts only.

### 2. Dicey, the objection, and the reply

**A.V. Dicey (1835-1922)**, in his *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885), **termed the rule of law the governing principle of the British constitution**. For him it stands for **'equality before the law' which implied equal subjection of all classes — including the officials — to ordinary law of the land, administered by ordinary law-courts** (PDF pp.298-299, printed 279-280). In England, since there is **no provision of 'constitutional guarantee' of the rights of the citizens** because of the unwritten constitution, **the remedies, benefits and liberties provided in their ordinary law are the foundation of their constitutional system**.

**The objection.** **Dicey's critics argue that the rule of law simply regulates the legal procedure; it is silent as regards the content of law. It has no mechanism to contain the unlimited sovereignty of the British Parliament. If the Parliament enacts an unjust or discriminatory law, it cannot be remedied by the rule of law.**

⚠️ This objection must be *stated* before it is answered, and it is the hinge between this lesson and Lesson 5. If validity can diverge from justice, then a doctrine that governs how law is made and applied cannot by itself guarantee that what is made is just. An answer that praises the rule of law without conceding this has not engaged the question a "critically examine" directive asks.

**The reply.** The criticism is **based on a very narrow interpretation of Dicey's thought**. Dicey himself **pointed out that the true meaning of this principle can be understood by carefully examining the manner in which the law of England deals with the right to personal freedom, freedom of discussion, public meetings, martial law, collection and expenditure of public revenue and the responsibility of ministers**. The conclusion is categorical: **the essence of the rule of law lies in the provision of legal protection of individual rights.** And the sovereignty point has an institutional answer too — unrestricted parliamentary sovereignty leaves no scope for constitutional guarantees, **yet this sovereignty is controlled by constitutional conventions which make the legal sovereign accountable to the political sovereign, that is the electorate**.

⚠️ That last clause connects directly to Lesson 2's **continuous consent of a vigilant electorate**. The same idea appears twice in the topic from opposite directions — as the ground of obligation, and as the ultimate control on an otherwise unlimited lawmaker — and pairing them is a genuinely strong move in an accountability answer.

**The wider meaning.** **Truly speaking, the term 'rule of law' comprehends those devices of limited government which are adopted for the protection of rights of the citizens.** In other democratic countries with written constitutions, **such as the United States and India**, **civil rights are protected by constitutional guarantees**, which **represent a concrete expression of the tenets underlying the wider interpretation of the rule of law**. ⚠️ The chapter names the two countries and stops; this edition stops there too, and asserts no article, amendment or judgment.

### 3. The trap, and the illustration that defuses it

**In common parlance, rule of law requires that laws should be definite and unambiguous, clearly notified and made known to everybody, and equally applicable to all. But it does not imply identical rights and duties of all citizens** (PDF p.300, printed 281).

The traffic illustration is the chapter's own and it is unusually precise. **Every road-user is governed by the same law, but it does not rule out streamlining of traffic according to the nature, size and speed of each vehicle.** What the rule of law then requires is that **every road-user should be fully aware as to which side or lane he or she should take, how to give way to faster traffic, how to overtake slower traffic, where one is allowed to turn, slow down or stop**. And differentiation may go further: the law **may even give priority to police vehicles, ambulance, fire service vehicles and other emergency services over the rest of the traffic provided they display proper signs of warning, and make necessary announcements to this effect**.

⚠️ Read the structure, not just the example. Differentiation is permitted when it tracks a **relevant** feature — nature, size, speed, emergency function — and when it is **announced in advance** on **stated conditions**. That is the rule-of-law test for differentiated treatment, derived from the chapter without importing a single constitutional doctrine, and it is the complete answer to the trap that generality means uniformity of outcome.

### Concept check

**Question:** A measure prescribes different compliance requirements for large and small enterprises. A candidate calls this a violation of the rule of law because laws must be **general in form**. Assess.

**Model answer:** The objection misreads generality as uniformity of content. Generality requires that laws be **uniformly applicable to all the citizens** with **no separate laws for any specific category of the people**, and it operates alongside notification, non-retrospectivity and the requirement that power be exercised **strictly in accordance with the procedures, principles and restraints provided in the body of law**. But the chapter states the limit expressly: in common parlance the rule of law requires laws **definite and unambiguous, clearly notified and made known to everybody, and equally applicable to all**, yet **it does not imply identical rights and duties of all citizens**. Its own illustration is traffic regulation, where **every road-user is governed by the same law** and this **does not rule out streamlining of traffic according to the nature, size and speed of each vehicle**, and where even **priority to police vehicles, ambulance, fire service vehicles and other emergency services** is permitted **provided they display proper signs of warning, and make necessary announcements to this effect**. The test is therefore whether the differentiation tracks a relevant feature and is notified in advance on stated conditions. A measure distinguishing enterprises by size satisfies generality if size is relevant to the regulatory purpose and the requirements are clearly notified; it fails if the category is a disguised exemption for particular persons, which is what the no-separate-laws clause actually prohibits.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not answer the procedure-versus-content objection by denying it. The chapter concedes that the criticism exists and defeats it by widening the principle to **legal protection of individual rights** and **devices of limited government**; an answer that never states the objection cannot claim the reply.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "The rule of law guarantees how power is exercised, not what law contains." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The criterion first: does the proposition survive as stated, or only narrowed? As stated it is the critics' case — the rule of law **simply regulates the legal procedure; it is silent as regards the content of law**, with no mechanism against an unlimited legislature, so an **unjust or discriminatory law cannot be remedied by the rule of law**. The chapter concedes that structure and rejects its scope as **a very narrow interpretation**: Dicey's meaning appears in how the law treats **personal freedom, freedom of discussion, martial law** and **the responsibility of ministers**, so that **the essence of the rule of law lies in the provision of legal protection of individual rights**; and sovereignty is **controlled by constitutional conventions which make the legal sovereign accountable to the political sovereign, that is the electorate**. ⚠️ Verdict: true of the narrow reading, false of the wider — **devices of limited government**.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks: 1 + 4 + 3 + 2):** 1 for naming the criterion before scoring, as a **critically examine** directive requires; 4 for the objection stated in its own strongest form, since an answer that only praises the doctrine cannot exceed 5; 3 for the reply given in both its forms, the rights-protection essence and the conventions-and-electorate control; 2 for a graded verdict that distinguishes the narrow from the wider interpretation rather than pronouncing the claim simply true or false. Deduct 2 for citing any constitutional provision or judgment, which lies outside this topic's boundary.

### 1. The question, and the shape of the disagreement

**The concept of punishment is that of inflicting some sort of pain on the offender for his violation of law. This is an instrument of public justice.** Over **the last two hundred years, the practice of punishment and public opinion concerning it have been drastically modified**, and the live question is posed as a choice of images: should the offender **be regarded as a nuisance to be abated, or a patient to be treated or a refractory child to be disciplined?** 📝 The local text prints *misance* for **nuisance**, an evident scan artefact. The philosophers' concern is **as to what end should punishment be directed** (SPP PDF p.164).

⚠️ That last clause fixes the shape of the disagreement and prevents the commonest error in a punishment answer. The three theories are not three degrees of harshness. They are three answers to a question about *purpose*, and each generates its own measure of a correct sentence. Severity is an output, not the point at issue.

### 2. Retribution

The theory is **based on the practice of getting even with a wrongdoer to diminish the perceived need for street justice, revenge**, and on **the philosophy of regaining equality or banishing the injustice** — in short, **an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth**. The judicial formulation: **a court of law repays to one only that which he has acquired**; since **he has done crime**, it is logical that **the reward of his crime, the equivalent of his destructive value, be given to him**. **According to Aristotle and Hegel, punishment is the negative reward of the criminal who acquires it by infringing the moral law. It is his award which he must receive.**

**Two features** are named: **personal revenge and punishment get merged**, and **the punishment is quantitatively equal to the crime**. **Two forms** exist, **rigorous** and **liberal**, the liberal form including **the consideration of the circumstances**. 📝 The sentence introducing those two forms is scan-corrupt in the local copy; the two names and the liberal form's circumstance-sensitivity are taught, and the corrupt clause is not reconstructed.

**Two criticisms**, taken from the source's own list. First, **it is not essential that criminal will, after having been punished, realize his mistake and feel remorse for his misdeeds** — and indeed **a criminal may become even worse after punishment**. Second, it is **not practical because it does not remove the causes of crime**: prevention or **transformation of the criminal can be achieved only by preventing crimes or transforming the criminal and not punishment**. **John Dewey** is cited for the governing point: **we are not relieved of the responsibility for the consequences of our procedure by the fact that the offender is guilty** — hence **it is more important to remove those economic, social, mental and physical causes which might lead to crime** (SPP PDF pp.164-165).

⚠️ Dewey's sentence is the most portable line in the lesson, because it concedes guilt and still refuses to let guilt settle what follows. That is precisely the move a GS-IV answer needs when a stem treats proven culpability as the end of the ethical question.

### 3. Deterrence

**The aim of punishment is to set an example to others and to prevent them from criminal tendencies**; the objective is **to discourage someone from future wrong-doing by making punishment severe enough such that benefit of crime is outweighed by the punishment**. The judge's formula states it exactly: **"You are not punished for stealing sheep, but in order that sheep may not be stolen."** The theory divides into **individual deterrence, that is, on offender himself such that crime is not paying**, and **general deterrence, that is, punishment sets an example for others**. Its characteristic consequence is severity: **the type of punishment under such theory is very harsh and severe and it even subscribes to capital punishment**, and its claimed strength is that **it discourages crime effectively** (SPP PDF p.165).

**Two criticisms.** First, **it uses offender as a means and not as an end**, and **does not affect any improvement in the culprit. Rather, he is made the means of improvement of the others.** Second, it **ignores impulsive factors** and **does not seek to remove the causes of crime** — to which the source adds that the example is wasted on those who would never offend, since **everyone cannot become a criminal**.

⚠️ Note the structure of the objection. The first criticism is moral and holds whatever the evidence shows; the second is empirical and depends on how offenders actually behave. Keeping them apart is what prevents a deterrence answer from collapsing into either pure principle or pure statistics.

### 4. Reform

**The aim of punishment is the improvement of the offender himself**, with the objective of transforming him **to a law-abiding citizen**; the belief is that **experience of punishment brings changes in his personality, attitude and behaviour**, so that **an offender is punished for his own benefits**. It is **closely related to rehabilitation program**, and its instruments differ accordingly: **type of punishment is not very harsh and often punishment is supplemented by counseling, education and training**. The source rates it **superior as compared to others because it is in tune with the modern humanitarian and philosophy of humanism trend**, effective where **criminals commit crimes in ignorance or compelled by circumstances**, and because **it seeks to eliminate the very causes of crime and thereby preventing it** (SPP PDF pp.165-166).

Its justifications are drawn from criminal anthropology, criminal sociology and psycho-analysis, and the source supplies **two criticisms** that cut across all three. First, the generalisation failure: each account **assumes that causes of a limited number of crimes to be the causes of all crimes**, and **all crimes cannot be attributed to diseased conditions**. Second, the deliberate-offender problem: **many people commit crimes while being fully conscious of the fact, especially the crimes of white-collar criminals cannot be included in such viewpoint**.

⚠️ The second criticism is the useful one, because it identifies the class of offending for which reform is weakest — planned, advantaged, non-desperate wrongdoing. A verdict that endorses reform without naming that class has not been tested.

### 5. Proportionality, and the verdict shape

🔶 Three refinements, owed to this topic's own owners rather than to either book, keep a punishment answer from overclaiming, and each is stated here as a flagged clarification:

- **Proportionality is a constraint, not a selector.** Seriousness of harm and culpability cap severity, but proportionality is **underdeterminate**: it does not by itself identify which of the three aims should govern, nor the single correct sentence within the permitted range.
- **Guilt settles liability, not quantum.** Proof of legal guilt is necessary before punishment and is not sufficient to settle its legitimate aim or amount — which is Dewey's point restated as a rule.
- **Severity does not entail deterrence.** Deterrent effectiveness is an empirical proposition; harshness is neither necessary nor sufficient proof of prevention.

⚠️ With these in place, the defensible synthesis can be stated without pretending the theories reconcile: legality and proof as the threshold, proportionality as the ceiling, public protection and humane treatment as constraints, and a realistic opportunity for reform as the default aim where the offending is not of the deliberate advantaged kind.

### 6. Capital punishment, taught as an unresolved dispute

The source prints two lists. **In favour**: capital punishment is **a definite deterrent to a crime**; **a moral war**, a weapon by which **society fights a moral war against criminals and immoralist**; **a social need**, **necessary for the maintenance of social order, peace and harmony**; **a selective process**, a method by which **nature eliminates those individuals who are dangerous for the society**, with **Plato** quoted — **"If a man cannot be harmless otherwise than in sleep, it is better for him to die than live."** 📝 The local text prints *If aman*; the spacing is restored. Next, **a legal demand**, since letting the criminal off **would militate against the majesty of the law** and drive people to **street-justice**; and **economic saving**. **Against**: it **can result in grave injustice**, because **the error of judgement cannot be ruled out**, evidence **can be manipulated**, and **winning the case is more dependent upon the competence of the lawyer rather than merit of the case**; it **leaves no room for repentance**; it is **not selective**, since **many innocents are hanged because they lack resources to defend themselves** while **many hardened criminals escape even detection**; it **does not save money**; it is **irreligious**; and it **does not reduce the crime level**, having been seen **not to act as definite deterrent** (SPP PDF pp.166-167).

⚠️ The two lists contradict each other at three points, and the contradiction is the teaching material:

| Point of conflict | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Deterrence | **a definite deterrent to a crime** | **does not reduce the crime level** · **does not act as definite deterrent** |
| Selectivity | **a selective process** eliminating the dangerous | **not selective** — the poor are hanged, the hardened escape detection |
| Cost | **economic saving** | **does not save money** |

⚠️ The source asserts both sides without adjudicating, and this edition does not adjudicate for it: no external figures and no campaigning source are introduced to settle a dispute the source leaves open. What *can* be concluded is structural. The deterrence claim on either side is empirical and unresolved in this source, so no verdict may rest on it. The error and selectivity objections are about the system that administers the penalty rather than about desert, and they are not answered by strengthening the desert argument. And 🔶 irreversibility raises the justificatory burden without settling the debate, since a penalty that forecloses both rectification of error and any prospect of reform must meet a higher standard of proof than one that does not — which is a statement about where the burden lies, not a conclusion about where it falls.

### Concept check

**Question:** "If a severe sentence is imposed and crime subsequently falls, deterrence is proved." Assess this reasoning.

**Model answer:** The reasoning fails twice over. First, as a matter of logic, 🔶 severity does not entail deterrence: deterrent effectiveness is an empirical proposition and a correlation between a sentencing change and a later fall does not isolate the sentence as its cause. Second, the source relied on here cannot settle the question, because it asserts both sides: its list in favour opens with capital punishment as **a definite deterrent to a crime**, while its list against closes with the claim that it **does not reduce the crime level** and **does not act as definite deterrent**. The same self-contradiction runs through **selectivity** — **a selective process** against **not selective**, since **many innocents are hanged because they lack resources to defend themselves** while **many hardened criminals escape even detection** — and through **cost**, where **economic saving** stands against **does not save money**. A disciplined answer therefore separates three questions that the proposition fuses: whether the offender deserved the sentence, which is a desert question; whether the sentence prevents future offending, which is empirical and unresolved here; and whether the system administering it reliably convicts the guilty, which is an objection about administration that no account of desert answers. On this evidence the deterrence claim is undetermined, and 🔶 irreversibility raises the justificatory burden for the capital case without by itself settling it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the deterrent theory's severity as its definition. Its claim is about the **aim** — **you are not punished for stealing sheep, but in order that sheep may not be stolen** — and that aim is defeated as readily by evidence of ineffectiveness as by the objection that it **uses offender as a means and not as an end**.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Proportionality limits punishment but does not justify it." Examine. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The distinction is sound. 🔶 Proportionality is a constraint rather than a selector: it caps severity by harm and culpability, but is underdeterminate: it cannot say which aim should govern, or which sentence within the range is right. Justification comes from a theory of purpose. Retribution supplies one — punishment **quantitatively equal to the crime**, the **negative reward of the criminal who acquires it by infringing the moral law** — but it **does not remove the causes of crime**, and 🔶 guilt settles liability, not quantum — Dewey's point that **we are not relieved of the responsibility for the consequences of our procedure by the fact that the offender is guilty**. Deterrence supplies another, **in order that sheep may not be stolen**, at the cost of using the **offender as a means and not as an end**. ⚠️ Verdict: proportionality is indispensable as a ceiling and silent as a ground.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Four checks: three consolidate a block, and the fourth spans the whole arc. Each model is held within 250 words.

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 to 3

**Question:** Build the chain from the question of political obligation to the definition of civil disobedience, naming each step and the clause that carries it.

**Model answer:** One: the question is **how far, when and why an individual is obliged to obey the law and commands of political authority** — about degree and justification, not compliance. Two: force majeure answers it with **superior strength**, which is **not based on any moral ground**, **simply invokes the dubious rule of 'might is right'**, and yields obedience **akin to surrender under the threat of an armed robber rather than following the advice of a doctor in the interest of individual's own health**. Three: divine right fails identically, denying any right **to exercise his judgment about right and wrong**. Four: the conservative rung supplies the first defensible ground — **legitimacy rather than consent or morality** — but no breach clause. Five: among the contractarians, **Locke alone postulates a conditional consent**, because he surrenders only **the right to be the judge in his own case** and creates government **as a trust which can be dissolved**. Six: once-for-all consent is replaced by **the continuous consent of a vigilant electorate, to be renewed and re-expressed at regular intervals**. Seven: Green makes obedience owed to laws that **promote the common good**, with defiance permitted in its service. Eight: Gandhi converts that permission into a disciplined test, since **the duty of civil disobedience to an unjust law is the counterpart of the duty of civil obedience to a just law** — which is why its first condition can require **a profound respect for law in general**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present the ladder as chronology. It is graded by one criterion — the room left to judge the law — and an answer that narrates thinkers in date order loses the grading that makes it an argument.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 4 and 5

**Question:** A citizen judges a law unjust. State what the chapter requires before resistance, and what it concedes about the law's legal standing meanwhile.

**Model answer:** The concession first. Prescriptive law carries two tests — **'right' or 'wrong' in the moral sense**, and **'valid' or 'invalid'** when **applied objectively, say, by lawyers and law-courts** — so a law can be valid while judged unjust, and positivism preserves it: **Kelsen remains a positivist** by **separating the questions of morality and moral obligation from those of legal validity**. The requirements run in sequence. The citizen's judgment is conceded — **the individual himself or herself remains the best judge as to whether a law is just or not** — and immediately limited, since **no single individual can become wiser than all others**. So there must be **full public debate and discussion**, with action only when opinion is **largely crystallized and mobilized**. The insufficiency test follows: failure to fulfil **all our aspirations** is not sufficient ground, because it may reflect **paucity of resources, enormity of the problem or genuine priority of claims of others** — naming **full employment, universal education and medical care**. Then exhaustion: before **direct action, strike, picketing, hunger strike or courting arrest in large numbers**, **all constitutional means and methods should be tried fairly**. Then Burke's ceiling — **resistance is the medicine of the constitution, not its daily bread** — with revolution only **as a long-term programme**, under Appadorai's warning about **others less conscientious**, and against the cost that **to destroy is easy, to reconstruct is difficult**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the validity concession become an argument for obedience. Validity is what makes the law identifiable and the breach real; it is not what makes the breach wrong, which is why the sequence exists at all.

### Cumulative check C — Lessons 6 and 7

**Question:** Show how law's limits and the rule of law together answer the claim that a legally correct government is necessarily a just one.

**Model answer:** They answer it from two directions. From the side of law's reach: **law is concerned with the external actions of man, and not with his inner motives**, and therefore **law can prevent us from certain immoral acts, but it cannot make us moral** — while conversely **it is morality which makes us law-abiding citizens**. A government can be legally correct and still fail at everything depending on disposition rather than compliance; and law that is not **adjusted with the changes in our moral consciousness** **will prove to be tyrannical**, being **not only frequently violated** but risking that **the whole system of law will be overthrown**. From the side of the rule of law: its critics say it **simply regulates the legal procedure; it is silent as regards the content of law**, so an **unjust or discriminatory law cannot be remedied by the rule of law**. The chapter calls that **a very narrow interpretation** and widens the principle — **the essence of the rule of law lies in the provision of legal protection of individual rights**, and it **comprehends those devices of limited government** — while adding the institutional control that conventions make **the legal sovereign accountable to the political sovereign, that is the electorate**. The combined verdict is that legal correctness is necessary, insufficient, and tested by whether restriction can be shown **intended to broaden the scope of liberty or to improve the moral standards of society on the whole**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use the critics' objection as the conclusion. The chapter states it and then answers it; an answer that stops at the objection has reported half the argument.

### Cumulative check D — the whole arc, Lessons 1 to 8

**Question:** In one connected argument, move from the grounds of obedience to a graded verdict on when coercion by the state is owed rather than merely imposed, marking where each lesson does work.

**Model answer:** Obligation asks **how far, when and why** obedience is owed, and grounds are graded by the room each leaves to judge the law — force and divine right leaving none, prudence little, **legitimacy rather than consent or morality** a third route (L1). Among contractarians **Locke alone postulates a conditional consent**, and once-for-all consent gives way to **the continuous consent of a vigilant electorate, to be renewed and re-expressed at regular intervals**, with Green permitting defiance in service of **the common good** (L2). Disobedience then becomes disciplined rather than sincere, being **the counterpart of the duty of civil obedience to a just law** under five conditions and three disqualifiers (L3). Resistance is bounded by the insufficiency test — **paucity of resources, enormity of the problem or genuine priority of claims of others** — by fair exhaustion of constitutional means, and Burke's **medicine of the constitution, not its daily bread** (L4). The law half explains why the question arises: a law may be **valid** and still **wrong in the moral sense** (L5). Law's limits follow: it **cannot make us moral** while **morality** makes citizens law-abiding, and restriction is rational only within the four spheres (L6). The rule of law supplies the anti-arbitrariness guarantee — **punished for breaches of law and for nothing else** — and the wider sense of **devices of limited government** (L7). ⚠️ Graded verdict: coercion is owed where the ground survives the citizen's judgment, the restriction passes the reconciliation test, and the penalty respects proportionality as a ceiling (L8).

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the chain become a tour of the lessons. Each step is load-bearing: remove the triple test and disobedience has no object; remove the insufficiency test and every grievance becomes a warrant; remove proportionality as a ceiling and the final verdict has no limit.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ Original practice prompts written for this edition. They are **not** previous-year questions and must not be cited as such.

**Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish political obligation from mere compliance with law. *(Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Compliance is a fact about behaviour; obligation is a claim about what is owed, and the question is **how far, when and why an individual is obliged to obey the law and commands of political authority**. The force-majeure doctrine delivers compliance alone: the individual **has no option but to obey**, a ground **not based on any moral ground** and **simply invokes the dubious rule of 'might is right'**, leaving him unable to **inquire whether a law is right or wrong**. The contrast is decisive — this is obedience **akin to surrender under the threat of an armed robber rather than following the advice of a doctor in the interest of individual's own health**. Obligation proper requires a ground the citizen could endorse: consent, the common good, or **legitimacy rather than consent or morality**. ⚠️ Sanction remains law's support; it simply cannot be the ground of the duty it enforces.

**Scoring note:** marks concentrate in the three-word formulation of the question, in the robber-and-doctor contrast used as a criterion, and in the closing clause, which prevents the answer from sliding into the claim that sanctions are irrelevant to law.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** Explain why the chapter treats legal validity and moral legitimacy as separable. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Separability follows from the tests, not from a preference. Prescriptive law **can be altered according to our sense of justice**, so **its test is 'right' or 'wrong' in the moral sense**; but **when applied objectively, say, by lawyers and law-courts, its test is 'valid' or 'invalid'**, and in interpretation **the 'letter of law' may be supplemented by the 'spirit of law'**. Two tests on one object may diverge. Legal positivism institutionalises the divergence: law is **direct command of a competent authority, enforceable by effective sanctions**, and **Kelsen remains a positivist because he separates the questions of morality and moral obligation from those of legal validity**. Natural law presses the other test, positive law **drawing its sanctity or authority from a higher law**, today invoked in **universal human rights**. ⚠️ Separable does not mean usually separate: the reform examples show law and moral consciousness moving together far more often than apart.

**Scoring note:** an **Explain** directive forbids a list of schools. Each element is given its function, and the final sentence pre-empts the standard follow-up by distinguishing conceptual separability from habitual divergence.

**Practice question 3 [15]:** "Civil disobedience is justified only where it is unnecessary." Critically examine this paradox. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The criterion first: does the paradox name a defect, or a feature correctly stated? The paradox draws on disqualifier B — civil disobedience **should be resorted to only against a tyrannical regime, foreign rule or unjust government**, and **if a government generally maintains the citizens' rights and can be influenced through democratic means, resort to civil disobedience will not be necessary**. Read crudely, it licenses disobedience only where it is least feasible and forbids it where it is most available. The reading fails, because the clause is a conditional about **necessity**, not a licence tied to regime type. Where rights are maintained and influence is available, the justifying necessity is absent; where those channels are unavailable or exhausted, it is present, and the test is factual rather than categorical. Condition 3 states the same requirement positively — breach only **when all methods of persuasion and petition for withdrawal of such law had been tried and had failed** — and Lesson 4 institutionalises it, requiring that **all constitutional means and methods should be tried fairly**, with **fairly** excluding a token attempt and a pre-planned failure. The other conditions keep the act communicative, not coercive: **non-violently**, **in full public view**, penalties **accepted willingly**, with **change of heart** as the object and no sectional demand **against the general or public interest**. ⚠️ Verdict: the paradox is dissolved, not merely resisted — justification tracks exhausted recourse, not the absence of democracy, and a democracy can generate the necessity by closing its own channels.

**Scoring note:** the directive requires a criterion before a verdict. The answer concedes the strongest reading of the paradox, locates the misreading precisely in *necessity* versus *regime type*, and closes by naming the case the corrected reading still admits.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** Examine the proposition that law can lead moral reform but cannot create moral conviction. *(Examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Both halves are the chapter's, and holding them together is the whole task. The limit comes from law's reach: **law is concerned with the external actions of man, and not with his inner motives**, so **envy, ingratitude and hypocrisy are morally wrong but these will not be legally punished until they result in such actions that are legally wrong**. Hence **law can prevent us from certain immoral acts, but it cannot make us moral**, since **morality can be inculcated only through proper education and a congenial cultural environment**. The dependence then reverses — **it is morality which makes us law-abiding citizens** — so enforcement rests on a disposition it cannot manufacture. The leadership claim is nevertheless real and bounded. Because **law is relatively clear and consistent** while moral standards **may be disputed or doubtful** and **change with time and place**, **law may take lead in social reform**: the abolition of **child marriage, widow-burning, untouchability or bonded labour** was **first conceived by a few reformers**, and it **created larger moral awareness when it was translated into concrete law**. The mechanism is enlargement of a standard, not conversion of a motive — which is why the halves do not conflict. ⚠️ The completing qualification is the failure mode: law which is not **adjusted with the changes in our moral consciousness** **will prove to be tyrannical**, and such laws **will not only be frequently violated but the whole system of law will be overthrown** — so leadership too has a limit.

**Scoring note:** the answer earns its marks by refusing the apparent contradiction and naming the mechanism — enlargement of a public standard — that reconciles leadership with the inability to create conviction.

**Practice question 5 [20]:** "Resistance to the state is justified by the injustice of the law alone." Critically examine with reference to the grounds and limits of political obligation. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Criterion: a ground is sufficient only if nothing further must be established before acting. On that test it fails, though its premise is accepted. The premise holds because obligation is conditional on more than enactment: **the individual himself or herself remains the best judge as to whether a law is just or not**, **Locke alone postulates a conditional consent** and makes government **a trust which can be dissolved**, and Green requires obedience **only those laws which will promote the common good**, permitting defiance in its service. It omits everything the chapter inserts between judgment and action. **No single individual can become wiser than all others**, so there must be **full public debate and discussion**, with resistance decided only when opinion is **largely crystallized and mobilized**. The insufficiency test then excludes the commonest grievance: failure to fulfil **all our aspirations** is not sufficient ground, since it may be **paucity of resources, enormity of the problem or genuine priority of claims of others** — **full employment, universal education and medical care** being its examples. Exhaustion follows — **all constitutional means and methods should be tried fairly** — and Burke caps the remedy: **resistance is the medicine of the constitution, not its daily bread**. Appadorai adds the succession risk — a conscientious leader **may be followed by others less conscientious** — and the cost: **to destroy is easy, to reconstruct is difficult**. ⚠️ Verdict: injustice is the necessary trigger and never the whole warrant; what completes it is threshold, exhaustion and proportion.

**Scoring note:** the strongest version of the answer concedes the premise generously before dismantling the inference. The three defeating explanations and the word **fairly** are where a 20-marker separates itself from a 15-marker.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** "A legal system can be valid, orderly and still unjust." Discuss with reference to legality, the rule of law and punishment. *(Discuss — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition is correct and its warrant runs through all three. On legality: prescriptive law is tested as **'valid' or 'invalid'** by courts and as **'right' or 'wrong' in the moral sense**, and positivism preserves the gap — **Kelsen remains a positivist** by **separating the questions of morality and moral obligation from those of legal validity**. Validity certifies pedigree, not content. On the rule of law: the critics' case is that it **simply regulates the legal procedure; it is silent as regards the content of law**, so an **unjust or discriminatory law cannot be remedied by the rule of law**. The chapter concedes it and widens the principle — **the essence of the rule of law lies in the provision of legal protection of individual rights**, and it **comprehends those devices of limited government which are adopted for the protection of rights of the citizens** — answering the narrow reading without claiming procedure suffices. On punishment: a lawful sentence may still misidentify its aim, and 🔶 proportionality is a ceiling rather than a selector, while **guilt settles liability, not quantum** — Dewey's point that **we are not relieved of the responsibility for the consequences of our procedure by the fact that the offender is guilty**. ⚠️ Graded verdict: validity and order are necessary to a just system and constitute none of it; justice is tested by whether restriction can be shown **intended to broaden the scope of liberty or to improve the moral standards of society on the whole**.

**Scoring note:** a **Discuss** at 20 marks requires a position, and the answer supplies one by grading each element as necessary and insufficient, then naming the single test that does the remaining work.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

Retrieval only. Teaching exposition is not repeated here.

## A. The question, and the two grounds that fail it

- **How far, when and why an individual is obliged to obey the law and commands of political authority**; attached duties — **payment of taxes, political participation and voting, jury service and military duty**.
- **Force majeure** = **superior strength, an irresistible compulsion or coercion**; obligation **unlimited** because the individual is too weak. Defects: **not based on any moral ground** · **might is right** · no inquiry into **right or wrong** · no **willing obedience**.
- The criterion sentence: **akin to surrender under the threat of an armed robber rather than following the advice of a doctor in the interest of individual's own health**.
- **Divine right**: **Filmer (1588-1653)** · **James I of England (1566-1625)** · early hints in **ancient Indian political thought** · **pre-communist Tibet and some tribal kingdoms** · **seldom invoked in the modern state**; **religious rather than moral** ground; a tyrant obeyed **as a punishment for their sin**.
- Shared failure: both **deny the individual any right to exercise his judgment about right and wrong**.
- 📝 Divine right resembles **traditional** authority, not **charismatic**, because it **concedes inheritance of the same authority**. ➡️ The typology is Topic 15's.

## B. Prudence, legitimacy and the two inverted grounds

- **Hume (1711-76)**: advantages of obedience to **any type of political authority** outweigh **having no government at all**.
- **Burke (1729-97)**: politics is **prudence and practicability**; revolution is evil **also because it results in misuse of power by those who manage to capture it**.
- **Oakeshott (1901-90)**: politics is **neither a product of contract nor of any mutuality of purpose**; obligation is **a diffuse obligation, like an obligation arising out of friendship**; the conservative ground is **legitimacy rather than on consent or morality**.
- **Marxist**: the state is **the organized power of the dominant class**; **in a class-divided society, individual can have no political obligation toward the state**; the worker has obligation **'against the state'**; obligation to society only if it is **classless and stateless**.
- **Anarchist**: **negative political obligation**; **Proudhon (1809-65)**, **Kropotkin (1842-1921)**; all governmental authority **illegitimate**, the state **a coercive institution suited only to a corrupt and unjust society**; the individual **is only obliged to uphold justice**.
- Bridge sentence: **the Marxist and Gandhian views, as upholders of stateless society, come closer to the anarchist view**.

## C. The three contracts, read on the resistance axis

| | Hobbes | Locke | Rousseau |
|---|---|---|---|
| State of nature | **Solitary, poor, brutish and short**; **war of each against all** | **Peace, good will, mutual assistance and preservation** | **Idyllic blissfulness** ended by population and scarcity |
| What is surrendered | **All their natural rights**, **final and irrevocable** | Only **the right to be the judge in his own case** | Subjection to the **general will** |
| Protected object | Self-preservation | **Life, liberty and property** | **Civil liberty** replacing natural liberty |
| Standing of authority | **Leviathan**; **no mechanism to curb the state** | **A trust which can be dissolved** | Absolute, but **does not stand above the community** |
| Obligation | Unlimited, **solely based on consent** | **Limited**, with **right to resistance and revolution** | Unlimited; **man can be forced to be free** |

- 📝 Hobbes's phrase is reproduced as printed, **without** *nasty*.
- Verdict: **among the exponents of the social contract, Locke alone postulates a conditional consent**; the other two only **seem** unlimited, since obligation rests on consent and is **intended to serve their interest**.
- Rousseau's practical defect: sovereignty exercised by **a group of persons who might project their selfish and foolish designs as the expression of the general will**; direct democracy in small communities **is not practicable in modern times**.
- **The formula**: consent **given once for all, is not a sound basis of political obligation**; government must rest on **the continuous consent of a vigilant electorate, to be renewed and re-expressed at regular intervals**.

## D. Idealism corrected — Hegel, Green, Laski

- **Hegel (1770-1831)**: the state as **the incarnation of divine reason** and **the march of God on earth**; unlimited obligation **without drawing a clear distinction between the state** (abstract) **and government** (**a group of fallible persons**); **fascist regimes invoked Hegel's argument** and led citizens **to the path of untold disaster**.
- **Green (1836-82)**: **government cannot claim an unconditional obedience**; allegiance is owed to **society, not to the state or government**; the **organized power of society** is the political authority; **it is society, not the state, which is the pivot of the common good**.
- Green's operative rule: the state must **promote the common good as conceived by its citizens**, and the individual is **obliged to obey only those laws which will promote the common good** — with defiance permitted where individuals judge they **will serve the cause of the common good** by it.
- Green's pairing: law **can force the individual to perform certain acts, but these would be external acts only**; **no law can make them moral because morality is dependent on something freely willed**. 📝 Printed **'will, nor force, is the basis of the state'**; read as *not*.
- **Laski (1893-1950)**: distinguishing state from government, a government claiming allegiance **will have to compete with other human associations in securing their highest welfare**.

## E. Civil disobedience — the test

- **Definition**: **deliberately disobeying an unjust authority and breaking an unjust law**; **the duty of civil disobedience to an unjust law is the counterpart of the duty of civil obedience to a just law**.
- **Occasions**: protest against **an unjust policy of government**, or to **draw attention of the government to a demand for political reform** — both communicative.
- **Five conditions**: **profound respect for law in general** · **only unjust law** · only after **all methods of persuasion and petition for withdrawal of such law had been tried and had failed** · **performed non-violently** · **in full public view**, with **penalties accepted willingly**.
- **Three disqualifiers**: the object must be **change of heart of the authorities concerned** · only against **a tyrannical regime, foreign rule or unjust government**, since where rights are maintained and the government **can be influenced through democratic means** it **will not be necessary** · never **for pressing the demands of any particular section against the general or public interest**.
- 📝 The civil-**obedience** typo is reproduced as printed where the quotation is used above.
- **Evidence**: **Thoreau (1817-62)** coined the term, essay **1848**, **refused to pay taxes for several years**, **imprisonment**; **Laski's** case of **conscientious objectors** to military service in the **First World War (1914-18)**, punished for believing in **peaceful solution of all human disputes**; **Gandhi (1869-1948)**, **satyagraha** as **the principle of insistence on truth**, and the **1930 march to the seashore** defying the **ban on making salt by the Indians**.
- 🔶 **Direct** breach of the unjust law against **indirect** breach of another law, the latter carrying the proportionate-disruption burden — owner vocabulary, flagged at every use.

## F. The limits of resistance — the engine, in order

1. **Realism.** Abstract authorities are encountered as **a band of government officials which must be considered with utmost caution**. **Laski**: **"the state's claim to preeminence always means, in fact, the sovereignty of a government composed of fallible men whose intentions alone are not a sufficient justification for so vast a claim"** (*A Grammar of Politics*; 1938).
2. **Judgment.** **The individual himself or herself remains the best judge as to whether a law is just or not** — and **no single individual can become wiser than all others**.
3. **Threshold.** **Full public debate and discussion**; act only when public opinion is **largely crystallized and mobilized**; analyse dissenting views **for arriving at a consensus**.
4. **Insufficiency test.** Failure to fulfil **all our aspirations** is not sufficient — it may be **paucity of resources**, **enormity of the problem** or **genuine priority of claims of others**. Named: **full employment, universal education and medical care**; **no government on earth is expected to solve all our problems**.
5. **Exhaustion.** Before **direct action, strike, picketing, hunger strike or courting arrest in large numbers**, **all constitutional means and methods should be tried fairly**.
6. **Ceiling.** **Burke**: **resistance is the medicine of the constitution, not its daily bread**; reform for a **definite objective**, then review; **if major issues are resolved, minor issues could be left to take care of themselves**.
7. **Revolution.** **Not ruled out**, but **as a long-term programme, not as a sudden overthrow of the system**. **Appadorai** (*The Substance of Politics*; 1975): the conscientious leader **may be followed by others less conscientious who may take advantage of the opportunity to gain their selfish ends**.
8. **Cost.** Chaos is **exploited by anti-social elements**; **to destroy is easy, to reconstruct is difficult**.

## G. What law is — two kinds, three tests, four schools

- **Descriptive / scientific law**: exists **independently of our will**; **we can only discover it**; **no external agency is required to enforce it**; test **true / false**; **law of gravitation**, **law of diminishing returns**; altered only by **a new scientific discovery and a proof supported by experimentation**.
- **Prescriptive / stipulative law**: what one **should or should not do under specified conditions**; **man-made**; **enforced by sanctions**; may rest on **long-standing custom**, **'human conscience'**, **the prevailing level of consciousness** or **dominant public opinion**.
- **Triple test**: **right / wrong** in the moral sense · **valid / invalid** when **applied objectively, say, by lawyers and law-courts** · the **letter of law may be supplemented by the spirit of law**.
- **Positive law** box: **the law that expresses the will of the sovereign. Usually it is duly enacted by a legislature. It is binding on all those coming within its jurisdiction, and its violation is effectively met with punishment.**
- **Natural law**: authority from **a higher law, 'the law of nature'**; **normative rather than positive**; a universal system of **'justice' or 'right'**; endpoint — **invoked in the arguments concerning universal human rights**.
- **Analytical / legal positivism**: law as **direct command of a competent authority, enforceable by effective sanctions**; denies the status of law to **'international law'**; rejects natural law as **rooted in confusion between law and morality**.
- **Austin (1790-1859)**: law is **a command of the sovereign person or body, as promulgated in a particular political society**; international law is **positive morality**, not **positive law**.
- **Kelsen**: validity from **proper promulgation** and conformity to a **'basic norm'**; a **hierarchy of norms**; **remains a positivist because he separates the questions of morality and moral obligation from those of legal validity and legal obligation**.
- **Hart**: the command model is **too narrow as it was largely derived from the model of criminal law**; **contracts, marriages and wills**; law = **rules which determine duties, obligations, rights and powers** and the **procedure of law-making and its amendment**; validity is **a matter of social fact** — **lawful in terms of English law, but not so in terms of French law**. 🔶 **Primary and secondary rules** and the **rule of recognition** are owner vocabulary.
- **Dworkin**: **hard cases where application of rules does not give a clear answer**; **principles** are **used by judges as part of law**; **a man should not benefit from his own wrong**, invoked **to prevent a murderer benefiting from his victim's will**; principles **may conflict**, **cannot be arranged in a hierarchy**, and **suggest reasons or justifications** without the decision being **derived** from them.
- ⚠️ Two moves, not four refinements: Kelsen and Hart revise validity inside positivism; Dworkin disputes that source-based standards exhaust law. ⚠️ The chapter's summation loosely groups all three as **critics of legal positivism**, which sits against its own statement that Kelsen remains one.
- **Historical**: **movement from status to contract**; **law has no fixed content**. **Sociological**: the state is **only an agency to impute legal value to the rules which already exist in society**; law is **not only prior to the state but also superior to the state**; **Pound (1870-1964)** — the proper function of law is **social engineering**. **MacIver (1882-1970)**: the state **does not create law but only declares it**.
- 📝 **No publication year is asserted for any jurisprudence work in this edition.** Name the works and the sequence.

## H. Law and morality — reach, limit, leadership, failure

- Law is **the product of a prescribed procedure**, valid if **properly enacted and recognized**; morality rests on **the sense of rightness and justice**; in practice the two **may take different courses**.
- Clash illustration: a worker morally committed to a union's **call for strike** that has been **legally banned**.
- Reach: law concerns **external actions**, **not inner motives**; **envy, ingratitude and hypocrisy** are not punished until they issue in legally wrong acts.
- Sanctions: **force** and punishment for law; **the prick of conscience, social disapproval and loss of reputation** for morality.
- **The pairing**: **law can prevent us from certain immoral acts, but it cannot make us moral**; morality **can be inculcated only through proper education and a congenial cultural environment**; **again, it is morality which makes us law-abiding citizens**.
- Leadership: **law may take lead in social reform** — **child marriage, widow-burning, untouchability or bonded labour**, **first conceived by a few reformers**, with **larger moral awareness** created once **translated into concrete law**.
- Neutral law: **traffic laws may direct us to keep to the left or to the right**, neutral **as long as they are equally applicable to all**, yet observed from **equal concern for the convenience and safety of all road-users**.
- Failure mode: unadjusted laws **will prove to be tyrannical**; such laws **will not only be frequently violated but the whole system of law will be overthrown**.

## I. Law and liberty — the four spheres

- **Liberty or freedom is formally defined as 'absence of restraint'**; a **tyrannical state may make tyrannical laws and destroy liberty**, but **under democratic and welfare state purpose of law is to protect and promote liberty**.
- Equal-liberty mechanism: liberty of one must not **endanger the liberty of another**, which **necessitates restriction**; **law is precisely the means**.
- **Raphael**, *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976): restriction may **protect the liberty of others (or sometimes of oneself)** **or** **promote other values than liberty**; **for either of these reasons the restrictions of law may be desirable, indeed essential**.
- **(a) Crime** — threat to the **liberty and safety of other individuals (or their own safety)**; traffic violation, **theft, causing physical injury, rape and murder**; and **alcoholism, drug-addiction, and attempt to suicide**.
- **(b) Civil disputes** — **private rights**, with **'individuals'** comprehending **'corporate bodies'**; negligence attracts **damages, and not to undergo punishment**; defamation under **the law of torts**; crossover at gravity — **fraud, misappropriation and criminal breach of trust**, and **rash driving** causing death.
- **(c) Economic control** — **minimum wages**, **dearness allowance**, **conditions of work**, compensations, labour laws; **essential services** and strike bans; **quality control, weights and measures, prevention of adulteration**.
- **(d) Social welfare** — regulation **tremendously expanded**; **taxation of relatively rich sections** for **schools, hospitals, cheaper transport and housing**; industries in **backward areas**; law as **an instrument of social justice**.
- **The reconciliation test**: restriction must be shown **based on logical or rational grounds, that is they are intended to broaden the scope of liberty or to improve the moral standards of society on the whole**.

## J. Rule of law — conditions, limbs, objection, reply, trap

- **Four conditions**: **properly notified so that the citizens know as to how it will effect them** · **general in form**, with **no separate laws for any specific category of the people** · **no retrospective effect** · power exercised **strictly in accordance with the procedures, principles and restraints provided in the body of law, and not on any other basis**.
- **Three limbs**: government **should not be arbitrary**, acting through **procedures authorized by legislation and passed in proper form** · citizens **should be punished for breaches of law and for nothing else** · **official status should not protect a person from the operation of legal sanctions, if he has broken the law**.
- **Dicey (1835-1922)**, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885): the **governing principle of the British constitution**; **equality before the law** = **equal subjection of all classes — including the officials — to ordinary law of the land, administered by ordinary law-courts**. England has **no provision of 'constitutional guarantee'**, so **remedies, benefits and liberties provided in their ordinary law are the foundation of their constitutional system**.
- **Objection**: it **simply regulates the legal procedure; it is silent as regards the content of law**; no mechanism against **the unlimited sovereignty of the British Parliament**; an **unjust or discriminatory law cannot be remedied by the rule of law**.
- **Reply**: **a very narrow interpretation**; Dicey pointed to **the right to personal freedom, freedom of discussion, public meetings, martial law, collection and expenditure of public revenue and the responsibility of ministers**; **the essence of the rule of law lies in the provision of legal protection of individual rights**; and sovereignty is **controlled by constitutional conventions which make the legal sovereign accountable to the political sovereign, that is the electorate**.
- **Wider sense**: **those devices of limited government which are adopted for the protection of rights of the citizens**; in **the United States and India**, **civil rights are protected by constitutional guarantees**.
- **The trap**: **it does not imply identical rights and duties of all citizens**. Traffic test — same law for every road-user, yet **streamlining of traffic according to the nature, size and speed of each vehicle**, and **priority to police vehicles, ambulance, fire service vehicles and other emergency services** on **proper signs of warning** and **necessary announcements**.
- ⚠️ Derived rule: differentiation is lawful when it tracks a **relevant** feature and is **notified in advance on stated conditions**.

## K. Punishment — three aims, two criticisms each

- Punishment = **inflicting some sort of pain on the offender for his violation of law**; **an instrument of public justice**; practice **drastically modified** over **the last two hundred years**; the governing question is **to what end should punishment be directed**. The three images: **a nuisance to be abated**, **a patient to be treated**, **a refractory child to be disciplined**.
- **Retribution**: **getting even**; **regaining equality or banishing the injustice**; **an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth**; the court **repays to one only that which he has acquired**; **Aristotle and Hegel** — the **negative reward of the criminal who acquires it by infringing the moral law**. Features: **personal revenge and punishment get merged**; **quantitatively equal to the crime**. Forms: **rigorous** and **liberal**, the latter admitting **consideration of the circumstances**. Criticisms: remorse **is not essential** and the offender **may become even worse**; it **does not remove the causes of crime** — **Dewey**: **we are not relieved of the responsibility for the consequences of our procedure by the fact that the offender is guilty**.
- **Deterrence**: **set an example to others**; **benefit of crime is outweighed by the punishment**; **"You are not punished for stealing sheep, but in order that sheep may not be stolen."**; **individual** and **general**; **very harsh and severe**, even subscribing to capital punishment. Criticisms: **uses offender as a means and not as an end**, made **the means of improvement of the others**; and it **ignores impulsive factors** and **does not seek to remove the causes of crime**.
- **Reform**: **improvement of the offender himself**; transformation into a **law-abiding citizen**; **counseling, education and training**; **in tune with the modern humanitarian** trend; **seeks to eliminate the very causes of crime**. Criticisms: it **assumes that causes of a limited number of crimes to be the causes of all crimes**; and **many people commit crimes while being fully conscious of the fact**, especially **white-collar criminals**.
- 🔶 **Proportionality is underdeterminate** — a ceiling, not a selector; **guilt settles liability, not quantum**; **severity does not entail deterrence**.
- **Capital punishment, for**: **a definite deterrent** · **a moral war** · **a social need** · **a selective process** (**Plato**: **"If a man cannot be harmless otherwise than in sleep, it is better for him to die than live."**) · **a legal demand**, since impunity **would militate against the majesty of the law** and invite **street-justice** · **economic saving**.
- **Capital punishment, against**: **grave injustice**, since **the error of judgement cannot be ruled out** and **winning the case is more dependent upon the competence of the lawyer rather than merit of the case** · **leaves no room for repentance** · **not selective**, as **many innocents are hanged because they lack resources** while **many hardened criminals escape even detection** · **does not save money** · **irreligious** · **does not reduce the crime level**, having been seen not **to act as definite deterrent**.
- ⚠️ The lists **contradict each other** on **deterrence**, **selectivity** and **cost**; the dispute is left unresolved, and 🔶 **irreversibility raises the justificatory burden without settling the debate**.
