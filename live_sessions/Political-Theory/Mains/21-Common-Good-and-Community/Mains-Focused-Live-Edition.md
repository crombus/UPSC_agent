# Common Good and Community — Mains-Focused Live Edition

A fast-preparation edition for GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay. It teaches only what enriches a Mains answer on what the common good is, what constitutes it, and whether it can be realised — and stops there.

**The one idea that organises everything.** Topic 19 asks *what justice is* and Topic 20 asks *which rule distribution should follow*; this topic asks a prior and different question — **is there a good that belongs to the whole community, distinct from the good of the state, the good of the majority and the bargained interest of civil society, and if there is, what constitutes it?** Gauba answers in two stages. First he builds an apparatus: a three-term distinction (common good · common interest · public interest), a working definition, a conflict test, two refuted misreadings and a method of pursuit. Only then do four named perspectives answer the constitution question — **Liberal, Communitarian, Marxian, Gandhian** — each with its own printed *A Critical Appraisal*, and the chapter closes on its own four-way comparison table. Miss the apparatus and the four perspectives become four summaries; miss the appraisals and the answer describes without judging.

**Scope.** This is **Topic 21 of the subject's twenty-three topics**, and it remains a standalone session. Depth and format drop to Mains level; the topic's syllabus share does not. Political Theory is supplementary conceptual support. The Commission prints no Political Theory paper, and **this topic owns no General Studies or Optional previous-year question** — the route count into its canonical files is zero by design, and no proxy, alias, mirror or synthetic route is created here.

**The boundary rule for this topic is object, not thinker — and in two places it is object *and* question.** Every one of the four perspectives already has an ideology owner elsewhere, and each enters here **only as an answer to what constitutes the common good, and a verdict on whether it can be realised**. ➡️ Liberalism as an ideology, and Macpherson's possessive individualism, belong to **Topic 03**; historical materialism, alienation, surplus value and the class-struggle sequence to **Topic 04**; Gandhism as an ideology — satyagraha, swaraj, swadeshi, the village republic, decentralisation, the critique of modern civilisation and the Tolstoy comparison — to **Topic 05**; communitarianism as a *theory of politics*, with the thinker-identity analysis, to **Topic 07**; the political-obligation taxonomy and resistance theory to **Topic 14**; property, Proudhon and Rousseau's general will as doctrine to **Topic 18**; every theory of justice, including **Walzer's complex equality as a distributive rule**, to **Topics 19 and 20**; majority rule, representation and liberal democracy as a form of government to **Topic 22**. ❌ Constitutional articles, case law, scheme administration, caste sociology and the GS-IV trusteeship administrative ladder appear nowhere in this edition; they belong to `Polity/`, `Governance/`, `Social-Justice/`, `Indian-Society/` and `Ethics/`.

**What is deliberately left out, and why.** The primitive-communism toolkit — the club, the stone axe, the flint knife, the stone-tipped spear — is narrative rather than argument and survives as one structural claim. The three pages of *Communist Manifesto* quotation, the oppressor/oppressed catalogue, the proletariat and lower-middle-class analysis and Hegel's clash of nations are Topic 04's and survive as two clauses carrying the one qualification this topic needs. The equality-of-religions and man–woman passages are compressed to a clause each, because the substantive debates belong to the Caste and Gender owners. The Buddha/Christ/Gandhi inspiration line is devotional and is dropped. **No compiled four-option question corpus appears anywhere in this edition** — practice runs as concept checks and solved Mains models. Definitions, causal logic, objections and replies, deployment, answer frameworks and required practice are kept intact.

**Four reading conventions, stated once.** Page anchors are given as `PDF p.N (printed M)` on a flat offset of **19** across Ch.21, which runs **PDF 474–490 = printed 455–471**. **Bold is not a quotation marker** — it also carries emphasis, headings and labels, and the diagrams and register notes paraphrase as well as reproduce. ⚠️ **Two printed tables were re-read from the source page images in this pass**, because the flat text layer emits their cells as separate lines with the column grid lost; the reconstruction method is stated at Lesson 1 and again in the Source Ledger. ⚠️ **Everything empirical in this chapter is book-period (5e, 2009)** — the Soviet and East-European evidence, the seven-decades and four-decades durations, the 1989–91 collapse, the broadcast advertising bans, the statutory cigarette warning and the population-control and literacy campaigns are dated to the book and never to the present.

## Roadmap

Ten lessons. The floor is **four**, because the chapter announces four named perspectives at PDF p.479 (printed 460) — Liberal, Communitarian, Marxian, Gandhian — each with its own printed appraisal, and dropping any one is a syllabus gap. Six adjustments produce ten.

1. **The conceptual apparatus takes three lessons, not one.** This is the structural fact that separates Ch.21 from Ch.20. Ch.20 opens straight into its perspectives; Ch.21 spends **eleven printed blocks, five of its seventeen pages and both boxed inserts** establishing what the common good *is*, what it is *not*, how a claim is *tested* and how it is *pursued*, before the word "liberal" appears as a perspective. That apparatus carries the topic's entire GS-IV weight and most of its GS-II weight. Taught as one lesson it is a definitions dump; taught as three it is the part a candidate can actually deploy.
2. **The liberal cluster fuses into one lesson.** The liberal view and its appraisal are two pages and three blocks, and **no verified Optional question in 2018–2026 asks about the liberal common good**. Split, the second half would duplicate Topic 03. Fused, the liberal perspective is one argument ending on Gauba's own conditional verdict, which is where the marks are.
3. **The communitarian block splits across two lessons.** It carries the topic's densest Optional pressure and its two sharpest boundary risks, and its two halves answer different questions: *why must the community come first* against *who says what, and does it work*. Taught as one lesson, the printed verdict — a strong moral philosophy not founded in equally strong political philosophy — compresses into a closing clause, and that clause **is** the evaluate-half of every stem routed here.
4. **The Marxian cluster fuses into one lesson, with two blocks compressed inside it.** What this topic needs from the class-struggle doctrine is two lines; split, the second lesson would be a Topic-04 duplicate.
5. **The Gandhian block splits across two lessons.** It is the only block in the chapter with a direct **20-mark** Optional anchor, a 15-marker and a direct GS-IV 10-marker, and its halves answer different questions: *how should wealth and status be held* against *how is the good of all actually constituted, and how does it differ from the utilitarian answer*. Taught as one lesson, the chapter's three highest-value printed items collapse into a list of terms.
6. **One lesson is added beyond the four.** The chapter closes on its own printed four-way comparison table, the topic's entire GS and Essay payoff is comparative, and concentrating the owner-only majoritarian-absorption safeguard in one labelled place is the only reliable way to keep it out of the eight book-grounded lessons.

The three refusals are the lesson. Each is a **negative test with a reason attached**, and an answer that states the reason rather than the refusal is already doing analysis.

### 1. Aristotle, and the clause both owners drop

The chapter opens where the idea begins: Aristotle held that the state exists **for the sake of the good life** and that it exists **to promote moral objectives, justice and the common good** (PDF p.474, printed 455). 🔶 Then comes the clause that is absent from both canonical owners and that converts the opening from decoration into argument: because constitutions in practice pursue different objectives, Aristotle distinguished the **'right' (or proper)** from the **'perverted' (or improper)** types, and held that the **right type of constitution pursued the common good, which implied the good of the whole community**.

⚠️ Read what that does. It makes the common good a **criterion of constitutional legitimacy**, not a sentiment — a constitution is assessable by whether it serves the whole community or one part. That is why the first refusal (not the good of the state) follows naturally two pages later, and it is the single clause that connects this topic to any *statism against individualism* stem. ❌ No Aristotelian constitutional theory and no Plato enter here; the classification is used for one inference and dropped.

### 2. Raphael's functional definition — the best opening line in the topic

🔶 **D.D. Raphael, *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976)** is quoted at PDF p.474 and is absent from both owners. He enumerates the functions of the modern state on Aristotle's lines: **"The State carries out its purpose by laying down laws, backed by force, requiring everyone to refrain from actions (crime and torts) that harm the common good, and to contribute in taxes and other imposts to upkeep of services (such as defence, public utility, and social services) that promote the common good."**

🔑 Memorise the architecture, not just the sentence. It is the only **institution-facing** definition in the chapter and it has two limbs: a **negative duty** — refrain from harming, enforced through criminal and tort law — and a **positive duty** — contribute through taxes to services that promote. Any GS-II stem about why a citizen owes taxes, why regulation is not an intrusion, or what the state is *for*, opens on this and never needs a scheme name.

### 3. The triad, which is the examinable core

Justice, the common good, the common interest and the public interest are **used interchangeably** in ordinary speech, but **they are not identical in their meaning and scope** (PDF p.474). The chapter separates them by the **kind of claim** each makes.

- The **common good** embodies a **normative** concept: it is concerned with **moral standards which are the subject of philosophical discussion**.
- The **common interest** embodies an **empirical** concept: it is concerned with **the understanding of the persons likely to be affected by a proposed decision**, and **it can be empirically ascertained**.
- The **public interest** is **a matter of judgment by a competent authority** who is expected to be **sensitive to the needs and aspirations of the people**. Those likely to be affected may **appeal** to that authority in its name, and a dispute about a decision taken in the public interest **can be resolved through discussion between the decision-makers and those affected by it**.

Then the two rules that make the triad usable. The **translation rule**: the notions of the common good and the public interest **are based on the same logic** — *what is described as the common good in moral philosophy becomes the public interest in the realm of politics and administration*. And the **comprehensiveness rule**: **all references to the public interest imply the common good, but all references to the common good cannot be expressed in terms of public interest**.

### 4. The three cases, and the one that carries the whole GS-IV cluster

The chapter proves the comprehensiveness rule with three instances, printed in this order.

| Case | Printed classification | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| A **soldier** dies in war so that his country wins | **A sacrifice made for the common good, not 'in public interest'** | The common good extends beyond what any authority can decide or administer — so it is the **wider** term |
| Government enforces **quarantine laws** to prevent the spreading of an infectious disease | **Taken in public interest, which also implies the common good** | The ordinary case: an authoritative judgment that also serves the moral standard |
| 🔑 A government **official takes a bribe for the welfare of his family** | **Prosecuted in public interest because he acts against the common good** | The two can come apart in the same act, and the public-interest action is justified *by* the common good |

🔶 The third case and the closing maxim — **a good citizen always puts the common good before his own or his family's interest** — are absent from both owners, and together they answer three separate verified stems without any additional material: the particularistic-against-universalistic corruption stem, the value-based-education stem, and the GS-IV public-interest stem. ⚠️ Note the precise structure of the bribe case: the official's motive is *benevolent* and *particular*. The chapter does not condemn him for selfishness; it condemns the **scope** of the good he serves. That is the whole particularistic/universalistic distinction in one printed example.

### 5. The three refusals, each with its reason

The concept is **frequently invoked** to focus political demands or to vindicate acts, decisions and policy measures, and references to it **abound in political speeches, posters and pamphlets, newspaper editorials, verdicts of the law-courts, arguments of the lawyers, guidelines of the government, and even on international forums** (PDF p.475). It is **a fascinating word which defies any precise definition** — which is why the chapter proceeds negatively.

1. ❌ **Not the good of the state**, *because* **a state like a tyranny may operate against the common good itself**. The state is an instrument that can be turned against the purpose that justifies it.
2. ❌ **Not the good of the majority**, *because* **the common good may be invoked to stop injustice against minority**. 🔑 Note the direction of the clause: the common good is not merely *compatible* with minority protection, it is the **ground on which a majority decision can be resisted**. The minority-safeguard stem routes here.
3. ❌ **Not the common interest of civil society**, *because* it is **not necessary for civil society to have a common interest** — it **may be characterized by conflicting interests**, and reconciliation of them **may not necessarily represent the common interest, although it is so described in liberal theory**. This reconciliation **may be the result of bargaining between contending parties; and any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good**.

🔑 The third refusal is the most deployable sentence in the chapter for GS-II, and it is also a **forward flag**: it names liberal theory as the position being refused, which is exactly what Lesson 4 will build and Lesson 5 will attack.

### 7. Standing qualification 1 — the majoritarian-absorption safeguard is owner-only

### Concept check

**Question:** Using the chapter's own instances, show why the common good is the wider term and the public interest the narrower one, and state what follows for an administrator.

**Model answer:** The relation is one of **comprehensiveness, not synonymy**: **all references to the public interest imply the common good, but all references to the common good cannot be expressed in terms of public interest**. Three printed instances fix the boundary. A **soldier** who dies in war so that his country wins makes **a sacrifice for the common good, not 'in public interest'** — the act is outside the range of anything a competent authority decides or administers, so the narrower term cannot carry it. **Quarantine laws** against an infectious disease are **taken in public interest, which also implies the common good** — the ordinary case, where an authoritative judgment and the moral standard coincide. An **official who takes a bribe for the welfare of his family** is **prosecuted in public interest because he acts against the common good** — the two are distinct enough that one supplies the *reason* for the other. ⚠️ The ground of the asymmetry is in the definitions: the common good is **normative**, the subject of **philosophical discussion**, while the public interest is **a matter of judgment by a competent authority** sensitive to **the needs and aspirations of the people**, and what is the common good in **moral philosophy** becomes the public interest **in politics and administration**. What follows for an administrator is a two-step discipline: a decision must first be justifiable as a **judgment** that can be defended to those affected — who may **appeal**, and whose dispute is **resolved through discussion** — and must second be answerable to the wider standard, since **a good citizen always puts the common good before his own or his family's interest**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the pair as "public interest is the legal form of the common good". That reading makes the bribe case unintelligible, because there the public-interest action is justified precisely by the common good it protects, and it also loses the chapter's own grid, where the two differ on **Sphere of Application** — the common good is **usually confined to the members of a community** while public interest is **universal, whether at national or international level** — and on **Criteria**, where only public interest is **usually determined by a competent authority**.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "The common good cannot be equated with the good of the state, the good of the majority, or the common interest of civil society." Examine. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The proposition is the chapter's own method: it defines negatively, and each refusal carries a reason. **Not the state's good**, because **a state like a tyranny may operate against the common good itself** — the state is an instrument assessable by the purpose that justifies it, as Aristotle's **'right'** constitution shows. **Not the majority's good**, because the common good **may be invoked to stop injustice against minority** — it is the ground of resistance to a majority, not its synonym. **Not civil society's common interest**, because such an interest need not exist; reconciliation may be **the result of bargaining between contending parties, and any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good**. ⚠️ Together the three make it a **standard**, not a count of preferences. The residual difficulty is honest: a concept defined by what it excludes still needs the positive test supplied next.

Deduct 2 for attaching the rights-and-dissent discipline to the chapter instead of labelling it owner-only, and 2 for importing majority rule as a decision procedure, which is Topic 22's.

Two misreadings, two refutations, from **opposite directions**: the first says the common good is *too aggregative*, the second says it is *too demanding*. The chapter refuses both, and an answer that runs the pair has already shown balance.

### 1. The definition, and the clause that separates this topic from justice

Before the definition the chapter states the negative: the common good **does not give precedence to the interests of a class, party or faction but gives due regard to the interests of all members of society** (PDF p.476). Then, in italics: the common good **stands for the goal which is regarded by the consciousness of the community as conducive to the welfare of the whole community, transcending the immediate interests of different individuals and groups. If its realization entails some loss or benefit to a particular individual or group, that loss or benefit is not treated as a matter of settling individual claims.**

Three moving parts, and the exam value is in keeping them apart. **Who judges** — the **consciousness of the community**, not an authority and not a majority count. **Whose welfare** — the **whole** community. **What is set aside** — the **immediate** interests of individuals and groups; note the word, which concedes that those interests are real and merely not decisive.

🔑 The last clause is the hinge of the whole topic. ➡️ Topic 19 treats allocation as the **settlement of individual claims**; this chapter's grid says so in the **Scope** row. The common good deliberately refuses that frame: a loss falling on one party is not a claim to be compensated or balanced, because the question being answered is not *who deserves what* but *what the community's goal is*. An answer that states this one clause has located the topic correctly and will not drift into Topic 19's apparatus.

### 2. The instances, and the civic test

The chapter immediately grounds the definition. **Taxation system of a welfare state is designed to accommodate the common good**, and the **measures concerning defence, law and order, public health, expansion of education and environmental cleanliness, etc. are geared to meet the needs of the common good** (PDF pp.476-477). ⚠️ Note that the list is the same family of services Raphael named at Lesson 1 — defence, public utility, social services — which is why the Raphael sentence and this list should be used together, never separately.

Then the civic test, which is easy to miss and useful to deploy: **if people are genuinely interested in the pursuit of the common good, their disputes will never take a violent form**. ⚠️ Read it as a diagnostic rather than a prophecy — the form that disagreement takes is evidence about whether the parties accept a shared standard at all. It is also the first appearance of the chapter's thesis that the pursuit of the common good runs through **consensus**, which Lesson 3 develops.

### 3. The conflict test, and why both its directions matter

The chapter concedes that **the nature of the common good is quite complex**, then fixes one thing as certain: **in the case of a conflict between different groups, the common good shall not coincide with the interests of a particular group** (PDF p.477). Three printed cases follow.

| Printed case | Verdict | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| **Labour–management dispute** | The common good **cannot be identified with the interest of the workers or that of the management** | Identifying the common good with either side of an organised bargain |
| **Heavy taxation of the urban population to provide liberal subsidies to the rural population** | **Cannot be equated with the common good** | Treating a transfer *towards* the less advantaged as automatically common |
| **Spending the revenue of villages for the advancement of cities** | **Cannot be equated with the common good** | Treating a transfer *away from* the less advantaged as development |

🔑 Cases two and three are **mirror images**, and the pairing is deliberate. The test is **symmetric**: it is not a rule about who is poorer or who deserves support, but a rule that a transfer defined by a group boundary is a sectional outcome whichever way it runs. ⚠️ That symmetry is what prevents the conflict test from collapsing into redistribution, and it is also why this lesson does not hand the chapter over to Topic 19.

Then the **procedural rule**, which is a small sentence with large consequences: if we want to test a proposal from the point of view of the common good, **we must consider the merit of its beneficiaries rather than the claims of its opponents**. ⚠️ It is a rule about *where to look*, not a licence to ignore opposition: a proposal is assessed by whether the gain it confers is defensible, not by how loudly it is resisted. An answer that applies it explicitly — naming the beneficiaries and asking whether the benefit is defensible to the whole community — is doing exactly what the chapter asks.

Finally, what invoking the concept **does** in a dispute: it reminds the parties **of certain objectives beyond their conflicting goals, competition and bargaining which are recognized by the community and which should not be overlooked by them**.

### 4. The hidden-cost clause — one sentence, four verified stems

The chapter's own worked example: when the common good is raised in an **employer–employee dispute in the industrial sector**, the point is to remind both parties **that they should not try to work out an arrangement that would serve the purpose of both parties but which might be repugnant to the interests of the large body of consumers or those of the general public** (PDF p.477).

🔑 This is the single cleanest GS-II deployment in the topic, because it states a complete test in one line: **agreement between the parties is not evidence of the common good; third-party cost is the test.** Set it beside Lesson 1's bargaining clause — *any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good* — and the pair becomes an argument rather than an assertion. It answers the pressure-group and corporate-lobby stems, the civil-society-organisation stem, and the Essay prompt about the grass trampled when two elephants fight, with the same sentence.

### 5. The two misreadings, and the one both owners drop

**(i) The utilitarian equation.** **Some writers tend to equate the common good with the utilitarian formula of the 'greatest happiness of the greatest number'. This view is misleading.** The refutation is by authority: **John Rawls (1921-2002) has amply shown how the 'greatest happiness of the greatest number' could be interpreted to mean injustice to the minority**, and the chapter extends the argument — if the minority is made to suffer **in the name of promoting the so-called general happiness, it would be against the spirit of the common good**.

⚠️ **Boundary, stated once.** Rawls enters this chapter **only** as the authority for this point. ❌ No original position, no veil, no two principles, no lexical priority, no difference principle — the architecture is Topic 20's and the concept of justice is Topic 19's. One sentence, then stop.

**(ii) 🔶 The individualist incentive objection**, which is absent from both owners and is therefore half the chapter's argument that most sessions never teach. **Some champions of individualism argue that insistence on the common good would undermine incentives for the individual to make best use of his talents and efforts. This view is also misleading.** Two replies are printed, and they work differently.

- **On its own ground:** **if the common good is ignored, no individual would be able to achieve his potential excellence or make full use of his personal assets.** The objection assumes that the common good competes with individual achievement; the reply denies the premise and makes it a **precondition**.
- 🔶 **By evidence, from J.K. Galbraith (1908-2006):** **the rich cannot have full enjoyment of their wealth unless there is adequate provision of public services in society.** ⚠️ Note what the Galbraith clause adds: it shifts the argument from excellence to **enjoyment**, and it directs the point at the group most likely to raise the objection. Private wealth cannot be consumed in a society without public goods.

🔑 Deploy the pair as a structure, not as two facts. The chapter refutes **two opposite errors** about the common good — that it is mere aggregation, and that it is a drag on individual effort — and an answer that names both and shows they fail for different reasons is visibly assessing rather than describing.

### Concept check

**Question:** A wage settlement satisfies both the employer and the union. Apply the chapter's apparatus to decide whether it serves the common good.

**Model answer:** Agreement between the parties settles nothing, because the chapter's test is not consent but **scope of effect**. Start from the definition: the common good is the goal **regarded by the consciousness of the community as conducive to the welfare of the whole community, transcending the immediate interests of different individuals and groups**, and a resulting loss or gain **is not treated as a matter of settling individual claims** — so a bilateral settlement is the wrong kind of object to begin with. Apply the conflict test: **in the case of a conflict between different groups, the common good shall not coincide with the interests of a particular group**, and the chapter's own instance is exactly this one — in a **labour–management dispute** the common good **cannot be identified with the interest of the workers or that of the management**. The operative question is therefore who is **not at the table** and what they will pay. Procedurally, examine **the merit of its beneficiaries rather than the claims of its opponents**: if the gain to the parties is defensible to the whole community and no third party absorbs a hidden cost, the settlement is consistent with the common good; if consumers or the general public fund it, it is a sectional outcome with two signatures.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not convert the test into a rule that favours the weaker party. The chapter prints **heavy taxation of the urban population to provide liberal subsidies to the rural population** and **spending the revenue of villages for the advancement of cities** as a mirror pair, and rejects **both**. The objection to a sectional transfer is its sectional definition, not its direction.

The lesson has one spine: **consensus is a process, not a condition**, and the chapter's own exception tells you how to distinguish a divisive mobilisation from a legitimate one.

### 1. The thesis, and the corollary that is easy to misquote

Under its own printed head **METHOD OF PURSUIT**, the chapter states: **consistent pursuit of the common good in a society can be ensured only through the process of consensus** (PDF p.477, printed 458). Then the corollary, which is sharper than most candidates expect: **those who focus on social cleavage as part of the basic structure of society cannot be devoted to the common good.**

⚠️ Read the corollary precisely, because a careless reading makes the chapter authoritarian. It is not a claim that identifying inequality is divisive. The object of the criticism is treating cleavage **as part of the basic structure of society** — that is, as permanent and constitutive, so that politics becomes the management of fixed blocs. The exception printed two paragraphs later proves this reading, and an answer that notices the qualifier before quoting the sentence is immediately safer.

### 2. The printed Indian cleavage list, and what politicians do with it

🔶 Both owners compress this to a line; it is printed in full and it is the closest the chapter comes to a ready-made Indian answer. **In contemporary Indian society** some focus on the **rural–urban divide**; others on **caste division**. Some sections **lay stress on religious differences and induce communalism**; others **harp on the division based on regional interests**. Some focus on the divide across **gender**; others **try to sow the seeds of dissention between white-collar and blue-collar workers** (PDF p.478).

🔶 Then the political-economy clause, also absent from the owners: **some politicians, too, capitalize one type of social cleavage or another, and after winning seats in legislative assemblies or the parliament, they try to encash their political support in the bargaining for a share in power.**

🔑 The clause is valuable because it supplies a **mechanism**, not a complaint. Cleavage is mobilised because it converts into a bargaining position, and Lesson 1 has already said that **any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good**. ⚠️ The unity-in-diversity stem is owned by Indian Society; this topic contributes the list and the mechanism, and ❌ no party, leader, election or event is named here.

### 3. The exception — the clause that routes four verified stems

**However, these divisive tendencies should not be confused with those humanitarian movements which aim at the preservation of environment or other laudable objectives. For example, when the so-called developmental activities render some poor tribals or villagers homeless, out of job or sick, the movements aiming at their rehabilitation should not be termed as divisive activities** (PDF p.478).

🔑 Three things make this the most useful sentence in the lesson. First, it is a **printed limit on the chapter's own thesis**, so using it demonstrates control of the source rather than selective quotation. Second, the phrase **so-called developmental activities** is the chapter's own scepticism about development claims, and it is the honest bridge to any development-against-displacement stem. Third, it yields a usable **test**: a movement is cleavage politics when it mobilises a **fixed attribute** against others, and it is not when it **repairs a specific harm** — homelessness, loss of work, sickness — caused by a particular project.

⚠️ Deployment limit: tribal development policy, forest rights, land acquisition and rehabilitation machinery belong to `Social-Justice/`, `Governance/` and `Polity/`. This topic supplies the conceptual permission and the harm-repair test, and nothing institutional.

### 4. The boxed definitions — and the verdict that is the lesson's payload

🔶 The box at printed 459 supplies the definition of **cleavage** that neither owner carries: the existence of cleavage means society is **sharply divided into antagonistic groups on the basis of some more or less fixed attribute** — for example along the lines of **class, region, religion, language, race, or gender**. Consensus, correspondingly, **implies the absence of such a rigid division in society**.

Consensus is then analysed **at two levels**.

| Level | Printed content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) As a process** | The different groups **have resolved their differences and arrived at an arrangement which is best suited to their needs under the existing constraints** | The achievable sense |
| **(b) As a condition** | The different groups **have no differences on any issue**; society is informed by **a deep-rooted and long-term agreement on all issues** | **It is a Utopian view of consensus** |

Printed verdict: **In modern societies, consensus is achieved only as a process.**

🔑 This verdict is the strongest internal defence the chapter has against the charge of enforced harmony, and it should be carried into every answer that uses the word *consensus*. ⚠️ Two riders raise it from a quotation to an argument. The phrase **under the existing constraints** concedes that a process-consensus is conditioned by the power and scarcity actually present — it is not an ideal agreement. And defining consensus as the **absence of a rigid division** rather than as agreement on all issues means that persistent disagreement is **compatible** with consensus, which is exactly what the owner-only safeguard at Lesson 10 needs in order to be more than an objection from outside.

### 5. The regulation cluster — stated once, and dated

The chapter closes the section by noting that the common good concerns **the hopes and aspirations of a larger community beyond the area of self-interests of different individuals and groups**, and that some people **may take to a dangerous path for want of proper knowledge, due to their temperamental weakness, or due to the fascinating effect of fashion or advertisements**. ### 1. The definition, and the premise that makes it an argument

The liberal view **regards the common good as the aggregate of individual goods** (PDF p.479). 🔶 Then the premise both owners omit, and without which the definition reads as an assertion: **the common good is not a metaphysical element which exists beyond the experience of ordinary persons**.

⚠️ That is a claim about **epistemic access**, and it is the liberal position's real first move. If the common good were a thing beyond ordinary experience, only someone with privileged access could identify it — a sage, a vanguard, a leader — and politics would become the administration of that insight. By denying the metaphysical reading, liberalism grounds the common good in what ordinary people can grasp about their own good. 🔑 This is also why the liberal perspective occupies the **Material** cell of Lesson 10's closing grid, and why the chapter can place the Gandhian perspective alone in the **Spiritual** cell without contradiction.

### 2. The chain from rationality to interest groups

**Man is a rational creature**, endowed with the **faculty of reasoning** which enables him to **grasp his own good** and **to realize the need of certain rules which are necessary for securing the good of all members of society**. Those rules are **instrumental to the reconciliation of conflicting interests of different individuals and groups**, and **indeed the point of their reconciliation represents the common good** (PDF p.479).

🔶 Then the step both owners compress, and the one that carries this lesson's GS-II value: individuals **who have a common interest in some issue organize themselves into an interest group**, and **the point of reconciliation between different interest groups represents the common interest of society which is coterminous with the common good**.

🔑 Set this immediately against two clauses already taught, because the three-way tension **is** the answer to the pressure-group stems.

| Printed claim | Where | What it asserts |
|---|---|---|
| The point of reconciliation **between different interest groups** is **coterminous with the common good** | Liberal view, PDF p.479 | Organised bargaining **produces** the common good |
| Reconciliation may be **the result of bargaining between contending parties; and any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good** | Lesson 1, PDF pp.475-476 | Organised bargaining is **incompatible** with it |
| An arrangement serving **both parties** may be **repugnant to the interests of the large body of consumers or those of the general public** | Lesson 2, PDF p.477 | The test is **third-party cost**, not agreement |

⚠️ The chapter is not contradicting itself; it is reporting the liberal claim at full strength and then refusing it with its own test. An answer on pressure groups that states all three, in this order, has an argument with a verdict — the represented interests may well be real, and their reconciliation is still not self-certifying, because the parties who are absent pay for it.

### 3. The three premises, and the scarcity clause

The chain only works if conflict is tractable, and the chapter prints the three assumptions that make it so: **any conflict existing in society is not very deep**; **every problem of society is capable of a peaceful solution**; and **every member of society realizes that we live in a scarcity situation where all wishes or demands cannot be fulfilled**. People **fulfil their reasonable demands by making suitable adjustment with others**, deciding their **respective rights and obligations by using their own intellect and by persuading each other to adopt a reasonable course of action** — and **this equilibrium of their respective rights and duties represents the common good**.

⚠️ **Scarcity is stated once, here, and belongs to the liberal position in this chapter.** ➡️ Topic 19's analysis of scarcity and the open society as the conditions under which the justice question arises is not re-run. ⚠️ Note also that the first premise is precisely what the Marxian perspective denies at Lesson 7 and what the communitarian and Gandhian perspectives recast at Lessons 5 and 9 — the closing grid's **Attitude toward Conflict** row is built from this sentence.

### 4. Barry, and a terminological collision worth flagging

🔶 **Norman Barry, *An Introduction to Modern Political Theory* (1989)** is quoted at PDF p.479 and is absent from both owners, as is the editorial note attached to him: Barry **uses the term 'public interest' as a synonym for the 'common good'**. His observation: **"Liberal advocates of the doctrine of the public interest... argue that the concept describes the shared interests of a community and that its promotion, so far from oppressing individual interests, actually enables individuals to secure advantages which they could not otherwise enjoy."**

⚠️ Flag the collision explicitly, because it is a trap. Lesson 1 separated the common good from the public interest on **status, sphere, criteria and who argues about it**; Barry treats them as interchangeable. Both are printed in the same chapter. 🔑 The resolution is that Gauba's own distinction governs this topic, and Barry's usage is reported as a **liberal convention**, not adopted. An answer that notices this and says so is demonstrating source control; an answer that silently uses the two as synonyms has lost Lesson 1's grid.

Note also what Barry's argument contributes substantively: it is the **enabling** defence of the common good — promotion of shared interests **enables individuals to secure advantages which they could not otherwise enjoy** — which is the liberal cousin of Galbraith's clause at Lesson 2 and should be paired with it in any public-goods answer.

### 5. The rules of civil society, and the exponents

🔶 Also absent from both owners: for liberal thinkers, **the rules of the civil society which provide safety of the individual from the oppressive powers of others, constitute the common good**. When everybody abides by them, **the strong will not oppress the weak; nobody will cheat anybody; everybody will enter into contract with others at his free will; and everybody will sincerely act according to the terms of the contract.** **State intervention will become necessary only when some anti-social element does not comply with the rules.**

⚠️ Read the four rules as the liberal **content** of the common good, which is what makes the position more than proceduralism: non-oppression, non-deception, free consent and sincere performance are substantive moral requirements. And read the intervention clause as the position's own limit — the state is a residual enforcer, which is exactly what the appraisal will find insufficient.

The chief exponents are named with printed dates: **John Locke (1632-1704), Adam Smith (1723-90), Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832) and John Stuart Mill (1806-73)**. ❌ No liberalism history, no biographies; Topic 03 owns the ideology.

### 6. A Critical Appraisal — the half that carries the marks

Liberalism **regards the rules of market society as the proper basis of the common good**, viewing **free competition, equality of opportunity and allocation according to merit as the rules of proper behaviour** (PDF p.480, printed 461). The defect is precise: **the reward of an individual's merit and effort would be governed by the market demand, which is beyond individual's control**.

🔶 The illustration is absent from both owners and is the chapter's most usable single comparison. **In ancient Greek city of Athens, philosophers were highly respected and soldiers held a lower position, but in Sparta soldiers were held in high esteem and philosophers languished.** Its modern twin: **in the present-day consumer society it is possible that highly talented and sincere poets, artists, scientists and other intellectuals may be constrained to lead a poor standard of living whereas semi-literate property dealers and mediocre businessmen may be rolling in riches.**

🔑 The logic is an invariance argument, and stating it as such is worth more than quoting it. **Hold merit constant and vary the society: the reward changes.** Therefore reward does not track merit, and a rule that allocates **according to merit** cannot deliver what it promises. ⚠️ That is also why the criticism in Lesson 10's grid is about **outcome** rather than machinery — liberalism's problem is not that nobody will comply, but that compliance produces the wrong result.

The consequence follows: the state **will have to protect and promote those talents which are indispensable for the preservation of human civilization and culture but whose existence is threatened by the free market competition**, and **only a welfare state can take care of this situation**. 🔶 The boxed definition, absent verbatim from both owners, is the hinge of the whole appraisal: **"A state that gives protection to the people and such components of human civilization and culture whose existence is threatened by the forces of a competitive market society."**

**C.B. Macpherson (1911-87)** supplies the one-line correction: the market-society model **subjects human talents to the rules of demand and supply**, and it **stunts the natural capabilities of man and destroys his creative freedom**. ⚠️ One line only — possessive individualism is Topic 03's.

Printed verdict: **liberal interpretation of the common good can only be accepted on the condition that it is linked with the concept of welfare state.**

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does Gauba accept the liberal account of the common good only conditionally, and what exactly is the condition?

**Model answer:** Because the liberal account is sound as a mechanism and defective as a measure. The mechanism is accepted at full strength: man is **a rational creature** who grasps his own good and **realizes the need of certain rules necessary for securing the good of all members of society**; those rules are **instrumental to the reconciliation of conflicting interests**, and **the point of their reconciliation represents the common good**, extended through interest groups to a common interest **coterminous with the common good**. The defect lies in the standard liberalism adopts. It treats **free competition, equality of opportunity and allocation according to merit** as the proper rules, but **the reward of an individual's merit and effort would be governed by the market demand, which is beyond individual's control**. ⚠️ The proof is an invariance argument: in **Athens** philosophers were **highly respected** and soldiers lower, in **Sparta** soldiers were esteemed and philosophers **languished**, and in the **present-day consumer society** poets, artists and scientists may live poorly while **semi-literate property dealers and mediocre businessmen may be rolling in riches**. Merit held constant, reward varies with the society — so merit-reward cannot measure the common good. Hence the state must protect talents **indispensable for the preservation of human civilization and culture but whose existence is threatened by the free market competition**, and **only a welfare state can take care of this situation** — a state that **gives protection to the people and such components of human civilization and culture whose existence is threatened by the forces of a competitive market society**. The condition is therefore exact: the liberal interpretation **can only be accepted on the condition that it is linked with the concept of welfare state**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not report the appraisal as "liberalism ignores the poor". The printed objection is about the **market's valuation of talent**, not about poverty as such, and the remedy is framed as the preservation of **human civilization and culture** rather than as transfer to the worst off. Converting it into a redistribution argument borrows Topic 20's vocabulary and loses the invariance point that makes the criticism work.

The lesson is one movement: individualism fails on its own terms → therefore aggregation cannot work → therefore the community is prior → and Green shows what that priority does to obligation.

### 1. The critique of individualism, which is about attachment and not about selfishness

Communitarianism is **a contemporary philosophy** that **points to the shortcomings of liberalism and attempts to redefine the relation between individual and the community** (PDF p.480). Its opening charge is specific: liberalism **promotes individualism to focus on individual freedom which undermines individual's affinity with the community**. **When every individual turns to seek his own good, no one is emotionally attached to any one.** An individual **would manage to have many means of comfort at the expense of his emotional security**. Therefore, **if an individual devotes himself to the pursuit of self-interest, he cannot secure good life in the fullest sense of the term**.

⚠️ Note the form of the argument, because it determines how it must be answered. It is an **internal** critique: it does not accuse the liberal individual of immorality, it says that the strategy **fails on its own terms** — the individual pursuing his own good ends with comfort and without emotional security, which is part of the good he was pursuing. 🔑 This is why the liberal reply cannot be "but people are entitled to choose", and why the communitarian objection survives contact with the standard liberal defence.

### 2. The aggregation question, which is a direct strike on Lesson 4

The chapter then prints the sentence that makes this perspective an argument rather than a mood: **"If isolated individuals cannot secure their own good by their efforts, how can we arrive at the common good by aggregating the outcome of their efforts?"** (PDF p.481, printed 462).

🔑 Teach this explicitly as the refutation of Lesson 4's chain, because the two lessons are printed in that relation. Liberalism defined the common good as the **aggregate of individual goods** and built a mechanism for reconciling them. The communitarian question attacks the **input**, not the arithmetic: if the individual good secured in isolation is itself defective — comfort without emotional security — then aggregating it, however perfectly, produces a defective total. ⚠️ A candidate who sets the two sentences side by side has produced the comparison that a "liberal and communitarian conceptions of the common good" stem actually demands, instead of two consecutive summaries.

### 3. The inversion, and its ground

**Communitarians hold that only a community is capable of realizing the common good.** If all individuals **pool their efforts for the attainment of the common good instead of striving to secure their individual goods, they would be able to realize the common good from which they would be able to derive their individual goods.** This **necessitates individual's first commitment to the community, and not to himself.**

The ground follows: **for communitarians, individual's own existence and personality are the product of his social situation, roles and conventions which are embedded in society.**

⚠️ Keep the logical order straight, because it is where answers go wrong. The claim is not that the community outranks the individual in value; it is that the individual good is **derived** rather than foundational — the community is the **source** from which individual goods are drawn, which is exactly how Lesson 10's grid states it in the **Constitution of the Common Good** row. ➡️ The theory of the situated self as an account of politics, and the thinker-by-thinker identity analysis, belong to Topic 07.

| Leave the individual to pursue his **self-appointed goals** | Want him to pursue the **community-determined goals** |
| Declare the individual **the sole proprietor of all his faculties** | Focus on his **indebtedness to society** for those faculties |
| Insist on the individual's **rights and liberties** | Emphasize his **duties and obligations** |
| — | Communitarianism **insists on our common identity** and **eulogizes those values and beliefs which are dear to all of us** |

🔑 The second row is the one that converts a duties-versus-rights answer from assertion into argument. The communitarian does not simply prefer duties; he denies the **proprietorship premise** on which rights-first reasoning rests. If the faculties a person exercises are owed to society, a claim to their unconditional disposal is not obviously well founded, and duty is not an external imposition on a prior owner. ⚠️ The fourth row is also where the owner-only safeguard at Lesson 10 attaches, because *eulogizing values dear to all of us* is precisely the move that a dissenting minority has reason to resist.

### 6. Green in full, including the clause both owners drop

**T.H. Green (*Lectures on the Principles of Political Obligation*; 1882)**, **the forerunner of communitarianism**, argued that human beings, **as self-conscious creatures, attain the knowledge of the common good in association with the members of their community**. He believed that **men knew the common good more intimately than their self-interest or individual good**. The common good **not only comprehends the good of all members of the community, but their conception of the common good is also identical**. **The state and politics come into existence for the realization of the common good.** **The idea of the common good is the foundation of political obligation.** The state **is authorized to make only those laws which promote the common good**, and **the individual is obliged to abide by only those laws which conform to the common good**.

🔶 Then the clause both owners compress away, and the one that converts Green from a label into an argument: **if an individual thinks that he can protect the common good more effectively by opposing a particular order of the state, his political obligation does not stop him from going ahead.** Finally: it is **the consciousness of the common good which induces people to accept their duties**; they are **prepared to forego their personal choice and self-interest for the sake of realizing the common good**; and they are convinced that they **can attain self-realization only by pursuing the common good**.

🔑 Three exam uses, in order of value.

1. **Obligation is conditional, not absolute.** Green grounds obligation in the common good and therefore **limits** it by the common good — the same principle that creates the duty licenses opposition to a particular order. ⚠️ An answer that cites Green only for "the state exists for the common good" has cited the premise and dropped the conclusion. ➡️ State this **once**, as the political consequence of the common good, and cross-refer **Topic 14**; ❌ no obligation taxonomy and no theory of resistance here.
2. **Rights and duties are connected through the common good.** The verified stem that duties are obligations while rights are entitlements, with **no necessary connection** between them, is answered by exactly this structure: on Green's account the connection is not direct but **mediated** — both are derived from the same common good, which is why a law that fails it binds no one.
3. ⚠️ **The identity claim is the weak point, and naming it is the critical half.** Green asserts that people's **conception of the common good is identical**. That is a strong empirical premise in any plural society, and it is where the owner-only safeguard at Lesson 10 attaches most naturally.

### Concept check

**Question:** "Duties are of the nature of obligation while rights are of the nature of entitlement; therefore there is no necessary connection between the two." How does the communitarian account of the common good answer this?

**Model answer:** It denies the inference by supplying the missing middle term. The proposition assumes that rights and duties are free-standing categories which must connect directly or not at all; the communitarian account connects them **through the common good**. The argument begins by refusing the premise on which entitlement-talk rests. While liberals **declare the individual to be the sole proprietor of all his faculties**, communitarians **focus on his indebtedness to society for these faculties** — so a claim to unconditional disposal of what one owes to others is not self-evidently well founded. It continues with the chapter's hinge question: **if isolated individuals cannot secure their own good by their efforts, how can we arrive at the common good by aggregating the outcome of their efforts?** Hence only a community realizes the common good, **from which they would be able to derive their individual goods** — entitlements included. **Green** completes the connection: **the idea of the common good is the foundation of political obligation**, the state **is authorized to make only those laws which promote the common good**, and the individual **is obliged to abide by only those laws which conform to** it. Both terms are therefore derived from one source, which also explains the asymmetry the proposition notices — duty follows from the common good directly, entitlement by derivation. ⚠️ Two qualifications keep the answer balanced. Green's clause that **if an individual thinks that he can protect the common good more effectively by opposing a particular order of the state, his political obligation does not stop him from going ahead** shows the connection does not licence unconditional obedience. And Green's premise that people's **conception of the common good is also identical** is a strong assumption in a plural society — which is why the connection must be institutionally safeguarded rather than assumed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not answer by asserting that rights and duties are "two sides of the same coin". The chapter supplies no such correlativity claim, and the examiner's proposition specifically denies a *necessary* connection. The communitarian answer is structural, not aphoristic: both are derived from the common good, and that is also why the derivation can fail — as Lesson 6's verdict concedes when it finds **no mechanism** to secure commitment.

Four claims and a verdict. ⚠️ The verdict's force comes from its precision: the objection is not that communitarianism is wrong, but that it is a **moral** philosophy standing where a **political** one is required.

### 1. MacIntyre — the sentence that wins the duties-versus-rights stems

**Alasdair MacIntyre (*After Virtue*; 1981)** has argued that individuals **flourish only within an atmosphere of 'socially established cooperative human activity'** (PDF p.481). 🔶 Then the consequence clause, which both owners paraphrase away: **if the state treats individuals as disconnected entities and lets them loose to realize their rights without realizing their duties, the result would be social disintegration and moral disaster.**

🔑 This is the single most valuable sentence in Lessons 5-6 for examination purposes, because it converts the duties-first position from a preference into a **predicted consequence** with a named mechanism. The mechanism is the state's own classification: treating people as **disconnected entities** is what produces rights-without-duties, and rights-without-duties is what produces disintegration. ⚠️ Deploy it as the second paragraph of any duty-and-accountability answer, immediately after Lesson 5's proprietorship contrast, and before the qualification that follows in section 5 below.

### 2. Sandel — the phrase the social-cohesion stem is actually asking for

**Michael Sandel (*Liberalism and the Limits of Justice*; 1982)** has asserted that **the person can only be understood in the context of his 'embeddedness' in a particular time, place and culture**. **Only with this understanding a political theory can generate laws, institutions and practices that would be genuinely good for us and contribute to a fully just society.** **This alone will create a 'deeper commonality' which will be informed by 'shared self-understanding' as well as affection** (PDF p.482).

🔑 The verified 10-mark stem asking whether liberal democracy requires **deeper principles for social cohesion** to balance its own strong affirmation of individual rights is answered from this paragraph plus the verdict in section 5. The stem's phrase and Sandel's printed **deeper commonality** informed by **shared self-understanding** are the same idea, and naming the correspondence is the move that converts a general answer into a precise one. ⚠️ The required second half is the qualification: Sandel's deeper commonality is a **moral** resource, and the chapter's own verdict says communitarianism supplies no mechanism for securing it — so the honest answer is that liberal democracy may **need** such principles and cannot **manufacture** them.

### 3. Walzer — and the boundary that must not be crossed

**Michael Walzer (*Spheres of Justice*; 1983)** **laid down elaborate criteria for the distribution of various social goods according to the proper spheres of their application, where they would contribute to the smooth functioning of the community** (PDF p.482).

⚠️ **Boundary, stated once and enforced.** Ch.21 does **not** print the term **complex equality**. That term, and Walzer as a distributive theorist of justice, belong to Ch.20 and therefore to **Topic 20**. ❌ Do not import the term, ❌ do not develop a distributive rule from it, and ❌ do not run the incommensurability argument here. What Topic 21 owns is the narrower and still useful claim that a good's distribution should follow the **sphere appropriate to it**, because that is what keeps the community functioning — in other words, Walzer's contribution to **what constitutes the common good**, not to what justice requires.

✅ The legitimate use here is as a refinement of the communitarian position: the common good is not one undifferentiated shared end, but a set of **sphere-appropriate** arrangements. That is also the cleanest available reply to the charge that communitarianism flattens a plural society into one purpose.

### 4. Taylor — situatedness as a condition of development

**Charles Taylor (*Philosophical Papers*; 1985)** **echoed MacIntyre's attack on the liberal conception of 'atomistic' individuals and confirmed the tenets of communitarianism.** Taylor argued that **if human beings want their genuine development, they must acknowledge first that they are situated in a society**, and that **they can realize their good only through cooperation in the pursuit of the common good** (PDF p.482).

⚠️ Taylor's printed contribution is narrower than his reputation and should be used as printed: the word is **development**, and the claim is that development is **conditional on acknowledging situatedness**. 🔑 That makes Taylor the natural bridge into any development-and-social-progress stem from the communitarian side, just as Ruskin at Lesson 9 is the bridge from the Gandhian side. 🔶 Same tagging correction as Sandel: he is printed, not a supplementary bridge.

The chapter then closes the perspective: **on the whole, communitarian notion of the common good requires the individual to pursue his goals within the structure of society, and to look for his good as part of the good of whole society.**

### 5. A Critical Appraisal — both halves, and why the positive half matters

🔶 The positive half is omitted by both owners, and omitting it costs marks because it shows the chapter crediting the perspective before judging it. The communitarian concept **insists on cooperation, and not competition between individuals, and thereby promotes social solidarity**. It **inspires the isolated individuals to establish cordial relations between each other, and shows them the way to obtain emotional security** (PDF p.482).

⚠️ Note the exact fit: the positive half answers the precise deficit Lesson 5 diagnosed. Liberal individualism left a person with **comfort at the expense of his emotional security**; communitarianism **shows them the way to obtain emotional security**. The appraisal is therefore internally coherent — the perspective delivers what it promised.

Then the verdict: **however, in spite of its strong ethical base, it has no mechanism to ensure that its principles will be adopted as the general rules of behaviour. In a nutshell, communitarianism embodies a strong moral philosophy, but it is not founded in equally strong political philosophy.**

🔑 Unpack the moral/political distinction, because unpacked it is a 15-mark-grade observation. A **moral** philosophy states what ought to be done and why. A **political** philosophy must additionally specify **how the ought is to be brought about** — through which institutions, under what authority, with what enforcement when people decline. Communitarianism asks for **first commitment to the community, and not to himself**, and then has nothing to say about the person who declines. ⚠️ The verdict is therefore not a complaint about vagueness; it identifies a **missing institutional layer**, which is also exactly the criticism the chapter records against the Gandhian perspective at Lesson 10 and, in a different form, against the Marxian vision. Lesson 10 makes that convergence explicit.

### 1. The thesis, and the head that does not describe it

**According to Marxist theory, since the division of human society into two antagonistic classes on the basis of ownership of private property, idea of the common good has become irrelevant.** It **can exist only in a classless society, i.e. either under primitive communism (which existed before the rise of civilization), or in a future communist society.** And since the state **is an instrument of the dominant class for the exploitation of the dependent class, a classless society does not need state power; it is bound to evolve into a stateless society**, under which **society uses its authority for the protection of common interests of all members of the community which represent the common good** (PDF pp.482-483).

📝 **One comprehension aid, stated once.** The printed head is **NECESSARY CONDITIONS FOR THE COMMON GOOD**, but the section does not set out conditions to be met; it argues that the common good is **unavailable** wherever class division exists. ✅ Name the mismatch once so the structure is legible; ❌ do not invent replacement wording for the head.

⚠️ Note how the thesis repositions the whole chapter. Lessons 4, 5 and 6 disagreed about *what constitutes* the common good while agreeing that the question is live. The Marxian perspective says that in a class society the individual **can discover his class interest** and nothing more — the question itself is premature. That is also why this perspective alone occupies a **negative** cell in Lesson 10's **Constitution** row.

### 2. Primitive communism, compressed to the claim that does work

The chapter narrates primitive communism at length — the instruments of labour, the common hunting and fishing, the sharing of the fruits. ⚠️ That narrative is dropped here; it is description, not argument. What survives is the structural chain, and it is worth stating as a chain because it is an **argument from material conditions rather than from virtue** (PDF p.483).

Production was **scarcely sufficient for the subsistence of each member of the community**; **there was no surplus that could be stored in private possession**; **hence, society was not divided into haves and have-nots**; **in the absence of class distinctions and consequent exploitation, there was no need for a special apparatus of coercion**; and the common affairs were **managed collectively or entrusted to the most respected and experienced members of the community**.

🔑 The load-bearing term is **storable surplus**. Classlessness there is a consequence of scarcity and non-storability, not of moral superiority — which is precisely why the chapter can treat the future communist society as requiring the *opposite* condition, fully developed productive forces, and still call both classless. ❌ No historical-materialist stage theory is developed here; that is Topic 04's.

### 3. The communist stage, and the gloss that answers the equity stem

Communist society **will come into existence after the socialist stage of historical development**. According to classical Marxism, when **all means of social production will be placed under social ownership**, **remnants of capitalism will be liquidated**, **labour will become compulsory for every able-bodied person**, and **forces of production will have been fully developed**, **the state will 'wither away'**. In that phase the economy is regulated by the principle **'from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs'** (PDF p.483).

🔶 Then Gauba's printed gloss, absent from both owners: **in communist society the distribution of burdens shall depend on abilities, while the distribution of benefits shall depend on needs. Benefits shall not be treated as the reward of contribution to the common good.**

🔑 That is the cleanest statement in either chapter of the **equity-against-equality** distinction a Marxian stem asks for: the two sides of the ledger run on **different criteria**, and the explicit severing of benefit from contribution is what distinguishes the principle from any desert-based rule. ⚠️ **One sentence, then stop.** The theory of justice is Topic 20's, and the equity-and-equality stem and the socialism-against-communism stem are both already claimed there; this lesson contributes the gloss and the withering-away conditions, and ❌ no second treatment.

The chapter adds the psychological assumption, which is the honest place to locate the difficulty: it is **the picture of an ideal society where men shall shed their selfishness and spontaneously contribute to the common good without the thought of the return**, using **their maximum abilities ... without any specific incentives**.

### 5. A Critical Appraisal — the mechanism that transfers

In a class-divided society, **as long as society is divided into antagonistic classes, it is not possible for the individual to discover the common good. At best, he can discover his class interest** — under capitalism the capitalist class's interest lies in maintaining the system and the working class's in overthrowing it. **The common good can only be realized in the communist society** (PDF p.486, printed 467).

**However, in actual practice the vision of communist society is nowhere realized.** ⚠️ Book-period evidence follows, dated to the 2009 edition: experience of **over seven decades of socialism in the former Soviet Union and over four decades in East Europe** has shown that it **never came close to the image of a classless society**.

🔑 Then the mechanism, which is the most transferable sentence in the lesson: **when economic power as the basis of class division is removed, political power brings a new class division into existence.** In due course, political power in socialist countries **proves to be more oppressive than that in liberal democracies** — because liberal democracy **at least provides for some mechanism of civil liberties which enable the citizens to exercise some control on political power**, whereas under the socialist system the new ruling class **not only appropriates economic resources of the state for personal enjoyment, it also suspends civil liberties**. Initially citizens are **compensated by the adequate provision of social and economic rights**, but **when these rights cannot be maintained due to increasing shortages, the system proves to be fragile** — **that is what precisely happened in the former Soviet Union and its satellites during 1989-91**. Printed verdict: **Marxian perspective on the common good must be taken with a pinch of salt.**

⚠️ Two cautions on use. First, the argument is **comparative and bounded**: it credits liberal democracy with *some* mechanism of civil liberties, not with having solved the problem — Lesson 4's appraisal already found liberalism defective on its own ground. Second, 🔑 the mechanism generalises beyond its historical setting: **removing one basis of stratification does not abolish stratification if another form of power remains concentrated**. That is the sentence worth carrying into an essay; the dated evidence stays dated.

The lesson is one doctrine with four parts: **to whom it is addressed**, **what two principles ground it**, **what it refuses**, and **how Gandhi himself answers the obvious objection**.

### 1. The essence claim, the context and the three keys

**Commitment to the common good is the essence of Gandhian philosophy** (PDF p.486). 🔶 **Mahatma Gandhi (1869-1948)** saw India's independence as an opportunity **to wipe every tear from every eye** — a clause absent from both owners and the best available opening line for any Gandhian stem, because it states the **object** of the project in one phrase. He wished **to transform the destiny of India at a critical juncture through moral regeneration**, at a time when India was **groaning under an oppressive foreign rule, abject poverty, vast social and economic inequalities** and was **also in the grip of communal tension and hatred**.

⚠️ The printed context is doing argumentative work and should not be skipped as background: it names **four simultaneous deficits**, and a doctrine addressed to all four cannot be purely economic. That is why Gandhi preached the gospel of **spiritualism, Ahimsa (non-violence), renunciation (non-possession), dignity of labour and moral courage, etc. for the uplift of man as well as society** — uplift of *man* and of *society*, in that order.

The chapter then states the architecture of the two Gandhi lessons: **his doctrine of trusteeship, his vision of a classless society and his concept of sarvodaya (uplift of all) hold the key to his idea of the common good** (PDF p.487).

### 2. The doctrine of trusteeship — address, demand and the two principles

**Gandhian doctrine of trusteeship is addressed to the conscience of the rich and resourceful members of society, particularly landlords and capitalists.** It urges them **not to consider themselves as the sole proprietors of their possessions, but only 'trustees' of a gift bestowed upon them by God for the service of humanity** (PDF p.487).

⚠️ Note the address, because it determines the doctrine's character. It is directed at **conscience**, not at a legislature or a court; its force is moral and its compliance voluntary. That is the root both of its distinctiveness and of the criticism recorded against it at Lesson 10. 🔑 Note also the structural echo of Lesson 5: liberals declare the individual **the sole proprietor of all his faculties**, and Gandhi refuses sole proprietorship of his **possessions**. The communitarian and Gandhian perspectives converge on refusing a proprietorship premise, which is why they share the same cell in two rows of Lesson 10's grid.

Two Sanskrit principles ground the demand, and 🔶 both are absent from the owners.

- **Non-possession (aparigraha)** implies that **worldly possessions make you morally deprave**. Hence **one should not take more than his immediate needs**, and to maintain one's moral strength **material things should be used with a spirit of renunciation**.
- **Non-stealing (asteya)** implies that **amassing of wealth, or even the possession of more than one's immediate needs amounts to theft because it is meant to fulfil others' needs**. In Gandhi's words: **"Earth has enough resources to satisfy everybody's need but not their greed."** **Any attempt to satisfy one's greed means theft.**

🔑 The theft argument is the sharpest thing in the lesson and is routinely misreported. The claim is not that property is wrong; it is that **a surplus beyond immediate need is already allocated** — it **is meant to fulfil others' needs** — so retaining it is a taking. That is a claim about the **destination** of resources, not about the institution of ownership.

The chapter adds Gauba's comparison: it **reminds us of nineteenth-century French philosopher, P.J. Proudhon's famous dictum: "Property is theft."** ⚠️ One clause only — and the comparison is useful precisely because the two differ. Proudhon indicts **property as such**; asteya indicts **surplus beyond immediate need**, which is why trusteeship can leave the holder in place while changing what he is. ➡️ The property-justification debate belongs to Topic 18.

### 3. The refusal that defines the doctrine

**However, Gandhi does not favour overthrowing the existing economic system. This system has become oppressive because of moral decline.** If **the organizers of agriculture and industry could be persuaded to act as public servants, they will win wide public esteem instead of the existing hatred. The feeling of class conflict will be replaced by the sentiment of class cooperation.** Gandhi wants a **'change of heart' of the rich and resourceful persons to enable the society to have full benefit of their talents and efforts without suffering injustice caused by the exploitation of the poor by the rich** (PDF p.487).

Evidence, printed: in **Amrita Bazar Patrika (1934)** — **"What is needed is not the extinction of landlords and capitalists, but a transformation of the existing relationship between them and the masses into something healthier and purer."**

🔑 This is the chapter's own printed contrast with Lesson 7, and the two should always be taught together because they share a diagnosis and split on the remedy.

| | Marxian (Lesson 7) | Gandhian (Lesson 8) |
|---|---|---|
| What is wrong | **Ownership of private property** divides society into **two antagonistic classes** | The system **has become oppressive because of moral decline** |
| What must change | The class structure; **remnants of capitalism liquidated** | The **relationship** — **not the extinction of landlords and capitalists, but a transformation** of it |
| Through what | Class struggle and a transition ending in a **stateless** society | **Persuasion** of conscience; a **change of heart**; organizers acting as **public servants** |

⚠️ This table is also the spine of any Gandhian-socialism stem: Gandhian socialism is distinctive precisely because it accepts a socialist *objective* while refusing the expropriative *method*. ➡️ Gandhian socialism as an ideology belongs to Topic 05; this lesson supplies its distributive content only.

### 4. 🔶 *The Modern Review* (1935) — the highest-value item in the chapter

Gandhi **realized that this was by no means an easy task**, and the chapter prints his own concession: **"You may say that trusteeship is a legal fiction. But if people meditate over it constantly and try to act up to it, then life on earth would be governed far more by love than it is at present. Absolute trusteeship is an abstraction like Euclid's definition of a point, and is equally unattainable. But if we strive for it, we shall be able to go further in realizing a state of equality on earth than by any other method."** (PDF p.487).

🔑 Teach this as an **answer-writing device**, not as a quotation to be reproduced. A *critically examine* directive on trusteeship is normally answered with "it is idealistic and difficult to implement", which earns little because it is obvious. The printed passage does three things that an examiner rewards.

1. It supplies the **strongest objection from inside the doctrine** — Gandhi names it himself as a **legal fiction**, and concedes that absolute trusteeship is **unattainable**.
2. It **changes the standard** of assessment from attainment to **direction**: the analogy with **Euclid's definition of a point** makes unattainability a feature of ideal constructs rather than a defect peculiar to this one.
3. It makes a **comparative** claim that can be tested: striving for it takes us **further in realizing a state of equality on earth than by any other method** — which is a claim about relative performance, and is exactly where Lesson 7's appraisal becomes relevant as the rival method's record.

⚠️ The honest residual criticism survives all three: a standard of direction cannot say **how much** progress suffices, and the doctrine still supplies no remedy when conscience does not move. That is the criticism the closing grid records.

### 5. The vision of a classless society, compressed

**Gandhi's faith in human equality made him a strong votary of a classless society. This means elimination of discrimination on any irrelevant ground.** In a country like India people are discriminated on many grounds — **religion, caste, sex** — and **Gandhi taught us to do away with such discrimination** (PDF p.488).

Two quotations are printed and are compressed here to a clause each, because the substantive debates belong elsewhere. On **equality of all religions**: **"Temples or mosques or churches ... I make no distinction between these different abodes of God. They are what faith has made them. They are an answer to man's craving somehow to reach the Unseen."** (***The Mind of Mahatma Gandhi***, compiled by **R.K. Prabhu and U.R. Rao**; **1945**). On man and woman: **fundamentally man and woman are one**, **the soul in both is the same**, **each is complement of the other** — but **somehow or other man has dominated woman from ages past, and so woman has developed an inferiority complex**, though **the seers among men have recognized her equal status** (***Selections from Gandhi*** by **Nirmal Kumar Bose**; **1948**).

⚠️ Note that the **classless** society is defined here by the **elimination of irrelevant grounds of discrimination**, not by economic levelling — which is why trusteeship and the classless society are compatible rather than competing keys. ❌ No Gandhi–Ambedkar debate, ❌ no annihilation of caste, ❌ no secularism doctrine and ❌ no gender theory follows; those belong to the Caste, Gender and Humanism-Secularism-Multiculturalism owners. ➡️ The caste-and-division-of-labour argument is picked up at Lesson 9, where the chapter attaches it to bread labour.

### Concept check

**Question:** How does the doctrine of trusteeship differ from the Marxian remedy, and why does Gandhi's own concession strengthen rather than weaken it?

**Model answer:** They share a diagnosis and divide on the remedy. The Marxian account holds that **since the division of human society into two antagonistic classes on the basis of ownership of private property, idea of the common good has become irrelevant**, recoverable only in a classless society reached by liquidating **remnants of capitalism**. Gandhi locates the fault elsewhere: the system **has become oppressive because of moral decline**, and therefore he **does not favour overthrowing** it. Trusteeship addresses **the conscience of the rich and resourceful members of society, particularly landlords and capitalists**, urging them to see themselves not as **the sole proprietors of their possessions, but only 'trustees' of a gift bestowed upon them by God for the service of humanity**. Its ground is **asteya**: possession beyond immediate need **amounts to theft because it is meant to fulfil others' needs** — **Earth has enough resources to satisfy everybody's need but not their greed**. Its mechanism is persuasion: organizers acting as **public servants** win **wide public esteem instead of the existing hatred**, so that **class conflict** is replaced by **class cooperation** through a **change of heart**. The 1934 formulation is exact — **not the extinction of landlords and capitalists, but a transformation of the existing relationship**. ⚠️ The concession strengthens the doctrine by pre-empting its strongest objection and changing the standard: Gandhi himself allows that it may be called **a legal fiction** and that **absolute trusteeship is an abstraction like Euclid's definition of a point, and is equally unattainable** — then argues that **if we strive for it, we shall be able to go further in realizing a state of equality on earth than by any other method**. Unattainability becomes a property of ideal constructs, and the test becomes comparative — a test the Marxian record, **nowhere realized**, does not obviously pass.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not describe trusteeship as a scheme of voluntary charity. The doctrine rests on a claim of **entitlement**, not generosity: surplus beyond immediate need **is meant to fulfil others' needs**, so withholding it **amounts to theft**. Reporting it as philanthropy loses the asteya argument and makes the Proudhon comparison unintelligible.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Absolute trusteeship is an abstraction like Euclid's definition of a point, and is equally unattainable." Critically examine trusteeship as a theory of the common good. *(Critically examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The objection is Gandhi's own, which is why the examination must go past it. Trusteeship addresses **the conscience** of the rich, **particularly landlords and capitalists**, asking them to hold possessions as **'trustees' of a gift bestowed upon them by God for the service of humanity**; its ground is **asteya**, since surplus beyond immediate need **amounts to theft because it is meant to fulfil others' needs**. Gandhi concedes it may be **a legal fiction** and is **equally unattainable**, then re-standardises: **if we strive for it, we shall be able to go further in realizing a state of equality on earth than by any other method** — a comparative claim, strengthened by the rival method's record of being **nowhere realized**. ⚠️ Two criticisms survive. A direction-standard fixes no threshold of sufficiency. And the doctrine **relies mainly on moral persuasion**, with no remedy when conscience does not move.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks: 3 + 3 + 2 + 2):** 3 for the doctrine stated with its address and its **asteya** ground, treating surplus as already allocated rather than as a candidate for charity; 3 for using the concession as a device — objection conceded, standard shifted from attainment to direction, claim made comparative — rather than merely quoting it; 2 for one accurate contrast with the Marxian remedy, **transformation** against **extinction**; 2 for a residual criticism that is not defeated by the concession, namely the absent threshold or the absent remedy against an unmoved conscience. Deduct 3 for importing satyagraha, swaraj, the village republic or the Tolstoy comparison, which belong to Topic 05, and 2 for presenting trusteeship as philanthropy.

The lesson has one argumentative spine: bread labour **obliterates ranking**, sarvodaya **uplifts all without conflict**, and the break with utilitarianism is a **four-step structure**, not a rejection.

### 1. Bread labour, and the mechanism that makes it anti-caste

The chapter introduces bread labour inside the classless-society section, as the answer to **caste and division of labour** as a ground of discrimination. His gospel of **'bread labour'** **expected everybody to do physical labour toward production, at least to compensate for the bread that he consumes**, where **bread is symbolic of various items of one's physical consumption** (PDF p.488).

The evidence is from ***Harijan* (1935)**: **"If all laboured for their bread and no more, then there would be enough food and enough leisure for all. Then there would be no cry of over-population, no disease and no misery as we see around. Such labour will be the highest form of sacrifice. Men will do many other things either through their bodies or through their minds, but all this will be labour of love for the common good. There will be no rich and no poor, none high and none low, no touchable and no untouchable ..."**

Then the three printed purposes: the principle **is designed to promote three things: (a) simple living among all citizens; (b) dignity of labour; and (c) a sense of equality transcending the prevailing division of labour** (PDF p.489).

🔑 🔶 And then the mechanism both owners drop, which is what makes purpose (c) intelligible: **originally caste symbolized the division of labour which strayed on to division of society into high and low ranks. The principle of bread labour would serve to obliterate this ranking.**

⚠️ Read the mechanism carefully, because it is the only Topic-21 route to any caste-related stem and it must be used precisely. The argument is that caste began as a **functional** division and **strayed** into a **hierarchical** one; bread labour attacks the hierarchy by removing its material basis — if everyone performs physical labour, no occupation can mark rank. It is therefore an **anti-ranking instrument**, not a dietary ethic or a productivity rule. ❌ No caste sociology, ❌ no Gandhi–Ambedkar debate and ❌ no annihilation-of-caste argument follows; those belong to the Caste owner.

### 2. *From Yervada Mandir*, and the clause that closes the trusteeship loop

The economic half is evidenced from ***From Yervada Mandir*** (published **1945**): **"There is a world-wide conflict between capital and labour, and the poor envy the rich. If all worked for their bread, distinctions of rank would be obliterated; the rich would still be there, but they would deem themselves only trustees of their property, and would use it mainly in the public interest."** (PDF p.489).

🔑 Two things make this the right place to close Lesson 8's loop. First, it shows that bread labour and trusteeship are **one doctrine from two ends** — universal labour obliterates rank, and the holders who remain become trustees. Second, Gandhi's phrase **use it mainly in the public interest** collides with Lesson 1's triad, where public interest was **a matter of judgment by a competent authority**. ⚠️ Flag the collision and resolve it: Gandhi uses the term in its **moral** sense, which is exactly the sense Lesson 1 assigned to the **common good**, and the chapter's own translation rule explains why the two vocabularies meet — what is the common good in moral philosophy becomes the public interest in politics and administration. 📝 This is the second such collision in the chapter, after Barry's at Lesson 4, and noticing both is a mark of source control.

The chapter adds that **Ahimsa (non-violence) is also conducive to the creation of a classless society**: when a person **avows not to harm anybody, not to cause injury to anybody and not to hurt anybody's feeling, what else is left for the realization of a classless society?** ⚠️ Note that this is where the Gandhian cell of Lesson 10's **Attitude toward Conflict** row comes from — adherence to Ahimsa and the common good **will eliminate all conflict** — and it is also the clause most exposed to the owner-only objection quarantined at Lesson 10.

### 3. Sarvodaya — three renderings, a target, and a refusal

The term **may be rendered as 'uplift of all', 'rise of all' or 'awakening of all'**, and **all the meanings of this term closely correspond to each other** (PDF p.489). Its target is stated as a social fact: in a society **where only the few are endowed with knowledge, power, prestige and wealth, and a very large number are languishing, sarvodaya wants them to rise above**.

❌ Then the refusal that distinguishes it from every conflict-based remedy: **since it believes in uplift of all, it does not envisage a conflict between the high and the low, between the rich and the poor.** The reason is methodological and is printed: **as a votary of purity of means as well as end, Gandhi was convinced that violent means cannot be used to achieve a non-violent end.**

⚠️ The reciprocal clause is what stops sarvodaya from collapsing into either charity or quietism: while the principle **gives prominence to the uplift of the deprived and the underprivileged sections**, it **also envisages that the rich would offer their riches for the welfare of the poor and service of the community**, so that they **will not only give a proof of self-control and magnanimity, but also raise their own spiritual level**. 🔑 Note the structure — the transfer is required of the rich **and** is described as a gain to them. That is the Gandhian answer to the incentive objection Lesson 2 refuted on liberal ground, and the two can be deployed together.

### 4. 🔶 The Ruskin origin — absent from both owners

**The idea behind sarvodaya was inspired by John Ruskin's *Unto This Last* (1860).** Gandhi **came across this work in the midst of passive resistance against the racist regime of South Africa**. It **contained a message of uplift of the last man or the most neglected lot**, and he was **so impressed by this work that he published its summary in his Gujarati articles under the title of *Sarvodaya***. Ruskin's own doctrine is printed: he **rejected the cult of machinery and consumerism as well as the idea of economic man and mercantile economy**, and **advocated simple technology, manual labour, communal enterprise and measurement of value in terms of quality of life** — and Gandhi **relied on these teachings to sharpen his own thinking on various issues of social reform** (PDF p.489).

🔑 This paragraph is the topic's cleanest bridge to any **development-and-social-progress** stem, and it is better than a current substitute because it supplies a **measure**: value in terms of **quality of life** rather than output, with **economic man** and **mercantile economy** named as the rejected alternatives. ⚠️ It is also the counterpart from the Gandhian side to Taylor's development claim at Lesson 6 and to Galbraith's public-services clause at Lesson 2 — three independent routes to the proposition that growth is not development, and the Ruskin route is the only one that supplies a positive measure.

⚠️ Handling note: the phrase **passive resistance** is the chapter's own and is used here only to date the encounter. ❌ Satyagraha as a method is Topic 05's and is not developed.

### 5. The break with utilitarianism, taught as a structure

**Gandhian concept of sarvodaya rejects the utilitarian view of 'greatest happiness of the greatest number' in favour of 'greatest good of all'** (PDF p.490, printed 471). The evidence is from ***Young India* (1926)**: **"A votary of Ahimsa cannot subscribe to the utilitarian formula (of the greatest good of the greatest number). He will strive for the greatest good of all and die in the attempt to realize the idea. He will, therefore, be willing to die, so that others may live ... The greatest good of all inevitably includes the good of the greatest number, and therefore, he and the utilitarian will converge in many points in their career, but there does come a time when they must part company, and even work in opposite directions. The utilitarian to be logical will never sacrifice himself. The absolutist will even sacrifice himself."**

Gauba's gloss follows: the term **'absolutist' stands for the person who has absolute faith in Ahimsa — one who will never depart from the path of Ahimsa in deference to any other ideal**. And the conclusion: **the message of self-sacrifice for the greatest good of all is the essence of sarvodaya.**

🔑 🔶 **Teach the four steps as a structure, because the structure is the answer.**

| Step | Printed claim | What it does in an answer |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Includes** | The greatest good of all **inevitably includes** the good of the greatest number | Refuses the easy contrast; sarvodaya is **not** indifferent to aggregate welfare |
| **2. Converges** | He and the utilitarian **will converge in many points in their career** | Concedes extensive practical agreement — which is why most policies cannot distinguish the two |
| **3. Parts company** | **There does come a time when they must part company** | Locates the disagreement at a specific point rather than across the board |
| **4. Works in opposite directions** | The utilitarian **to be logical will never sacrifice himself. The absolutist will even sacrifice himself** | Identifies the point: the treatment of the **agent's own** interest |

⚠️ Both owners reduce this to "rejects the utilitarian greatest happiness", which loses step 1 and therefore the whole argument. An answer built on the four steps is structurally superior to one built on a contrast, because it explains **where** and **why** the two diverge instead of asserting that they do.

🔑 Finally, carry Lesson 2's Rawls clause back here in one sentence, because the convergence is the mark-winning observation: **this chapter refutes the utilitarian reading of the common good twice, from opposite directions.** At Lesson 2, through **Rawls**, because the greatest happiness **could be interpreted to mean injustice to the minority** — the formula asks **too little of the majority's conscience toward others**. Here, through **Gandhi**, because the utilitarian **to be logical will never sacrifice himself** — the formula asks **too little of the agent toward himself**. Naming that pair converts a descriptive answer into an analytical one.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain the relation between sarvodaya and utilitarianism as the chapter prints it, and say why the printed structure is better than a simple contrast.

**Model answer:** The chapter does not print an opposition; it prints a **convergence that ends in divergence**, and the sequence is the argument. Sarvodaya **rejects the utilitarian view of 'greatest happiness of the greatest number' in favour of 'greatest good of all'**, but Gandhi's own statement in ***Young India* (1926)** begins by conceding the overlap: **the greatest good of all inevitably includes the good of the greatest number**. Because it includes it, **he and the utilitarian will converge in many points in their career** — the two will approve the same measures over a wide range. **But there does come a time when they must part company, and even work in opposite directions**, and the chapter identifies the point exactly: **the utilitarian to be logical will never sacrifice himself. The absolutist will even sacrifice himself.** Gauba glosses **absolutist** as **the person who has absolute faith in Ahimsa — one who will never depart from the path of Ahimsa in deference to any other ideal**, and concludes that **the message of self-sacrifice for the greatest good of all is the essence of sarvodaya**. ⚠️ The structure is better than a contrast for two reasons. It is accurate — a flat opposition cannot explain why a Gandhian and a utilitarian agree about most public measures. And it locates the disagreement at one identifiable place, the treatment of the **agent's own** interest, which is testable in a case rather than merely asserted. 🔑 Read it beside the chapter's other refutation: at the start, **Rawls** shows that the greatest happiness **could be interpreted to mean injustice to the minority**. Utilitarianism is therefore faulted twice and in opposite directions — for asking too little of the majority toward others, and too little of the agent toward himself.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not write that sarvodaya rejects the good of the greatest number. It **inevitably includes** it; what it rejects is the formula's **sufficiency**, and specifically its inability to require self-sacrifice. Dropping the inclusion clause is the error both canonical owners make, and it destroys the convergence step that makes the comparison intelligible.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Explain how bread labour and sarvodaya together constitute the Gandhian account of the common good. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** They supply two halves — the **levelling instrument** and the **objective**. Bread labour expects **everybody** to do **physical labour toward production, at least to compensate for the bread that he consumes**, promoting **(a) simple living among all citizens; (b) dignity of labour; and (c) a sense of equality transcending the prevailing division of labour**. Its mechanism is anti-hierarchical: caste **originally symbolized the division of labour which strayed on to division of society into high and low ranks**, which bread labour **would serve to obliterate** — so that **the rich would still be there, but they would deem themselves only trustees of their property**. Sarvodaya states the objective — **uplift of all**, **awakening of all** — against a society where **only the few** hold **knowledge, power, prestige and wealth**. ⚠️ It refuses conflict, since **violent means cannot be used to achieve a non-violent end**, which is also where its reliance on persuasion becomes criticisable.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks: 3 + 3 + 2 + 2):** 3 for bread labour stated with all three printed purposes and with the compensation clause, not as a general endorsement of manual work; 3 for the caste mechanism — functional division **straying** into ranking, and bread labour **obliterating** it — which is what makes purpose (c) intelligible; 2 for sarvodaya's renderings and its target, with the **trustees** clause used to join the two halves; 2 for a qualification naming the refusal of conflict and its cost in reliance on persuasion. Deduct 3 for drifting into caste sociology or the Gandhi–Ambedkar debate, which belong to other owners, and 2 for presenting sarvodaya as simply the opposite of utilitarianism.

**Ultimate Nature of the Good** (**Material · Material · Material · Spiritual**); and **Grounds of Criticism**. 🔑 The first observation is that the Ultimate-Nature row is **three against one**, and that it is the **only** row on which the communitarian and Gandhian perspectives differ — they share both the Object and the Constitution cells — so that row alone explains why Gandhi can make **self-sacrifice** the essence of his account while the others cannot. 🔑 The second is that three of the four criticisms are the **same** criticism: **no machinery to ensure individual Commitment to the Community**, a vision that **seems Unrealizable**, and reliance **mainly on Moral Persuasion** with **No Effective Machinery to Realize the Ideal in Practice** are all objections to a missing implementing mechanism, while the liberal criticism — that conformity to the **Market Society model may destroy Creative Freedom of the Individual** — is about the **outcome** of a mechanism that works. ⚠️ Together they reorganise the comparison: the operative division is not individual against community, but one perspective that can be implemented and produces the wrong result against three that would produce the right result if they could be implemented. ⚠️ The required counterweight is owner-only and must be labelled: the two **No real conflict** cells risk treating real hierarchy as misunderstanding, which is why common-good politics must be disciplined by rights, dissent and plural representation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read the identical communitarian and Gandhian cells as making the two perspectives interchangeable. They share the Object, Constitution and broad Conflict cells and divide on **Ultimate Nature** — **Material** against **Spiritual** — and that single difference is what produces the different criticisms, machinery in one case and moral persuasion in the other.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** Compare any two perspectives on the common good and state which criticism each attracts. *(Compare — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** Take the **liberal** and the **Gandhian**, the grid's extremes. **Object**: the individual's good against the community's good. **Constitution**: the **aggregate of individuals' goods**, reached where interests reconcile, against the **community as the source of everybody's good**, realised through trusteeship, bread labour and sarvodaya. **Conflict**: **not very deep; may be resolved through discussion** against **no real conflict**, since **adherence to Ahimsa and the common good will eliminate all conflict**. **Ultimate nature**: **Material** against **Spiritual** — the decisive line, since the liberal view insists the common good is **not a metaphysical element**, while Gandhi makes **self-sacrifice** its essence. ⚠️ The criticisms differ in kind. Liberalism is faulted for an **outcome**: conformity to the market model **may destroy creative freedom of the individual**, which is why it is acceptable only if **linked with the concept of welfare state**. # CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Three checks: two consolidate a block, and the third spans the whole arc. Each model is held within 250 words.

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 to 3, the apparatus

**Question:** Build the apparatus in one chain, from the three-term distinction to the method of pursuit, naming the clause that carries each step.

**Model answer:** One: the three terms differ in kind — the common good is **normative**, concerned with **moral standards** for **philosophical discussion**; the common interest is **empirical**, the understanding of **persons likely to be affected**, and **can be empirically ascertained**; the public interest is **a matter of judgment by a competent authority** sensitive to **needs and aspirations**. Two: they translate but do not coincide — the common good **in moral philosophy becomes the public interest in politics and administration**, yet **all references to the public interest imply the common good** while **all references to the common good cannot be expressed in terms of public interest**, proved by the **soldier**, **quarantine** and **bribe** cases. Three: three equations are refused — not the **state's** good, since **a tyranny may operate against the common good itself**; not the **majority's**, since it **may be invoked to stop injustice against minority**; not civil society's, since **any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good**. Four: positively — the goal regarded by the **consciousness of the community** as conducive to the welfare of the **whole community**, **transcending the immediate interests** of individuals and groups, a resulting loss **not treated as a matter of settling individual claims**. Five: the tests — it **shall not coincide with the interests of a particular group**, judged by **the merit of its beneficiaries rather than the claims of its opponents**, failing where an arrangement is **repugnant to the interests of ... consumers**. Six: the method — **the process of consensus**, achieved **only as a process**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat steps three and four as alternatives, one negative and one positive. The refusals are what make the definition non-trivial: without them, "the welfare of the whole community" is satisfied by any majority decision or any negotiated settlement, which is precisely what the chapter denies.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 4 to 7, three answers and three verdicts

**Question:** Show how the liberal, communitarian and Marxian perspectives disagree about what constitutes the common good, and give the chapter's own verdict on each.

**Model answer:** **Liberal**: the common good is **the aggregate of individual goods**, **not a metaphysical element beyond the experience of ordinary persons**; rational agents see the need of **rules necessary for securing the good of all**, and **the point of their reconciliation represents the common good**, extended through **interest groups** to a common interest **coterminous with the common good**. Verdict: reward follows **market demand, beyond individual's control** — **Athens** against **Sparta** — so it is acceptable **only on the condition that it is linked with the concept of welfare state**. **Communitarian**: aggregation fails at the input — **if isolated individuals cannot secure their own good by their efforts, how can we arrive at the common good by aggregating the outcome of their efforts?** Only a community realizes it, **from which they would be able to derive their individual goods**, requiring **first commitment to the community, and not to himself**; **MacIntyre** warns that rights **without realizing their duties** end in **social disintegration and moral disaster**. Verdict: **in spite of its strong ethical base, it has no mechanism** — **a strong moral philosophy, but it is not founded in equally strong political philosophy**. **Marxian**: the question is premature — with class division on **ownership of private property** the idea **has become irrelevant** and the individual **can discover his class interest** only. Verdict: **nowhere realized**, because **when economic power as the basis of class division is removed, political power brings a new class division into existence** — **a pinch of salt**. ⚠️ Two verdicts fault a mechanism; one faults an outcome.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the three become a progression in which each corrects the last. The chapter appraises each separately and faults all three, and its own preferred correction to liberalism — the welfare state — is not supplied by either of the other two.

### Cumulative check C — the whole arc, Lessons 1 to 10

**Question:** In one connected argument, move from the definitional question to a qualified verdict on whether the common good can be realised, marking where each lesson does work.

**Model answer:** The common good is **normative**, distinct from the **empirical** common interest and from public interest as **judgment by a competent authority**, and wider than both (L1). It is not the state's, the majority's or a bargained good, each refused with a reason. Positively it is the goal regarded by the **consciousness of the community** as conducive to the welfare of the **whole community**, a loss **not treated as a matter of settling individual claims**; tested by refusing to let it **coincide with the interests of a particular group**, by weighing **the merit of its beneficiaries**, and by asking who bears a cost **repugnant to ... the general public** (L2). It is pursued through **consensus**, which in modern societies exists **only as a process** (L3). Four answers follow. Liberal aggregation works as a mechanism, fails as a measure, since reward tracks **market demand, beyond individual's control** — hence the **welfare state** condition (L4). Communitarians refute aggregation at its input and ground the good in community (L5), evidenced by MacIntyre, Sandel, Walzer and Taylor, defeated by having **no mechanism** (L6). Marxism denies the question is answerable, and its own record answers it (L7). Gandhi relocates it: trusteeship with **asteya**, conceded **unattainable** but directional (L8), bread labour **obliterating ranking**, and sarvodaya which **includes** the utilitarian end, then **parts company** (L9). ⚠️ Verdict (L10): one machinery works and misfires; three would work if any existed — the common good is a usable **standard** and an unsecured **achievement**, disciplined by rights and dissent: the owners' addition, not the chapter's.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the chain become a tour. Each step is load-bearing: remove the triad and public interest swallows the concept; remove the refusals and the majority supplies it; remove the individual-claims clause and the topic collapses into justice; remove the process-verdict and consensus becomes unanimity; remove the printed appraisals and four positions become four summaries with no verdict.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ Original practice prompts written for this edition. They are **not** previous-year questions and must not be cited as such. The six items are **demand-sized to the verified weighting**: the pressure in this topic is back-loaded and concentrated at two ends — Gandhi, who carries the only 20-mark anchor, and the duties-against-rights axis, which carries two 15-markers and two 10-markers — with the concept apparatus carrying the entire GS-IV cluster. The practice follows that distribution and ❌ does not give the liberal or Marxian perspectives a dedicated item, because neither carries a direct question inside this topic.

**Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish the common good from the public interest, and explain why the distinction matters for a public official. *(Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** They differ in **kind**, in **reach** and in **who settles them**. Kind: the common good is **normative** — **moral standards** for **philosophical discussion** — while the public interest is **a matter of judgment by a competent authority** **sensitive to the needs and aspirations of the people**. Reach: **all references to the public interest imply the common good**, but **all references to the common good cannot be expressed in terms of public interest** — a **soldier** dying for his country acts **for the common good, not 'in public interest'**. Settlement: the common good is **subject to constant debate**; the public interest is **usually determined by a competent authority**, disputes **resolved through discussion** with those affected. ⚠️ For an official this is operative: an **official who takes a bribe for the welfare of his family** is **prosecuted in public interest because he acts against the common good** — authority is justified by the wider standard it serves.

**Scoring note:** a **Distinguish** directive is answered by differentia, not by two definitions. The three axes — kind, reach, settlement — supply them, and the bribe case shows the distinction doing work rather than sitting in the answer.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** How far does the concept of the common good protect minorities? *(Answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** It protects them conceptually, not institutionally. Conceptually the protection is part of the definition rather than an exception to it: the common good **cannot be equated with the good of the majority because the common good may be invoked to stop injustice against minority** — so it is a ground on which a majority decision may be **resisted**. The chapter reinforces this by refusing the utilitarian reading: **Rawls** has shown that **the greatest happiness of the greatest number could be interpreted to mean injustice to the minority**, and a minority sacrificed for **so-called general happiness** is treated **against the spirit of the common good**. ⚠️ Institutionally the chapter supplies nothing. Its communitarian and Gandhian cells read **no real conflict**, and its communitarian verdict concedes **no mechanism** to secure commitment. ⚠️ The further discipline — rights, dissent and plural representation, failing which common-good language becomes **moralized coercion** — is the owners' analytical addition, labelled as such.

**Scoring note:** the answer earns its marks by separating a conceptual safeguard from an institutional one, and by labelling the owner-only half instead of attributing it to the chapter. An answer that presents the safeguard as Gauba's own is factually wrong, however persuasive it reads.

**Practice question 3 [15]:** "The communitarian conception of the common good is a strong moral philosophy without an equally strong political philosophy." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The verdict is the chapter's own; a critical examination must first establish how strong the moral philosophy is. Its diagnosis is internal: liberalism **undermines individual's affinity with the community**, and comfort is bought **at the expense of his emotional security** — hence self-interest **cannot secure good life in the fullest sense of the term**. Its central challenge follows: **if isolated individuals cannot secure their own good by their efforts, how can we arrive at the common good by aggregating the outcome of their efforts?** The alternative inverts the order — pooled effort realizes a common good **from which they would be able to derive their individual goods** — and rests on a denial: while liberals make the individual **the sole proprietor of all his faculties**, communitarians stress his **indebtedness to society**. Its exponents are precise. **MacIntyre**: rights pursued **without realizing their duties** produce **social disintegration and moral disaster**. **Sandel**: **'embeddedness'** alone generates a **'deeper commonality'** informed by **'shared self-understanding'**. **Walzer**: criteria **according to the proper spheres of their application**. **Taylor**: development requires acknowledging one is **situated in a society**. The appraisal credits the delivery — **cooperation, and not competition**, **social solidarity**, **emotional security** — repairing exactly the deficit diagnosed. ⚠️ The deficiency is a missing layer, not a vague principle: it **has no mechanism to ensure that its principles will be adopted as the general rules of behaviour**, and says nothing about the person who declines. ⚠️ Green shows the cost of supplying one coercively, since obligation holds only for laws that **conform to the common good**.

**Scoring note:** a **critically examine** directive requires the positive case at full strength before the verdict. The decisive move is naming the deficiency as **institutional** — silence about the non-complier — rather than as vagueness, and the Green clause prevents the easy conclusion that the state should simply enforce the ideal.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** Examine the claim that duty and accountability should be given priority over rights for the better functioning of a State. *(Examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The claim is defensible on a communitarian reading; the chapter supplies the argument and its limit. The argument denies the premise behind rights-first reasoning: while liberals **declare the individual to be the sole proprietor of all his faculties**, communitarians **focus on his indebtedness to society**, and while liberals **insist on individual's rights and liberties**, communitarians **emphasize his duties and obligations**. If the capacities a person exercises are owed to others, an unconditional claim over their use is not self-evident. The consequence of ignoring this is a prediction: if the state **treats individuals as disconnected entities and lets them loose to realize their rights without realizing their duties, the result would be social disintegration and moral disaster**. ⚠️ Two limits prevent a licence. First, the priority is **derivational**, not coercive: **Green** bounds obligation by the common good — the state **is authorized to make only those laws which promote the common good**, the individual **is obliged to abide by only those laws which conform to** it, and **if an individual thinks that he can protect the common good more effectively by opposing a particular order of the state, his political obligation does not stop him from going ahead**. Second, the chapter's verdict denies it an enforcement route: it **has no mechanism to ensure that its principles will be adopted as general rules of behaviour**. ⚠️ Verdict: duties are **prior in justification** and cannot be made prior **by compulsion**; common-good language unchecked by rights and dissent becomes moralized coercion — the owners' caution, not the chapter's.

**Scoring note:** an **Examine** directive rewards a verdict with a distinction inside it. Separating *prior in justification* from *prior by compulsion* is that distinction; Green's opposition clause is what makes it textual rather than invented.

**Practice question 5 [20]:** State and examine the Gandhian account of the common good, and assess whether it can be realised. *(Examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** **Commitment to the common good is the essence of Gandhian philosophy**, and three keys constitute it. **Trusteeship** addresses **the conscience of the rich**, **particularly landlords and capitalists**, asking them to be not **sole proprietors** but **'trustees' of a gift bestowed by God for the service of humanity**. It rests on **asteya**: possession beyond immediate need **amounts to theft because it is meant to fulfil others' needs** — **Earth has enough resources to satisfy everybody's need but not their greed**. Its method refuses expropriation: the system is oppressive **because of moral decline**, so **what is needed is not the extinction of landlords and capitalists, but a transformation of the existing relationship**, replacing **class conflict** with **class cooperation** through a **change of heart**. **Bread labour** is the levelling instrument — **simple living**, **dignity of labour** and **a sense of equality transcending the prevailing division of labour** — because caste **originally symbolized the division of labour which strayed on to division of society into high and low ranks**, which it **obliterates**. **Sarvodaya** states the objective — **uplift of all** — refusing conflict, since **violent means cannot be used to achieve a non-violent end**, and measuring value, after **Ruskin**, as **quality of life**. ⚠️ Realisability is the weak point, conceded by Gandhi: trusteeship is **an abstraction like Euclid's definition of a point, and is equally unattainable**, defensible only because **if we strive for it, we shall be able to go further ... than by any other method**. The printed criticism stands: it **relies mainly on moral persuasion**, with **no effective machinery**.

**Scoring note:** a 20-mark Gandhian stem is won by sequence and by the concession. Following the chapter's own order — trusteeship, classless society through bread labour, sarvodaya — keeps the three keys distinct, and raising the Euclid concession before the examiner does converts the standard from attainment to direction, which is the only defensible ground for the doctrine.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** "The common good is a usable standard but an unsecured achievement." Critically examine with reference to all four perspectives. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Usable as a standard: the chapter equips it with tests. It is distinguished from the **empirical** common interest and from public interest as **judgment by a competent authority**; it is refused to the state (**a tyranny may operate against the common good itself**), to the majority (it **may be invoked to stop injustice against minority**) and to bargaining (**any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good**); and it is applied by refusing to let it **coincide with the interests of a particular group**, weighing **the merit of its beneficiaries**, and rejecting arrangements **repugnant to the interests of the large body of consumers**. Unsecured as an achievement, because each perspective's constitution fails on the chapter's own appraisal. Liberal **aggregate of individuals' goods** works but lets reward follow **market demand, which is beyond individual's control**, so it is acceptable only if **linked with the concept of welfare state**. Communitarian **community as the source of everybody's good** has **no machinery to ensure individual Commitment to the Community**. Marxian **common good not feasible in a class-divided society** proves **nowhere realized**: **when economic power as the basis of class division is removed, political power brings a new class division into existence**. Gandhian **relies mainly on moral persuasion**, with **no effective machinery**. ⚠️ Decisively, three of the four criticisms are the **same** criticism; only liberalism's concerns an outcome.⚠️ Hence the verdict: the concept disciplines argument more reliably than power, and the owners' safeguard of rights, dissent and plural representation is the necessary, non-Gauba close.

**Scoring note:** the directive demands a criterion before a verdict, and the criterion here is the standard/achievement split stated in the prompt. The machinery observation is the analytical turn that lifts the answer above a four-paragraph survey, and labelling the closing safeguard as owner-only is a factual requirement, not a stylistic one.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. The three terms, and the rule that joins them

- **Common good** — **normative**; **moral standards which are the subject of philosophical discussion**.
- **Common interest** — **empirical**; the understanding of **persons likely to be affected by a proposed decision**; **can be empirically ascertained**.
- **Public interest** — **a matter of judgment by a competent authority** expected to be **sensitive to the needs and aspirations of the people**; appeals lie to that authority; disputes **resolved through discussion between the decision-makers and those affected**.
- Translation rule: the common good **in moral philosophy** becomes the public interest **in politics and administration**.
- Comprehensiveness rule: **all** public-interest references imply the common good; **not all** common-good references can be expressed as public interest.
- Cases: **soldier** (common good only) · **quarantine** (public interest **which also implies** the common good) · **bribe-taking official** (prosecuted **in public interest because he acts against the common good**).
- Maxim: **a good citizen always puts the common good before his own or his family's interest**.

## B. Origin clauses carried for exam use only

- **Aristotle**: the state exists **for the sake of the good life**, to promote **moral objectives, justice and the common good**; the **'right'** constitution pursues the common good, **which implied the good of the whole community**; the **'perverted'** does not.
- **D.D. Raphael, *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976)**: the state **lays down laws, backed by force**, requiring everyone to **refrain** from crime and torts that **harm** the common good and to **contribute in taxes and other imposts** to services — **defence, public utility, social services** — that **promote** it.
- **Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762)**: indignation at **the selfishness of men** and at interests that **appropriate the sacred name of the public good**. **Green (1882)**: the state as **an instrument for the promotion of the common good**. This tradition **led to the development of communitarian perspective**.

## C. The three refusals, each with its reason

|---|---|
| The good of the **state** | **A state like a tyranny may operate against the common good itself** |
| The good of the **majority** | The common good **may be invoked to stop injustice against minority** |
| The common interest of **civil society** | It need not have one; reconciliation may be **bargaining**, and **any type of bargaining is not compatible with the common good** |

## E. The working definition and the three tests

- Definition: the goal **regarded by the consciousness of the community** as conducive to the welfare of **the whole community**, **transcending the immediate interests** of individuals and groups; a resulting loss or benefit **is not treated as a matter of settling individual claims**.
- Preceding negative: no precedence to **a class, party or faction**; **due regard to the interests of all members** of society.
- Instances: the **taxation system of a welfare state**; **defence, law and order, public health, expansion of education, environmental cleanliness**.
- Civic test: genuine pursuit means disputes **will never take a violent form**.
- Conflict test: the common good **shall not coincide with the interests of a particular group** — **labour–management** · **urban taxation for rural subsidy** · **village revenue for city advancement**; the last two are **mirror images**, so the test is symmetric.
- Procedural rule: **the merit of its beneficiaries rather than the claims of its opponents**.
- Hidden-cost rule: an arrangement serving **both parties** may be **repugnant to the interests of the large body of consumers or those of the general public**.

## F. The two refuted misreadings

| Misreading | Why it fails | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| The common good **is** the **greatest happiness of the greatest number** | The formula **could be interpreted to mean injustice to the minority**; a minority sacrificed for **so-called general happiness** is treated against the spirit of the common good | **John Rawls (1921-2002)** — authority only |
| Insisting on the common good **undermines incentives** to use one's talents | Without it **no individual would be able to achieve his potential excellence**; and **the rich cannot have full enjoyment of their wealth unless there is adequate provision of public services in society** | **J.K. Galbraith (1908-2006)** |

## G. Method of pursuit — cleavage and consensus

- Thesis: **consistent pursuit ... can be ensured only through the process of consensus**.
- Corollary **with its qualifier**: those who focus on social cleavage **as part of the basic structure of society** cannot be devoted to the common good.
- Printed Indian list: **rural–urban · caste · religious differences inducing communalism · regional interests · gender · white-collar against blue-collar**.
- Mechanism: politicians **capitalize** a cleavage and then **encash their political support in the bargaining for a share in power**.
- Exception: humanitarian and environmental movements are not divisive; movements for the **rehabilitation** of tribals or villagers made **homeless, out of job or sick** by **so-called developmental activities** **should not be termed as divisive activities**.
- Cleavage defined: **sharply divided into antagonistic groups on the basis of some more or less fixed attribute** — class, region, religion, language, race, gender.
- Consensus **as a process** (differences resolved, an arrangement **best suited to their needs under the existing constraints**) against **as a condition** (**no differences on any issue** — **a Utopian view**). Verdict: **in modern societies, consensus is achieved only as a process**.
- ⚠️ Regulation cluster — broadcast advertising bans, the **statutory warning**, drug and drinking warnings, environment, cleanliness, disease prevention, **population control**, literacy — all **book-period (5e, 2009)**.

## H. The liberal answer, and the condition attached to it

- Definition: **the aggregate of individual goods**; **not a metaphysical element which exists beyond the experience of ordinary persons**.
- Chain: rational man → **rules necessary for securing the good of all** → rules **instrumental to reconciliation** → **the point of their reconciliation represents the common good** → **interest groups** → reconciliation between them is **coterminous with the common good**.
- Premises: conflict **not very deep** · every problem **capable of a peaceful solution** · a **scarcity situation**; the conclusion is **this equilibrium of their respective rights and duties**.
- **Norman Barry (1989)** uses **'public interest' as a synonym for 'common good'** — reported, not adopted; promotion **enables individuals to secure advantages which they could not otherwise enjoy**.
- Rules of civil society: the strong **will not oppress the weak** · **nobody will cheat anybody** · free-will contract · sincere performance; intervention **only when some anti-social element does not comply**.
- Exponents: **Locke (1632-1704) · Adam Smith (1723-90) · Bentham (1748-1832) · J.S. Mill (1806-73)**.
- Appraisal: market rules are **free competition, equality of opportunity and allocation according to merit**; reward is governed by **market demand, which is beyond individual's control** — **Athens** against **Sparta**, and the consumer-society twin.
- The state must protect talents **indispensable for the preservation of human civilization and culture**; the box — a welfare state **gives protection to the people and such components of human civilization and culture whose existence is threatened by the forces of a competitive market society**.
- **Macpherson (1911-87)**: the market model **stunts the natural capabilities of man and destroys his creative freedom**.
- Verdict: acceptable **only on the condition that it is linked with the concept of welfare state**.

## I. The communitarian answer, its exponents and its verdict

- Critique: individualism **undermines individual's affinity with the community**; **no one is emotionally attached to any one**; comfort **at the expense of his emotional security**; self-interest **cannot secure good life in the fullest sense of the term**.
- Hinge: **if isolated individuals cannot secure their own good by their efforts, how can we arrive at the common good by aggregating the outcome of their efforts?**
- Inversion: only a community realizes it; pooled effort yields the common good **from which they would be able to derive their individual goods**; hence **first commitment to the community, and not to himself**.
- Ground: existence and personality are **the product of his social situation, roles and conventions which are embedded in society**.
- Four contrasts: **self-appointed** against **community-determined** goals; **sole proprietor of all his faculties** against **indebtedness to society**; **rights and liberties** against **duties and obligations**; plus **our common identity**.
- Genealogy: **Aristotle · Rousseau (1712-78) · Hegel (1770-1831) · Green (1836-82)** → **MacIntyre (1929- ) · Taylor (1931- ) · Walzer (1935- ) · Sandel (1953- )**, dates as printed.
- **Green (1882)**: knowledge **in association**; known **more intimately than** self-interest; **conception is identical**; state and politics exist **for the realization of the common good**; it is **the foundation of political obligation**; the state may make **only** conforming laws; and **if an individual thinks that he can protect the common good more effectively by opposing a particular order of the state, his political obligation does not stop him from going ahead**.
- **MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981)**: flourishing only within **socially established cooperative human activity**; rights **without realizing their duties** → **social disintegration and moral disaster**.
- **Sandel (1982)**: **'embeddedness'** → laws and practices **genuinely good for us** → **'deeper commonality'** informed by **'shared self-understanding'**.
- **Walzer, *Spheres of Justice* (1983)**: criteria **according to the proper spheres of their application** — ❌ **complex equality** is not printed here.
- **Taylor, *Philosophical Papers* (1985)**: against **'atomistic'** individuals; **genuine development** requires being **situated in a society**.
- Closing formulation: pursue goals **within the structure of society**; seek one's good **as part of the good of whole society**.
- Appraisal: **cooperation, and not competition** → **social solidarity**, **cordial relations**, **emotional security**; but **no mechanism to ensure that its principles will be adopted as the general rules of behaviour** — **a strong moral philosophy, but it is not founded in equally strong political philosophy**.

## J. The Marxian denial, and its own record

- Thesis: since class division on **ownership of private property**, the idea **has become irrelevant**; it exists only in a classless society — **primitive communism** or a **future communist society**; the state being a class instrument, such a society **is bound to evolve into a stateless society**.
- 📝 The printed head **NECESSARY CONDITIONS FOR THE COMMON GOOD** misdescribes a section about unavailability.
- Primitive communism, structurally: production **scarcely sufficient for subsistence** → **no surplus that could be stored in private possession** → no **haves and have-nots** → **no need for a special apparatus of coercion** → affairs **entrusted to the most respected and experienced members**.
- Communist stage: **social ownership** · **remnants of capitalism liquidated** · **labour compulsory for every able-bodied person** · fully developed productive forces → the state will **wither away**; **from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs**; gloss — **burdens shall depend on abilities, benefits shall depend on needs**, and **benefits shall not be treated as the reward of contribution to the common good**.
- Two clauses retained: the **Communist Manifesto (1848)** opening sentence, and **Marx has nowhere given a clearcut definition of class** — **Capital** vol. III (**1894**, ed. Engels).
- Appraisal: he **can discover his class interest**; the vision is **nowhere realized**; **over seven decades** in the Soviet Union, **over four decades** in East Europe; **when economic power as the basis of class division is removed, political power brings a new class division into existence**; more oppressive than liberal democracies, which **at least provide for some mechanism of civil liberties**; fragility through **increasing shortages**; **1989-91**; **a pinch of salt**.

## K. The Gandhian relocation — three keys

- **Commitment to the common good is the essence of Gandhian philosophy**; independence as a chance **to wipe every tear from every eye**; context — **oppressive foreign rule, abject poverty, vast social and economic inequalities, communal tension and hatred**; gospel — **spiritualism, Ahimsa, renunciation (non-possession), dignity of labour, moral courage**.
- **Trusteeship**: addressed to **the conscience of the rich and resourceful**, **particularly landlords and capitalists**; not **sole proprietors** but **'trustees' of a gift bestowed upon them by God for the service of humanity**. **Aparigraha** — possessions **make you morally deprave**; take **not more than his immediate needs**. **Asteya** — possession beyond immediate need **amounts to theft because it is meant to fulfil others' needs**; **Earth has enough resources to satisfy everybody's need but not their greed**; cf. **Proudhon**, one clause.
- Refusal: he **does not favour overthrowing the existing economic system**, oppressive **because of moral decline**; organizers **persuaded to act as public servants** → **class conflict** replaced by **class cooperation** → **change of heart**. **Amrita Bazar Patrika (1934)**: **not the extinction of landlords and capitalists, but a transformation of the existing relationship ... into something healthier and purer**.
- **The Modern Review (1935)**: **a legal fiction**; **absolute trusteeship is an abstraction like Euclid's definition of a point, and is equally unattainable. But if we strive for it, we shall be able to go further in realizing a state of equality on earth than by any other method.**
- **Classless society**: **elimination of discrimination on any irrelevant ground** — religion, caste, sex; **Prabhu and Rao (1945)** on equality of religions; **Bose (1948)** on man and woman, with **inferiority complex**.
- **Bread labour**: **everybody** does **physical labour toward production, at least to compensate for the bread that he consumes**; ***Harijan* (1935)** — **labour of love for the common good**; three purposes — **(a) simple living; (b) dignity of labour; (c) a sense of equality transcending the prevailing division of labour**; mechanism — caste **strayed on to division of society into high and low ranks**, which bread labour **would serve to obliterate**; ***From Yervada Mandir*** (**1945**) — the rich **would deem themselves only trustees** and use property **mainly in the public interest**; **Ahimsa** itself **conducive to the creation of a classless society**.
- **Sarvodaya**: **uplift of all · rise of all · awakening of all**; against a society where **only the few** hold **knowledge, power, prestige and wealth**; ❌ **does not envisage a conflict between the high and the low**, since **violent means cannot be used to achieve a non-violent end**; the rich offer their riches, proving **self-control and magnanimity** and raising **their own spiritual level**.
- **Origin**: **Ruskin, *Unto This Last* (1860)**, read during passive resistance in **South Africa**, summarised in **Gujarati** as *Sarvodaya*; **uplift of the last man or the most neglected lot**; Ruskin rejected **machinery, consumerism, economic man, mercantile economy** and advocated **simple technology, manual labour, communal enterprise** and value as **quality of life**.
- **The break**: **includes** → **converge in many points** → **part company** → **work in opposite directions**; **the utilitarian to be logical will never sacrifice himself. The absolutist will even sacrifice himself**; **absolutist** = **absolute faith in Ahimsa**; **the message of self-sacrifice for the greatest good of all is the essence of sarvodaya**.
