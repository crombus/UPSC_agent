# Justice: Concepts and Dimensions — Mains-Focused Live Edition

A fast-preparation edition for GS-II, GS-IV and the Essay. It teaches only what enriches a Mains answer on fair allocation, legal and political justice, socio-economic justice, procedure against substance, and the minimum-needs floor, and stops there.

**The one idea that organises everything.** Justice in this topic is not a list of dimensions; it is a single allocative question asked under two conditions. The question is: *by what criteria are the advantages and the obligations of organised social life distributed?* The two conditions are **scarcity** and an **open society** — advantages too short to satisfy all contestants, and a society in which the criteria of allocation may be publicly argued and revised. Everything else in the chapter is an elaboration of that one structure. The triad is its ordering principle: liberty qualified by equality, equality further qualified by fraternity. Legal, political and socio-economic justice are its spheres of operation. Procedure against substance is the dispute about *which* criterion settles it. Need and desert are the two criteria that survive. Hold the structure and every verified stem in this topic's deployment set has a route; lose it and the answer becomes a parade of thinkers.

**Scope.** This is **Topic 19 of the subject's twenty-three topics**, and it remains a standalone session. Depth and format drop to Mains level; the topic's syllabus share does not. Political Theory is supplementary conceptual support. The Commission prints no Political Theory paper, and every question touching these ideas is owned by another subject — Social Justice, Polity, Governance, Ethics, Indian Society, the Essay, or the Philosophy Optional's own socio-political owners. **This topic owns no General Studies or Optional previous-year question, the count of question routes into its canonical files is zero by design, and no proxy, alias, mirror or synthetic route is created here.** Concepts it needs but does not own are named and deferred: liberty, equality, property, Berlin's two concepts, Rousseau's two inequalities and the rational grounds of discrimination to **Topic 18**; every rival theory of justice developed beyond a named flag — the original position, the veil, the two principles, lexical priority, the difference principle, acquisition/transfer/rectification, capability, recognition — to **Topic 20**; punishment theory of every kind to **Crime and Punishment**; gender, caste, multiculturalism and secularism as doctrines to their own owners; and every article, amendment, judgment, reservation rule and scheme administration to `Polity/`, `Social-Justice/` and `Economy/`.

**What is deliberately left out, and why.** The chapter's four historical illustrations of changing consciousness are kept, because they are the examinable evidence for a dynamic conception; its bibliographic accumulation is not. The quotation micro-method and the three-register apparatus of the Basic owner survive as a two-line reminder inside the answer frameworks, not as a lesson. The canonical question ledger is dropped entirely: **no compiled four-option question corpus appears anywhere in this edition** — practice runs as concept checks and solved Mains models. The Advanced owner's five current-affairs-free applications duplicate the worked examples of Lessons 6 to 8 and are not repeated; its answer-engine prompts are replaced by the single deployment grid at Lesson 10. Rawls, Nozick, Sen and Fraser are named, bounded and handed off; they are never expounded. Definitions, causal logic, objections and replies, deployment, answer frameworks and required practice are kept intact.

The elimination has four outputs, not two, and that is the point. A candidate who writes only "justice means fairness in distribution" cannot explain why the question is urgent in one society and empty in another.

### 1. What is allocated — and the half of the sentence most answers drop

The chapter's own formulation is unusually complete, and the completeness is examinable. The contemporary problem of justice is **concerned with determining logical criteria for the allocation of goods, services, opportunities, benefits, power and honours as well as obligations in society, particularly in a scarcity situation**, which the chapter then compresses into a single clause: **justice is concerned with the allocation of benefits as well as burdens** (PDF p.432, printed 413).

Two details decide marks. First, the list is not only material: **power and honours** are allocated goods, which is why political representation and social status come inside the same frame as income. Second, **obligations** are allocated too. ⚠️ An answer that treats justice purely as a distribution of advantages cannot evaluate taxation, conscription, compliance costs or the distribution of care work, each of which is a distribution of burdens. The clause **benefits as well as burdens** is one of the three or four formulations in this topic worth carrying close to verbatim.

⚠️ The chapter also records where the problem sits intellectually: justice **is primarily a problem of moral philosophy**, but **since it has to be implemented by a political order, it also becomes a problem of political philosophy** (PDF p.432). That sentence is the licence for everything institutional in Lessons 6 and 7 — the moral criterion must be executed by an order that can be examined politically.

### 2. The elimination argument, which is this lesson's spine

🔶 This argument is printed in the chapter and **absent from both canonical owners**, which carry *open society plus scarcity* as a conclusion but not the reasoning that produces it. It is the single most efficient way to show why justice is a political question at all.

**The quest for justice will not be relevant in a purely authoritarian, purely competitive or purely communist regime** (PDF p.432).

- In a **purely authoritarian system** — the chapter is explicit that this covers **the traditional type or the modern type** — **all allocation is made according to the dictates of an established authority**, so **there is no use of search for the new criteria of allocation**. The question is not answered; it is foreclosed.
- In a **purely competitive system**, **all allocation is decided by the free play of market forces**, so **there is no scope of applying any new criteria of allocation**. The criterion is already fixed by the mechanism.
- In a **hypothetical communist system** where **allocation according to need** is supposed to be applied, **it will be futile to consider any alternative principles of justice**, because **if everybody's needs could be met, the problem of injustice in society would automatically disappear**.

⚠️ Note the logical shape, because it is what makes the argument portable. The first two regimes eliminate the question by removing the *discretion* — nothing is open to criteria. The third eliminates it by removing the *scarcity* — nothing is contested. So the question of justice requires both an unsettled criterion and a shortage, and the chapter's own conclusion follows: **search for justice would, therefore, be relevant only in an open society in a situation of scarcity** (PDF p.433).

### 3. The two conditions, stated as the chapter states them

The open-society condition is not a slogan about freedom. It is a precise requirement about *how criteria are settled*: the question becomes significant **where there is a widespread demand of social advantages that are in short supply, and where the criteria of allocation of these advantages can be openly discussed and adopted**, and those criteria **should be determined in such a manner that they prove to be acceptable to all individuals or categories of the individuals concerned** (PDF p.433).

Then the clause that converts the condition into an institutional demand, and the one most worth deploying: **it may be conceded that there can be no final word in this behalf. Hence suitable channels of appeal and even of protest should be provided within the system so that the prevalent criteria could be revised as and when so warranted by reason.**

⚠️ Three consequences are examinable. **Fallibility is built in** — the chapter concedes there is no final word, so a conception of justice that claims completeness has already failed its own test. **Protest is a justice institution, not a disturbance of justice**; this is the conceptual root of Lesson 6's right to criticise the government and freedom of peaceful protest, and it means a system that suppresses challenge cannot correct its own allocative errors. And **revision must be warranted by reason** — the channel is for argument, not for pressure, which is the first appearance of the guard Lesson 3 develops in full.

The two printed boxes fix the contrast in definitional form. An **authoritarian system** is **a system of social organization where everything is required to conform to an established order, and it is regulated by the person or group which is recognized as the guardian of that order**. An **open society** is **a society where there is freedom to criticize the existing order in the light of a new logic and to reform or reformulate the existing institutions by attaining public support for the proposed changes** (PDF p.433). ⚠️ Read the second definition closely: it requires *public support*, not merely permission to complain. Criticism without a route to institutional change does not meet the chapter's own standard.

⚠️ One qualification the Basic owner adds and this edition keeps (owner §7): the open-society condition explains when allocative criteria become **publicly contestable**; ❌ it does not imply that an authoritarian order is beyond judgement. Authoritarian institutions can still be judged unjust even where contestation is suppressed — what is absent there is the *process* by which criteria are revised, not the possibility of the verdict.

### 4. Aristotle's pair, and the job it does for the rest of the topic

⚠️ This material is **owner-only** (Basic owner §12). ❌ No Gauba page may be attached to it, and the attribution is limited to Aristotle by name. It is taught here, in one compact block, because the chapter's allocative frame presupposes exactly this vocabulary and because the distinction performs a boundary function later.

- **Distributive justice** allocates goods, honours and offices among the members of a community in **geometric proportion** to a relevant standard of worth: equals receive equal shares, unequals proportionally unequal ones. ⚠️ Rival constitutions select rival standards of worth, which is precisely what Lessons 8 and 9 fight about; ❌ modern need-based allocation should not be presented as one of Aristotle's own coordinate criteria.
- **Corrective (rectificatory) justice** restores **arithmetic equality** after a wrong in a specific bilateral transaction — **voluntary** (contract, exchange) or **involuntary** (theft, injury, fraud) — by correcting unjust gain and loss **irrespective of the parties' relative merit or social standing**.

Two jobs follow. First, the pair **names what the chapter is about**: everything from Lesson 4 to Lesson 9 is distributive in Aristotle's sense, and knowing that lets an answer say which *kind* of justice claim is being made. Second, it **keeps the punishment cluster out of this topic**. ❌ Corrective justice is not retributive justice and is not restorative justice: it restores a transactional balance between two parties, it is indifferent to desert, and it settles nothing about whether the prior distribution was fair. ➡️ Punishment theory of every kind — retributive, deterrent, reformative, proportionality — belongs to **Crime and Punishment**, and this topic supplies only the distinction.

The diagram is the lesson. Everything the chapter says about utility is derived from the claim that good and bad admit of degrees while right and wrong do not.

### 1. The device, and why the negative prevails

Justice, the chapter says, is **primarily a problem of discovering the 'right' course of action**, so the distinction between **right** and **wrong** becomes decisive — and it is **sometimes confused with the distinction between 'good' and 'evil'**, as if good corresponded to right (PDF p.433, printed 414).

The two pairs behave differently. **Good is akin to useful, profitable, beneficial**; **bad or evil implies harmful, damaging, disadvantageous**. Crucially, **good and bad do not fall in absolute categories**: in actual practice something **may be good in some respects, and bad in others** — **partly good and partly bad** — so they **constitute a continuum**, plotted on a scale **which permits free access from one extreme to another in any direction**.

**But the position of 'right' and 'wrong' is different. These are absolute categories, opposed to each other in the absolute sense.** Something **can be either right or wrong, and nothing else**, exactly as with **true** and **false**; they are **mutually exclusive categories** whose relationship **may be represented by a dichotomy**. And then the sentence that does the analytical work: **it is a peculiar dichotomy where negative would prevail over the positive. If something is partly true, it must be false … If something is partly right, it must be wrong** (PDF p.434, printed 415). The conclusion is one clause: **justice corresponds to right; injustice corresponds to wrong.**

The printed box fixes the vocabulary. A **continuum** represents **a range of variables which fall between two opposites** which **provide for easy access from one extreme to the other**; a **dichotomy** rules that out — the positions are **like the two banks of a big river which are not connected by a bridge or navigation facility** (PDF p.434).

⚠️ The examinable payload is the prevalence of the negative, and it should be stated carefully rather than triumphantly. It means that a **partly just** arrangement is, in the relevant respect, **unjust** — a scheme that is fair to most and arbitrary to a remainder does not average out to fairness, because the remainder's claim is not a quantity that can be offset. ⚠️ It does **not** mean that justice is all-or-nothing in practice, or that incremental improvement is worthless; the chapter's own dynamic account (Lesson 3) and its minimum-needs floor (Lesson 9) are both gradualist. What the device establishes is that **the arithmetic of aggregation is the wrong arithmetic** for this category, not that degrees of progress are unreal.

### 2. From the device to the critique of utility

The second difference does the rest: **good and bad are marked by quantitative differences**, while **right and wrong are characterized by qualitative differences**, and **good and bad are susceptible to measurement and quantification** (PDF p.434).

Hence the chapter's verdict on the school: **utilitarianism deals with good and bad**. **That is why Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832), the exponent of utilitarianism, founded his 'felicific calculus'**, which is **concerned with the balancing between the quantities of pleasure and pain** — and **that is also why the mainstream utilitarianism proved to be indifferent to the problem of justice**. The printed box defines the school: pleasure and pain are **the chief motive force behind all human actions**, the balance of pleasure over pain is **utility** which is **the source of happiness**, and **the guiding principle of public policy should be 'the greatest happiness of the greatest number'** (PDF p.435, printed 416).

🔶 Then the chapter's sharpest and fairest concession, **absent from both canonical owners** and worth carrying verbatim: **Bentham advanced a theory of allocation of goods and services without pretending that it was a theory of justice** (PDF p.434).

⚠️ This single clause changes the quality of an answer. It converts the critique from an accusation into a boundary claim: utilitarianism is not charged with giving a *bad* account of justice, but with giving an account of something else — allocation by aggregate advantage — and with being **indifferent** to the justice question rather than wrong about it. That is both more accurate and harder to rebut.

### 3. Mill's two departures, and the mechanism of the drift toward justice

**J.S. Mill (1806-73)** is the hinge, and the chapter credits him with two distinct departures.

- **Qualitative differences.** **When J.S. Mill (1806-73) focused on the importance of qualitative differences between different types of pleasure, it marked a significant departure from the mainstream utilitarianism** (PDF p.434). ⚠️ The structural point: once pleasures differ in *kind*, the calculus loses its common unit, and a theory built on measurement is weakened from inside.
- **The individual against the majority.** Utilitarianism **tends to subordinate individual to the collectivity**; so **when J.S. Mill gives precedence to liberty of the individual over the opinion of the majority, he seems to be distancing himself from the mainstream utilitarianism and coming closer to the idea of justice**. The chapter names the mechanism explicitly: **here Mill accords primacy to the moral worth of a policy instead of subjecting it to cost-benefit analysis** (PDF p.435).

And then the closing line of the movement: **this tendency to restore the dignity of the individual on moral grounds was brought to its logical conclusion in Rawls's theory of justice** — **John Rawls (1921-2002), who accords primacy to the problem of justice, is strongly opposed to utilitarianism**, and **has pointed to certain situations that satisfy the needs of utilitarianism but they fail to satisfy the needs of justice** (PDF p.434).

### 1. The double claim: a fixed ideal with a moving grasp

The chapter opens the block with the ordinary-language definition: **the term 'justice' implies the quality of being 'just', 'right' or 'reasonable'. - **untouchability was 'justified' in India a few decades ago**;
- **the inferior status of women was taken for granted the world over not so long ago**;
- **racial discrimination was 'justified' till recently in some parts of the world.**

**But these conditions are now widely regarded as unjust.** And the chapter names its own proof: **our changing outlook about these problems and changing public opinion in favour of human liberty and equality are ample proof of the dynamic character of the idea of justice.**

📝 Note the chapter's own punctuation: *justified* appears in inverted commas each time. The chapter is not conceding that those arrangements *were* just in their time; it is reporting that they were defended as just. ⚠️ That typographic detail is a genuine analytical signal and should survive into an answer, because it is the difference between "standards changed" and "defences failed."

⚠️ Two cautions on use. **Dating discipline:** these are the chapter's **2009-vintage** phrasings — *a few decades ago*, *not so long ago*, *till recently* — and must be reproduced with that vintage attached. ❌ They are not updated, and ❌ no claim whatever is made here about the present condition of any society. **Boundary discipline:** untouchability appears here as one of four illustrations of changing consciousness. ➡️ Caste as a subject, its annihilation and its contemporary policy framework belong to the **Caste** and **Social Justice** owners; this lesson borrows the fact and ❌ stops.

### 3. The guard, which is the lesson's real payload

Having established dynamism, the chapter immediately fences it: **it is necessary that the conception of justice should always be based on 'reason'. But man develops his faculty of reasoning according to the social consciousness of his age. The true meaning of justice should, therefore, be determined in the light of prevalent social consciousness, or what D.D. Raphael in his Problems of Political Philosophy (1976) described as 'modern consciousness'** (PDF p.435).

And then the disclaimer, which is the mark of an honest theory rather than a weakness in it: **we do not claim that this would be the perfect idea of justice. Social consciousness is an ever-growing phenomenon, and we should be prepared to accept new and progressive ideas as far as they are supported by reason.**

⚠️ Unpack the two-step, because the compression hides the argument. Step one: the **criterion** is reason — not consensus, not custom, not authority. Step two: the **capacity** to exercise reason is historically situated, because people reason with the materials their age supplies. So social consciousness is the **medium** through which reason works, not the **standard** that replaces it. The practical test follows directly from the last clause: a new idea is admissible **as far as it is supported by reason**, which gives a filter that pure consensus-relativism cannot supply — an age's approval is not self-validating.

⚠️ The honest limit should be stated too, because examiners reward it: this does not yield an algorithm. It tells you what to argue about (reasons, not preferences) and which direction counts as correction (toward human **liberty and equality**, which is the chapter's own named test in the proof sentence). It does not tell you, in advance, which contested present arrangement will later be seen as the next reversal.

### 4. How the guard converts a context-relativity stem into a strong answer

A stem asserting that the just and the unjust are **context-relative** is a trap with two bad exits. Agreeing without qualification concedes that justice is whatever a society believes, which cannot condemn slavery, untouchability, the subordination of women or racial discrimination at the time those were defended. Rejecting it outright denies the chapter's own evidence and makes the four reversals inexplicable.

The route between them is the chapter's: **context shapes the realization, reason supplies the standard.** Stated as an answer spine — justice is an ideal **akin to the absolute truth** whose **comprehension is a continuous process**; contexts differ in what they can see, which is why defences of slavery and untouchability were once sincerely offered; but the later judgement is a **correction**, not merely a difference, because the criterion throughout is **reason** and the direction of movement is toward **human liberty and equality**; and the position remains open — **we do not claim that this would be the perfect idea of justice** — because new ideas are accepted **as far as they are supported by reason**.

⚠️ For an ethics answer, add the administrative corollary: context-sensitivity in applying a standard (different circumstances, different means) is not the same as context-dependence of the standard itself, and conflating them is how discretion becomes arbitrariness.

### 1. The thread, and what kind of claim it is

**Ernest Barker** (*Principles of Social and Political Theory*; **1951**) **has shown that justice represents a synthesis of the principles of liberty, equality and fraternity** (PDF p.436, printed 417). The chapter then states the relation four ways in four sentences, and the repetition is deliberate: **justice is the thread which runs through all these values and makes them parts of an integrated whole**; **it reconciles their conflicts and contradictions and gives them the shape of universal principles of governance**; **justice is the basic idea behind these values — we hold them in high esteem because they are manifestations of justice**; **justice is the final goal to which all these values should conform.**

⚠️ Note what is being claimed, because answers routinely weaken it. This is not the familiar proposition that the three values must be *balanced* against each other. It is the stronger claim that they are **derivative**: we value liberty and equality **because** they are manifestations of justice, so justice is prior and supplies the standard by which conflicts between them are settled. That is why the chapter can resolve the liberty-equality conflict by **ordering** rather than by compromise. ⚠️ An answer that says "justice balances the three" has stated the weak version; the strong version is that justice *generates* them and therefore adjudicates them.

### 2. Step one — why liberty at all, and the dignity argument beneath it

The chapter opens the ladder with a question most answers never ask: **why do we demand that human relations in society should be governed by the principle of liberty? Why do we not leave them to the whims of individuals, to the free play of the natural forces of survival of the fittest, to the element of chance, or to irrational traditions and superstitions?** (PDF p.436).

The four rejected alternatives are worth reproducing exactly, because together they define what liberty is *for*: not self-assertion, but the replacement of whim, force, luck and superstition by **reason**. **Obviously, it is our sense of justice that impels us to postulate that human relations in society should be regulated by 'reason'.**

The ground is dignity: **our sense of justice recognizes the dignity of the human being as such. It is the rational nature of man which clothes him with this dignity. It demands that each individual should be treated as an 'end-in-itself', not a means to an end.** From which equality follows immediately rather than as a separate value: **in this respect, all individuals should be treated as equal to each other. No individual can be treated as superior or inferior to another because of his place of birth, race, religion, language, culture, sex, education or economic status.** And the reason the chapter gives for the equal treatment is capacity-based: **irrespective of these differences, all individuals are potentially capable of acquiring excellence and making suitable contributions to the social good. They need equal freedom of personal development in their own right so as to prove their worth to society.**

🔶 The historical anchor, **absent from both canonical owners**: **that was the idea behind the 'French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen' (1789) which read: "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions can be based only upon public utility."** ⚠️ The second sentence of the Declaration is the useful one in an answer, because it licenses differentiation only on a public ground — which is exactly the test Step 3 will apply to protective discrimination.

### 3. Step two — the contradiction inside absolute liberty

**Now the principle of liberty will not conform to the principle of justice until the benefit of liberty is equally extended to each individual in society. The idea of absolute liberty is a contradiction in terms** (PDF p.436). The argument is definitional, not prudential: if liberty is **the absence of restraint**, it **cannot become a universal principle until it is qualified by the principle of equality**, which **postulates such restraints on liberty that liberty or freedom of one does not become a threat to another's equal and similar liberty**. The chapter's consequence is severe and quotable: **an unrestrained liberty of one or the chosen few will turn out to be the sentence of slavery for the rest of the community.**

Barker's block quotation supplies the formal version: **the need of liberty for each is necessarily qualified and conditioned by the need of liberty for all; and the liberty of A will, therefore, be such liberty as he can enjoy concurrently with the enjoyment of similar and equal liberty by B and C and D … Because the liberty of each is, thus, relative to that of others, and has to be adjusted to that of others, it must always be regulated; and indeed it would not exist unless it were regulated** (PDF pp.436-437).

Then the chapter adds the test that prevents the argument being abused, and it is routinely dropped by candidates: **on the same principle, any restraint on liberty can only be upheld when it is duly proved that it is imposed in the interests of justice and equality** (PDF p.437). ⚠️ The burden of proof lies on the restraint, and the permitted ground is specified. That one sentence is what distinguishes this position from a general licence to regulate, and it is the single most useful line in the lesson for a governance stem.

### 4. Step three — where the absence of discrimination stops working

**The principle of equality, again, is not the final principle of justice.** Equality **may be defined as the 'absence of discrimination'**, and the chapter immediately divides the spheres (PDF p.437).

- **In the legal or political sphere, this interpretation of equality will not present much difficulty.** It reads as **equality before the law, equal legal personality of each individual, equal voting rights — 'one man, one vote' principle**.
- **But as we turn to the economic sphere, the mere 'absence of discrimination' may not meet the requirements of justice.** The reason is structural and the chapter states it precisely: **when society is divided into different strata on the basis of private property and conventional sources of prestige and power — one class enjoying special privileges, the other being permanently in an underprivileged position — the cause of justice will not be served by treating them entirely without discrimination.**

The prescription follows: **the principle of justice requires that the deprived and underprivileged sections should be given special protection in order to save them from the excesses of the dominant sections.** Its illustration is the relational pairs that Lesson 7 will develop — **if an ordinary man in his capacity as worker, consumer or tenant is open to exploitation by the employer, trader or landlord, the law should make reasonable discrimination in favour of the weaker party.**

And then the formulation that is the most valuable single clause in this topic: **it is just like extending the principle of brotherhood or fraternity to the regulation of human relations in society as a matter of right, not charity.** The chapter explains why fraternity is required rather than optional: it resolves **the contradiction of the principle of equality which would otherwise leave the individual at the mercy of the cruel market forces of demand and supply and competition**, which **would eliminate the weaker sections, not to speak of securing them equal dignity, opportunity or advantage.**

⚠️ The symmetry of the burden of proof must be carried, because without it Step 3 looks like an open warrant: **any limitation of any section's right to equality can only be upheld when it is duly proved that it involves the discrimination in favour of the weaker section in the interests of justice and fraternity.** Protective discrimination is therefore not self-justifying; it must be shown to run in the weaker party's favour and to serve justice. ⚠️ Note also the word **permanently** in the diagnosis — the argument is addressed to entrenched stratification, not to any difference of outcome. ➡️ The rational grounds of discrimination as a doctrine, and the objections to preferential treatment, belong to **Topic 18**; ❌ no reservation policy verdict is offered here.

### 5. The final principle, and the two formulations that close answers

**Thus, in the final principle of justice, we seek to regulate human relations in society by the principle of liberty; but liberty is qualified by the principle of equality; and equality is further qualified by the principle of fraternity** (PDF p.437). The purpose: **to make sure that each individual gets a fair share in the advantages accruing from organized social life.**

Two closing formulations are worth carrying close to verbatim.

- The distributive settlement: **each individual gets a common civic minimum beyond which one's work is rewarded according to the value of the contribution made to the public welfare** — with the guard that **it must be ascertained that an unduly large share of wealth, prestige and power is not cornered by any privileged section or sections by dint of its money and manipulative power**. ⚠️ This is the floor-then-reward structure that Lesson 9 derives independently from need and desert; the convergence of two separate arguments on the same shape is itself an answer-worthy observation.
- The programmatic close: **the principle of justice postulates not merely formal liberty and equality, but the transformation of those social conditions which obstruct the enjoyment of freedom and equality by ordinary men and women.**

📝 One clause only, for the sovereignty adjacency: a classical theory of sovereignty that locates law's authority in the sovereign's will must still answer this ladder, because the ladder makes the *content* of a legal order testable against an ordering principle the sovereign did not author. ➡️ **Bodin and sovereignty as a doctrine belong to the Sovereignty owner** and are not argued here.

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Liberty and equality are opposed, so justice consists in striking a balance between them." Identify what is wrong with both halves and state the repaired claim.

**Model answer:** Both halves misdescribe the chapter's structure. The first half treats liberty and equality as independent ideals that happen to collide. On the chapter's account they are not independent: justice is **the thread which runs through all these values and makes them parts of an integrated whole**, and **we hold them in high esteem because they are manifestations of justice**, so equality enters as the *correction of a defect inside liberty itself* — **the idea of absolute liberty is a contradiction in terms**, because unqualified liberty for **the chosen few will turn out to be the sentence of slavery for the rest of the community**. Barker states the repair: the liberty of A is **such liberty as he can enjoy concurrently with the enjoyment of similar and equal liberty by B and C and D**, so it **must always be regulated; and indeed it would not exist unless it were regulated**. The second half fails because *balance* has no direction and no test. The chapter supplies both: an **ordering** — liberty **qualified by** equality, equality **further qualified by** fraternity — and a burden of proof at each joint, since a restraint stands only **when it is duly proved that it is imposed in the interests of justice and equality**, and a departure from equal treatment stands only when **it involves the discrimination in favour of the weaker section**. Repaired claim: justice is not a balance between two rival values but the ordering principle that generates both, qualifying liberty by equality and equality — in the economic sphere, where one class is **permanently in an underprivileged position** — by fraternity extended **as a matter of right, not charity**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read Step 3 as a general licence for unequal treatment. The chapter confines it to the **economic sphere** and to **permanent** stratification, retains **absence of discrimination** as sufficient in the legal and political sphere, and places the burden of proof on the departure. ❌ It yields no verdict on any particular preferential policy, which belongs to Topic 18 and to the Social Justice owner.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Equality as the absence of discrimination is sufficient for justice." Examine. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** It is sufficient in one sphere and insufficient in another, which is why the proposition fails. The chapter concedes the first half: **in the legal or political sphere, this interpretation of equality will not present much difficulty** — **equality before the law**, **'one man, one vote'**. It then marks the limit: **as we turn to the economic sphere, the mere 'absence of discrimination' may not meet the requirements of justice**, because where one class enjoys **special privileges** and another is **permanently in an underprivileged position**, **the cause of justice will not be served by treating them entirely without discrimination**. Hence **the law should make reasonable discrimination in favour of the weaker party**, extending fraternity **as a matter of right, not charity**. ⚠️ The qualification: the departure is not self-justifying — it stands only when **duly proved** to involve **discrimination in favour of the weaker section** — and the floor it secures comes before desert.

### 1. What the traditional concept actually said

**The traditional concept of justice focused on the 'just man'. It was primarily concerned with the virtues befitting a man for enhancing his moral worth. It consisted in the performance of his duties attached to his status determined by the prevalent law, social customs and mode of thought** (PDF p.438, printed 419).

⚠️ Read the last clause slowly, because it contains the whole critique in advance. The duties are attached to a **status**, and the status is **determined by** three things — **prevalent law, social customs and mode of thought** — none of which the individual chooses and none of which is itself under examination. So the traditional conception can evaluate how well a person discharges a role, but it has no vocabulary for asking whether the distribution of roles was defensible. That is the precise sense in which the chapter later calls it **conservative**: not that its holders were reactionary, but that the object of assessment is the individual's conformity rather than the arrangement's justifiability.

### 2. Plato as the type case, with both printed quotations

**A typical example of the traditional approach to the problem of justice is provided by Plato's theory of justice which sought to prescribe the duties of different citizens and required them to develop virtues befitting those duties.** The chapter then expounds it through two secondary sources, and both are 🔶 **absent from both canonical owners** — which is precisely why they are valuable in an answer on the most-asked cluster in the set.

**Richard Lewis Nettleship**, *Lectures on the Republic of Plato* (**1962**): **justice, in Plato's sense, is the power of individual concentration on duty. If a soldier is just in this sense, he is, of course, a brave man; if a man in subordinate position is just, he of course accepts and maintains authority, or is 'self-controlled'. Justice therefore … is really the condition of the existence of all the virtues; each of them is a particular manifestation of the spirit of justice, which takes different forms according to a man's function in the community. In modern phrase it is equivalent to sense of duty** (PDF p.438).

⚠️ Two things to extract. First, justice is **the condition of the existence of all the virtues** — it is not one virtue among four but the structural condition of the rest, which is why it can be the organising concept of an entire ideal state. Second, the virtues are **function-relative**: the same disposition expresses itself as courage in a soldier and self-control in a subordinate. That relativity to function is the hinge of both the theory's elegance and its vulnerability.

The chapter's own summary: **justice results from each element in society doing its appropriate task, doing it well and doing it only** — and the condition of harmony, **it is imperative that reason must rule within the man as well as within the state**, which is why **in a just or ideal state, the reins of government shall remain in the hands of a class of philosopher-kings who are supposed to be the living embodiments of reason, whereas material production and military defence shall be entrusted to the producer and warrior classes respectively** (PDF p.438).

**Will Durant**, *The Story of Philosophy* (**1954**), gives the arrangement its memorable form: **the industrial forces would produce but they would not rule; the military forces would protect but they would not rule; the forces of knowledge and science and philosophy would be nourished and protected and they would rule.** ⚠️ The triple repetition of **rule** is the quotation's analytical point: the question Plato is answering is not who does what, but who **decides**, and the answer makes political authority a function of knowledge rather than of production or force.

⚠️ **The caste parallel, handled exactly as printed and no further.** The chapter adds one sentence: **it is significant that Hindu caste system, as enunciated by the ancient lawgivers, strikes a similar note regarding the nature of justice** (PDF p.438). Reproduce it as what it is — the author's 2009 comparison between Plato's functional scheme and the schemes set out in the **ancient lawgivers' texts**. ❌ It is not a claim about contemporary Indian society, it carries no implication for any present community, and ❌ no caste-policy content follows from it here. ➡️ Caste as a subject belongs to its own owner.

⚠️ **The guard the Advanced owner insists on** (§7), and it is worth a clause in any Plato answer: Plato's order secures harmony through hierarchical functional differentiation, but **it should not be simplified into absolutely closed hereditary caste, because limited movement by aptitude remains conceptually possible**. An answer that equates Plato's three classes with closed heredity has made the commonest error in the most-asked cluster.

### 3. The shift, and why it is a change of subject rather than of opinion

**The modern concept of justice, on the contrary, is marked by a shift of emphasis, from the idea of a just or virtuous man to that of a just society.** The chapter draws the consequence in two balanced pairs: **the traditional view of justice embodied a conservative idea; the modern view embodies a progressive idea. The traditional view insisted on the individual conforming to a pre-conceived image of society; the modern view of justice seeks to transform society itself for the realization of certain human values** (PDF p.438).

⚠️ The examinable point is that this is a change in the **bearer of the predicate**, not merely a change in moral fashion. Once societies rather than persons are the things called just or unjust, three questions open that the older view could not frame: whether the distribution of statuses is defensible, whether those disadvantaged by it have any claim, and against whom that claim lies. Lessons 6 to 9 are the chapter's answers to exactly those three.

### 4. Social justice as the voice of the oppressed

🔶 **Raphael's** line, **absent from both canonical owners**, is the chapter's honest statement of the concept's political temperature: **the term 'social justice' tends to issue from the mouths of reformers, and to be regarded with suspicion by those who are satisfied with the existing order** (PDF p.439, printed 420).

The chapter then defines the idea by its occasion: **the idea of social justice comprises a force behind social change. It is precisely when people find fault with the existing social order, involving oppressive and exploitative social conditions, that they raise the demand for social justice, seeking suitable changes in social policy for determining an 'authoritative allocation of values'.** The relation to the triad is explicit: **the ideas of liberty and equality derive their substance from the idea of social justice when these principles seek the transformation of the existing social conditions to eliminate injustice in society.**

And then the formulation to carry verbatim: **'social justice' is the voice of the oppressed and the underprivileged against the excesses of the social system. It is an expression of what is due to the individual from society, especially to the individual who is condemned to a wretched and subhuman living because of a defective system of distribution of advantages accruing from the organized social life.** The chapter closes the block by naming the task that the rest of the chapter executes: **the main problem of social justice is to decide an appropriate allocation of the advantages that are available or that can be secured through the instrumentality of the social organization.**

⚠️ Three usable consequences. **Social justice is a demand, not a benefaction** — *what is due to the individual from society* is the language of a claim, which is why Lesson 4's **right, not charity** and this block say the same thing from two directions. **Its diagnosis is systemic** — the condition is produced by **a defective system of distribution**, so the remedy addresses the system, not the sufferer's character. And **suspicion of the term is predicted by the theory itself**, which is a rhetorically powerful move in an essay: Raphael's observation means that resistance to the vocabulary is evidence about the resister's position, not an argument about the concept.

⚠️ **Boundary, stated once.** The *voice of the oppressed* formulation is also the apparatus a punishment stem would reach for. ➡️ It may be used to say what social justice *is*; ❌ no theory of punishment — retributive, deterrent, reformative or restorative — is taught or evaluated here, and Lesson 1's corrective/retributive non-identity is the only further apparatus this topic supplies. Punishment belongs to **Crime and Punishment**.

### 1. The formalist case, stated at full strength

The chapter opens by splitting the term: **legal justice is broadly applied in two contexts: (a) 'justice according to law' — here we do not question the validity of law but focus on the principles of administration of justice according to the prevalent law; and (b) 'law according to justice' — here we examine the substance of the law itself to ensure that it conforms to the requirements of justice** (PDF p.439, printed 420).

**Alf Ross** in his *On Law and Justice* (**1958**) **argued that justice consists in an efficient administration of law and that it should not be tested on some imaginary moral values**. 🔶 Note the citation route, which both canonical owners omit: the chapter expounds Ross **through Morris Ginsberg**, *On Justice in Society* (**1965**), and the passage it quotes is Ginsberg's: **Ross accepts what may be called the formal principles of justice as a basis of law. The notion of a legal order implies that decisions shall be made not arbitrarily but in accordance with general rules, and that these general rules (whatever they are) shall be correctly (that is what moralists call impartially) applied; that is by criteria defining the class of cases coming under the rule, and not affected by the subjective reactions of the judge. Justice then is conformity with existing law** (PDF p.439).

The position is then stated at its most uncompromising: **it does not question whether a particular law itself is right or wrong, on the plea that there is no scientific way of such verification. A good law is known by its efficiency in attaining its purpose, whatever that purpose may be. In this sense, the problem of justice has nothing to do with the purpose of law; it is solely concerned with the efficacy of law** (PDF p.440, printed 421).

🔶 And then the sentence that gives the position its best card, **absent from both canonical owners** and the single most useful line in this lesson for a critical answer: **justice in totalitarian societies may be distorted not because of bad laws, but because of the power given to judges to disregard fixed rules.**

⚠️ Take that seriously rather than dismissing it. The formalist is pointing at a real mechanism: arbitrary power rarely announces itself as bad legislation; it more often works by *exception*, by discretion exercised outside the general rule. So the formal requirements — generality, impartial application, **criteria defining the class of cases**, immunity from **the subjective reactions of the judge** — are not an empty ritual. They are protections, and an answer that treats proceduralism as mere formalism has given away an argument it will need in Lesson 8.

### 2. The substantive reply, and the distinction that carries it

**Ernest Barker**, in *Principles of Social and Political Theory* (**1951**), **has dwelled on the concept of legal justice as 'law according to justice'**. 🔶 His apparatus is also absent from both owners, which carry only the validity/value pair: **Barker draws a distinction between 'positive law' and 'natural law' to show that law derives its validity and value from two different sources. Positive law denotes a particular law — the law defined and declared by each community for its own members. On the other hand, natural law is a universal law — a law founded on what is right-in-itself, on what is just everywhere and at all times, for all mankind, on what is valuable whether it is valid or not** (PDF p.440). **Barker demonstrates that this distinction between the two aspects of law can be traced in the political theory of Aristotle, Roman jurisprudence as well as Christian doctrine.**

The conclusion is the quotation to carry: **if we make this distinction, we may say that authority gives validity to law, and justice gives it value. A law has validity, and I am legally obliged to obey it, if it is declared, recognized, and enforced as law by the authority of the legally organized community, acting in its capacity of a State. A law has value, and I am bound to obey it not only legally, and not only by an outward compulsion, but also morally and by an inward force, if it has the inherent quality of justice.**

⚠️ The analytical gain is a **two-source account of obligation**: validity produces a legal obligation enforced from outside; value produces a moral obligation that operates **by an inward force**. A law may have the first without the second, which is exactly the condition that formalism cannot describe. **Barker points out that, ideally, law ought to have both validity and value. Thus, law will be most effective if it conforms to the principle of justice and also has the authority of the state.**

The chapter's verdict is printed and unhedged: **of the two views of legal justice, one represented by Ross and the other by Barker, the latter is definitely more sound. Justice in the legal sphere consists not only in an efficient administration of law, but it postulates that law itself should embody human values according to the prevailing social consciousness.** And the consequence of ignoring it: **if law does not reflect the changing social values, it will turn out to be a dead weight on the wheels of social progress, only to be discarded in a violent overthrow** (PDF p.440).

⚠️ Note the link back to Lesson 3 — **according to the prevailing social consciousness** — which is why these blocks must be taught in this order: the standard by which law is evaluated is the reason-through-consciousness standard, not a free-floating moral intuition. ⚠️ And note the practical argument in the last clause: a legal order that cannot absorb changing values does not merely become unjust, it becomes **unstable**, since the alternative to revision is overthrow. That is a prudential argument for substantive legal justice, and it is available to an answer that cannot rely on moral premises alone.

### 3. Political justice — two broad usages named, then the specific sense taught

The chapter disposes of the broad usages in two sentences, and this edition does the same. **William Godwin**, in his *Essay on Property* (**1793**), **used the term 'political justice' to denote a moral principle whose object was the 'general good' and which was especially invoked to evolve a genuine system of property**; **Otto Kirchheimer**, in *Political Justice: The Use of Legal Procedure for Political Ends* (**1961**), **has conceived of 'political justice' as 'the search for an ideal in which all members will communicate and interact with the body politic to assume its highest perfection'** (PDF pp.440-441). 🔶 Both are absent from both canonical owners; both are named once, for citation value, and ❌ neither is developed.

**A more specific usage of the term 'political justice', however, refers to the transformation of political institutions, political process and political rights according to current conceptions of justice** (PDF p.441, printed 422). The chapter then gives a checklist in three movements, and the checklist is the examinable content:

- **In the first place**, **the establishment of democratic institutions in the political life of the community so that these institutions represent and take care of the interests of the people, not of any privileged class**. Concretely: **representative institutions — the legislature and executive — should be constituted on the principle of universal adult franchise**, **the independence of the judiciary should be protected and maintained**, and this **also implies 'rule of law', i.e. the principle that government should not be arbitrary, but should be conducted through procedures authorized by legislation passed in the proper form**. **A constitutional government is also an essential ingredient of political justice in this sense.**
- **In the second place, political justice implies a full guarantee of the liberty of thought and expression, especially the right to criticize the government and its policies.**
- **Finally, there should be complete freedom for forming associations and interest groups to enable the citizens to articulate their interests through the normal channels of communication and through political parties as well as to express their approval or disapproval of particular measures, to organize peaceful protests against harmful measures, and to pressurize the working of the government through democratic channels and procedures.**

The summary sentence is the one to deploy: **political justice postulates the universal availability of the mechanism for resolving the conflicting claims of different interests in society.**

⚠️ Two observations make this more than a list. First, the checklist is the **institutional completion of Barker's argument**: validity is supplied by authority, and these are the institutions through which *value* enters — universal franchise decides whose values count, an independent judiciary and the rule of law prevent the exception that the formalist himself fears, and the right to criticise plus freedom of association are the **appeal and protest** channels Lesson 1 required for an open society. The fusion of these two blocks into one lesson is justified by exactly that continuity. Second, the word **universal** in the summary is load-bearing: the mechanism must be *available to all*, which is why Lesson 7's point about economic disparity defeating access is a direct attack on political justice and not a separate complaint.

⚠️ **Discipline on this block.** This is Gauba's **2009 conceptual specification**. ❌ No constitutional article, amendment, schedule, case or institution of any country is named here; ❌ no claim is made about how any present system performs against the checklist. The checklist is a standard, and applying it to a jurisdiction is `Polity/`'s work.

### Concept check

**Question:** An administrator argues: "Our orders are issued under valid rules and applied impartially, so no question of injustice arises." State the strongest version of this defence, then show precisely what it leaves out.

**Model answer:** The defence is the position the chapter calls **'justice according to law'**, and at full strength it is serious. On **Ross's** account, expounded through **Ginsberg**, a legal order requires that decisions be made **not arbitrarily but in accordance with general rules**, **correctly (that is … impartially) applied**, **by criteria defining the class of cases coming under the rule**, and **not affected by the subjective reactions of the judge**, so that **justice then is conformity with existing law**; on this view **a good law is known by its efficiency in attaining its purpose, whatever that purpose may be**. The defence even has an argument the critic must answer: **justice in totalitarian societies may be distorted not because of bad laws, but because of the power given to judges to disregard fixed rules** — arbitrariness usually enters through exception, not through legislation. What it leaves out is the second source of obligation. **Barker's** distinction between **positive law** and **natural law** — the latter **valuable whether it is valid or not** — yields the pivot: **authority gives validity to law, and justice gives it value**. Validity obliges legally and by **outward compulsion**; value binds **morally and by an inward force**; and a rule can possess the first entirely while lacking the second. The chapter's verdict follows — Barker is **definitely more sound**, since law must **embody human values according to the prevailing social consciousness** or become **a dead weight on the wheels of social progress**. ⚠️ The qualified conclusion keeps both halves: valid, impartial application is necessary and is the main protection against arbitrary exception, but it is not sufficient, and the remedy for an unjust rule is revision of the rule rather than selective disregard of it.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the formalist position as a straw man or as indifference to justice. ⚠️ Its demand for generality and immunity from the judge's subjective reactions is a protection, and the same demand reappears as the legitimate half of the procedural case in Lesson 8. The chapter rejects its *sufficiency*, not its content.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Authority gives validity to law, and justice gives it value." Elucidate. *(Elucidate — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (within 150 words):** The clause separates two sources of obligation. On **Barker's** distinction, **positive law** is **declared by each community for its own members**, while **natural law** rests **on what is right-in-itself … valuable whether it is valid or not**. Hence a law **has validity, and I am legally obliged to obey it**, when **declared, recognized, and enforced … by the authority of the legally organized community**; it **has value**, binding **not only legally, and not only by an outward compulsion, but also morally and by an inward force**, when it has **the inherent quality of justice**. The force of this is that the two can come apart: an impeccably administered rule may still be worthless to those it governs — which **Ross's** test, **a good law is known by its efficiency in attaining its purpose**, cannot register. ⚠️ Hence the verdict and its limit: ideally law has **both**, and validity remains necessary against the arbitrary exception.

### 1. Why the two words are joined

**The term 'socio-economic justice' comprehends two important elements: 'social justice' and 'economic justice'. Their combination into 'socio-economic justice' is significant because social life of the community cannot be transformed according to the principle of justice unless its economic relations are suitably transformed** (PDF p.441, printed 422).

⚠️ The compound is therefore an argument, not a convenience. The claim is one of **dependence**: changes in social standing that are not matched by changes in economic relations do not hold, because the social relation is sustained by the economic one. An answer that uses the compound term without being able to say why the two halves are joined has missed the chapter's reasoning.

The two senses are then separated precisely. **Economic justice** is used **in the restricted sense of reordering human relations in the economic sphere**, and the chapter lists four relational pairs — **relations between employer and worker, between trader and consumer, between landlord and tenant, between moneylender and borrower, etc.** — with the stated purpose **so as to eliminate exploitation of the vulnerable sections of society**. 🔶 Three of the four pairs are absent from both canonical owners, which carry the employer/worker pair alone; the full set is worth reproducing because it establishes that the argument is about **asymmetric bargaining relations generally**, not about industrial relations in particular.

**Social justice** is used **more comprehensively so as to include economic justice as also to restore the dignity of human beings who have lost it due to a lower economic, educational and cultural status**. ⚠️ Note the three sources of lost status — **economic, educational and cultural** — and note the verb: **restore**. The claim is that the dignity was removed by a social arrangement, which is why its restoration is owed rather than offered. This is the same structure as Lesson 4's **right, not charity** and Lesson 5's **what is due to the individual from society**; three blocks converge on it, and an answer that notices the convergence is demonstrating command of the chapter rather than recall of a line.

### 2. The growth formula, and the chapter's own constitutional illustration

🔶 **When we speak of 'economic growth with social justice', the term social justice suggests that the benefits of economic growth — which are largely economic in nature — should accrue to larger and larger sections, especially to the lower strata of society** (PDF p.442, printed 423). The chapter adds that socio-economic justice **comprehends the reallocation of both material and moral advantages of social life** and **denotes a progressive concept and a model of development**.

⚠️ The formula is the chapter's answer to a very common examination proposition — that growth and justice are alternatives. The structure of the reply is that **social justice is a specification of the distribution of growth**, not a subtraction from it: the question is not whether to grow but whose sections the increment reaches. ⚠️ Note also **moral advantages**: the reallocation is not only of goods but of standing, which is what connects this block to the dignity sentence above.

🔶 Then the chapter's own illustration, absent from both canonical owners because they deliberately exclude constitutional detail: **the Indian Constitution, particularly in its Preamble and the part dealing with the Directive Principles of State Policy, holds the promise of justice — social, economic and political.** ⚠️ Reproduce this as **Gauba's 2009 textual illustration** of the three-fold division he has just drawn. ❌ No article number, no case, no amendment, no adjudication of how far the promise has been met — all of that is `Polity/`'s and `Social-Justice/`'s, and this edition asserts none of it.

### 3. The hinge, which is the lesson's deployment engine

**In common parlance, the term 'social justice' is usually applied to comprehend all the three aspects of justice in society — social, economic and political. Of these, the economic aspect is most crucial because economic disparities and injustice are bound to erode the foundations of legal and political justice** (PDF p.442).

⚠️ This is a causal claim and should be used as one. It asserts a mechanism of **erosion**: the legal and political institutions of Lesson 6 remain formally intact while their *usability* decays, because a person without means cannot in practice invoke a court, contest an election or sustain an association. The checklist of Lesson 6 demanded the **universal availability of the mechanism**; this sentence identifies what destroys the universality without touching the mechanism. That is why items (a) and (b) of the synthesis below are not welfare demands but **defences of political justice**.

### 4. The four-part synthesis, which is the best structure in the topic for a welfare stem

**In a nutshell, the term social justice, in the wider sense, implies a reordering of social life in such a manner that the material and moral benefits of social effort are not cornered by a tiny privileged class but accrue to the masses to ensure the uplift of the lower, weaker and underprivileged sections. This involves a logical synthesis of liberty, equality and fraternity in their substantive aspects so that:**

- **(a) liberty of thought and expression, etc. is supplemented by a democratic electoral system free from the decisive influence of money and manipulative power;**
- **(b) formal equality is not rendered ineffective due to vast economic disparities, especially in the field of seeking justice in the law-courts and availing of educational and career development opportunities;**
- **(c) special safeguards for minorities and weaker sections help to raise their dignity and promote the spirit of fraternity; and**
- **(d) the right to property is subordinated to the common good and the pattern of production of goods and services tends to subserve social needs.**

🔶 Only (d) is partly carried by the canonical owners; (a), (b) and (c) are absent from both, which makes the full set unusually valuable.

⚠️ Read the set as the **substantive aspect of Lesson 4's ladder**, which is exactly how the chapter introduces it: (a) is liberty made substantive, (b) is equality made substantive, (c) is fraternity made substantive, and (d) is the property condition that makes all three sustainable. That mapping is what turns the list into an argument and is the single most efficient structure available for a rights-based welfare stem: name the formal guarantee, name the economic condition that defeats it, name the corrective.

⚠️ Two cautions. The two specifics inside (b) — **seeking justice in the law-courts** and **educational and career development opportunities** — are the chapter's own and should be retained, because they are what make the erosion claim concrete. And (d) is a **statement of the chapter's position on property**, not an argument for it. ➡️ The property debate — Locke, labour, Nozick's entitlement, Hobhouse's social origin, Tawney's function test, abolition and trusteeship — is **Topic 18's**, and this edition takes only the single clause the chapter prints here.

### 1. The thesis, stated as its holders would state it

**The contemporary debate on the nature of justice focuses on the distinction between procedural justice and substantive justice (also called social justice or distributive justice)** (PDF p.442, printed 423).

**The champions of procedural justice hold that it is necessary to determine a just procedure for the allocation of social advantages, viz. goods and services, opportunities and benefits, power and honours; then its outcome will automatically be accepted as just. In other words, the allocation resulting from a just procedure must be treated as just.** The opposing camp inverts the priority: **the allocation or distribution of social advantages among various sections of society itself should be just — that is the primary issue; the procedure for making such allocation is a secondary issue, which can be adjusted suitably to meet the requirements of just distribution.**

⚠️ The disagreement is therefore about **which is the dependent variable**, and saying so is worth more than any amount of description. For the proceduralist the outcome is whatever a fair process yields; for the substantivist the process is whatever a just outcome requires.

The chapter then grounds the procedural view: **the notion of procedural justice is closely related to the tradition of liberalism**, on which **the function of justice is to regulate the mutual relations between individuals and groups**, so **the quest for justice should aim at evolving reasonable rules which should be applied impartially to all categories**. Two further commitments are named: **freedom of contract is the mainstay of procedural justice**, and **it requires the state to ensure that no individual or group would oppress another by force or fraud** (PDF p.443, printed 424).

🔶 The analogy, with its attribution restored — the canonical owners use the fair race but never attribute it — is **Norman Barry**, *An Introduction to Modern Political Theory* (**1989**): **procedural justice is exemplified in competitions, such as races. A fair race is not one in which the person who wins morally deserves to win but one in which there is no cheating, nobody jumps the gun or has an unfair advantage through the use of drugs.**

⚠️ The analogy is stronger than its usual caricature, and an answer should say why before attacking it. It explicitly **severs fairness from desert**: the proceduralist is not claiming that winners deserve to win, only that the race was clean. That is a coherent and modest position, and it is immune to the objection that market outcomes do not track merit — because it never claimed they did.

The chapter completes the picture: procedural justice **treats the rules of market economy as the model rules of human behaviour**, holds that **the market mechanism creates necessary conditions for the most efficient use of resources**, and concludes that **any artificial social policy designed to disturb this process will lead to wastage of the rare material and human resources**. The roster is given exactly as printed: **Herbert Spencer (1820-1903), F.A. Hayek (1899-1992), Milton Friedman (1912-2006) and Robert Nozick (1938-2002)**; **besides, John Rawls (1921-2002) has sought to accommodate the requirements of substantive justice or social justice in his well-drawn scheme of procedural justice.**

### 2. The concession, which is not optional

**Procedural justice repudiates all discrimination between human beings on grounds of caste, creed, sex, race, region, language and culture, etc. and accepts equal dignity and moral worth of all human beings. In this respect, it is a progressive idea** (PDF p.443).

⚠️ Reproduce this. It is the chapter's own, it is unusual in a critical passage, and it does two things for an answer. It establishes that the critique is not an attack on impartiality — the anti-discrimination commitment is kept, and Lesson 9's floor is built on top of it rather than against it. And it fixes the precise location of the disagreement, which the next sentence states: **but it stretches the principle of open competition too far**. The charge is one of **overextension**, not of error. That single word is the difference between a graded critique and a denunciation.

### 3. The critique, thinker by thinker, in the printed order

**Spencer.** He **went to the extent of suggesting that the state should not extend any help to the handicapped and let the weakest go to the wall**, arguing that **if the state gives any support to the incapable, the imprudent and the weak, it would amount to depriving the capable, the prudent and the strong of their genuine share, and thereby impeding social progress**. The chapter's verdict: **Spencer's social philosophy implies applying Darwin's principle of 'natural selection' and 'survival of the fittest' to the realm of human relations. It obliterates the distinction between rules of the animal world and those of civil society.** ⚠️ Present this as **the chapter's characterisation**, with the Social-Darwinist vocabulary marked as Gauba's framing; 🔶 note also that Spencer is absent from the Basic owner's procedural roster, which lists Hayek, Friedman and Nozick alone, so the name is carried here on the chapter's authority.

**Hayek.** He **suggested that the state should positively promote competition and ensure that the market is not reduced to an instrument of distributive justice**. ⚠️ Note that this is a *positive* role for the state, not an absence of one, which already distinguishes Hayek from Spencer.

**Friedman.** He **eulogized competitive capitalism as an essential condition of freedom and opposed all measures of human welfare and social security**.

**Nozick.** As the chapter prints it, he **contended that the state has no authority to redistribute the property of its citizens who were originally its clients**. ⚠️ Flag this precisely: it is **Gauba's statement of Nozick**, an idiosyncratic compression of the protective-agency story, and ❌ it must never be presented as Nozick's own words. ➡️ The standard formulation — a historical, unpatterned entitlement test of acquisition, transfer and rectification — is given once at Lesson 10, and the entitlement-with-property development belongs to **Topic 18**, the libertarian perspective to **Topic 20**.

⚠️ **The doctrinal guard both owners insist on**, and the single most valuable correction available in a 20-marker on proceduralism: the four names are **not one position**. Spencer's is a developmental argument about progress; Hayek's is a claim about the kind of order a market is, so that assessing an unplanned pattern as just or unjust is a mistake of category; Friedman's is a claim that uncoerced exchange is itself the fair procedure; Nozick's is a historical entitlement test with a rectification principle built in. ⚠️ The Basic owner (§13) records explicitly that **Gauba groups these thinkers more closely than their doctrines warrant**. An answer that treats the roster as a single doctrine will be unable to explain why a critique that defeats one of them leaves the others standing.

### 4. The reply, and the test it yields

**C.B. Macpherson (1911-87) has rightly pointed out that the capitalist system destroys the creative freedom of human beings who are constrained to employ their talents, skills and energies to cater to the needs of the market place instead of pursuing their self-appointed goals. Where is the scope of justice under such conditions of constraint?** (PDF p.443).

⚠️ The objection's structure is what makes it powerful, and it should be stated structurally rather than quoted alone. It does not say the race is rigged; it says that the **contestants' purposes are supplied by the race**. The fair-race analogy presupposes independent runners who choose to compete; if the system determines what counts as a talent worth developing, then procedural cleanliness is assessed inside a frame the loser never chose. That is why the objection reaches the strongest version of the procedural case rather than a caricature of it — and it is also the point at which Lesson 9's four-factor analysis of desert becomes necessary.

Then the reply's positive content: **the idea of substantive justice corresponds to the philosophy of socialism. It holds that test of justice in society consists in ascertaining whether the poor and the underprivileged have adequate opportunity to improve their lot. It demands that the opportunities of self-development should be progressively extended to the underprivileged and disadvantaged sections of society** (PDF pp.443-444).

⚠️ Note that the test is stated in terms of **opportunity to improve**, not of equal outcomes — which is a more defensible position than the one usually attributed to it, and which keeps Lesson 9's reward-above-the-floor structure available.

⚠️ **The balance both canonical owners require, and the standing qualification of this topic:** procedure is **necessary but not sufficient**, and ❌ substantive justice does **not** abolish due process. The chapter rejects the *sufficiency* of fair procedure, not its value; impartial general rules remain the protection against the arbitrary exception that Lesson 6's formalist correctly identified. An answer that writes as though substantive justice licenses disregarding procedure has adopted a position neither the chapter nor its owners hold.

### 1. The conditions restated, and the contrast that sharpens them

**Justice is primarily a problem of moral philosophy. In politics, the concept of justice is used as a guide to public policy** (PDF p.444, printed 425). The chapter then restates Lesson 1's two conditions with an added specification: the question arises **(a) in a scarcity situation — where goods, services, opportunities, etc. are too scarce to satisfy all contestants; and (b) in an open society — where allocation of various benefits is not tied to fixed status of various individuals but they are free to demand a fair share on some reasonable ground**.

⚠️ The second condition is sharper here than at Lesson 1, because of the contrast it is set against: **in a hierarchical or authoritarian society where all social advantages are allocated to different sections of society according to a predetermined scheme, justice is conceived as the strict adherence to that scheme**. That sentence is the bridge back to Lesson 5 — it is exactly the traditional conception, where justice is fidelity to an assigned position — and it shows that the chapter's argument is internally closed: the conclusion presupposes the shift it described two blocks earlier.

**In an open society, we may consider two major criteria of allocation: (a) allocation according to need; and (b) allocation according to desert, merit and ability.** Those are the two, and the rest of the block is their adjudication.

### 2. The matched reductios — the reason the floor is a conclusion, not a preference

🔶 Both arguments are absent from both canonical owners, and they must be run **as a pair**. Taken singly each looks like an ideological complaint; taken together they define a space with two closed ends.

**Against pure need.** **In a scarcity situation it is impossible to meet everybody's needs. If we resort to an equal distribution of social advantages for the purpose of equal satisfaction of everybody's needs irrespective of their contribution, then no incentive will be left to work hard. The result will be less production and a lesser availability of goods and services, etc. for distribution and a general impoverishment of society.**

**Against pure competition.** **On the other hand, if we introduce a purely competitive system, ignoring needs altogether, some human beings might be constrained to lead sub-human life. This would result in a general moral degradation as well as loss of potential for social progress.**

⚠️ Note the symmetry of the damage claimed, because it is what makes the pair an argument rather than a balancing act: each extreme is charged with **destroying the very thing it was meant to maximise**. Pure need-based distribution is charged with impoverishing the society whose needs it meant to meet; pure competition is charged with destroying the **potential for social progress** that competition was meant to generate. ⚠️ The Basic owner (§14) adds a fair caution worth carrying in one clause: the incentive objection is **a contested claim rather than an established consequence** of satisfying need. Carrying that caution strengthens rather than weakens the answer, because it shows the candidate knows which premise is doing contested work.

### 3. The floor, with the reason attached to its hardest item

**Justice, therefore, requires that a provision should be made for meeting some minimum needs of all human beings irrespective of their individual contribution to society. Minimum needs could include means of subsistence, health care, some level of education or literacy, and some job opportunities because a job is not only a means of subsistence but also a means of self-respect** (PDF p.444).

⚠️ Three features repay attention. The qualifier **irrespective of their individual contribution** is what makes this a floor rather than a reward — it is the one allocation in the chapter that is explicitly detached from desert. The list is **modest and concrete**: *some level* of education or literacy, *some* job opportunities; the chapter is specifying a minimum, not a comprehensive welfare programme, which makes the claim easier to defend in an answer. And the fourth item carries its own argument: a job is defended **not only** as income but as **a means of self-respect**, which connects the floor to Lesson 7's **restore the dignity** and to Lesson 4's **right, not charity**. ⚠️ That last point is the one to deploy in an ethics answer, because it explains why a transfer that replaces employment is not equivalent to employment.

### 4. The three refinements — the highest-value content in the topic

🔶 All three are absent from both canonical owners, and almost no candidate carries them.

- **While making allocation according to need, it is imperative that the neediest should get priority.**
- **Needs should be determined on individual basis (or family-unit basis).**
- **If some groups or classes are declared to be entitled to special concessions, they are bound to develop the vested interest in the system. As a consequence, the claims of the neediest individuals (or family-units) among other categories are bound to be ignored, resulting in injustice.**

⚠️ The argument is internal to need-based allocation, and that is the point to make explicit: it is not an objection to helping the disadvantaged but an objection to a particular *unit of assessment*. The reasoning has two steps — a declared group entitlement creates an interest in preserving the declaration (**vested interest**), and because the entitlement attaches to the category rather than to the condition, the **neediest individuals in other categories** are passed over. The chapter's verdict is blunt: **resulting in injustice**.

⚠️ **Handling discipline, which is not optional here.** Teach this as **Gauba's argument**, name the counter-case, and give no verdict.

The counter-case is the Basic owner's (§18), and it is a genuine theoretical alternative rather than a concession: **preferential provision for historically excluded groups is better framed through substantive equality, representation, anti-subordination and historical exclusion than as ordinary need-based welfare**, because the injury being addressed is *group-based subordination* rather than individual want, and a purely individual means test cannot register it. ⚠️ So the two positions differ about **what kind of injury is being remedied**, not about whether the disadvantaged should be helped. An answer that states both and declines to adjudicate is stronger than one that picks a side, because the adjudication is a policy question this topic does not own. ❌ No verdict on reservation policy, ❌ no creamy-layer or ceiling claim, ❌ no constitutional or case material — all of that belongs to `Polity/` and `Social-Justice/`, and the Topic-18 material adjacent to it is itself frozen at the chapter's 2009 vintage.

📰 **The anchor's tension, stated openly.** The National Overseas Scholarship recorded at Lesson 7 is precisely the configuration this block warns about and the owner's counter-case defends: **125 slots** distributed as **115 / 06 / 04** across **category groupings**, with **30 per cent** earmarked by sex — a **group-and-category** allocation with an income condition inside it, not an individual or family-unit need ranking. ⚠️ Name the tension and leave it standing: Gauba's criterion would ask whether the neediest individual across all categories is reached; the anti-subordination case would answer that the scheme targets a structural exclusion that an individual means test would miss, and that the income condition and the female earmark are attempts to address both at once. ❌ Neither claim is adjudicated here, and ❌ the scheme is not offered as evidence for either theory.

### 5. Desert, decomposed — and the chapter's refusal of an easy close

**Once the provision for minimum needs has been made, an additional reward can be given according to desert. Criterion of desert is, again, by no means a simple issue. A purely competitive system is no answer to the question.** The decomposition: **in a system based on desert, reward will depend on four factors: (a) one's talents and skills; (b) effort; (c) opportunity; and (d) social demand. Of these, the first two factors, viz. talents and skills combined with effort, are hardly controversial, but the last two factors, viz. opportunity and social demand might prove to be beyond control of the individual** (PDF pp.444-445).

The two failure modes are then named: **talents and effort may go unrewarded or scantily rewarded for want of adequate opportunity**, and **variations in social demand might result in overvaluation or undervaluation of talents**.

🔶 **John H. Schaar**, 'Equality of Opportunity, and Beyond', in *Contemporary Political Theory*, edited by **Anthony de Crespigny and Alan Wertheimer** (**1971**), supplies the evidence: **talents might not be rewarded according to their intrinsic worth due to variations in the prevailing hierarchy of values in different social settings** — **thus, soldierly qualities and virtues were highly admired and rewarded in the ancient Sparta, while the poets languished.**

⚠️ The Sparta example is doing real analytical work and should not be reduced to an anecdote. It shows that **social demand is a social fact, not a measure of worth**, so a reward system indexed to demand is indexed to a society's current valuations — which is exactly Macpherson's point in Lesson 8 restated from the supply side. **C.B. Macpherson has lamented on the lack of creative freedom for ordinary human beings in a capitalist system**, and the chapter's final sentence draws the prescription: **in view of these constraints, the state should create adequate opportunities for and give suitable encouragement to sublime arts and professions in order to meet the requirements of justice.**

⚠️ Two observations close the lesson. First, the four-factor analysis gives an answer the vocabulary to say *precisely* what is wrong with a crude meritocratic claim: factors (a) and (b) can justify differential reward, (c) and (d) cannot, and real rewards are produced by all four together. ⚠️ The Basic owner (§14) adds the further caution that effort itself is partly shaped by unchosen circumstance, which narrows the uncontroversial zone further still. Second, the chapter's own close is **not** a redistribution demand but an **opportunity-creation** demand — the state corrects factor (c) and offsets factor (d) rather than equalising outcomes, which keeps the position consistent with the floor-then-reward structure and with Lesson 4's **common civic minimum beyond which one's work is rewarded according to the value of the contribution**.

### Concept check

**Question:** Derive the minimum-needs floor from the chapter's own argument rather than asserting it, and then state the strongest difficulty the chapter itself raises about what lies above the floor.

**Model answer:** The floor is derived by closing both ends of the space. Under scarcity, advantages are **too scarce to satisfy all contestants**, and the open society leaves people **free to demand a fair share on some reasonable ground**, so two criteria present themselves — **allocation according to need** and **allocation according to desert, merit and ability**. Each fails if taken alone, and each fails by destroying its own objective. Pure need: equal distribution **irrespective of their contribution** means **no incentive will be left to work hard**, hence **less production** and **a general impoverishment of society** — the needs it meant to meet become harder to meet. Pure competition: **ignoring needs altogether**, some are **constrained to lead sub-human life**, producing **general moral degradation as well as loss of potential for social progress** — the progress it meant to generate is lost. What survives both is a provision for **some minimum needs of all human beings irrespective of their individual contribution**: **means of subsistence, health care, some level of education or literacy, and some job opportunities because a job is not only a means of subsistence but also a means of self-respect**. The difficulty above the floor is the chapter's own and it does not resolve it: **an additional reward can be given according to desert**, yet desert depends on **(a) talents and skills; (b) effort; (c) opportunity; and (d) social demand**, and while the first two are **hardly controversial**, the last two **might prove to be beyond control of the individual** — **talents and effort may go unrewarded … for want of adequate opportunity**, and **Schaar (1971)** shows reward tracking **the prevailing hierarchy of values**, as in **Sparta**, where soldierly virtues were rewarded **while the poets languished**. ⚠️ Hence the chapter's modest close: create **adequate opportunities** and encourage **sublime arts and professions** — correcting (c) and offsetting (d), not equalising outcomes, with the floor still coming before desert.

### 1. Rawls — the flag, and the hand-off

⚠️ Owner-only material (Basic owner §15), which itself instructs that the architecture must **not** be duplicated outside Topic 20.

Rawls uses a **fair procedure** to *derive* principles of justice rather than to validate whatever outcome emerges: reasoning from an **original position** behind a **veil of ignorance** about one's own future place in society, parties select **two principles**. The first secures an equal scheme of **basic liberties** and holds **lexical priority** over the second; the second contains **fair equality of opportunity**, which in turn precedes the **difference principle** permitting inequalities only where they benefit the least advantaged.

⚠️ Two sentences of placement, which is all this topic needs. First, this is why the chapter can list Rawls among proceduralists while also calling him **strongly opposed to utilitarianism**: his procedure generates constraints rather than ratifying results, which is exactly what Lesson 8 recorded as **sought to accommodate the requirements of substantive justice … in his well-drawn scheme of procedural justice**. Second, the lexical structure is the answer to the common error of describing him as simply redistributive — the liberties are not tradeable against distributive gains.

➡️ **Stop there.** The derivation, the motivational assumptions, the content of the principles, the criticisms and the comparisons are **Topic 20's**. ⚠️ This flag serves Philosophy **2023 Q1(a)**, **2026 Q1(a)** and the Rawls half of **2019 Q1(a)** — all owned by **Social and Political Ideals** — by supplying placement only.

### 2. Nozick — the flag, and the correction that matters most

⚠️ Owner-only material (Basic owner §13; Advanced owner §5).

Nozick's test is **historical**, **unpatterned** and **entitlement**-based: a distribution is just if it arose justly, through **justice in acquisition** (constrained by a proviso that appropriation must not worsen others' position), **justice in transfer** (voluntary exchange, gift or bequest from a just holding), and **justice in rectification** where acquisition or transfer was unjust. Whether the resulting pattern matches any preferred shape is, on this view, beside the point.

⚠️ Two corrections, both of which earn marks precisely because the chapter's compression invites the opposite impression. **The roster is not one doctrine**: Hayek's objection is that an unplanned order has no distributor and so cannot be assessed as a just or unjust *pattern*; Friedman's criterion is that uncoerced exchange is itself the fair procedure; Nozick's is a historical test about how holdings came about. Different objections defeat each. And **rectification is load-bearing**: it is what stops the theory from blessing the status quo, since any holding traceable to unjust acquisition or transfer remains open to correction under Nozick's own framework — though critics reasonably note that establishing the historical facts needed to trigger rectification is extremely difficult in practice.

### 3. Sen, and the recognition turn — the flag, and the hand-off

⚠️ Owner-only material (Basic owner §16), framed there explicitly as boundary markers.

Sen's contribution at this boundary is methodological as much as substantive. **Capability** functions as an **informational space** for judging the real freedoms people have — what they are actually able to do and be — rather than their holdings or their stated satisfaction; and his method is **comparative** and **realization-focused**, asking how remediable injustice in actual lives can be reduced through public reasoning rather than how a perfectly just institutional scheme would be specified. ⚠️ Nussbaum's capability approach is related and should not be collapsed into Sen's methodological account.

**Fraser** analyses injustice through **redistribution** and **recognition** under the normative aim of **parity of participation**, her mature framework adding **representation**. ⚠️ She is named here only to mark the place where purely distributive analysis needs status and representation analysis; ❌ she is not the originator of every recognition theory.

⚠️ The placement sentence this topic is entitled to: both refine rather than replace the procedural/substantive distinction of Lesson 8 — Sen by changing the informational space in which outcomes are judged, Fraser by adding an axis that distribution alone cannot capture.

**The two standing qualifications.** Every answer in this topic ends on one of them, and an answer that ends on neither has not used the edition.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

Three checks: two consolidate a block, and the third spans the whole arc. Each model is held within 250 words.

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 to 4

**Question:** Build the chain from what justice allocates to the ordering principle, naming each step and the clause that carries it.

**Model answer:** One: justice determines criteria for allocating **goods, services, opportunities, benefits, power and honours as well as obligations** under scarcity — **benefits as well as burdens**. Two: the question has a domain, fixed by elimination — it is irrelevant in a **purely authoritarian** regime (**the dictates of an established authority**), a **purely competitive** one (**the free play of market forces**) and a **purely communist** one (**if everybody's needs could be met, the problem of injustice … would automatically disappear**), surviving only in **an open society in a situation of scarcity** with **channels of appeal and even of protest**. Three: the allocation is a question of **right**, not of **good**, because good and bad form a **continuum** admitting **partly good**, while right and wrong form a **dichotomy** in which **negative would prevail** — so aggregate advantage cannot discharge a justice claim, which is why **Bentham advanced a theory of allocation … without pretending that it was a theory of justice**. Four: the standard changes without becoming arbitrary, since the ideal is **akin to the absolute truth** while **our realization … is a continuous process**; the criterion is **reason**, and **man develops his faculty of reasoning according to the social consciousness of his age** — **Raphael's** **modern consciousness** (1976). Five: the criterion is then ordered — **Barker's** **thread** running through the three values — liberty **qualified by** equality, equality **further qualified by** fraternity, yielding **reasonable discrimination in favour of the weaker party** **as a matter of right, not charity**, and a **common civic minimum**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat step two as historical scene-setting. The elimination is what makes the later steps necessary: without scarcity there is nothing to allocate under criteria, and without an open society there is no revisable criterion for reason to work on.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 5 to 7

**Question:** Show how the shift from the just man to the just society makes the three dimensions of justice intelligible, and name the claim that orders them.

**Model answer:** The traditional conception attached *just* to the person: **the performance of his duties attached to his status determined by the prevalent law, social customs and mode of thought**, with **Nettleship's** Plato making justice **the power of individual concentration on duty**, function-relative, and **Durant's** arrangement giving rule to knowledge while industry and arms **would not rule**. Because status is assigned, the arrangement is never assessed — a **conservative** idea requiring **the individual conforming to a pre-conceived image of society**. The modern conception moves the predicate to the arrangement — **a progressive idea** that **seeks to transform society itself** — which is why **Raphael** records that *social justice* **tends to issue from the mouths of reformers**, and why it is **the voice of the oppressed**, expressing **what is due to the individual from society**. Only then do the dimensions become askable. **Legal justice** divides into **justice according to law** and **law according to justice**, resolved by **authority gives validity to law, and justice gives it value**, since otherwise law becomes **a dead weight on the wheels of social progress**. **Political justice** supplies the institutions: **universal adult franchise**, **independence of the judiciary**, **rule of law**, **constitutional government**, **the right to criticize the government**, association and **peaceful protests** — the **universal availability of the mechanism**. **Socio-economic justice** supplies the condition, since social life **cannot be transformed … unless its economic relations are suitably transformed**. ⚠️ The ordering claim is the hinge: **the economic aspect is most crucial because economic disparities … erode the foundations of legal and political justice**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present the three dimensions as parallel compartments. The chapter calls them **by no means watertight compartments** but a **continuum**, and ranks them causally — which is why items (a) and (b) of the socio-economic synthesis are defences of political justice rather than additions to it.

### Cumulative check C — the whole arc, Lessons 1 to 10

**Question:** In one connected argument, move from the allocative question to a graded verdict on how a just allocation should be made, marking where each lesson does work.

**Model answer:** Justice allocates **benefits as well as burdens**, and the question lives only in **an open society in a situation of scarcity** (L1). The allocation is a question of **right**, which aggregate advantage cannot settle, since right and wrong form a **dichotomy** where **negative would prevail** (L2). Its standard moves without becoming arbitrary: an ideal **akin to the absolute truth** grasped through **reason** developing **according to the social consciousness of his age** (L3). Ordered, it reads: liberty **qualified by** equality, equality **further qualified by** fraternity, giving **reasonable discrimination in favour of the weaker party** **as a matter of right, not charity**, and a **common civic minimum** (L4). It became a question about *society* only after the shift from the **just man** to the **just society**, which made social justice **the voice of the oppressed** (L5). It operates through law — **authority gives validity to law, and justice gives it value** — and through institutions securing **the universal availability of the mechanism** (L6); but **economic disparities … erode the foundations of legal and political justice**, so the conditions (a)-(d) are required (L7). The dispute over whether a fair procedure suffices fails, since the system can supply the contestants' purposes (L8). ⚠️ Graded verdict: set a floor of **minimum needs … irrespective of … individual contribution**, **neediest** first, assessed on an **individual … or family-unit basis**, then reward **desert**, conceding **opportunity** and **social demand** lie **beyond control of the individual** (L9) — with rival architectures handed off (L10). Procedure is necessary but not sufficient; the floor comes before desert.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not let the chain become a tour. Each step is load-bearing: remove the dichotomy and justice becomes tradeable; remove the dynamism guard and it becomes arbitrary; remove the just-man/just-society shift and only individuals can be judged; remove the erosion hinge and the socio-economic conditions look like generosity; remove the reductios and the floor is a preference.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ Original practice prompts written for this edition. They are **not** previous-year questions and must not be cited as such. **Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish procedural justice from substantive justice. *(Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** They differ on which variable depends on which. Procedural justice fixes **a just procedure for the allocation of social advantages** and holds that **its outcome will automatically be accepted as just**; substantive justice holds that **the allocation … itself should be just — that is the primary issue**, while **the procedure … is a secondary issue, which can be adjusted suitably**. Three differentia follow. Tradition: the first is **closely related to the tradition of liberalism**, with **freedom of contract** as its **mainstay**; the second **corresponds to the philosophy of socialism**. Test: **no cheating, nobody jumps the gun** against **whether the poor and the underprivileged have adequate opportunity to improve their lot**. State's role: preventing **force or fraud** against **progressively extending** opportunities of self-development. ⚠️ Not opposed in practice: the chapter concedes procedural justice **repudiates all discrimination** and is **in this respect … a progressive idea**, so procedure is necessary though not sufficient.

**Scoring note:** a **Distinguish** directive is answered by differentia, not by two descriptions. The three-axis split — tradition, test, role of the state — supplies them, and the closing concession prevents the standard error of treating procedural fairness as worthless.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** Explain why Gauba treats socio-economic justice as the most crucial of the three dimensions. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** Because of a causal claim, not a preference: **the economic aspect is most crucial because economic disparities and injustice are bound to erode the foundations of legal and political justice**. The mechanism is erosion rather than contradiction — the institutions remain intact while their usability decays. Political justice requires **the universal availability of the mechanism for resolving the conflicting claims of different interests**; disparity destroys the universality without touching the mechanism, which is why the synthesis demands an electoral system **free from the decisive influence of money and manipulative power** and formal equality that is **not rendered ineffective due to vast economic disparities, especially in … seeking justice in the law-courts and availing of educational and career development opportunities**. The underlying dependence is stated at the outset: social life **cannot be transformed … unless its economic relations are suitably transformed**. ⚠️ The ranking presupposes the value of the other two: it is a reason to protect legal and political institutions, not to discount them.

**Scoring note:** an **Explain** directive forbids a list of dimensions. The answer gives the mechanism — erosion of usability — and pre-empts the obvious objection by showing the ranking depends on the worth of what is being eroded.

**Practice question 3 [15]:** "Justice is a dynamic idea, yet it is not a relative one." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The criterion first: does the chapter's dynamism leave any standard by which an age may be judged mistaken? It does, which is why the proposition holds. The dynamic half is conceded and evidenced. Justice **embodies an ideal which is akin to the 'absolute truth' yet it is a dynamic idea because our realization of that ideal … is a continuous process**, so **what was regarded as just some centuries ago is not so regarded today**: **slavery and serfdom** in **ancient and medieval Europe**, **untouchability** in India **a few decades ago**, **the inferior status of women** **the world over**, **racial discrimination** **till recently** — all **'justified'** then and **now widely regarded as unjust**. Note what moves: **our realization**, not the ideal. The non-relative half rests on the criterion stated separately and first: **the conception of justice should always be based on 'reason'**. Social consciousness enters only at the next step, as a fact about the reasoner — **man develops his faculty of reasoning according to the social consciousness of his age** — so it is the medium through which reason works, not a substitute standard, read as **D.D. Raphael's** **'modern consciousness'** (1976). Hence the reversals are corrections, with a named direction: **changing public opinion in favour of human liberty and equality**. ⚠️ The honest limit, supplied by the chapter: **we do not claim that this would be the perfect idea of justice**, and new ideas are admissible only **as far as they are supported by reason** — so no formulation is final, while relativism does not follow.

**Scoring note:** the directive requires a criterion before a verdict. The answer states one, concedes the evidence in full before resisting the inference, and closes on the chapter's own disclaimer rather than on an assertion of permanence.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** Examine the claim that equal treatment is sufficient to secure justice for the weaker sections. *(Examine — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Sufficient in one sphere, insufficient in another. Equality may be defined as **the 'absence of discrimination'**, and **in the legal or political sphere, this interpretation of equality will not present much difficulty** — **equality before the law**, **equal legal personality of each individual**, **equal voting rights — 'one man, one vote'**. The limit appears elsewhere: **as we turn to the economic sphere, the mere 'absence of discrimination' may not meet the requirements of justice**, because where society is stratified **on the basis of private property and conventional sources of prestige and power**, one class holding **special privileges** and another **permanently in an underprivileged position**, **the cause of justice will not be served by treating them entirely without discrimination**. The remedy is relational: where an ordinary man **as worker, consumer or tenant is open to exploitation by the employer, trader or landlord**, **the law should make reasonable discrimination in favour of the weaker party**, extending fraternity **as a matter of right, not charity**. The socio-economic block repeats this institutionally, requiring that formal equality not be **rendered ineffective due to vast economic disparities, especially in … seeking justice in the law-courts and availing of educational and career development opportunities**. ⚠️ Two qualifications bound the conclusion. The departure is not self-justifying: it stands only when **duly proved** to involve **discrimination in favour of the weaker section in the interests of justice and fraternity**. And the floor it secures is a **common civic minimum**, beyond which reward follows **the value of the contribution**.

**Scoring note:** an **Examine** directive is answered by locating the claim's domain rather than accepting or rejecting it whole. Conceding sufficiency in the legal and political sphere before denying it in the economic is what earns the higher band; the burden-of-proof clause prevents the answer from reading as an open warrant.

**Practice question 5 [20]:** "Liberty and equality remain incomplete as social ideals until justice orders them." Discuss. *(Discuss — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition states the chapter's own architecture. **Barker (1951)**: justice is **the thread which runs through all these values and makes them parts of an integrated whole**; it **reconciles their conflicts and contradictions**; the values are **manifestations of justice**; justice is **the final goal to which all these values should conform**. So the three are not coordinate ideals to be balanced but a single ordered principle. Step one asks why liberty at all: we refuse **the whims of individuals**, **survival of the fittest**, **the element of chance** and **irrational traditions and superstitions** because our **sense of justice recognizes the dignity of the human being**, demanding each be treated **as an 'end-in-itself', not a means to an end** — the idea behind the **French Declaration (1789)**. Step two shows liberty incomplete alone: **the idea of absolute liberty is a contradiction in terms**, since the liberty of A is **such liberty as he can enjoy concurrently with … similar and equal liberty by B and C and D**, so it **would not exist unless it were regulated**. Step three shows equality incomplete alone: **absence of discrimination** suffices legally and politically but not economically, where one class is **permanently in an underprivileged position**, requiring **reasonable discrimination in favour of the weaker party** **as a matter of right, not charity**. ⚠️ The ordering yields a settlement, not a slogan — a **common civic minimum** with reward by **the value of the contribution**, and **not merely formal liberty and equality, but the transformation of those social conditions**.

**Scoring note:** a **Discuss** directive rewards movement from definition through argument to a reasoned position. The decisive structural choice is to order rather than balance, and to show each value's incompleteness as a defect internal to it — which converts a slogan stem into a derivation.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** "A fair procedure, impartially applied, is the most that justice can reasonably demand." Critically evaluate. *(Critically evaluate — answer in 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The criterion of evaluation must be whether procedural fairness delivers what justice is *for* — an allocation of **benefits as well as burdens** defensible to those it binds. Its strength is real. Procedural justice evolves **reasonable rules … applied impartially to all categories**, rests on **freedom of contract**, and requires the state to prevent oppression **by force or fraud**; on **Barry's (1989)** analogy, **a fair race is not one in which the person who wins morally deserves to win but one in which there is no cheating, nobody jumps the gun**. It severs fairness from desert, so objections that markets fail to reward merit miss it. The chapter concedes it is **a progressive idea**, repudiating discrimination on **caste, creed, sex, race, region, language and culture** and accepting **equal dignity and moral worth**. The failure is overextension — it **stretches the principle of open competition too far**. **Macpherson's** reply reaches the strongest version: the system **destroys the creative freedom of human beings** who are **constrained … to cater to the needs of the market place instead of pursuing their self-appointed goals**, so the race defines what counts as a talent worth having. Hence the substantive test — **whether the poor and the underprivileged have adequate opportunity to improve their lot**. ⚠️ The qualified verdict keeps both halves. Procedure is necessary, since generality protects against distortion **because of the power given to judges to disregard fixed rules**; it is not sufficient, and due process is not abolished by a floor of **minimum needs … irrespective of … individual contribution**.

**Scoring note:** a **Critically evaluate** directive requires the opposing case at full strength before the verdict. The two moves that lift the answer are the severance of fairness from desert, and the use of the formalist's own warning about discretion as the reason procedure survives the critique.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. The allocative frame and its two conditions

- Justice determines **logical criteria for the allocation of goods, services, opportunities, benefits, power and honours as well as obligations**, **particularly in a scarcity situation** — **benefits as well as burdens**.
- **Primarily a problem of moral philosophy**; a problem of political philosophy **since it has to be implemented by a political order**.
- 🔶 Eliminated regimes: **purely authoritarian** (**the dictates of an established authority**, so **no use of search for the new criteria**), **purely competitive** (**the free play of market forces**, so **no scope of applying any new criteria**), **purely communist** (**if everybody's needs could be met, the problem of injustice … would automatically disappear**).
- Survives in **an open society in a situation of scarcity**; criteria **openly discussed and adopted** and **acceptable to all individuals or categories … concerned**.
- **There can be no final word in this behalf** → **channels of appeal and even of protest** → criteria **revised as and when so warranted by reason**.
- Boxes: **Authoritarian System** — conformity to **an established order** under its recognised **guardian**; **Open Society** — freedom **to criticize … in the light of a new logic** and to reform **by attaining public support**.
- ⚠️ Owner-only, no page: **Aristotle** — **distributive** justice in **geometric** proportion to worth; **corrective/rectificatory** justice restoring **arithmetic** equality in a **voluntary or involuntary** bilateral transaction. ❌ Corrective ≠ retributive ≠ restorative.

## B. Why justice is a question of right

- **Good** is **akin to useful, profitable, beneficial**; **bad** is **harmful, damaging, disadvantageous**; they form a **continuum** admitting **partly good and partly bad**.
- **Right** and **wrong** are **absolute categories**, **mutually exclusive** like **true and false** — a **dichotomy**, **two banks of a big river**.
- **Negative would prevail over the positive**: **if something is partly right, it must be wrong**. **Justice corresponds to right; injustice corresponds to wrong.**
- Good/bad are **quantitative** and **susceptible to measurement**; right/wrong are **qualitative**.
- **Utilitarianism deals with good and bad**: **Bentham (1748-1832)**, the **felicific calculus**, **the quantities of pleasure and pain**; **the greatest happiness of the greatest number**; hence **indifferent to the problem of justice**.
- 🔶 **Bentham advanced a theory of allocation of goods and services without pretending that it was a theory of justice.**
- **Mill (1806-73)**: **qualitative differences between different types of pleasure**; **liberty of the individual over the opinion of the majority**; **the moral worth of a policy instead of … cost-benefit analysis** → **closer to the idea of justice**.
- **Rawls (1921-2002)**: **strongly opposed to utilitarianism**; names situations that **satisfy the needs of utilitarianism but … fail to satisfy the needs of justice**.
- ⚠️ Owners' limit: the target is **reduction to aggregate advantage**, not every utilitarian account.

## C. Dynamism without relativism

- An ideal **akin to the 'absolute truth'**, **yet … a dynamic idea because our realization … is a continuous process**.
- Reversals: **slavery and serfdom** (**ancient and medieval Europe**); **untouchability** (India, **a few decades ago**); **the inferior status of women** (**the world over**); **racial discrimination** (**till recently in some parts of the world**) — all **'justified'**, **now widely regarded as unjust**.
- Proof: **changing outlook** and **changing public opinion in favour of human liberty and equality**.
- Guard: the conception **should always be based on 'reason'**, but **man develops his faculty of reasoning according to the social consciousness of his age** → **D.D. Raphael, *Problems of Political Philosophy* (1976)**, **'modern consciousness'**.
- Disclaimer: **we do not claim that this would be the perfect idea of justice**; accept new ideas **as far as they are supported by reason**.
- ⚠️ Consciousness is the **medium** of reason, not a substitute standard; reversals are corrections. ⚠️ All four illustrations are 2009-vintage and carry no claim about any present society.

## D. The ordering ladder

- **Barker (1951)**: justice is **the thread which runs through all these values and makes them parts of an integrated whole**; it **reconciles their conflicts and contradictions**; the values are **manifestations of justice**; justice is **the final goal to which all these values should conform**.
- Step 1 rejects **the whims of individuals**, **survival of the fittest**, **the element of chance**, **irrational traditions and superstitions**; affirms **dignity**, the **rational nature of man**, each as **an 'end-in-itself', not a means to an end**; no superiority by **place of birth, race, religion, language, culture, sex, education or economic status**.
- 🔶 **French Declaration (1789)**: **Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions can be based only upon public utility.**
- Step 2: **the idea of absolute liberty is a contradiction in terms**; unrestrained liberty of **the chosen few** is **the sentence of slavery for the rest of the community**; **Barker** — concurrent and **similar and equal liberty by B and C and D**, so liberty **would not exist unless it were regulated**; a restraint stands only **when it is duly proved that it is imposed in the interests of justice and equality**.
- Step 3: equality as **absence of discrimination** works in the legal/political sphere (**equality before the law**, **equal legal personality**, **'one man, one vote'**) and fails in the economic sphere, where one class is **permanently in an underprivileged position** — **the cause of justice will not be served by treating them entirely without discrimination**.
- Remedy: **the law should make reasonable discrimination in favour of the weaker party**, fraternity **as a matter of right, not charity**; the departure stands only when **duly proved** to favour **the weaker section**.
- Close: a **common civic minimum beyond which one's work is rewarded according to the value of the contribution**; guard against shares **cornered … by dint of its money and manipulative power**; **not merely formal liberty and equality, but the transformation of those social conditions**.

## E. From the just man to the just society

- Traditional: the **'just man'**, **virtues befitting a man for enhancing his moral worth**, **duties attached to his status determined by the prevalent law, social customs and mode of thought**.
- 🔶 **Nettleship (1962)**: **the power of individual concentration on duty**; **the condition of the existence of all the virtues**; forms vary **according to a man's function**; **in modern phrase … sense of duty**.
- Plato: **each element … doing its appropriate task, doing it well and doing it only**; **reason must rule within the man as well as within the state**; philosopher-kings as **the living embodiments of reason**.
- 🔶 **Durant (1954)**: industrial forces **would produce but they would not rule**; military forces **would protect but they would not rule**; knowledge, science and philosophy **would rule**.
- ⚠️ 🔶 The **Hindu caste system, as enunciated by the ancient lawgivers** parallel — a 2009 analogy about ancient texts; ❌ never about present society. ⚠️ Plato's order permits limited movement **by aptitude**.
- Shift: **from the idea of a just or virtuous man to that of a just society**; **conservative** against **progressive**; **conforming to a pre-conceived image of society** against **transform society itself**.
- 🔶 **Raphael**: social justice **tends to issue from the mouths of reformers, and to be regarded with suspicion by those who are satisfied with the existing order**.
- Social justice is **the voice of the oppressed and the underprivileged**, **an expression of what is due to the individual from society**, especially one **condemned to a wretched and subhuman living because of a defective system of distribution**; it seeks an **'authoritative allocation of values'**.

## F. Legal justice — validity and value

- Two senses: **'justice according to law'** (validity unquestioned, administration in focus) and **'law according to justice'** (the **substance of the law itself** examined).
- **Alf Ross, *On Law and Justice* (1958)**, via 🔶 **Morris Ginsberg, *On Justice in Society* (1965)**: decisions **not arbitrarily but in accordance with general rules**, **correctly … impartially applied**, **by criteria defining the class of cases**, **not affected by the subjective reactions of the judge** — **justice then is conformity with existing law**.
- **A good law is known by its efficiency in attaining its purpose, whatever that purpose may be.**
- 🔶 Its best card: totalitarian distortion arises **not because of bad laws, but because of the power given to judges to disregard fixed rules**.
- 🔶 **Barker**: **positive law** **declared by each community for its own members**; **natural law** **founded on what is right-in-itself … valuable whether it is valid or not**; traced through **Aristotle, Roman jurisprudence and Christian doctrine**.
- **Authority gives validity to law, and justice gives it value** — validity obliges legally and by **outward compulsion**; value binds **morally and by an inward force**; ideally **both**.
- Verdict: Barker **definitely more sound**; law must **embody human values according to the prevailing social consciousness**, or become **a dead weight on the wheels of social progress, only to be discarded in a violent overthrow**.

## G. Political justice — the institutional checklist

- 🔶 Broad usages, named once: **Godwin, *Essay on Property* (1793)** — the **general good**; **Kirchheimer, *Political Justice* (1961)** — all members **communicate and interact with the body politic**.
- Specific sense: **the transformation of political institutions, political process and political rights** — institutions representing **the interests of the people, not of any privileged class**.
- **Universal adult franchise** for legislature and executive; **independence of the judiciary … protected and maintained**; **rule of law** — government **not arbitrary** but by **procedures authorized by legislation passed in the proper form**; **constitutional government**.
- **Liberty of thought and expression, especially the right to criticize the government and its policies.**
- **Complete freedom** of association and interest groups, articulation through **political parties**, approval or disapproval, and **peaceful protests against harmful measures**.
- Summary: **the universal availability of the mechanism for resolving the conflicting claims of different interests in society**. ❌ No article, case or named institution attaches to this checklist here.

## H. Socio-economic justice — the deciding sphere

- The dependence: social life **cannot be transformed according to the principle of justice unless its economic relations are suitably transformed**.
- 🔶 **Economic justice** (restricted): reordering **employer and worker**, **trader and consumer**, **landlord and tenant**, **moneylender and borrower**, **so as to eliminate exploitation of the vulnerable sections of society**.
- **Social justice** (comprehensive): also **restores the dignity of human beings who have lost it due to a lower economic, educational and cultural status**.
- 🔶 **'Economic growth with social justice'**: benefits **should accrue to larger and larger sections, especially to the lower strata**; **a progressive concept and a model of development**; reallocating **both material and moral advantages**.
- 🔶 Gauba's own illustration: **the Indian Constitution, particularly in its Preamble and the part dealing with the Directive Principles of State Policy, holds the promise of justice — social, economic and political.** ❌ Nothing further follows here.
- Hinge: **the economic aspect is most crucial because economic disparities and injustice are bound to erode the foundations of legal and political justice**.
- 🔶 Synthesis: **(a)** an electoral system **free from the decisive influence of money and manipulative power**; **(b)** formal equality **not rendered ineffective due to vast economic disparities, especially in … seeking justice in the law-courts and availing of educational and career development opportunities**; **(c)** **special safeguards for minorities and weaker sections**; **(d)** **the right to property … subordinated to the common good**, production **subserving social needs**.
- ⚠️ Mapping: (a) substantive liberty, (b) substantive equality, (c) substantive fraternity, (d) the property condition.

## I. The contemporary debate

- Procedural: fix **a just procedure** and **its outcome will automatically be accepted as just**; **the tradition of liberalism**; **freedom of contract** the **mainstay**; the state prevents **force or fraud**.
- 🔶 **Barry (1989)**: **a fair race is not one in which the person who wins morally deserves to win but one in which there is no cheating, nobody jumps the gun or has an unfair advantage through the use of drugs**.
- Market rules as **the model rules of human behaviour**; **artificial social policy … will lead to wastage**.
- Roster: **Spencer (1820-1903)**, **Hayek (1899-1992)**, **Friedman (1912-2006)**, **Nozick (1938-2002)**; **Rawls (1921-2002)** accommodates substance **in his well-drawn scheme of procedural justice**.
- Concession: **repudiates all discrimination … on grounds of caste, creed, sex, race, region, language and culture**; **equal dignity and moral worth**; **in this respect, it is a progressive idea** — **but it stretches the principle of open competition too far**.
- Critique in order: **Spencer** — **let the weakest go to the wall**, **obliterates the distinction between rules of the animal world and those of civil society**; **Hayek** — **positively promote competition**, market not **an instrument of distributive justice**; **Friedman** — **eulogized competitive capitalism** and **opposed all measures of human welfare and social security**; ⚠️ **Nozick**, as Gauba states him — **no authority to redistribute the property of its citizens who were originally its clients**.
- **Macpherson (1911-87)**: the system **destroys the creative freedom of human beings** constrained **to cater to the needs of the market place instead of pursuing their self-appointed goals** — **where is the scope of justice under such conditions of constraint?**
- Substantive justice **corresponds to the philosophy of socialism**; test: **whether the poor and the underprivileged have adequate opportunity to improve their lot**, opportunities **progressively extended**.
- ⚠️ The four exponents are **not one doctrine**; ⚠️ procedure is necessary, not sufficient; ❌ due process is not abolished.

## J. The floor, the refinements and the four factors of desert

- Conditions: **too scarce to satisfy all contestants**; allocation **not tied to fixed status**, with people **free to demand a fair share on some reasonable ground**; against the **hierarchical** society where justice is **the strict adherence to that scheme**.
- 🔶 Reductios: pure need → **no incentive … to work hard** → **less production** → **a general impoverishment of society**; pure competition → **sub-human life** → **general moral degradation as well as loss of potential for social progress**. ⚠️ The incentive claim is contested, not established.
- 🔶 Floor: **minimum needs of all human beings irrespective of their individual contribution** — **means of subsistence**, **health care**, **some level of education or literacy**, **some job opportunities**, a job being **also a means of self-respect**.
- 🔶 Refinements: **the neediest should get priority**; needs on an **individual basis (or family-unit basis)**; declared **groups or classes** develop a **vested interest in the system**, so **the claims of the neediest individuals … among other categories are bound to be ignored, resulting in injustice**.
- ⚠️ Counter-case named, ❌ not adjudicated: group preference framed through **substantive equality, representation, anti-subordination and historical exclusion** addresses an injury an individual means test cannot register.
- 🔶 Desert, **by no means a simple issue**: **(a) talents and skills; (b) effort** — **hardly controversial**; **(c) opportunity; (d) social demand** — **beyond control of the individual**; talents **may go unrewarded … for want of adequate opportunity**; demand produces **overvaluation or undervaluation**.
- 🔶 **Schaar (1971)**: reward follows **the prevailing hierarchy of values** — **Sparta** rewarded soldierly virtues **while the poets languished**.
- 🔶 Closing prescription: the state **should create adequate opportunities for and give suitable encouragement to sublime arts and professions** — correcting opportunity, offsetting demand, ❌ not equalising outcomes.

## K. The boundary, the flags and the decision rules

- ⚠️ Outside Gauba's framing, names and years only: **Rawls** — a fair procedure used to derive **two principles**, **lexical priority** of equal basic liberties, **fair equality of opportunity** before the **difference principle**; **Nozick** — a **historical, unpatterned entitlement** test of **acquisition, transfer, rectification**; **Sen** — **capability** as an **informational space**, comparative and realization-focused; **Fraser** — **redistribution** and **recognition** under **parity of participation**. ➡️ All developed in Topic 20; entitlement-with-property in Topic 18.
