# Ideology and End of Ideology — Live Session Edition

This session teaches the second topic of Political Theory as a single sustained argument about a word that does two incompatible jobs. It opens with the two senses the chapter gives "ideology" — a set of ideas a group accepts as true without further examination, and a science of ideas that studies how ideas form and become distorted — and shows why the ambiguity is not sloppiness but the structure of the problem. It then follows ideology into politics: the four questions a group's ideology answers, the point at which an idea-set becomes part of politics, and the manipulative power it signifies. It teaches the action-orientation that makes ideology both capable of inspiring sacrifice and capable of inciting destruction, with the chapter's own paired examples. From there it takes up the hardest conceptual problem in the topic: political theories and political ideologies are described by the same words, so a criterion is needed to tell them apart. Sabine's challenge is stated at full strength, and the chapter's answer — separate the origin of a theory from its validity — is tested on the chapter's own worked cases of Aristotle and Machiavelli before the disinterestedness criterion is fixed.

The second movement recovers the older meaning. Tracy's science of ideas, Bacon's idols, and the modern successors — the sociology of knowledge and critical theory — set up the four great interpreters. Marx supplies false consciousness and the base-superstructure claim; Engels and Lenin turn ideology into a neutral concept that the proletariat also needs; Lukács grounds its falsity in the structural limits of a class situation rather than in ideology as such; Mannheim generalises the critique until it includes Marxism itself, splits false consciousness into ideology and utopia, and proposes a remedy that his own critics find more dangerous than the disease. Popper and Arendt then move the question from distortion to closure, where ideology fuses with a state's monopoly of truth.

The third movement is the end-of-ideology debate, taught in its proper sequence: the 1955 Milan conference and Shils's report, Bell's speech and his 1960 book, Dahrendorf's post-capitalist thesis, Lipset's verdict on affluent democracies, Rostow's stages, Galbraith's technocratic managerialism, and the later End of History extension — followed by the four critics who argue that a claim of post-ideological neutrality is itself an ideology, and by the chapter's own refusal to endorse the thesis. The session closes with answer architecture and the provenance discipline this topic demands of anyone who uses it.

What this topic does **not** own is equally important. It is not a survey of liberalism, Marxism, socialism, fascism, anarchism, Gandhism or feminism as doctrines; those are separate topics, and this one supplies only the conceptual category under which they are classified. It carries no party politics, no manifesto analysis and no constitutional or institutional facts. Lesson 21 teaches the boundary directly.

### 1. The two senses, in the chapter's own terms

The chapter opens by saying that in the realm of political theory the term "ideology" is applied in two contexts: **(a)** a set of ideas which are accepted to be true by a particular group, party or nation *without further examination*; and **(b)** the science of ideas, which examines how different ideas are formed, how truth is distorted, and how we can overcome distortions to discover true knowledge (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28).

Take the clause "without further examination" seriously, because it is the whole of the first definition. The content of the ideas is not what makes them ideological. What makes them ideological, in sense (a), is the *attitude* taken towards them: they are accepted as true, and their validity is held not to require verification.

Three consequences follow in the chapter's own text, and all three are examinable:

- **Function.** These ideas are invoked in order to **justify or denounce** a particular way of social, economic or political organisation. Note that the definition is symmetrical from the start: condemning an arrangement is as ideological as defending it.
- **Epistemic status.** In this sense ideology is "a matter of faith; it has no scientific basis", and its adherents think its validity need not be subjected to verification.
- **Social consequence.** Different groups adhere to different ideologies, so differences among them are *inevitable*; ideology therefore gives rise to a **love-hate relationship**, which is not conducive to scientific temper.

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The third consequence is derived, not asserted. Because the ideas are exempted from verification, a disagreement between two ideologies cannot be settled by evidence; and a disagreement that cannot be settled by evidence tends to be settled by loyalty. That is why the chapter reaches "love-hate relationship" from an epistemological premise rather than from a psychological one. An answer that reproduces the conclusion without the derivation has given the examiner a sentiment instead of an argument.

### 2. The chapter's own list

The chapter names ten examples of ideologies: **liberalism, capitalism, socialism, Marxism, communism, anarchism, fascism, imperialism, nationalism, internationalism** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28).

The list repays a second look, because it is deliberately mixed. It contains doctrines of individual liberty and doctrines of total state power; doctrines of economic organisation and doctrines of political belonging; a doctrine of expansion and a doctrine of its transcendence. ⚠️ That mixture is itself the point: ideology is a *formal* category, defined by how a set of ideas is held and what it is used for, not by what it recommends. Candidates who treat "ideology" as naming a family of bad doctrines have already lost the ability to say that their own preferred doctrine is also an ideology — which is exactly the self-exemption Mannheim will attack in Lesson 11.

### 3. Why the second sense is not an afterthought

Sense (b) is doing structural work. If ideologies in sense (a) exempt themselves from verification, then nothing *within* an ideology can assess it. The assessment has to come from an inquiry whose business is precisely how ideas form and how truth gets distorted. That is the science of ideas, and the chapter will trace it from Tracy and Bacon through Marx, Lukács and Mannheim in Lessons 6 to 12.

So the relation between the two senses is not that of two dictionary entries competing for the same headword. It is the relation between an object and the instrument that examines it.

| | Sense (a): set of ideas | Sense (b): science of ideas |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A group's accepted idea-set | An inquiry into how ideas form and distort |
| What it does | Justifies or denounces an arrangement | Separates true knowledge from distortion |
| Attitude to verification | Validity need not be verified | Verification is the whole activity |
| Who uses it | A group, party or nation | The analyst, including of her own side |
| Its relation to the other | The object | The instrument |

### 4. A caution about the word "faith"

⚠️ The chapter's phrase "a matter of faith" is doing descriptive rather than pejorative work, and conflating the two is a common error. It means that acceptance is not conditional on evidence; it does not mean that the content is religious, irrational or false. Lesson 15 will show that the same chapter insists oppressed classes have their own ideologies which cannot be set aside as false consciousness. Holding both is possible only if "faith" is read as a description of *how* the ideas are held.

### 5. Where this lands in an exam answer

- A stem asking you to *define* ideology should give both senses and the "without further examination" clause, then one contrast — because a definition that omits the second sense cannot explain how ideology is ever criticised.
- A stem asking whether a particular doctrine is an ideology should be answered by applying the formal test — how is it held, and what is it used for — rather than by assessing whether its content is agreeable.
- A stem on the relation between ideology and political theory, which is Lesson 4's subject, starts here: the first sense is what theory examines, the second is the mode in which theory examines it.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Liberalism and socialism are ideologies, but constitutionalism is not, because constitutionalism is reasonable." Apply the chapter's definition and say precisely what has gone wrong.

**Model answer:** The candidate has used the *content* of the doctrine as the test, when the chapter's test is formal. In sense (a), ideology is a set of ideas accepted to be true by a group **without further examination** and invoked to justify or denounce a way of social, economic or political organisation (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28). Nothing in that definition turns on whether the ideas are reasonable; it turns on whether their validity is held to require verification and on what work they are made to do. So constitutionalism could function as an ideology — if it is accepted as beyond question and used to defend or condemn an arrangement — and liberalism could function as a political theory, if its claims are placed under critical examination. The right correction is therefore to drop "because constitutionalism is reasonable" and ask instead how each doctrine is held and what each is used for. ⚠️ The further damage in the original sentence is that it exempts the candidate's own commitments from the category, which is precisely the self-exemption Mannheim's generalisation of ideology-critique later closes off.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read "ideology is a matter of faith; it has no scientific basis" as a verdict that ideologies are false or irrational. It describes the *mode of acceptance* — validity is not held to require verification. The same chapter concludes that oppressed classes have their own ideologies that cannot be set aside as false consciousness, and that conclusion is unintelligible if "faith" is read as "falsehood".

### 1. The four questions

A group will invoke its ideology to determine the best form of government, the basis of the right to rule and the procedure of selection of rulers. Broadly speaking, it answers four questions: **Who should rule? How should rulers be selected? According to what principles should a government operate? And what institutions should be maintained or replaced for the realisation of those principles?** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and a genuinely useful one.** The four questions are in a dependency order, and noticing it turns a list into an analytic tool. The fourth depends on the third — which institutions you keep depends on which principles you want realised. The third depends on the second and first, because principles of operation presuppose a decision about who holds office and how. So an ideology is not a bag of opinions; it is an *ordered* set, and its deepest commitment is usually the answer to the first question. That is why two doctrines that agree about institutions can still be irreconcilable: they are agreeing at the shallowest level.

### 2. The threshold into politics

The chapter is precise about when an idea-set becomes political. **When an ideology is used to defend an existing system, or to advocate a limited or a radical change in that system, it becomes a part of politics** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28).

Three features of this formulation deserve attention:

- It is a threshold of **use**, not of content. The same doctrine is not political while it is merely held and becomes political when it is deployed for or against an existing arrangement.
- The three options — defend, change limitedly, change radically — exhaust the field. There is no fourth, politically neutral option on offer, which is a point Lesson 20's critics will press hard against the end-of-ideology thesis.
- It connects directly to Lesson 1's symmetry: justification and denunciation are both political uses.

The consequence the chapter draws is stated in one sentence: a political ideology **may lend legitimacy to the ruling class or it may involve an urge for revolution** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF pp.28-29).

### 3. "Manipulative power" — the phrase to read carefully

The chapter then says that a political ideology **signifies the manipulative power of a dominant class or of a social movement** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.29).

⚠️ This is the sentence most likely to be misread, and reading it correctly is worth a mark. Two observations:

- The phrase covers **both** sides. It is not a charge against rulers only. A dominant class and a social movement are named together, and the same capacity is attributed to each. Lesson 15's conclusion — that oppressed classes also have ideologies — is consistent with this precisely because "manipulative power" is being used descriptively, as a capacity to move people, rather than as moral condemnation of one side.
- "Signifies" is weaker than "is". The chapter says ideology *signifies* that power: it is the visible form the capacity takes, the evidence that such a capacity exists and is being exercised. So the analytical question an answer should ask is not "is this ideology manipulative?" but "whose capacity to move people does this ideology make visible?"

| Reading | What it claims | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Ideology is manipulation by rulers" | A one-sided moral charge | ❌ Not the chapter's claim; movements are named alongside the dominant class |
| "Ideology is identical with power" | An equation | ❌ The text says "signifies", not "is" |
| "Ideology is the visible form of a capacity to move people, held by rulers and by movements alike" | A descriptive claim about what ideology indicates | ✅ The defensible reading |

### 4. Why this matters for the rest of the topic

This lesson quietly installs two things the later lessons need.

First, it establishes that ideology is **inescapably positioned**: to operate at all, it must stand for, or against, an existing system. That is why the later claim that a society has moved "beyond ideology" is so vulnerable — a position of no position is not among the three options the chapter lists.

Second, it establishes that ideology's political work is **legitimation or its opposite**. Lessons 8 to 12 are, in effect, four competing accounts of how legitimation of that kind manages to succeed: Marx by false consciousness, Lenin by organised class consciousness, Lukács by structural limitation, Mannheim by the social conditioning of all thought.

### 5. Exam use

- A stem asking what a political ideology does should answer with the four questions in their dependency order, the defend-or-change threshold, and the legitimacy-or-revolution pair.
- A stem on ideology and legitimacy should use this lesson rather than importing material on authority from elsewhere; what is owned here is the conceptual claim that a political ideology either lends legitimacy or carries an urge for revolution.
- Where an answer needs an illustration, use a structural one — a doctrine that keeps institutions while changing principles, or the reverse — rather than naming any contemporary actor.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two groups agree entirely on the institutions a state should have but remain bitterly opposed. Using this lesson, explain how that is possible and what it tells you about where their disagreement lies.

**Model answer:** It is possible because institutional agreement is agreement at the shallowest of the four questions an ideology answers. The chapter's sequence runs from who should rule, through how rulers are selected and according to what principles a government should operate, to which institutions should be maintained or replaced for the realisation of those principles (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28); the fourth is derived from the third, and the third from the first two. Two groups can therefore converge on the same institutional list while deriving it from incompatible answers about who is entitled to rule and on what principles — in which case the shared institutions are being asked to serve different purposes, and the agreement will not survive the first case in which those purposes diverge. The further diagnostic this lesson supplies is the threshold of use: ask of each group whether its doctrine is being deployed to defend the existing system, to seek limited change, or to seek radical change, since that is what fixes whether the doctrine is lending legitimacy or carrying an urge for revolution.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read "manipulative power of a dominant class or of a social movement" as a condemnation of rulers. The sentence names a dominant class *and* a social movement, and the operative verb is "signifies". It describes what ideology makes visible — a capacity to move people — rather than passing a verdict on who possesses it.

### 1. The mechanism

An ideology is **action-oriented**. It presents a cause before its adherents and induces them to fight for that cause, and to make sacrifices for its realisation (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.29).

That sentence contains three components, and separating them makes the lesson's central point visible:

| Component | What it supplies | Why it is double-edged |
|---|---|---|
| A **cause** | Something larger than the individual's own interest | The same transcendence that justifies self-sacrifice can justify sacrificing others |
| An inducement to **fight** | Motivation to act rather than merely believe | Action against an arrangement is also action against the people identified with it |
| A demand for **sacrifice** | Willingness to bear costs | Costs willingly borne by adherents may be imposed on non-adherents |

### 2. The chapter's paired examples

The first pair is the sharpest thing in the chapter. **Nationalism** may inspire people to sacrifice their wealth or life for defending the freedom of their nation. But **communalism** may induce hatred among people towards members of another community and prompt them to destroy life and property of innocent persons (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.29).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the most valuable move available here.** The pair is not offered as "one good ideology, one bad ideology". It is offered to show that the *same structural feature* — a cause that commands action and sacrifice — produces both outcomes. The difference lies not in whether a cause is held, nor in how intensely, but in **whom the cause constitutes as its outside**. An answer that says "nationalism is positive and communalism is negative" has restated the example; an answer that says "both operate by presenting a cause and demanding sacrifice, and they diverge at the point where the cause requires an enemy" has explained it.

The chapter adds a third case on the same page: **one stream of fundamentalism, based on obscurantism, has given rise to worldwide terrorism**, and it prints a definition of the mechanism:

> **Obscurantism** — a policy or tendency involving deliberate effort at making things obscure so as to prevent people from knowing the truth. (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.29)

📰 **Date-bounding this claim.** The sentence about worldwide terrorism is the Fifth Edition's own reading of its moment, and it is used here exactly as the chapter uses it: as a dated illustration of the mechanism obscurantism makes possible, not as a current description of any movement, community or region, and not as licence to name one. Note also what the definition itself asserts: obscurantism is the deliberate suppression of truth, which is why it is the limiting case of Lesson 1's "validity need not be subjected to verification" — the exemption from verification has become an active policy.

### 3. The second pair: vested interests against progress

In the sphere of politics, conflicting ideologies may be invoked to defend conflicting norms or ideals. Of these, some ideals may be designed to serve some **vested interests**, and some ideals may seek to **challenge irrational beliefs and conventions**, and thus pave the way for progress. The chapter's examples: the ideology of **imperialism** may be invoked to facilitate the exploitation of colonial territories and their people, while **environmentalism** may be invoked to save humanity from the curse of atmospheric pollution and depletion of valuable natural resources (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.29).

This pair does different work from the first. The first pair showed that one mechanism yields opposite outcomes. The second shows that the *content* of ideals genuinely differs in its relation to existing power: some are built to protect an interest, some to dislodge an entrenched belief.

⚠️ Hold both pairs together and you have the chapter's actual position, which is neither celebration nor dismissal: ideology is a mechanism of mobilisation that is indifferent as between emancipation and domination, and the evaluation of any particular ideology therefore has to be done case by case, on the content and the consequences, not by the category.

### 4. Why "action-orientation" is the hinge for the whole topic

Three later lessons depend on this one:

- **Lesson 12 (Mannheim).** Utopia, like ideology, mobilises — it "relies on false consciousness by projecting unrealizable principles to muster support for the forces of change". That is the action-orientation mechanism operating on the side of change rather than conservation.
- **Lesson 13 (Popper).** Ideology is the tool which enables a totalitarian state to **mobilise its manpower and other resources** for a goal declared to embody absolute truth. That is this mechanism captured by a state.
- **Lesson 15 (the chapter's conclusion).** Some ideologies have given rise to strong social movements for the emancipation of various oppressed sections. That is this mechanism working for those without power.

One mechanism, four destinations. Saying so explicitly is how a long answer achieves unity instead of becoming a list of thinkers.

### 5. The trap

❌ **Trap:** "Ideology is dangerous because it makes people irrational and violent."
✅ **Correction:** the chapter attributes to the same mechanism the willingness to sacrifice wealth and life for a nation's freedom. What it identifies as dangerous is not mobilisation but mobilisation around a cause that requires an enemy, and, in the limiting case, obscurantism — the deliberate prevention of people's knowing the truth. The analytical question is therefore what a given cause does with its outside, and whether its adherents are permitted to know what is true about it.

### 1. The difficulty stated honestly

The chapter does not pretend the problem is easy. It records that in many cases political theories and political ideologies are described by the same terminology, asks directly whether they indicate identical things, and notes that **some writers think it is so**. It then lists three reasons for thinking so (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.29):

- at times a political theory **seems to justify and prescribe a course of action** as if it were a political ideology;
- the **genesis** of a political theory may be sought to be explained in the light of the stresses and strains emanating from actual politics;
- sometimes the **clash of political theories** may be explained in the light of a clash in a political situation.

⚠️ Note how strong this is. All three observations are concessions. The chapter is conceding that theories prescribe, that they arise from political conditions, and that their disagreements track political disagreements. Anyone who wants to defend a distinction has to do so *after* granting all three.

### 2. Sabine's challenge, quoted

The chapter then quotes **G. H. Sabine**, from the Preface to the first edition of *A History of Political Theory* (1937):

> "This history of political theory is written in the light of the hypothesis that theories of politics are themselves a part of politics... Reflection upon the ends of political action, upon the means of achieving them, upon the possibilities and necessities of political situations, and upon the obligations that political purposes impose is an intrinsic element of the whole political process." (Sabine, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.29)

Read the second sentence carefully, because it is doing the argumentative work. Sabine does not say that theorists are partisans. He says that **reflection** — on ends, on means, on what political situations make possible and necessary, and on the obligations political purposes impose — is an *intrinsic element of the whole political process*. The claim is that thinking about politics is itself part of politics, not an external commentary on it.

⚠️ That is a stronger and more interesting thesis than the one candidates usually attribute to it. It does not depend on any theorist being biased. It would hold even if every theorist were scrupulous.

### 3. The reply: origin is not validity

The chapter's answer is one sentence, and it is the most transportable idea in this topic: **Sabine's view cannot be accepted as the final word. We must draw a distinction between the origin and the validity of a theory** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF pp.29-30).

The structure of the reply is worth setting out as a two-step, because an answer that states only the slogan has not shown why it works:

| Step | Claim | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Concede the origin** | It may be conceded that political theories arise from different political situations | Sabine's thesis is accepted in full, not evaded |
| **Separate the question** | The study of political theory *also* includes a critical evaluation of these theories | A second question is opened that the first does not answer |
| **Specify the operation** | That evaluation involves segregating the truth embodied in these theories from those elements which are the product of political considerations | The distinction becomes a procedure, not an assertion |

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The reply works by a logical point rather than a sociological one: where a belief *came from* and whether it is *true* are different questions, and an answer to the first is not an answer to the second. A theory born of a specific political crisis may still be valid; a theory born in philosophical tranquillity may still be false. Sabine is therefore right about genesis and silent about validity — and silence about validity is exactly the gap into which critical evaluation moves.

### 4. Why this is not a cheap escape

An alert objector will say: the claim that origin and validity are distinct is itself a theory, and it too has an origin. ⚠️ **Reply.** The point is not that the distinction floats free of history; it is that *using* the distinction is what critical evaluation consists of, and a theory that refuses to use it has no way of ever revising itself. The test is practical: a body of thought that can examine its own claims and discard some of them is doing something an ideology, by Lesson 1's definition, has exempted itself from. ⚠️ **Residual:** this converts the distinction from a metaphysical claim into a methodological commitment, which is weaker than it first appears — it tells you what theory must *do*, not that any particular theorist has succeeded in doing it. Mannheim, in Lesson 11, will press exactly this residual.

### 5. The payoff: the question an answer should ask

This lesson gives you a question that works on any stem of the form "is X a theory or an ideology?":

```text
   DOES THE BODY OF THOUGHT SUBMIT ITS OWN CLAIMS TO CRITICAL EVALUATION,
   SEGREGATING WHAT IS TRUE IN THEM FROM WHAT IS THE PRODUCT OF
   POLITICAL CONSIDERATIONS?

        YES -> it is functioning as THEORY, whatever its origin
        NO  -> it is functioning as IDEOLOGY, however sophisticated
```

Lesson 5 shows the chapter performing exactly this operation on two named thinkers, which is why that lesson is the demonstration this one requires.

**The Aristotle case.** We denounce Aristotle's defence of slavery and subjugation of women **because they were designed to promote the interests of 'freemen' in ancient Greek society**. But his explanation of the instability of constitutions, stating that **'power and virtue cannot coexist'**, must be accepted as valid **as it embodies an eternal truth** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.30).

Three features of this verdict are examinable.

- The ground of rejection is **functional**, not merely moral. The claim is rejected because of what it was designed to do — promote a particular group's interests within a specific society — which is precisely the "elements which are the product of political considerations" that Lesson 4's procedure told us to segregate.
- The ground of retention is **not** that Aristotle was a great thinker. It is that the particular proposition survives examination on its own.
- ⚠️ The phrase "eternal truth" is the chapter's own, and it is a strong claim that an answer should handle with care. A defensible restatement is that the proposition is not tied to the interests of any particular group in any particular society, and therefore survives the segregation test — which is what the operation can establish. Whether any political proposition is *eternal* is a larger question the chapter does not argue here.

**The Machiavelli case.** Machiavelli's advice to the Prince to set aside all moral considerations **cannot be accepted**, but his insights regarding human behaviour **can be profitably used in the sphere of diplomacy and statecraft** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.30).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the point that makes this lesson worth its length.** Notice that the two cases are split along *different* lines. In Aristotle the division is between a claim serving a social interest and a claim about institutional dynamics. In Machiavelli it is between a prescription and a description — the advice is rejected, the observation retained. So the operation is not a single cleavage applied mechanically; it is the identification, in each case, of which part of the work is carrying a political purpose and which part is carrying a finding. An answer that treats "separate the valid from the political" as one fixed cut has missed that the cut's location is itself a matter for judgment.

### Part B — The criterion

Having shown the operation, the chapter states what makes it possible: **it is the critical function of political theory that distinguishes it from the set of ideas which are the product of politics** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.30). Then it fixes three positions:

| | **Politics** | **Political ideology** | **Political theory** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Activity | Involves the pursuit of **partisan ends** | A **passionate search** for a better society **according to the prescribed model** | A **disinterested search** for a better society |
| Status of its goals | Fixed by the contest | Given in advance by the model | Under **constant investigation and critical examination** |
| Relation to an existing system | Contest over it | Designed to **defend** it, or to **condemn** it in order to prove the superiority of a different system | Examines its rightness before favouring or opposing it |

Note how "passionate" and "disinterested" are being used. They are not psychological descriptions of the writer's temperament. The operative difference is in the third row: whether the goals are **given** by a prescribed model or held **under constant investigation**.

**Hacker's formulation.** The chapter cites **Andrew Hacker**, in *Political Theory: Philosophy, Ideology, Science* (1961), for the observation that whether we look at the philosophical side of political theory or its scientific side, it is **always dispassionate and disinterested**. In other words: the theorist has **no fascination for a particular political arrangement**; his image of a good life is **not affected by any prejudice**; he does **not favour or oppose any particular arrangement without examining its rightness**. On the contrary, **an ideology is designed to defend the existing system, or to condemn it in order to prove the superiority of a different system** (Hacker, as reported in Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.30).

The chapter then states the consequence and the keynote in four compressed sentences (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.30):

- **If theory is loaded by an ideology, it is bound to be distorted.**
- Political philosophy or political theory calls for a **disinterested search for the best form of state and society**; ideology seeks to **justify** an existing or a future political and social arrangement.
- Political science calls for **impartial observation** of political and social reality; ideology **focuses on selected parts** of political and social reality, and gives its **distorted description as well as explanation**.
- **Absolute impartiality is the keynote of genuine political theory.**

⚠️ **The sharpest point in this passage, and one most candidates miss.** The failure mode specified for political science is **selection**, not fabrication. Ideology "focuses on selected parts" and thereby distorts both the description and the explanation. So an ideologically loaded political science need not contain a single false statement; it distorts by what it chooses to look at. That is a far more interesting charge than "ideology tells lies", and it is directly usable against any claim that a body of work is objective because its individual findings are accurate.

### The closing instruction

The chapter ends the section with the operating rule: in the realm of political theory, **each political theory should be critically examined**; its **strong and weak points must be discerned**; it should be **compared with other relevant theories and evaluated in that light**. It is therefore imperative to understand ideology as the **science of ideas** also (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.30).

That last clause is the bridge to Lesson 6. The critical examination demanded here needs an account of how ideas form and how they become distorted — and that account is the second sense of ideology, which the chapter now goes back to recover.

### A difficulty worth naming

⚠️ **Objection.** "Absolute impartiality" is an impossible standard, and a criterion nobody can satisfy cannot distinguish anything.

⚠️ **Reply.** Read as a description of achieved states of mind, the objection is strong. Read as a description of the *activity* the preceding paragraphs describe — holding goals under constant investigation, examining rightness before favouring or opposing, attending to the whole of a reality rather than selected parts — it is a standard that can be met more or less well, and failures of it can be identified case by case, as the chapter has just done with Aristotle and Machiavelli. ⚠️ **Residual:** the activity reading rescues the criterion at the cost of its absoluteness; what remains is a matter of degree, and degrees are contestable. Mannheim's generalisation in Lesson 11 is the most serious attempt to press that residual to breaking point.

- Operating rule: each theory is critically examined, its strong and weak points discerned, and it is compared with other relevant theories and evaluated in that light — which is why the **science of ideas** must now be recovered.
- ⚠️ Objection and residual: "absolute impartiality" survives as a description of an activity rather than of an achieved state of mind, which makes it a matter of degree.

### Concept check

**Question:** An examiner claims that a particular study of politics is non-ideological because every factual statement in it is accurate. Using Part B, say why that defence is insufficient, and what would actually have to be shown.

**Model answer:** The defence is insufficient because accuracy of individual statements is not the failure mode the chapter identifies. Political science calls for impartial observation of political and social reality, whereas ideology **focuses on selected parts** of that reality and gives its distorted description as well as explanation (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.30). A study can therefore consist entirely of true statements and still be ideologically loaded, because the distortion is produced by what has been chosen for examination and what has been left outside the frame. What would actually have to be shown is threefold: that the study's goals are held under constant investigation rather than given in advance by a prescribed model; that it does not favour or oppose a particular arrangement without examining its rightness, which is Hacker's test; and that its selection of material is not organised so as to defend an existing system or to condemn it in order to prove the superiority of a different one. ⚠️ Each of these is a matter of degree rather than a yes-or-no verdict, which is the honest limit of the criterion.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read the Aristotle verdict as "we reject the bad parts of great thinkers". The ground of rejection is functional and specific — the claim was designed to promote the interests of 'freemen' in ancient Greek society — and the ground of retention is that a different proposition in the same body of work survives examination on its own. The operation splits the work; it does not grade the author.

**Tracy.** It was **Destutt de Tracy (1754-1836)**, a French scholar, who first used the word 'ideology' during **1801-15** in his writings on the Enlightenment. He defined it as a study of the process of forming ideas — a science of ideas. Tracy observed that **ideas are stimulated by the physical environment**; hence **empirical learning (gained through sense-experience) is the only source of knowledge**. Supernatural or spiritual phenomena have no role to play in the formation of real ideas. Science is founded on these ideas, and people could use science for the improvement of social and political conditions (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF pp.30-31).

⚠️ **Coinage and publication span are two different dates, and the source states only one.** The span the source gives, **1801-15**, is the period across which Tracy's *Éléments d'idéologie* appeared in four volumes. The **coinage** of the word *idéologie* is independently dated to **1796**, five years earlier. Both facts are useful and they answer different questions: 1796 is when the term was made, 1801-15 is when the programme was set out. This session reproduces the source's span where the source is being reproduced and adds the coinage date as a separately sourced fact; no page of either work is asserted, and the two must not be run together into a single claim.

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and a strong answer-point.** Tracy's programme is *emancipatory and optimistic*: if ideas come from the environment and are checked by sense-experience, then bad ideas are correctable and science can improve social and political conditions. The later, pejorative sense of "ideology" — a closed idea-set exempt from verification — is therefore almost the exact inversion of what the word was coined to mean. Opening an answer with that inversion is both accurate and arresting.

**Bacon.** Although Tracy was the first to use the term in this sense, he was not the first to study the process of formation of ideas. **Francis Bacon (1561-1626)**, an English philosopher, before him insisted that knowledge should come from **careful and accurate observation and experience**, and held that knowledge deduced from less scientific methods of inquiry was **distorted by false impressions or 'idols'** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.31). In short, Bacon and Tracy both focused on the validity of knowledge obtained by scientific method and cautioned against distorted forms of knowledge.

### Part B — The modern successors

The chapter then updates the vocabulary, and the update is substantial (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.31):

> In contemporary literature, the term **'ideology'** is applied to the set of ideas which are adopted by a group in order to **motivate it for the achievement of predetermined goals**. **Science of ideas** is described by different terms, like **'sociology of knowledge'** (the term introduced by Karl Mannheim), or **'critical theory'** (the term popularized by the Frankfurt School). ### The puzzle in the critical-theory box, and why it is worth teaching

The last row contains something that looks, at first reading, like a contradiction. Lesson 5 fixed "disinterested" as the mark of theory and "interested" as the mark of ideology. The box now says critical theory is **'interested'** — and concludes that it therefore *cannot* be equated with an ideology.

⚠️ **Resolution.** The two uses of "interested" are different. In Lesson 5 the term meant *having a stake in a particular political arrangement* — a fascination for one model, which is what makes a theory special pleading. Here it means *having a purpose*, namely human emancipation, as against the achievement of a narrow goal. The box's own contrast makes this explicit: emancipation is set against a **narrow** goal. So a body of thought can have a purpose without having a stake, and critical theory's purpose is of a kind that does not attach it to any particular arrangement to be defended.

⚠️ **The residual, which an honest answer should keep.** That resolution is clean but not costless. "Human emancipation" is itself a substantive commitment about what is good, and a body of thought organised around it is not neutral between all conceptions of the good. The defensible position is that critical theory is not an ideology in the chapter's sense — it does not exempt its claims from examination and it is not designed to defend a particular arrangement — while conceding that it is not value-free either, which Topic 01's argument already established no political inquiry can be.

### 2. Reading the table as an argument

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the reason this lesson is not merely a transcription exercise.** The six rows are not six independent observations; they form a chain in which each row follows from the one above it.

```text
   ROW 1  the two have different SUBJECT-MATTER

              v

              a claim constituted by examination requires openness to it)
              |

              an open claim is held only so long as it survives scrutiny)
              |
              v

             (an instrument of politics needs compliance -> subordination;
              an instrument of theory needs dissent -> permission to question)
```

Reconstructing the chain is worth real marks because it converts a memorised grid into a derivation. If asked why ideology demands subordination to authority, an answer that says "because that is the sixth dimension" has said nothing; an answer that traces it back to the exemption from verification in row 2 has explained it.

### 3. The one row that needs a caution

⚠️ Row 3 — "closed mind" against "open mind" — is the row most likely to be over-read. Two cautions:

- It is a claim about the **mode of holding**, not about the intelligence or sincerity of the holder. A brilliant and honest person holding a doctrine as exempt from verification is, in this scheme, exhibiting a closed mind about that doctrine.
- It is **not** a claim that everyone who holds an ideology is incapable of examining anything. The table contrasts two *uses* of a word, and a single person routinely does both — holding a political commitment in the first mode while examining the formation of ideas in the second.

⚠️ Row 4 carries a related caution already established in Lesson 6: "interested" here means having a stake in a particular arrangement, not merely having a purpose.

### 4. How the table is deployed under exam conditions

- As a **10-mark skeleton.** A question that asks you to distinguish ideology from political theory can be answered almost entirely from rows 2, 4, 5 and 6, with one illustration.

- As a **structure for comparison.** The axes are shared, so two rival doctrines can be run down the same six rows — which is what makes the table a comparison device and not merely a definition.

### Concept check

**Question:** Derive row 6 of the table — that ideology demands subordination to authority while the science of ideas allows the individual to question it — from an earlier row, rather than simply asserting it.

**Model answer:** The derivation runs from row 2. Ideology in the first sense is a matter of faith: its claims are accepted as true without further examination and their validity is held not to require verification (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF pp.28, 32). A claim held in that mode cannot be settled by evidence, so when two holders differ there is no evidential procedure to appeal to; what settles the matter instead is the authority of whoever is entitled to state the doctrine. That is why the idea-set is an instrument of politics, which is row 5 — politics requires coordinated action, and coordinated action around an unverifiable claim requires compliance — and why it ends in row 6 as a demand for **subordination to authority**. The science of ideas runs the opposite way for the same reason: it is constituted by critical examination, so a claim within it is only ever as good as the scrutiny it has survived, scrutiny requires that objections be admissible, and admitting objections means the individual must be allowed to question whoever asserts the claim. Row 6 is therefore not an independent property of the two senses but the social consequence of row 2.

The table contrasts two uses of a word, and the same person may hold a political commitment in the left-hand mode while conducting an inquiry in the right-hand mode. ### 1. The texts and the thesis

**Karl Marx (1818-83)**, in *German Ideology* (1845-46) and *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), dwelt on the nature of ideology. According to him, ideology is **a manifestation of 'false consciousness'** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.32).

The chapter then quotes the Preface to the *Critique of Political Economy* at length:

> "In the social production which men carry on they enter into definite relations that are indispensable and independent of their will; these relations of production correspond to a definite stage of development of their material powers of production. The sum total of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society—the real foundation, on which rise legal and political superstructures and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness. The mode of production in material life determines the general character of the social, political and spiritual process of life. It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but, on the contrary, their social existence determines their consciousness." (Marx, Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.32)

Three things in that passage repay slow reading:

- **"Independent of their will."** The relations people enter into are not chosen. This is what makes the account structural rather than conspiratorial: nobody has to intend the distortion.
- **"Correspond to."** The relation between the economic structure and the forms of social consciousness is stated as correspondence, not as manufacture. The passage says consciousness is *determined by* social existence; it does not say any individual belief is manufactured by any individual capitalist.
- **The inversion in the last sentence.** It is the hinge of the whole account: not consciousness determining existence, but social existence determining consciousness. ⚠️ Note what this costs. If social existence determines consciousness, then Marx's own consciousness is also socially determined — a difficulty Mannheim will turn into an objection in Lesson 11, and which Lukács will try to answer in Lesson 10.

### 2. How distortion gets in: the lag

The chapter's explanation of *why* consciousness becomes false is specific, and candidates almost always omit it. According to Marx, **in the process of social development the material needs of people advance, but their social consciousness lags behind. This distorted consciousness or false consciousness is reflected in their ideology** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.32).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the single most useful point in this lesson.** False consciousness is here a **timing** phenomenon, not simply an error. Consciousness is not false because someone inserted a lie into it; it is false because it was formed under conditions that have since moved on, and it continues to describe and justify an arrangement that the development of material powers has already outgrown. That is why the chapter's own phrase is "lags behind".

Two consequences follow:

- Ideology in this account is characteristically **out of date** rather than simply untrue — it presents an arrangement that was once functional as though it were natural and permanent.
- The remedy implied is not refutation but **development**: consciousness catches up when the contradiction between advancing material powers and inherited relations becomes unsustainable.

The dominant class at any stage of social development then **makes use of ideology to maintain its authority** (PDF p.32) — note that this is a second, additional claim, about deliberate use, layered on top of the structural account of formation. Keeping the two apart is a mark of precision.

### 3. The worked case: the French Revolution

The chapter supplies one illustration, and it is unusually concrete (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.32):

| Stage | What happened, as the chapter states it |
|---|---|
| The slogan | The makers of the **French Revolution (1789)** raised the slogan of **'Liberty, Equality, Fraternity'** to enlist the support of the masses |
| The settlement | They **settled for liberty**, which served their interest — the interest of the **new entrepreneurial class of those days** |
| The omission | They **did not proceed to win freedom for the common man** |
| The endpoint | They **stopped after winning freedom for a new dominant class to ensure inviolability of property** |

⚠️ **What the example is designed to show, and what it is not.** It is not an argument that the revolutionaries were hypocrites. It is a demonstration of the structural claim: a universal vocabulary — liberty, equality, fraternity — was mobilised to enlist mass support, and the content that was actually realised was the content that corresponded to the position of the rising class. The three terms were not equally realised, and the one that was realised is the one that secured property. Ideology here is the mechanism by which a sectional outcome arrives wearing universal language.

⚠️ 📰 **A dating note on this lesson.** The chapter later says Marx evolved the concept of ideology "in late nineteenth century" in order to expose capitalism (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.35), while this section dates *German Ideology* to 1845-46 and the *Critique* Preface to 1859 — both mid-century. The two statements sit awkwardly together. This session follows the dated work-titles, which are the more specific evidence, and records the inconsistency rather than silently harmonising it.

### 4. The objection Marx has to face

⚠️ **Objection.** If social existence determines consciousness, then every claim — including the claim that social existence determines consciousness — is determined by the social position of whoever makes it. The theory appears to undermine its own standing.

⚠️ **Reply, and the honest statement of its limits.** Three replies are available within the chapter's own material, and they are of unequal strength. The weakest is to exempt Marx's own position by fiat, which is simply the self-exemption Lesson 1 warned against. The second, which Lukács will develop in Lesson 10, is to locate a class position whose structural situation does not require distortion. The third, which Mannheim will take in Lesson 11, is to accept the objection and generalise it — conceding that Marxism is itself the ideology of a class. ⚠️ **Residual:** the chapter does not settle which reply succeeds, and an answer that pretends it does has invented a resolution. What the chapter does establish is that the objection is the engine of everything that follows: Lukács and Mannheim are both responses to it.

Revision notes:
- **Marx (1818-83)**, in ***German Ideology* (1845-46)** and ***A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859)**: ideology is a **manifestation of 'false consciousness'** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.32).
- Quoted Preface passage: men enter into **definite relations** of production, **indispensable and independent of their will**, corresponding to a definite stage of development of their **material powers of production**; the **sum total** of these relations constitutes the **economic structure of society — the real foundation**, on which rise **legal and political superstructures** and to which correspond **definite forms of social consciousness**; the mode of production in material life determines the general character of the social, political and spiritual process of life; **"It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but, on the contrary, their social existence determines their consciousness."**
- ⚠️ "Independent of their will" makes the account **structural, not conspiratorial**; "correspond to" is weaker than "manufacture".
- **The mechanism of distortion:** material needs **advance**, social consciousness **lags behind**; the resulting distorted or **false consciousness** is reflected in ideology.
- ⚠️ False consciousness is therefore characteristically **out of date** rather than simply untrue; the remedy implied is development, not refutation.
- A second, separable claim: the **dominant class at any stage makes use of ideology to maintain its authority**.
- **French Revolution (1789) case:** the slogan **'Liberty, Equality, Fraternity'** enlisted mass support; the makers **settled for liberty**, which served the interest of the **new entrepreneurial class**; they did **not** proceed to win freedom for the common man; they stopped after winning freedom for a **new dominant class to ensure inviolability of property**.
- ⚠️ The case shows a **sectional outcome arriving in universal language**, not personal hypocrisy.
- ⚠️ 📰 Dating inconsistency inside the source: the concept is elsewhere said to be evolved "in late nineteenth century", while the works cited here are 1845-46 and 1859. The dated titles are followed and the inconsistency is recorded.
- ⚠️ The reflexive objection — the thesis applies to itself — drives the rest of the topic: Lukács answers by structural position, Mannheim by generalisation.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain, using the chapter's own mechanism, why Marx's account of ideology does not require anyone to be lying.

**Model answer:** Because the account locates the source of distortion in structure and in timing rather than in intention. The quoted Preface states that in social production men enter into definite relations that are **indispensable and independent of their will**, that the sum total of those relations constitutes the economic structure which is the real foundation on which legal and political superstructures rise, and that social existence determines consciousness rather than the reverse (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.32). Nothing in that sequence requires an intention to deceive; the forms of social consciousness *correspond to* the economic structure, and correspondence is not manufacture. The specific mechanism of falsity is then a lag: material needs advance while social consciousness lags behind, so the inherited consciousness continues to describe and justify an arrangement that development has outgrown. The French Revolution case illustrates exactly this — a universal slogan enlisting mass support while the content realised was the content corresponding to the position of the rising entrepreneurial class. ⚠️ The chapter does add a separate claim that the dominant class **makes use of** ideology to maintain its authority, which does involve intention; the precise position is therefore that falsity is explained structurally while *deployment* may be deliberate, and the two claims should not be merged.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat base and superstructure as a claim that economics mechanically dictates every belief. The passage says the relations of production **correspond to** forms of social consciousness and that the mode of production determines the **general character** of the social, political and spiritual process of life. Reading it as a one-to-one causal lever produces the caricature that Lukács's and Mannheim's refinements were devised to escape.

### 1. The Marx-Engels position, and the asymmetry inside it

**Marx and Engels** — the dates the chapter supplies at this point, **1820-95**, are **Engels's**; Marx's are **1818-83**, given earlier in the same chapter — held that ideology is an **instrument for protecting the interests of the dominant class**. Thus the bourgeoisie — the capitalist class — **needs ideology to maintain itself in power**. On the contrary, when the proletariat — the working class — comes to power after the socialist revolution, it has **no vested interests in maintaining itself in power**. It strives to create such conditions where the state will **'wither away'**. It does not want to continue as the dominant class but works for the creation of a **classless society** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33).

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** This passage contains an asymmetry that is easy to miss and worth stating explicitly in an answer. Ideology is tied to the *maintenance of position*. A class that intends to abolish its own position therefore has, on this reasoning, no use for ideology. The argument is not that proletarians are more honest; it is that the functional requirement which generates ideology — the need to make a sectional arrangement look natural and permanent — is absent where no permanent position is sought. That is a genuinely elegant argument, and it is exactly what Lenin's intervention disturbs.

### 2. Lenin's move, and the reason for it

**V. I. Lenin (1870-1924)**, in his *What is to be Done?* (1902), held that ideology is **not necessarily a distortion of truth to conceal the prevailing contradictions**, but that it has become a **neutral concept** which refers to the **political consciousness of different classes, including the proletarian class**. He argued that the **class struggle will continue for a very long time during the socialist phase**. So the proletariat **also need an ideology — the ideology of scientific socialism — for their guidance, lest they are overpowered by the bourgeois ideology** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33).

The reasoning has three steps, and separating them shows why this is an argument rather than a redefinition by decree:

| Step | Claim | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The class struggle continues **for a very long time** during the socialist phase | The Marx-Engels assumption that the need to hold a position lapses at the revolution |
| 2 | Bourgeois ideology therefore remains an **active force** during that phase | The idea that the contest of ideas ends when the contest of classes is formally won |
| 3 | The proletariat consequently needs an ideology of its own, or it will be **overpowered** | Ideological abstention as a viable option for a class in struggle |

⚠️ So Lenin's neutralisation of the term is not a verbal concession; it follows from a **disagreement about timing**. If the struggle ends at the revolution, the Marx-Engels asymmetry holds. If it continues for a very long time, a class that abstains from ideology in that period simply loses the field to the ideology already in possession.

### 3. What the neutralisation costs

Two gains and two costs should be stated together, because this is where a strong answer separates itself.

**Gains.** First, the term becomes analytically usable: "ideology" can now name the political consciousness of *any* class, so the analyst is not forced to describe one side's beliefs as consciousness and the other's as deception. Second, it accounts for something the Marx-Engels position struggles with — the observed fact that emancipatory movements do have doctrines, symbols and commitments, and are not simply in direct possession of the truth.

**Costs.** ⚠️ First, the critical edge is blunted. If every class's political consciousness is an ideology, then calling something an ideology is no longer a criticism, and the diagnostic force of "false consciousness" has to be recovered some other way — which is precisely what Lukács attempts in Lesson 10. ⚠️ Second, and more seriously, there is a risk of **self-exemption**: an ideology described as *scientific* socialism claims for itself a status it denies to its rival, and a claim of that kind is not established merely by the adjective. An honest answer notes that the chapter reports the position without adjudicating this point.

### 1. The thesis

**Georg Lukács (1885-1971)**, a Hungarian Marxist, in *History and Class Consciousness* (1923), proposed a theory of the **dependence of thought on social life**, which primarily consisted of **class relations of material production**. He held that **consciousness was always class consciousness**. The proletariat, by virtue of its **increasing estrangement within the socio-economic sphere**, occupied a **unique historical position from which it could achieve universal consciousness** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the point that answers Lesson 8's reflexive objection.** Notice how the second sentence functions. Lesson 8 ended with the difficulty that if social existence determines consciousness then Marx's own consciousness is determined too. Lukács does not deny the premise — he strengthens it, since consciousness is *always* class consciousness. His answer is positional: one class's situation is such that seeing its own condition clearly requires seeing the whole system, because its estrangement *is* the system's operation on it. On that reading, universality is not escape from a class standpoint but the content of one particular standpoint.

### 2. The crucial refinement

Here is the sentence that distinguishes Lukács from both Marx and Lenin, and it should be reproduced carefully. On the nature of ideology, Lukács maintained that it refers **both to bourgeois and proletarian consciousness, without implying a necessary negative connotation**. **Marxism itself is the ideological expression of the proletariat.** Lukács held that **bourgeois ideology is false, not because ideology itself is 'false consciousness', but because the bourgeois class situation is structurally limited** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33).

Set out as an argument:

| Element | Lukács's position | What it repairs |
|---|---|---|
| Definition of ideology | Neutral, covering both classes — Marxism itself is an ideological expression | Accepts Lenin's neutralisation rather than reverting to Marx's pejorative use |
| Source of falsity | Not the category "ideology" but the **structural limitation of a class situation** | Restores the critique that Lenin's neutralisation had blunted |
| Content of the limitation | The bourgeoisie **cannot stand on its own**: it **must exploit the proletariat to maintain itself** | Gives the limitation a specific mechanism rather than leaving it as an epithet |
| Why it is deplorable | It **dominates and contaminates the psychological consciousness of the proletariat** | Explains the harm as the spread of a limited standpoint, not merely as error |

⚠️ **Why this is the most elegant move in the topic.** The pejorative charge is relocated. It no longer attaches to *ideology*, where Lenin had shown it cannot stay, but to the *class situation* whose ideology it is. Bourgeois thought is false because the position it is thought from is one that cannot be universalised: a class that must exploit another to sustain itself cannot give an account of society as a whole that is both accurate and self-justifying. The falsity is a property of the standpoint, not of the genre.

### 3. The warning, which carries real weight

Lukács closes with a caution the chapter reports without softening: **ideological struggle should not become a substitute for class struggle** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33).

⚠️ This warning does double duty, and both uses are examinable.

- As **internal self-criticism**, it concedes the cost of everything he has just argued. Having made consciousness central, he has made it possible for a movement to mistake winning arguments for changing conditions.
- As a **general proposition about ideology-critique**, it generalises beyond Marxism: any programme that identifies distorted consciousness as the problem is liable to treat correcting consciousness as the solution, and that substitution is a failure even on its own terms.

### 4. A supplementary term, carefully flagged

⚠️ **Reification — supplementary vocabulary, flagged as such.** The term *reification* is commonly associated in secondary political-theory literature with Lukács's wider theory of class consciousness. It names the process by which historically produced social relations between people come to appear as fixed, natural relations between things, so that their origin in class domination is obscured and they seem unchangeable. The concept fits the structural-limitation argument closely, and it is useful for naming how a limited standpoint becomes invisible to those occupying it.

⚠️ **The provenance caution that must travel with it.** The term is **not** page-anchored against the source chapter used to build this session, which does not use it. It is therefore offered as an analytical gloss only. Do not attribute to it a specific book title, publication year or page number beyond the 1923 work the chapter itself names, and do not present it as the chapter's own vocabulary.

### 5. Exam use

- Where a stem invites "ideology is false consciousness", Lukács supplies the sharpest correction available: the falsity is traced to a **class situation that is structurally limited**, not to ideology as such.
- Where a stem asks how Marxism can avoid self-refutation, Lukács supplies the positional answer — and an honest answer will add that it is contestable, since the claim that one class position has privileged access to universal truth is itself a strong claim later disputed within Marxism.
- Where a stem concerns the limits of ideology-critique, the closing warning is the most economical evidence available.

### 1. The three grounds, taken one at a time

**Karl Mannheim (1893-1947)**, a German sociologist, in his famous work *Ideology and Utopia* (1929), rejects Marx's theory of ideology on three grounds (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33).

**(a) The correlation is indirect.** The 'style of thought' of any group is **only indirectly related to its interests**; there is **no direct correlation between its consciousness and its economic interests**.

⚠️ This is a methodological objection, and the most empirically testable of the three. It does not deny that thought is socially shaped; it denies that the shaping runs through economic interest in a way tight enough to predict. Note the phrase the chapter preserves — "style of thought" — which is broader than belief: it covers how a group reasons, what it takes as obvious, what questions it finds worth asking. A style of thought can be socially produced without being the output of a calculation of interest.

**(b) Marxism is itself the ideology of a class.** All thought is shaped by its social background; hence Marxism itself is the ideology of a class.

⚠️ This is the reflexive objection of Lesson 8 pressed to its conclusion, and the key is that Mannheim derives it *from Marx's own premise*. If social existence determines consciousness, the determination does not stop at the boundary of one's own doctrine. Lukács had tried to escape by locating a standpoint whose limitation was not disabling; Mannheim denies that any such exemption is available. ⚠️ **What makes this devastating rather than merely clever:** it cannot be answered by showing that Marxism is true, since the objection is not that Marxism is false but that its social determination is of the same kind as everyone else's. Any reply must either produce a principled exemption — which is what Lukács attempted — or accept the generalisation and work out what follows, which is Mannheim's own path.

**(c) Classes are not the only determinant.** Apart from classes, other social groups — like **different generations** — also have a significant influence upon consciousness.

⚠️ The smallest of the three in the chapter's text and the largest in its consequences. If generation shapes consciousness, then so, in principle, may region, occupation, religion, language or gender, and the single-variable model of ideology-critique is no longer adequate. The chapter names only generations, and an answer should name only generations as the chapter's example while noting that the objection opens the category.

### 2. The programme that follows

Mannheim **introduced the term 'sociology of knowledge'** to focus on the **social determination of knowledge**. He **sought to generalize the Marxist framework as a tool of analysis** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33). The boxed definition recorded in Lesson 6 gives its content: a systematic attempt to inquire how our knowledge is determined, conditioned or distorted by our social background (PDF p.31).

⚠️ **The crucial word is "generalize".** Mannheim is not discarding Marx's apparatus; he is removing its restriction to one social variable and one exempted standpoint. What was a weapon aimed by one class at another becomes a general method applicable to everyone, including its user. That is the entire content of the move, and stating it in one sentence is the most efficient thing an answer can do with Mannheim.

| | Marx's ideology-critique | Mannheim's sociology of knowledge |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | The opponent's consciousness | All thought, including one's own |
| Determining variable | Class position and economic interest | Social background broadly, including generation |
| Tightness of the link | Consciousness corresponds to economic structure | Style of thought only **indirectly** related to interests |
| Status of the critic | Implicitly exempt, or exempt by standpoint (Lukács) | **Not exempt** — Marxism is itself the ideology of a class |
| What it is for | Exposing the dominant class's distortion | Studying the social determination of knowledge as such |

### 3. The problem this creates, named honestly

⚠️ If all thought is socially determined and nobody is exempt, what becomes of the claim that any political proposition is *true*? The chapter poses this directly at the start of Lesson 12's material, observing that the relative character of all knowledge as postulated by Mannheim makes the **knowledge of objective truth extremely difficult** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34).

So Mannheim's position at the end of this lesson is unstable by design: he has generalised ideology-critique so successfully that he has made the standing of his own inquiry a problem. ⚠️ Note that this is exactly the difficulty Topic 01's treatment of theory and ideology ran into from the other direction, where the distinction between a theory's origin and its validity was the proposed solution. Mannheim's reply is not that distinction — it is a social procedure, and Lesson 12 examines whether it works.

### 4. Supplementary vocabulary, flagged

⚠️ **Total and particular conceptions; relationism — supplementary vocabulary, flagged as such.** Secondary literature on Mannheim distinguishes a **particular** conception of ideology, in which only a rival's thought is accused of distortion, from a **total** conception, which attributes a whole thought-system to a social position; and describes his search for more objective knowledge as **relational** — perspective-bound but not therefore arbitrary — rather than fully relativistic.

⚠️ **One precision that is easy to lose.** Reflexivity does not belong to the total conception as such. The literature further divides the total conception into a **special** form, which still applies the whole-system analysis only to an opponent, and a **general** form, in which the analyst's own thought-system is included within the analysis. It is the **general** form that is reflexive, and it is that form which corresponds to the chapter's ground (b) — that all thought is shaped by its social background, **hence Marxism itself is the ideology of a class**. Saying flatly that "the total conception is reflexive" over-states it and erases the distinction that does the work.

⚠️ **Provenance caution.** This finer terminology — particular and total conceptions, the special and general forms, and relationism — is **not** page-anchored against the source chapter used for this session, which does not employ it. Use it as an analytical gloss only; attach no page, and do not present it as the chapter's own vocabulary. The chapter's own equivalents — "all thought is shaped by its social background" and "the relative character of all knowledge" — are what the page anchors support.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can Mannheim's second objection not be answered by demonstrating that Marx's analysis of capitalism is correct?

**Model answer:** Because the objection is not about truth but about determination. Mannheim's ground (b) is that **all thought is shaped by its social background; hence Marxism itself is the ideology of a class** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.33), and the premise he uses is Marx's own — that social existence determines consciousness. Showing that a particular Marxian analysis is correct leaves that claim untouched, because the objection concedes in advance that a socially determined body of thought may contain accurate findings; what it denies is that Marxism occupies a position outside the social determination it describes in others. Answering it therefore requires one of two things: a principled exemption, which is what Lukács attempts when he locates a class whose estrangement opens a route to universal consciousness, or acceptance of the generalisation together with an account of how objective knowledge remains possible under it, which is Mannheim's own route through the sociology of knowledge. ⚠️ The chapter does not declare either route successful, and an answer that claims it does has supplied a verdict the source withholds.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not report Mannheim as holding that thought is directly determined by economic interest. His first objection is precisely that the correlation is **indirect** — the style of thought of a group is only indirectly related to its interests. Attributing the tight correlation to him reverses his position and destroys the distinction between his sociology of knowledge and the Marxian account he is criticising.

### 1. The pair, and why it is a genuine advance

Mannheim held that false consciousness may be manifested in **two forms: ideology and utopia**. **Ideology** represents the **tendency of conservation**: it relies on false consciousness to muster support for the **maintenance of the status quo**. **Utopia** represents the **impetus to change**: it relies on false consciousness by **projecting unrealizable principles** to muster support for the **forces of change**. A **ruling class** makes use of ideology; the **opposition** may project a utopia (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34).

The chapter boxes the key term:

> **Utopia** — vision of a perfect society where everyone is happy. In social sciences, this term is applied to designate a **set of fascinating but unrealizable principles**. (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34)

⚠️ **Why the pair is an advance, not merely a classification.** Up to this point the critique of distorted consciousness had been directed at those defending an existing order; the accusation ran in one direction. Mannheim makes it symmetrical. Opposition thought can be distorted too, and distorted in a specific way — not by denying what exists but by projecting what cannot be realised. That is why he can then say, consistently with his ground (b) in Lesson 11, that **the Marxist vision of a classless society was nothing short of utopia**, and therefore **also makes false consciousness its tool**.

| | **Ideology** | **Utopia** |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Conservation | Change |
| Characteristic operation | Supports maintenance of the status quo | Projects **unrealizable principles** |
| Typical holder | A ruling class | The opposition |
| Shared feature | Relies on false consciousness | Relies on false consciousness |

⚠️ The last row is the one to put in an answer: the pair is a *division within* false consciousness, not a contrast between false and true. Both are modes of distortion; they differ in what they distort and in whose service.

### 2. The relativism problem, posed by the chapter itself

The chapter then poses the difficulty directly: the **relative character of all knowledge** as postulated by Mannheim makes the **knowledge of objective truth extremely difficult**. "Is there no hope, then, to discover truth? Well, there is a **silver lining**" (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34).

⚠️ The phrasing matters. The chapter does not say objective truth becomes impossible; it says extremely difficult. Mannheim's remedy is therefore an attempt to make a difficult thing achievable, not to rescue something already lost — a distinction worth preserving, because it is the difference between Mannheim and the relativism he is often accused of.

### 3. The remedy, step by step

Mannheim **hinges on the possibility of a 'free-floating stratum' of intellectuals between the contending classes to achieve disinterested knowledge** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34). The mechanism has five steps, and reproducing them in order is what distinguishes a reconstruction from a label:

1. Some **enlightened individuals within the conflicting groups** will realise that their **perception of truth is partial**.
2. They will see that it **could be complemented by understanding their opponent's view**.
3. Such individuals **from both sides will come together with an open mind**.
4. They will **enter into a dialogue and incessantly strive to arrive at the objective truth**.
5. They will thus open the way to **synthetic common knowledge of the prevailing historical situation and a realistic assessment of actual possibilities** — in other words, to a **realistic vision between ideology and utopia**.

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The remedy is **social, not epistemological**. Mannheim does not claim that any individual can step outside social determination. He claims that partial perspectives can be *combined* under conditions of open dialogue, so that objectivity becomes a property of a procedure among situated knowers rather than of a view from nowhere. Noticing that is what allows an answer to defend him against the charge of simple relativism — and also to see exactly where the proposal becomes vulnerable.

### 4. The step too far

Mannheim **identifies these intellectuals as social scientists**. He **recommends that these social scientists, who have proved their ability to grasp the objective truth, should be given authority to rule** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34).

⚠️ This is a different kind of claim from steps 1-5, and a strong answer says so. Steps 1-5 describe a procedure for producing better knowledge. This sentence converts epistemic achievement into a **title to political power**. The inference from "has proved able to grasp objective truth" to "should be given authority to rule" is precisely the move the chapter's critics attack, and it does not follow from anything in the procedure.

### 5. The four criticisms, as the chapter records them

| # | Criticism | What it targets |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mannheim has created a **confusion between the origin and validity of knowledge** | The core of the sociology of knowledge — exactly the distinction Lesson 4 established as the reply to Sabine |
| 2 | His **extreme relativism contemplates the existence of ideas without upholders** | The coherence of treating thought-styles as detachable from the people who hold them |
| 3 | **Giving power to social scientists is fraught with the danger of absolutism** | The political recommendation, not the method |
| 4 | Let these social scientists function as **critics of power-holders instead of wielding power themselves** — better serving as **organizers of agitations and demonstrations, journalists, writers, and 'conscience-keepers' of society** | Supplies the constructive alternative |

(Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34)

⚠️ **Criticism 1 is the one to lead with, because it connects the whole topic.** Lesson 4 established that the reply to Sabine was to distinguish the **origin** of a theory from its **validity**. The sociology of knowledge studies origin exhaustively. The charge is that Mannheim slides from an account of where a belief comes from to a verdict on what it is worth — the very conflation the chapter had earlier identified as the error to avoid. Whether the charge is fair is arguable; that it is the decisive charge is not.

⚠️ **Criticism 4 deserves emphasis because it is constructive rather than merely negative.** It accepts that the people who can see across perspectives have a public role, and relocates that role from ruling to criticising. The phrase the chapter uses — **'conscience-keepers' of society** — is precise and quotable, and it gives an answer a positive conclusion instead of a flat rejection.

### 6. What survives

⚠️ An honest verdict separates the parts. The **ideology/utopia distinction** survives: it is a genuine refinement that makes the critique of distorted consciousness symmetrical and can be applied by anyone, including to Marxism, as Mannheim himself does. The **sociology of knowledge** survives as a research programme, with the standing caution that studying origins does not settle validity. The **free-floating stratum** survives only as a description of a procedure — dialogue across partial perspectives — and not as a sociological claim that any actual group of intellectuals is unattached. The **recommendation that social scientists should rule** does not survive the chapter's own recorded criticism.

**Question:** Mannheim is routinely described as a relativist. Using this lesson, give the strongest defence against that description and then state honestly what the defence cannot save.

**Model answer:** The strongest defence is that his remedy is social rather than epistemological, and that he never claims objective truth is unavailable. A thinker who proposes a procedure for reaching objective truth is seeking better-grounded objectivity, not denying it; objectivity is relocated from an individual's view from nowhere to a disciplined procedure among situated knowers. What the defence cannot save is twofold. It cannot save him from the first recorded criticism, that he confuses the **origin** of knowledge with its **validity** — the distinction the chapter had earlier used to answer Sabine — because the dialogue procedure describes how beliefs are produced rather than how they are tested. And it cannot save the political recommendation that social scientists who have proved their ability to grasp objective truth should be given authority to rule, which converts an epistemic achievement into a title to power and which the recorded criticism answers by relocating them to the role of critics and 'conscience-keepers' of society.

### 1. The bridge, and why it is not a non sequitur

The chapter moves from Mannheim to Popper through a single step: **when ideology is conceived as an instrument of motivating people for the achievement of predetermined goals, it comes close to totalitarianism** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34).

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The bridge rests on the contemporary definition recorded in Lesson 6 — ideology as a set of ideas adopted by a group to motivate it for the achievement of **predetermined** goals. The weight is on "predetermined". If the goals are fixed in advance, then the function of the idea-set is to generate compliance with them rather than to examine them, and an apparatus that generates compliance with fixed goals is, in its structure, what a totalitarian state requires. The inference is therefore not from content to regime but from the *logic of a motivational idea-set* to the *logic of total direction*.

### 2. The two boxed definitions

The chapter defines both poles precisely, and the definitions are directly examinable (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.35):

| | **Totalitarianism** | **Open society** |
|---|---|---|
| Scope of state action | Seeks to **regulate and control all aspects of life** of its citizens — **whether public or private** | Public policy represents a **reconciliation of diverse interests** |
| Direction of activity | Seeks to **direct** all political, economic, social-cultural and intellectual activities towards aims **determined by the state itself** | Free flow of information regarding public affairs and matters of public importance |
| Claim to truth | Implicit in the aims being state-determined | Power-holders **do not claim that they have found the truth** |
| Citizen's position | **No** right or opportunity to oppose or criticize the state, **or to propose any new aim** | Encourages **freedom of expression**; shows **readiness to adopt new ideas**; **permits citizens to criticize the government** |

⚠️ **Two details candidates routinely lose.** First, in the totalitarianism definition the denial is not only of the right to *oppose* but of the right to **propose any new aim** — that is, the closure operates on the agenda, not merely on dissent from it. Second, the open-society definition is built around what power-holders **do not claim**: it is defined by a disclaimer about truth, not by a doctrine. That is why Popper can say an open society does not *need* an ideology; its organising principle is the refusal of the claim that an ideology makes.

### 3. Popper's argument

**Karl Popper (1902-94)**, in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), argued that **ideology is the characteristic of totalitarianism; it has nothing to do in an open society**. He maintained that **science and freedom flourish together** in a society which is open in the sense that it is **willing to accept new ideas**. In contrast, a totalitarian society **claims that it has already found the absolute truth, and strives to implement it ruthlessly**. **Ideology is the tool which enables the state to mobilize its manpower and other resources for a goal which is declared to embody the absolute truth.** It does not allow anyone to oppose or criticize the public policy which is **exclusively determined by the ruling group** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.35).

⚠️ The pairing of **science and freedom** is the argument's centre and is worth stating explicitly. They flourish together because they depend on the same condition — willingness to accept new ideas — and a claim to possess the absolute truth withdraws that condition from both at once. Science cannot proceed where conclusions are fixed in advance; nor can freedom, where the agenda is. That is why Popper treats a regime's epistemology and its politics as one subject rather than two.

### 4. The claim that invites the objection

Popper's conclusion goes further than the argument strictly requires: **in Popper's view, Western liberal-democratic societies are open societies; hence they do not need an ideology for working smoothly. Citizens of these societies are absolutely free to criticize the existing institutions and structures of power** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.35).

⚠️ **Objection, which an answer should raise because the chapter's own later material supports it.** The claim has two weak points. The first is empirical: "absolutely free" is an absolute, and absolutes are refutable by a single counter-instance; the chapter's own later section records Wright Mills describing an **ideology of political complacency** operating within exactly those societies, and Titmuss pointing to monopolistic concentration of economic power within the capitalist system (PDF p.39). The second is conceptual: Lesson 2 established that an ideology becomes political when it is used to defend an existing system or to advocate change, and that the three options exhaust the field — so a society whose dominant thought defends its existing arrangements is operating ideologically whether or not it uses the word.

⚠️ **Reply available to Popper.** The claim is best read as *functional* rather than descriptive: an open society does not need an ideology **for working smoothly**, because its coordination comes from the reconciliation of diverse interests and from revisable policy rather than from a shared doctrine held beyond question. On that reading Popper is not asserting that no ideology circulates in such societies, only that the system does not depend on one.

⚠️ **Residual.** The functional reading rescues the claim and weakens it. It leaves entirely open whether an actual society, called open, is in fact coordinating itself by a doctrine held beyond examination — which is precisely the question the end-of-ideology critics in Lesson 20 insist on asking. Popper's framework supplies the test; it does not supply the finding.

### 5. What is owned here, and what is not

This lesson owns the **threshold**: the point at which an idea-set stops being one position among others and becomes a monopoly claim to truth backed by the state. It does not own the comparative study of regime-types, nor any claim about present-day governments. ⚠️ The chapter's own examples, taken up in Lesson 14, are explicitly historical and bounded, and this session names no contemporary state, party or leader anywhere.

Revision notes:
- **Bridge:** when ideology is conceived as an **instrument of motivating people for the achievement of predetermined goals, it comes close to totalitarianism**; some writers therefore assert it is found **only** in totalitarian systems and has **no place in an open society** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.34).
- ⚠️ The weight falls on **"predetermined"** — an apparatus for generating compliance with fixed goals has the structure total direction requires.
- **Totalitarianism (boxed):** the state seeks to **regulate and control all aspects of life — public or private**; to **direct** all political, economic, social-cultural and intellectual activities towards aims **determined by the state itself**; **no citizen has the right or opportunity to oppose or criticize the state, or to propose any new aim**.
- **Open society (boxed):** **free flow of information** on public affairs; public policy as a **reconciliation of diverse interests**; **power-holders do not claim that they have found the truth**; freedom of expression; readiness to adopt new ideas; citizens permitted to criticize the government.
- ⚠️ Totalitarian closure operates on the **agenda** (no new aim may be proposed), not merely on dissent; the open society is defined by a **disclaimer about truth**, not by a doctrine.
- **Popper (1902-94)**, ***The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945):** ideology is the **characteristic of totalitarianism** and has nothing to do in an open society; **science and freedom flourish together** where a society is **willing to accept new ideas**; a totalitarian society claims it has **already found the absolute truth** and implements it **ruthlessly**; **ideology is the tool** enabling the state to **mobilize manpower and other resources** for a goal declared to embody absolute truth; criticism of policy **exclusively determined by the ruling group** is not allowed.
- Popper's further claim: Western liberal-democratic societies **are** open societies and therefore **do not need an ideology for working smoothly**; their citizens are **absolutely free** to criticize institutions and structures of power.
- ⚠️ Objection: "absolutely free" is refutable by a single counter-instance, and the chapter's own later critics — Mills on political complacency, Titmuss on monopolistic concentration — supply candidates; conceptually, Lesson 2's three exhaustive options leave no neutral place to stand.
- ⚠️ Reply: read the claim **functionally** — such a society does not need an ideology *to work smoothly*. ⚠️ Residual: that leaves open whether any actual society is in fact coordinated by an unexamined doctrine, which is Lesson 20's question.

### Concept check

**Question:** Popper holds that an open society does not need an ideology. Lesson 2 held that an ideology becomes political precisely by defending or opposing an existing system, with no neutral option available. Show how both can be maintained, and say what the reconciliation leaves unsettled.

**Model answer:** They can be maintained together once Popper's claim is read as functional rather than descriptive. Lesson 2 established that an idea-set enters politics when it is used to defend an existing system or to advocate limited or radical change in it, and that these options exhaust the field (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28); that claim is about what any political idea-set does. Popper's claim is about what a *system* requires in order to operate: an open society, defined by free flow of information, policy as a reconciliation of diverse interests, and power-holders who **do not claim that they have found the truth**, coordinates itself through revisable policy and criticism rather than through a doctrine held beyond question, so it does not need an ideology **for working smoothly** (PDF p.35). Ideologies may therefore circulate freely in such a society — indeed they must, since every position defends or opposes something — without the system depending on any of them. What the reconciliation leaves unsettled is the empirical question: whether a society that calls itself open is in fact coordinating itself by an unexamined doctrine. Popper's framework supplies the test and not the finding, which is why Wright Mills's charge of an ideology of political complacency is a challenge his framework cannot dismiss in advance.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read Popper as holding that open societies are value-free or doctrine-free. The open society has strong commitments — openness, criticism, revisability, readiness to adopt new ideas. What it refuses is the claim to have **found the truth**, which is a refusal of a particular epistemic posture rather than an absence of values.

### 1. The definition, and the second term in it

**Hannah Arendt (1906-75)**, a German Jew philosopher, in *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951), defined totalitarianism as a **system of total domination, characterized by ideology and terror** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.35).

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the decisive difference from Popper.** Popper's account needs only one element: a monopoly claim to absolute truth, implemented ruthlessly. Arendt's needs two, and the second is not reducible to the first. **Terror** is not merely the enforcement arm of the ideology; on her account the pair is constitutive. The practical consequence for an answer is that Arendt's definition is **narrower** than Popper's: a closed doctrine without terror is, on her criteria, not yet totalitarianism. That narrowness is a strength, because it prevents the concept from expanding to cover every dogmatic politics — a danger the chapter's own trap-list flags.

### 2. The three enabling factors

Totalitarianism was made possible **in recent Europe** by three factors (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.35):

| Factor | As the chapter states it | What it supplies |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | The specific **political and social position of the Jews**, which had given **anti-semitism** — the tendency of hatred toward Jews — **a new force** | A target constituted as the system's outside |
| (b) | **Imperialism**, which generated **racist movements** and **worldwide expansion of power** | A tested repertoire of racial hierarchy and limitless expansion |
| (c) | **Dissolution of European society into uprooted masses**, so **lonely and disoriented** that they could be **mobilized behind ideologies** | A population available for total mobilisation |

⚠️ **Factor (c) is the one to build an answer around**, because it is the most general and the most analytically portable. It specifies a *precondition* rather than a cause: ideology can totalise a society only where the ordinary social attachments that give people bearings have already dissolved, leaving them lonely and disoriented. That connects directly to Lesson 3's mechanism — an ideology presents a cause and demands sacrifice — and explains why the same mechanism has vastly different purchase in different societies. Where people are embedded in a dense web of attachments, a cause competes with those attachments; where they are uprooted, it can replace them.

⚠️ **Precision caution on factors (a) and (b).** These are historically specific claims about recent Europe as Arendt analysed it, and the chapter frames them that way. They are not a general template to be applied to any society, and this session does not extend them. Factor (c) is the element that generalises, and even it generalises as a *condition to look for*, not as a prediction.

### 3. The division of attention

The chapter then notes something useful about how the two thinkers are used: **Popper largely focused on the communist regime, and Arendt on the fascist regime, to bring out the close correlation between ideology and totalitarianism** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.36).

⚠️ An answer that records this earns a mark cheaply, because it blocks a common misreading. The two are not offering competing theories of the same case; they are offering convergent analyses of different cases. That convergence — from opposite ends of the mid-century ideological spectrum — is itself part of the evidence for their shared conclusion, and is worth saying.

### 4. The historical frame, and a dating problem inside it

The chapter closes the section with a chronology (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF pp.35-36):

- **Marx** had evolved the concept of **ideology** in order to **expose capitalism**.
- The concept of **totalitarianism** was evolved in the **early twentieth century** to describe the dictatorial way of working of the **communist regime of the Soviet Union till the end of the Stalin era (1953)** and the **fascist regime of Italy (under Mussolini) and Germany (under Hitler) till the end of the Second World War (1945)**.
- Both communist and fascist regimes **made ample use of their respective ideologies for the mobilization of their citizens toward the achievement of their respective goals**.

⚠️ 📰 **The dating problem, stated rather than smoothed.** The chapter says Marx evolved the concept of ideology **"in late nineteenth century"** (PDF p.35), while its own earlier section dates *German Ideology* to 1845-46 and the *Critique of Political Economy* Preface to 1859 (PDF p.32) — both mid-century, and the second more than two decades before Marx's death in 1883. This session follows the dated work-titles as the more specific evidence and records the inconsistency rather than silently correcting the source. Lesson 8 carries the same note, and the source ledger records it as an open uncertainty.

📰 **Date-bounding the whole frame.** Every regime named here is named as a completed historical case within the period the chapter specifies. Nothing in this lesson licenses applying the term to any present-day state, party, movement or leader, and this session does not do so.

### 5. The trap this lesson defuses

❌ **Trap:** "Popper and Arendt show that politics should be free of ideology."

### 1. The refusal

Ideology has been variously condemned as the reflection of false consciousness or as an instrument of totalitarianism. **But it is not fair to look at all ideologies in this light** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.36).

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The sentence is doing a specific logical job, not expressing a preference. Lessons 8 to 14 established that ideology **can** manifest false consciousness and **can** serve total domination. Neither establishes that it **must**. The conclusion therefore blocks a quantifier error — the slide from "some ideologies do X" to "ideology is X" — and an answer that names the error rather than merely quoting the sentence shows that it has understood why the refusal is warranted.

### 2. What ideologies will continue to do

In actual practice, different ideologies as sets of ideas **will continue to exist as the vehicles of value-systems evolved by different groups**. They will be used for **motivating people to achieve the goals cherished by their upholders**. They may also be used by some groups to **convince others regarding their rightful claims** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.36).

⚠️ The third clause is the one to notice. "Convince others regarding their rightful claims" is a *communicative* function, not a manipulative one — it describes the use of an idea-set to make a claim intelligible and compelling to people who do not already share one's position. That is markedly different from the mobilisation of adherents described in Lesson 3, and it is what makes the later world-politics illustration coherent.

### 3. The sentence that settles the Marx question

**Ideologies do not belong exclusively to dominant classes; oppressed classes also have their own ideologies. They cannot be set aside as 'false consciousness'** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.36).

⚠️ This is the chapter's own verdict on the quarrel that ran from Lesson 8 to Lesson 12, and it is closer to Lenin and Lukács than to the pejorative Marxian usage. Two consequences:

- The term cannot be used as a one-sided accusation. Calling an oppressed group's political consciousness "false consciousness" is precisely what the chapter forbids.
- Lukács's refinement is vindicated in substance: falsity, where it exists, has to be traced to the **structural limitation of a particular class situation**, not inferred from the fact that a set of ideas is an ideology.

### 4. Four further functions

| # | Function, as the chapter states it | Why it matters analytically |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ideologies could serve as a **meeting ground for like-minded people, instead of confining themselves to their tribe, caste, religion, region, etc.** | Ideology is here an *alternative* to ascriptive identity, not an intensifier of it — the opposite of Lesson 3's communalism case |
| 2 | They may **reflect changing social consciousness on crucial issues** | Ideology registers change rather than only resisting it — against Mannheim's "tendency of conservation" |
| 3 | Some have **given rise to strong social movements for the emancipation of various oppressed sections** | The mobilisation mechanism of Lesson 3 working for those without power |
| 4 | Some **manifest a deep concern with the future of humanity** | Ideology can have a horizon wider than any group's interest |

⚠️ **Function 1 is the sharpest of the four and the most useful in an answer.** It directly answers the worry raised by Lesson 3's communalism example. The complaint there was that a cause can be organised around an enemy defined by community. Here the chapter observes that an ideology can equally organise people *across* tribe, caste, religion and region, on the basis of shared conviction rather than inherited membership. The same mechanism, pointed differently.

### 5. The positive definition

The chapter then states what an ideology **is**, positively (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.36):

- An ideology is **identified by commitment to a cause**.
- It **rules out personal interest, bias or submission to a particular person, group or dynasty**.
- It signifies a **set of coherent ideas — perception of the real and the ideal from one's own position**.
- It **may also be used to make others realize that position**.

⚠️ **This definition is strikingly different from Lesson 1's, and the difference is worth confronting rather than hiding.** Lesson 1 defined ideology in sense (a) as ideas accepted without further examination, a matter of faith with no scientific basis. Here it is defined by commitment to a cause, by the *exclusion* of personal interest and of submission to a person or dynasty, and by coherence. The two are not contradictory, but they are differently weighted: the first emphasises the exemption from verification, the second emphasises the orientation to a cause beyond self-interest.

⚠️ **The reconciliation, which an answer should supply rather than ignore.** Both definitions describe the same object from different angles. Commitment to a cause is *why* the claims are not held conditionally on evidence — the commitment is prior. And the exclusion of personal interest and dynastic submission is what distinguishes an ideology from mere factional loyalty, which is a distinction the first definition alone cannot draw. ⚠️ **Residual:** the positive definition sets a high bar that many things called ideologies would fail — anything that is in substance a vehicle for a person or a dynasty would, on this definition, not be an ideology at all. The chapter does not pursue that consequence, and an answer should note it as a consequence rather than asserting it as the chapter's position.

⚠️ Note what this illustrates — the communicative function from §2, used by the weaker party. First, a logical one: the preceding analyses show what ideology *can* be, and the chapter blocks the slide to what it *must* be — "it is not fair to look at all ideologies in this light" (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.36). Second, a distributional one: ideologies do not belong exclusively to dominant classes, oppressed classes also have their own, and these **cannot be set aside as 'false consciousness'**. Third, a functional one: ideologies serve as a meeting ground for like-minded people instead of confinement to tribe, caste, religion or region, may reflect changing social consciousness, have given rise to strong social movements for the emancipation of oppressed sections, and may manifest a deep concern with the future of humanity. Fourth, a communicative one: they may be used to convince others regarding their rightful claims, as developing nations do in world politics. The positive definition — commitment to a cause, ruling out personal interest, bias or submission to a particular person, group or dynasty, and signifying a set of coherent ideas as a perception of the real and the ideal from one's own position — sits with the opening definition as a second angle rather than a replacement: commitment to a cause explains **why** the claims are not held conditionally on evidence, while the exclusion of personal and dynastic interest supplies a distinction from mere factional loyalty that the opening definition could not draw. ⚠️ Its consequence, which the chapter does not pursue, is that anything functioning as a vehicle for a person or a dynasty would not qualify as an ideology at all.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat this conclusion as a reversal of the preceding lessons. Nothing in it withdraws Marx's account of false consciousness, Mannheim's ideology-utopia pair or the Popper-Arendt account of totalitarian closure. What it withdraws is the inference from those analyses to a general verdict on the category, which is a different and much weaker claim than the one the lessons established.

### 1. The two distinct claims inside the thesis

The chapter reports that the current status of ideology in the world was **reviewed in the mid-1950s and in the 1960s**, and that **in Western liberal-democratic countries it was declared that the age of ideology had come to an end** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.36). Two quite different reasons are then given, and conflating them is the commonest error on this topic.

| | **The Popper line** | **The convergence claim** |
|---|---|---|
| Content | These countries looked at ideology as a **tool of totalitarianism** which had **no place in open societies** | At the advanced stage of industrial development, a country's **social-economic organization is determined by the level of its development, and not by its political ideology** |
| Kind of claim | Definitional and normative — a claim about what ideology *is* and where it belongs | Empirical and predictive — a claim about what industrial societies will come to resemble |
| What would refute it | Showing that open societies also operate ideologically | Showing that ideologically divergent societies did **not** converge |
| Whom it is aimed at | Totalitarian systems | The assumption that doctrine shapes social-economic organisation |

⚠️ **Analytical inference.** The two claims support each other rhetorically and are independent logically. One could hold the convergence claim while denying that open societies are ideology-free, and one could hold the Popper line while denying convergence. Separating them is what allows an answer to assess the thesis properly, because the critics in Lesson 20 attack the **first**, while the historical record bears mainly on the **second**.

The convergence claim is then stated at its sharpest: **capitalist and communist countries were bound to evolve similar characteristics at the advanced stage of their industrial development, irrespective of their ideological differences** (PDF p.36). Note "bound to" — the claim is not that they might converge but that development makes it necessary. That strength is what makes the thesis testable, and it is also what makes it vulnerable.

### 2. The setting

**Early indications of this view may be found in the proceedings of a conference on 'The Future of Freedom' held in Milan, Italy, in 1955.** The chapter records that **the conference urged its participants to forget their minor differences and discover common grounds to face the danger of Communism** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF pp.36-37).

⚠️ **Why the setting is evidence and not background.** A thesis announcing the exhaustion of ideological conflict was formulated at a gathering convened to consolidate one side of an ideological conflict. That is not a refutation — a claim's origin does not settle its validity, as Lesson 4 established at length. But it is exactly the kind of fact the sociology of knowledge exists to register, and it is the opening that Lesson 20's critics will use. **Question:** Quote the limiting phrases in Bell's Milan statement and explain what difference they make to how the thesis should be assessed.

**Model answer:** The limiting phrases are "In the Western World", "a **rough** consensus", "among **intellectuals**", "on political issues", and "**In that sense** too the ideological age has ended" (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.37). The difference this makes to assessment is substantial. ### 1. Dahrendorf: the structural argument

**Ralph Dahrendorf**, in *Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society* (1957), argued that Western societies had entered a new phase of development: they were no longer capitalist societies, they had become **'post-capitalist societies'**. The **coincidence of economic conflict and political conflict, which was the foundation of Marx's theory, had ceased to exist** in them (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.37).

⚠️ **Edition note, because the title-year pairing can mislead.** The source pairs the English title with 1957, and that pairing is reproduced above. The 1957 publication is in fact the **German original**, *Soziale Klassen und Klassenkonflikt in der industriellen Gesellschaft*; the **English** edition titled *Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society* appeared in **1959** from Stanford University Press, translated, revised and expanded by Dahrendorf himself. Both years are correct for what they name. A candidate writing the English title against 1957 is reproducing the source and should say so; a candidate asked for the English edition's date should give 1959.

The mechanism is given precisely, and it is the best-specified argument in the whole debate:

- In a **capitalist society**, the lines of industrial and political conflict were **superimposed**: the opponents within the industrial sphere — capitalists and workers — **met again as bourgeoisie and proletariat in the political arena**.
- In a **post-capitalist society**, industry and society have been **dissociated**. The social relations of the industrial sphere, including industrial conflict, **no longer dominate the whole society** but remain **confined in their patterns and problems to the sphere of industry**. Industry and industrial conflicts are **institutionally isolated** — they remain within the borders of their proper realm and **do not influence politics and other spheres of social life**.
- Therefore, in Dahrendorf's view, **the framework of Marxian ideology was no longer suitable for the analysis of the Western societies**.

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the point to lead with.** Dahrendorf's argument is not that conflict had ended. It is that conflict had been **relocated and contained**. Industrial antagonism persists; what has changed is that it no longer propagates into politics, because the institutions that handle it keep it within their own sphere. That is a far more interesting and more defensible claim than "class conflict disappeared", and an answer that reports the latter has substituted a straw thesis for the real one.

⚠️ **What would test it.** The claim is checkable in principle: it predicts that industrial disputes will not reorganise political alignments. Any case in which a workplace conflict becomes the axis of a national political division is evidence against it. The chapter does not conduct that test, and this session does not supply contemporary instances; what an answer can legitimately say is that Dahrendorf's thesis is **conditional on a particular institutional separation holding**, and therefore says nothing about societies in which it does not.

### 2. Bell's 1960 book: the sectoral argument

**Daniel Bell**, in his noted work *The End of Ideology* (1960), asserted that **post-industrial societies are prone to similar development irrespective of their ideological differences**. They have a **lesser proportion of workers in industry than in services**: at the advanced stage of industrial development in any country, the **services sector expands at a faster rate than the manufacturing sector**. Besides, such a society is characterised by the **increasing dominance of technical elites**. The change in this direction **is not affected by its political ideology** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.37).

⚠️ Note that Bell's 1960 argument is of a different type from his Milan observation. The Milan statement reported a **consensus among intellectuals**; the book asserts a **structural trajectory of societies**. The first is a claim about what people believed, the second about what economies do. An answer that cites Bell should say which Bell it means, since the two claims face different objections — the first is vulnerable to the charge that an intellectual consensus is not an absence of ideology, the second to the charge that structural similarity is not political convergence.

### 3. Lipset: the verdict on affluent democracies

**Seymour M. Lipset**, in *Political Man: The Social Bases of Politics* (1960), observed (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.37):

> "Democracy is not only or even primarily a means through which different groups can attain their ends or seek the good society; it is the good society itself in operation."

Lipset observed that in the Western democracies the **differences between the left and the right are no longer profound**; the only issues before politics are concerned with **marginal increase in wages, marginal rise in prices, and extension of old-age pensions**, and the like (PDF pp.37-38).

The chapter continues with a list of problems Lipset regarded as solved — that the workers had achieved industrial and political citizenship, that the conservatives had accepted the welfare state, and that the democratic left had recognised that an increase in overall state power carries dangers to freedom as well as solutions to economic problems — concluding that the triumph of democracy in the West had made intellectuals realise they no longer needed ideologies or utopias to motivate them to political action (PDF p.38).

⚠️ **Text-integrity note.** That passage is visibly damaged in the local scanned copy used for this session: several words are broken or missing mid-line. The substance is clear and is reported above as paraphrase; **no part of it is quoted**, and the damage is recorded here and in the source ledger rather than repaired by guesswork.

⚠️ **A cross-chapter wording variance, disclosed.** The same author's claim is quoted in the source's first chapter in a different form — that the prevailing form of democracy in the United States was "the closest approximation to the good society itself in operation". The present chapter's quotation is the stronger, unqualified form: democracy **is** the good society itself in operation. Both are reproduced here as the source prints them in their respective places. ⚠️ The difference matters for assessment, because the first is a comparative claim and the second an identification, and a critic's reply to each would differ.

### 4. Reading the three together

| | **Dahrendorf (1957)** | **Bell (1960)** | **Lipset (1960)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kind of evidence | Institutional structure | Sectoral and occupational trends | Content of political disagreement |
| What it claims ended | The **superimposition** of industrial and political conflict | Ideology's influence on the **trajectory of development** | The **profundity** of left-right difference |
| What it does **not** claim | That industrial conflict ended | That ideologies ceased to exist | That political disagreement ended |
| Characteristic weakness | Conditional on an institutional separation holding | Structural similarity is not political convergence | Judges the stakes of politics by the issues currently in contention |

⚠️ **The fourth row on Lipset is the sharpest critical point available in this lesson.** Measuring whether ideological conflict has ended by looking at the issues currently on the agenda assumes that the agenda registers everything that is at stake. If some questions are not being contested *because* a settlement has placed them beyond contention, then a narrow agenda is evidence of a successful settlement rather than of an absence of ideology. That is exactly the argument Wright Mills and Macpherson will make in Lesson 20, and it can be anticipated here from the structure of Lipset's own evidence.

### 1. Rostow: the staircase

**W. W. Rostow**, in *The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-communist Manifesto* (1960), built a **unidimensional model of economic growth** which was **applicable to all countries irrespective of their political ideologies**. He suggested that all societies pass through **five stages of growth: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, road to maturity and the age of high mass consumption**. He believed that the process of development going on at that time in **Asia, Latin America, Africa and the Middle East** was **analogous** to the preconditions-for-take-off and take-off stages which prevailed in Western societies in the **late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries**. Rostow asserted that **the adoption of different political ideologies played no role in determining the course of economic development in different countries** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.38).

⚠️ **Three observations an answer can make without leaving the text.**

- The **subtitle is part of the argument.** *A Non-communist Manifesto* announces a position in the very contest the model claims to be independent of. ⚠️ That is not a refutation — origin does not settle validity — but it is the kind of fact the critics in Lesson 20 will treat as evidence that the thesis performs ideological work while disclaiming it.
- The **analogy carries the weight.** Rostow's claim about Asia, Latin America, Africa and the Middle East is that their development was *analogous* to an earlier Western sequence. An analogy is only as strong as the respects in which the cases are alike, and the model as the chapter reports it is **unidimensional** — it measures one dimension and infers a trajectory.
- The **conclusion is stronger than the model.** A staircase that all societies climb shows, at most, that ideology does not change the *steps*. Rostow asserts that ideology played **no role** in determining the **course** of development, which is a larger claim than the model establishes.

### 2. Galbraith: the four processes and the new ruling class

**J. K. Galbraith**, in *The New Industrial State* (1967), identified characteristics of advanced industrial societies corresponding to the end-of-ideology thesis. He observed that **all industrialized societies are destined to similar development**, involving greater **centralization, bureaucratization, professionalization and technocratization**. These characteristics were visible in the **Russian as well as the American systems**, although they had adopted ideologies as divergent as **communism and capitalism**. It means that a country's **techno-economic structure is shaped by the level of its industrialization, and not by its distinctive political ideology** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.38).

The second half is the part that makes Galbraith indispensable to this topic. He claimed that a **new ruling class consisting of the bureaucratic and technocratic elite had emerged in all advanced industrial societies**. This class belonged **neither to the working class nor to the capitalists**. In liberal societies its members occupied **high positions in an open meritocratic system**; because of a **high rate of social mobility** they are **not attached to particular capitalists**. **Power in society is vested in bureaucracy and technocracy, and not in capitalists.** Galbraith concludes that in the contemporary world, **emancipation of humanity should be sought in anti-bureaucratism rather than in anti-capitalism** (PDF p.38).

### 3. Why this is "technocratic managerialism", and why it matters

⚠️ **Analytical inference, and the central idea of this lesson.** Galbraith's argument does something none of the earlier end-of-ideology writers does: it supplies a **mechanism** for the disappearance of ideological conflict. If power has moved to a stratum defined by expertise and organisational position rather than by ownership, then the questions that formerly divided left from right — who owns, who should own — no longer locate where power is. Policy comes to be justified by technical competence and organisational efficiency rather than by open argument about ends. Debate therefore *looks* post-ideological.

⚠️ **The sting, which an answer must add.** Nothing in that account shows that the ends have stopped being chosen. It shows that the choosing has moved into a vocabulary — expertise, efficiency, administration — in which it is harder to contest, because contesting it requires contesting a technical claim rather than a political one. A particular way of allocating power is thereby **naturalised** rather than abolished. That is precisely why the critics in Lesson 20 can say the end-of-ideology thesis is itself ideological: the argument for it, taken seriously, describes a *relocation* of ideological work rather than its cessation.

| | What Galbraith establishes | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| Convergence of techno-economic structure across divergent ideologies | Yes, on his evidence from the Russian and American systems | That the societies converged politically or normatively |
| A new ruling class of bureaucratic and technocratic elite | Yes, as his claim | That this class has no interests of its own |
| That power is vested in bureaucracy and technocracy, not capitalists | As his claim | That the choice of ends has ceased, rather than moved |
| That emancipation lies in anti-bureaucratism rather than anti-capitalism | As his conclusion | That anti-bureaucratism is itself a non-ideological programme |

⚠️ The fourth row is worth stating in an answer. A programme of emancipation through anti-bureaucratism is a political programme with a preferred distribution of power — which makes Galbraith's own conclusion a counter-example to the thesis it supports.

### 4. The message to the new nations

The chapter records that the end-of-ideology thesis **had a message for the new nations of Asia, Africa and Latin America**: it implied that they **should focus on their industrial development, and should not run after the mirage of communism as a remedy of their ills** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.38).

⚠️ This sentence is the most direct evidence in the chapter that the thesis carried prescriptive content. A description of convergence does not by itself tell any country what to do; the move from "development is ideologically determined nowhere" to "therefore pursue industrial development and avoid a named alternative" is a recommendation, and recommendations are what Lesson 5 identified as the mark of an interested position. 📰 It is recorded here as the chapter's report of what the thesis implied in its period, with no country named and no present-day application.

### Concept check

**Question:** Explain how Galbraith's account can be used *against* the end-of-ideology thesis it was offered to support.

**Model answer:** By taking his own description seriously. Galbraith holds that all industrialized societies are destined to similar development through centralization, bureaucratization, professionalization and technocratization, that this was visible in the Russian as well as the American systems despite communism and capitalism, and that a new ruling class of bureaucratic and technocratic elite — neither working class nor capitalist — has emerged, so that **power in society is vested in bureaucracy and technocracy, and not in capitalists** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.38). What that describes is a relocation of power, and with it a relocation of the vocabulary in which decisions are justified: from open argument about ends to claims of technical competence and organisational efficiency. Decisions about ends therefore continue to be made; they have simply become harder to contest, because contesting them now requires disputing an expert claim rather than a political one. On that reading a particular allocation of power is naturalised rather than abolished, which is the opposite of ideology having ended. The point is clinched by his own conclusion that emancipation should be sought in **anti-bureaucratism rather than in anti-capitalism** — a programme with a preferred distribution of power, and therefore itself a political position of exactly the kind the thesis says has become unnecessary.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat Rostow's five stages as a neutral empirical finding because the model is presented as applicable irrespective of ideology. The model is unidimensional, its application to Asia, Latin America, Africa and the Middle East rests on an **analogy** with an earlier Western sequence, and its subtitle announces a position in the very contest it claims to transcend. None of this refutes it — origin does not settle validity — but an answer that reports it as ideologically innocent has missed what the critics in the next lesson are pointing at.

### 1. The occasion and the claim

With the **collapse of communist systems in East European countries in 1989**, followed by a similar collapse in the then **Soviet Union in 1991**, the end-of-ideology view **got a new impetus in the form of the 'End of History' thesis**. **Francis Fukuyama**, in his paper entitled **'The End of History'**, published in **The National Interest (1989)**, argued that the **failure of socialism** — communism in the present context — meant an **unabashed victory of economic and political liberalism**. It marked the **end point of mankind's ideological evolution** and the **universalization of Western liberal democracy as the final form of human government**. Fukuyama maintained that **liberal democracy contains no basic contradictions** and that it is **capable of fulfilling the deepest aspirations of mankind**. Its victory has **heralded an end to the long historical struggle which had obstructed its expansion in the past** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF pp.38-39).

⚠️ **Title variance, disclosed before the argument proceeds.** The source prints the paper's title as **'The End of History'**, and that form is reproduced above because it is what the source says. Independent verification in this pass establishes that the 1989 article is titled **"The End of History?"** — with a question mark — in *The National Interest*, No. 16 (Summer 1989), pages 3-18, and that the **declarative** title belongs to the later book, **_The End of History and the Last Man_ (Free Press, 1992)**, which is not cited by the source chapter. The variance is material rather than cosmetic: an interrogative article title advances a question about whether an end point has been reached, while the declarative book title asserts one, and a candidate who attributes the declarative form to the 1989 article has strengthened the claim beyond what its author then made. Where this session reproduces the source it keeps the source's form; where it describes the article independently it uses the interrogative title.

⚠️ **Note the escalation, because it is the point of teaching this lesson in sequence.** The 1950s thesis was bounded: a rough consensus among Western intellectuals on four issues, and a prediction of convergence among industrial societies. The 1989 version is unbounded in three directions at once:

| Dimension | 1950s-60s thesis | 1989 thesis |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Western liberal-democratic countries; advanced industrial societies | **Mankind** — universalization as the final form of human government |
| Modality | Convergence is what development produces | Liberal democracy contains **no basic contradictions** — a claim about its internal structure |
| Temporality | An observation about a period | An **end point** of ideological evolution |

⚠️ The practical consequence is that the 1989 version is far easier to attack than the 1950s version, and an answer that attacks only the easy version has not engaged the harder one. Conversely, an answer that notices the escalation has an analytical point most candidates never reach.

### 2. The chapter's own framing of its reception

The chapter adds a sentence that is itself a judgment: **this thesis was given wide publicity in the Western press and academic circles as it was suited to their mode of thought** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39).

⚠️ Two things to say about this. First, it is an *assessment*, not a report, and an answer should mark it as such rather than present it as a neutral fact. Second, it is a sociology-of-knowledge observation of exactly the kind Lesson 11 described: a claim's reception is being explained by its fit with the existing thought-style of the audience receiving it. ⚠️ The same caution from Lesson 4 applies with full force — this explains the thesis's *circulation*, not its *validity*, and an answer that uses the sentence as though it refuted Fukuyama has committed precisely the origin-validity conflation the chapter elsewhere forbids.

### 1. Titmuss and Mills

**Richard Titmuss** observed that the champions of the end-of-ideology thesis **overlook the problems of monopolistic concentration of economic power, social disorganization and cultural deprivation within the capitalist system** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39).

⚠️ This is an **evidential** objection. It does not deny that affluence had increased or that an intellectual consensus existed; it says the description is **incomplete**, and names three omissions. Note how it connects to Lesson 5: ideology's specified failure mode was focusing on **selected parts** of political and social reality and thereby giving a distorted description as well as explanation. Titmuss is charging the thesis with exactly that failure — which is why his objection, modest as it looks, is the one that sets up MacIntyre's.

**C. Wright Mills** dubbed the upholders of the end-of-ideology thesis **the advocates of status quo**. In his view, it is an **ideology of political complacency which appears to be the only way now available for many social scientists to acquiesce in or to justify the established social structure**. So far as human and political ideas are concerned, the end-of-ideology thesis **stands for a denial of their relevance** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39).

⚠️ Mills's objection is **functional**: it asks what holding the thesis *does for* those who hold it. His phrase "the only way now available" is precise and worth reproducing — it describes the thesis as an available posture, a way of being a social scientist without either endorsing or opposing the established structure. ⚠️ Note also the second half, which candidates usually drop: the thesis implies that human and political **ideas** are not relevant, which is a substantial claim about the discipline and not merely about politics.

### 2. Macpherson

**C. B. Macpherson** asserted that the champions of the end-of-ideology thesis make a **futile attempt to solve the problem of equitable distribution within the market society** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39).

⚠️ This is a **structural** objection and the most technical of the four. It does not say the thesis's proponents are complacent or selective; it says their project is impossible on its own terms. The end-of-ideology position holds that the great distributive questions have been substantially settled within existing arrangements — Lipset's workers who have achieved citizenship, Bell's welfare-state consensus. Macpherson's charge is that an equitable distribution cannot be reached **within the market society**, so what is being presented as a settlement is at best an accommodation. ⚠️ Keep this distinct from Mills: Mills says the thesis functions to justify the status quo; Macpherson says its central claim cannot be delivered by the structure it assumes.

### 3. MacIntyre: the self-exemption

**Alasdair MacIntyre** (*Against the Self-images of the Age*; 1971) significantly observed that the 'end of ideology' theorists

> "failed to entertain one crucial alternative possibility: namely that the end-of-ideology, far from marking the end-of-ideology, was itself a key expression of the ideology of the time and place where it arose." (MacIntyre, as quoted in Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39)

⚠️ **Name normalisation, disclosed.** The local extraction of this page renders the surname as "Maclntyre" — a scanning artefact in which a capital "I" is read as a lower-case "l". The standard form **MacIntyre** is used throughout this session, every occurrence has been audited, and the variant is recorded in the source ledger. No other element of the citation is altered.

⚠️ **Why this is the decisive objection.** The three preceding critics attack the thesis from outside: its evidence is incomplete, its function is apologetic, its project is structurally impossible. MacIntyre attacks it from **inside its own category**. The end-of-ideology thesis is a set of ideas, held by a group, in a particular time and place, used to characterise an existing arrangement — which is exactly what the chapter's own opening definition of ideology describes. The theorists applied the category to everyone except themselves. That is the self-exemption Lesson 1 identified, Lesson 11 showed Mannheim closing off, and Lesson 15's positive account made unavailable.

⚠️ **The reply the thesis's defenders can make, stated fairly.** Showing that a claim arose in a particular time and place does not show it is false — the origin-validity distinction of Lesson 4 cuts both ways and protects Fukuyama and Bell as much as it protects anyone. ⚠️ **Why the reply is weaker here than usual, and this is the crux:** the end-of-ideology thesis is not an ordinary claim that happens to have an origin. It is a claim *about ideology's scope*, and it exempts itself from the scope it specifies. The charge is therefore one of **inconsistency**, not of tainted origin — and inconsistency is a defect of the claim itself. ⚠️ **Residual:** a sufficiently careful proponent could restate the thesis reflexively — as itself one ideology among others, advancing a bounded empirical claim — but at the cost of the thesis's main rhetorical force, which lay in standing outside the contest it described.

### 4. The four kept apart

| Critic | Target | Charge in one line | Available reply |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Titmuss** | The evidence base | The description omits monopolistic concentration of economic power, social disorganization and cultural deprivation | The omissions could be supplied without abandoning the thesis |
| **Mills** | The function | It is an **ideology of political complacency**, the only way available for many social scientists to acquiesce in or justify the established structure | Function does not establish falsity |
| **Macpherson** | The project | Equitable distribution cannot be achieved **within the market society**, so the settlement claimed is not available on its own terms | Contests what counts as equitable and as settled |
| **MacIntyre** | The self-exemption | The thesis was **itself a key expression of the ideology of the time and place where it arose** | Origin does not settle validity — but the charge is inconsistency, not origin |

### 5. The chapter's own verdict

The chapter does not simply hand the argument to the critics. It states its own position in five moves (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39):

1. **The end of ideology debate, and its latest version, are designed to project the supremacy of the liberal-democratic system in theory as well as practice.**
2. 📰 **In the contemporary climate of increasing urge for liberalization, privatization and globalization, this idea seems to be riding high.** — a claim about the Fifth Edition's own moment (2009), not about any later year.
3. **However, it needs a close scrutiny.** The collapse of socialism in a large part of the world **could be the outcome of human faults in its implementation**.
4. **Moreover, the Western democratic world is by no means an epitome of justice and morality.**
5. **Human emancipation is a complex venture. There are no readymade answers to all human problems. In devising their solution, relevant ideas from different ideologies may be drawn and examined** — of which the chapter names **liberalism, Marxism, socialism, fascism, anarchism, Gandhism and feminism** as particularly important.

⚠️ **Read move 3 carefully; it is a logical point disguised as a historical remark.** The inference from "socialism collapsed" to "socialism was wrong" requires the premise that the collapse was caused by the doctrine rather than by its implementation. The chapter does not assert that implementation was at fault; it says the collapse **could be** that outcome, which is enough to block the inference. That is precisely the right move — it defeats the argument without claiming a counter-fact.

⚠️ **Move 5 is the chapter's positive conclusion and the best ending available for any answer on this topic.** Ideologies are not to be adopted wholesale or discarded wholesale; **relevant ideas from different ideologies may be drawn and examined**. That returns the topic to where Lesson 5 left it — examination as the defining activity of theory — and it names the seven doctrines that the rest of this subject goes on to teach.

Revision notes:
- **Titmuss:** the champions of the thesis **overlook monopolistic concentration of economic power, social disorganization and cultural deprivation within the capitalist system** — an **evidential** objection, echoing Lesson 5's failure mode of focusing on **selected parts** of reality (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39).
- **C. - **Macpherson:** the champions make a **futile attempt to solve the problem of equitable distribution within the market society** — a **structural** objection: the claimed settlement is not available on the terms assumed.
- **MacIntyre** (*Against the Self-images of the Age*; 1971), quoted: the theorists "failed to entertain one crucial alternative possibility: namely that the end-of-ideology, far from marking the end-of-ideology, was **itself a key expression of the ideology of the time and place where it arose**."

### Concept check

**Question:** Of the four critics, which objection cannot be met by the reply that a claim's origin does not settle its validity, and why?

**Model answer:** MacIntyre's. The origin-validity reply works against objections of the form "this claim arose in circumstances that favoured it, therefore it is suspect", and it is available against Mills, whose charge is that the thesis is an **ideology of political complacency** serving as the only way available for many social scientists to acquiesce in or justify the established structure — a claim about function, which leaves the thesis's truth untouched. MacIntyre's charge is different in kind. He says the theorists **failed to entertain one crucial alternative possibility: namely that the end-of-ideology, far from marking the end-of-ideology, was itself a key expression of the ideology of the time and place where it arose** (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.39). The thesis asserts something about the scope of ideology, and it places itself outside that scope without argument; since it is a set of ideas accepted by a group and used to characterise an existing arrangement, it falls within its own definition. The defect is therefore **internal inconsistency**, and inconsistency is a property of the claim rather than of its circumstances, so pointing to the origin-validity distinction does not reach it. ⚠️ A proponent can escape by restating the thesis reflexively, as one bounded ideological position among others making an empirical claim — but that concession removes the standing-outside posture that gave the thesis its force.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not merge the four critics into a single charge that the thesis defends the status quo. Titmuss attacks the evidence base by naming three omissions; Mills attacks the function the thesis performs for social scientists; Macpherson attacks the possibility of its distributive project within a market society; MacIntyre attacks its self-exemption from its own category. Merging them loses three of the four and makes the chapter's own balanced verdict look like simple agreement with the critics, which it is not.

### Part B — The evidence units

An evidence unit in this subject is a **reconstructed argument attached to a named source**: a named thinker without a mechanism is name-dropping, a mechanism without a source is unattributed assertion. Six are available on this topic, each with the limitation that lets you deploy it without over-claiming.

| # | Claim | Named source | What it lets the answer do | Its limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ideology is false consciousness serving dominant-class interest; social existence determines consciousness, and consciousness **lags** behind advancing material needs | **Marx**, *German Ideology* (1845-46); *Critique of Political Economy* Preface (1859) | Explain how exploitation comes to look natural, without requiring anyone to lie | Risks treating all ideology as trickery, which Lenin and Lukács both deny |
| 2 | Ideology is a **neutral concept** — the political consciousness of any class, including the proletariat, which needs scientific socialism because class struggle continues long into the socialist phase | **Lenin**, *What is to be Done?* (1902) | Show that ideology is not inherently dominative | Blunts the critical edge, and "scientific" risks self-exemption |
| 3 | Bourgeois ideology is false **not because ideology is false consciousness** but because the bourgeois class situation is **structurally limited** | **Lukács**, *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) | Recover the critique after Lenin's neutralisation | The claim that one class position opens universal consciousness is contestable |
| 4 | All thought is socially shaped, so **Marxism itself is the ideology of a class**; false consciousness divides into **ideology** (conservation) and **utopia** (unrealizable projection) | **Mannheim**, *Ideology and Utopia* (1929) | Generalise ideology-critique and block self-exemption | The free-floating-stratum remedy confuses origin with validity and ends in rule by social scientists |
| 5 | Ideology is the characteristic of **totalitarianism**; a totalitarian society claims to have found the absolute truth and mobilises resources for it. Arendt adds **terror** and three enabling conditions | **Popper**, *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945); **Arendt**, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951) | Fix the threshold at which ideology becomes closure | Popper's claim that open societies need no ideology is open to Mills's counter-example |
| 6 | Industrial societies converge through centralization, bureaucratization, professionalization and technocratization; a **bureaucratic-technocratic elite** holds power, so emancipation lies in **anti-bureaucratism** | **Galbraith**, *The New Industrial State* (1967) | Explain how "post-ideological" politics is produced | His own conclusion is a political programme — evidence that the work moved rather than stopped |

**Three objection-and-reply chains**, each with its residual:

- **Chain 1 — Objection:** Marx says ideology is false consciousness, so all ideology is false and should be discarded. **Reply:** Lenin's neutral concept, Lukács's non-pejorative usage and the chapter's own conclusion that **oppressed classes also have their own ideologies** which **cannot be set aside as 'false consciousness'** establish that ideology is action-oriented belief, not automatic falsehood. **Residual:** the critical force of "false consciousness" then has to be re-grounded, which is what Lukács's structural-limitation argument attempts.
- **Chain 2 — Objection:** the end-of-ideology thesis shows advanced societies have moved beyond ideological conflict. **Reply:** Titmuss names three omissions in its evidence, Mills identifies it as an ideology of political complacency, Macpherson denies that equitable distribution is achievable within the market society, and MacIntyre charges it with being itself a key expression of the ideology of its time and place. **Residual:** the critics defeat the unbounded version; Bell's bounded Milan claim about a rough consensus among Western intellectuals on four issues is not thereby refuted.
- **Chain 3 — Objection:** Mannheim's sociology of knowledge proves objective political truth is impossible. **Reply:** the chapter records only that it becomes **extremely difficult**, and Mannheim proposes a dialogue procedure aimed at **synthetic common knowledge** and a **realistic assessment of actual possibilities**. **Residual:** the recorded criticism that he confuses **origin with validity** is not answered by the procedure, which describes how beliefs are produced rather than how they are tested.

### Part C — Application, and the boundary

⚠️ This topic supplies a **lens**, not a stock of facts. Two applications are owned here, and both are stated generically on purpose.

**Application 1 — the technocratic-managerial check.** When a governance decision is defended purely as technical, evidence-based or administrative, and therefore as standing outside political argument, Galbraith's analysis supplies the missing question: *which interests does framing the decision as purely technical make harder to contest?* The point is not that technical claims are false; it is that the move from a technical finding to a decision requires a choice of ends, and a vocabulary that conceals the choice protects it from challenge.

**Application 2 — the asymmetric-labelling check.** When one position is dismissed as "merely ideological" while its opponent is described as pragmatic or neutral, MacIntyre's objection supplies the test: apparent neutrality can itself be a key expression of the prevailing ideology, so the label has to be earned by showing that the "neutral" position submits its own claims to examination — not merely asserted by contrast.

| Both senses of ideology, and the six dimensions | The doctrinal content of liberalism, Marxism, socialism, fascism, anarchism, Gandhism and feminism |
| Marx, Lenin, Lukács and Mannheim on distortion and situated knowledge | Historical materialism as a theory of history, and the detail of class analysis |

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cumulative check A — Lessons 1 to 3

**Question:** Build the chain from the definition of ideology to the claim that it signifies manipulative power, naming each step.

**Model answer:** Ideology in its first sense is a set of ideas accepted as true by a group, party or nation **without further examination**, invoked to justify or denounce a way of social, economic or political organisation; it is a matter of faith with no scientific basis, and its adherents hold that its validity need not be subjected to verification (Lesson 1). Because such claims cannot be settled by evidence, differences between groups holding different ideologies are inevitable and resolve into a love-hate relationship rather than into agreement. Such an idea-set answers four questions — who should rule, how rulers should be selected, on what principles government should operate, and which institutions should be maintained or replaced — and becomes part of politics when it is used to defend an existing system or to advocate limited or radical change in it (Lesson 2). At that point it either lends legitimacy to the ruling class or carries an urge for revolution, and it therefore **signifies the manipulative power of a dominant class or of a social movement** — a capacity to move people, attributed to rulers and movements alike. The capacity is made concrete by the action-orientation of Lesson 3: an ideology presents a cause and induces adherents to fight and sacrifice for it, which is why it can produce sacrifice for a nation's freedom and destruction of innocent life through the same mechanism.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read "manipulative power" as a charge against rulers. The sentence names a dominant class **and** a social movement, and the verb is "signifies" rather than "is" — it describes what ideology makes visible rather than passing a verdict on who possesses it.

### Cumulative check B — Lessons 4 to 7

**Question:** A single distinction does most of the work across these four lessons. Name it, and show it operating in three different places.

**Model answer:** The distinction is between the **origin** of a body of thought and its **validity**. It is introduced in Lesson 4 as the answer to Sabine's thesis that theories of politics are themselves a part of politics: the chapter concedes that political theories arise from political situations and denies that this settles their worth, because the study of political theory also includes a critical evaluation that segregates the truth embodied in a theory from the elements which are the product of political considerations. It operates in Lesson 5 as a procedure rather than a slogan: Aristotle's defence of slavery is rejected because it was designed to promote the interests of 'freemen' in ancient Greek society, while his claim that power and virtue cannot coexist is retained; Machiavelli's amoral advice is rejected while his insight into human behaviour is retained. It operates again in Lesson 6, where Tracy's science of ideas and Bacon's warning against distortion by 'idols' are precisely attempts to supply a method for assessing validity independently of the conditions in which ideas form. And it underwrites row 2 of Lesson 7's table, since "a matter of faith" versus "a matter of critical examination" is just the difference between holding a claim because of where it came from and holding it because it has survived testing.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the distinction as a claim that theory escapes its social conditions. The chapter concedes the conditions in full; what it denies is that conceding them answers the separate question of validity. The distinction names a task, not an immunity.

### Cumulative check C — Lessons 8 to 12

**Question:** Each of the four interpreters is responding to a difficulty created by the one before. Set out the sequence.

**Model answer:** Marx holds that social existence determines consciousness and that material needs advance while social consciousness lags, so the resulting false consciousness is reflected in ideology and the dominant class uses it to maintain authority. The difficulty is reflexive: if the thesis is true it applies to itself, and a revolutionary movement cannot then have a valid ideology. Marx and Engels answer by asymmetry — the proletariat has no vested interest in maintaining itself in power and works towards a classless society in which the state withers away, so it needs no ideology. The difficulty is Lenin's: class struggle continues for a very long time during the socialist phase, so a class that abstains is overpowered by bourgeois ideology; he therefore makes ideology a neutral concept covering the political consciousness of any class, including the proletariat, which needs scientific socialism. The difficulty is that this blunts the critique. Lukács answers by relocating the charge: ideology is not itself false consciousness, and Marxism is the ideological expression of the proletariat, but bourgeois ideology is false because the bourgeois class situation is structurally limited — the class cannot stand on its own and must exploit the proletariat to maintain itself. The difficulty is that this still claims a privileged standpoint. Mannheim answers by generalising: all thought is shaped by its social background, so Marxism itself is the ideology of a class, the correlation with interest is only indirect, and groups other than classes shape consciousness too — after which false consciousness divides into ideology and utopia, and the remaining problem is how objectivity survives at all.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not present the four as four opinions about what ideology means. Each is a repair to a specific failure in the position before it, and an answer that does not name the failures has reduced a developing argument to a list.

### Cumulative check D — Lessons 13 to 15

**Question:** Popper and Arendt both connect ideology with totalitarianism, yet the chapter refuses to condemn ideology as such. Show that these are consistent.

**Model answer:** They are consistent because the Popper-Arendt analysis fixes a **threshold**, not a verdict on the category. Popper's target is a society that claims to have **already found the absolute truth** and strives to implement it ruthlessly, using ideology as the tool to mobilise manpower and resources for a goal declared to embody that truth, and allowing nobody to oppose or criticise policy exclusively determined by the ruling group. Arendt's is narrower still: total domination characterised by **ideology and terror**, made possible by a prior dissolution of society into uprooted masses. Both describe what happens when an idea-set fuses with a state's monopoly of truth; neither describes ordinary political mobilisation in a society that permits criticism. The chapter's conclusion then blocks the quantifier slide from "ideology can do this" to "ideology is this": it is **not fair to look at all ideologies in this light**, because ideologies will continue as the vehicles of value-systems, because **oppressed classes also have their own ideologies** which **cannot be set aside as 'false consciousness'**, and because they can serve as a meeting ground across tribe, caste, religion and region and have produced strong social movements for the emancipation of oppressed sections. The threshold account and the refusal are therefore answers to different questions — one about when ideology becomes dangerous, the other about whether the category as such is to be condemned.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not use Popper's claim that open societies do not need an ideology as though it established that none operates in them. Read functionally, the claim is that such a system does not depend on a doctrine held beyond question; it leaves entirely open whether one is in fact doing that work, which is the question the end-of-ideology critics insist on asking.

### Cumulative check E — Lessons 16 to 21

**Question:** State the four-sentence account of the end-of-ideology debate you would use to open a 20-mark answer, then name the two things it deliberately refuses to say.

**Model answer:** The thesis was formulated in the mid-1950s and 1960s on two independent grounds — that ideology is a tool of totalitarianism with no place in open societies, and that at an advanced stage of industrial development a country's social-economic organisation is determined by its level of development rather than by its political ideology, so capitalist and communist countries were bound to evolve similar characteristics. It was elaborated by Dahrendorf's claim that industrial and political conflict, once superimposed, had become institutionally isolated in post-capitalist societies, by Bell's account of post-industrial trajectories towards services and technical elites, by Lipset's verdict that left-right differences in Western democracies were no longer profound, by Rostow's five stages applicable irrespective of ideology, and by Galbraith's finding that power had passed to a bureaucratic and technocratic elite in whom it is vested rather than in capitalists. It was revived after 1989 in the stronger End of History form, which claimed the end point of mankind's ideological evolution and the universalization of Western liberal democracy as the final form of human government. Against it, Titmuss named omitted evidence, Mills called it an ideology of political complacency, Macpherson denied that equitable distribution is achievable within a market society, and MacIntyre charged that the thesis was itself a key expression of the ideology of its time and place. The account refuses to say, first, that ideological conflict ended — Galbraith's own evidence describes a relocation of where ends are chosen and of the vocabulary in which they are defended; and second, that the collapse of socialism refuted it, since the chapter itself notes that the collapse could be the outcome of human faults in implementation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the convergence claim and the End of History thesis as one lineage. Convergence predicted mutual approximation irrespective of doctrine; the End of History asserts the victory of one doctrine. The collapse of one of the two converging systems is awkward for the first claim, not a confirmation of it.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

⚠️ The questions below are original practice prompts written for this session. They are not previous-year questions and must not be cited as such.

### 10-mark practice (~150 words)

**Practice question 1 [10]:** Distinguish the two senses of ideology. *(Define/Distinguish — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** The term is used in two contexts. In the first, ideology is a set of ideas accepted as true by a group, party or nation without further examination, invoked to justify or denounce a way of social, economic or political organisation; it is a matter of faith, its adherents holding that its validity need not be verified. In the second, ideology is the science of ideas, examining how ideas are formed, how truth is distorted, and how distortions may be overcome to discover true knowledge. The relation is that of object and instrument: nothing inside a faith-held idea-set can assess it, so assessment must come from an inquiry into how ideas form. The chapter's table runs the contrast across six axes, from faith against critical examination to subordination to authority against permission to question it. ⚠️ The second sense survives under other names — sociology of knowledge and critical theory.

**Scoring note for this answer:** marks concentrate in three places — the "without further examination" clause, which is the whole of the first definition; the object-instrument relation, which explains why two senses are needed rather than merely noting that they exist; and the closing observation that the second sense was renamed, which shows the distinction is live rather than antiquarian. A Define stem needs a boundary line, not a verdict.

**Practice question 2 [10]:** Explain why the chapter refuses to treat all ideology as false consciousness. *(Explain — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model answer:** The refusal rests on four grounds. The first is logical: the preceding analyses show what ideology can be, and the inference from "some ideologies manifest false consciousness" to "ideology is false consciousness" is invalid. The second is distributional: ideologies do not belong exclusively to dominant classes, oppressed classes also have their own, and these cannot be set aside as false consciousness. The third is functional: ideologies serve as a meeting ground for like-minded people instead of confinement to tribe, caste, religion or region, may reflect changing social consciousness, have produced strong social movements for the emancipation of oppressed sections, and may show deep concern with the future of humanity. The fourth is communicative: they may be used to convince others of rightful claims, as developing nations do in world politics. ⚠️ Nothing in the refusal withdraws Marx's analysis; it withdraws only the general verdict drawn from it.

**Scoring note for this answer:** an Explain directive forbids a list, so each ground is stated with the work it does, and the four are of four different kinds — logical, distributional, functional, communicative. The closing qualification is what prevents the answer reading as a reversal of the preceding doctrine, which is the error this stem is designed to catch.

### 15-mark practice (~200-250 words)

**Practice question 3 [15]:** Examine the claim that political theory and political ideology cannot be distinguished. *(Examine — answer in 200 to 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** Restated as a testable proposition, the claim is that no criterion separates the two. The supporting case is strong and must be stated first. The same terminology — liberalism, socialism, communism — describes both; a theory may seem to justify and prescribe a course of action as an ideology does; the genesis of a theory may be explained by the stresses and strains of actual politics; and a clash of theories may be explained by a clash in a political situation. Sabine states the thesis at its strongest: theories of politics are themselves a part of politics, and reflection on ends, means, the possibilities and necessities of political situations, and the obligations political purposes impose is an intrinsic element of the whole political process.

The counter-case concedes all of this and separates a second question. Origin and validity are distinct, and the study of political theory also includes a critical evaluation that segregates the truth embodied in a theory from the elements which are the product of political considerations. The operation is demonstrable: Aristotle's defence of slavery is rejected as designed to promote the interests of 'freemen', while his claim that power and virtue cannot coexist is retained.

The finding: the claim holds about origin and fails about validity. ⚠️ The criterion is an activity — submitting one's own claims to examination — rather than an immunity, so it admits of degree and cannot be settled by inspection of a label.

**Scoring note for this answer:** "Examine" is satisfied only by restating the claim as testable, evidencing both sides and closing with a finding. The structural move that earns the marks is conceding Sabine entirely, because an answer that resists him has to defend an implausible claim about theorists' detachment instead of the defensible claim about what theory does.

**Practice question 4 [15]:** "The sociology of knowledge destroys the possibility of objective political knowledge." Critically examine. *(Critically examine — answer in 200 to 250 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition assumes that showing knowledge to be socially conditioned shows it to be unreliable. Mannheim's position gives it some support: all thought is shaped by its social background, so Marxism itself is the ideology of a class; groups other than classes, such as generations, shape consciousness; and the relative character of all knowledge makes the knowledge of objective truth extremely difficult.

Three considerations defeat the proposition as stated. First, the chapter's own word is "difficult", not impossible, and Mannheim immediately offers a route. Second, that route is a procedure, not a claim of escape: individuals who recognise their perception as partial come together across sides with an open mind, enter into dialogue, and strive towards synthetic common knowledge and a realistic assessment of actual possibilities. Objectivity is relocated to a procedure among situated knowers. Third, the proposition commits the error the chapter identifies elsewhere — treating an account of a belief's origin as a verdict on its validity.

Attacking the internal logic: the proposition is self-undermining, since it is itself a claim about knowledge advanced by a socially situated speaker.

⚠️ The qualification owed is that Mannheim's own remedy attracts exactly the charge just made — the recorded criticism is that he confuses origin with validity, and his recommendation that social scientists be given authority to rule converts an epistemic achievement into a title to power. Verdict: the proposition fails, and the strongest version of the worry survives against Mannheim's cure rather than his diagnosis.

**Scoring note for this answer:** the "critically" element is discharged by a named move — exposing the proposition's self-undermining structure — rather than by hostile adjectives. The final paragraph is what keeps the answer honest, because defending Mannheim's diagnosis while conceding the failure of his remedy is the position the evidence actually supports.

### 20-mark practice (~250-300 words)

**Practice question 5 [20]:** Critically evaluate the end-of-ideology thesis. *(Critically evaluate — answer in 250 to 300 words.)*

**Model answer:** Evaluation requires a criterion. The one adopted here is whether contested choices about ends are still being made and whether they remain contestable.

The thesis rests on two independent claims: that ideology is a tool of totalitarianism with no place in open societies, and that at an advanced stage of industrial development a country's social-economic organisation is determined by its level of development, not by its political ideology. Its originating statement is bounded — Bell at Milan claimed a rough consensus among Western intellectuals on four issues: the welfare state, decentralised power, a mixed economy, political pluralism. Elaborations followed: Dahrendorf's institutional isolation of industrial conflict in post-capitalist societies; Bell's post-industrial trajectory towards services and technical elites; Lipset's verdict that left-right differences were no longer profound; Rostow's five stages irrespective of ideology; and Galbraith's bureaucratic-technocratic elite in whom power is vested rather than in capitalists.

Against the criterion the thesis fails in its unbounded form, and Galbraith's own evidence shows why. A shift of power to a stratum defined by expertise relocates the justification of decisions into technical and organisational language; ends continue to be chosen but become harder to contest. His conclusion that emancipation lies in anti-bureaucratism is itself a programme with a preferred distribution of power. The critics converge: Titmuss on omitted evidence, Mills on an ideology of political complacency, Macpherson on distribution within a market society, MacIntyre on the thesis as a key expression of the ideology of its own time and place.

⚠️ Graded verdict: the bounded claim about a rough intellectual consensus substantially held for its period and place; the unbounded claim fails. What the evidence shows is relocation, not cessation. The rival would win only if a politics could be exhibited in which no contested choice of ends was being made.

**Scoring note for this answer:** "Critically evaluate" is failed by an unnamed criterion, so the criterion is declared first and every judgment refers back to it. The move that distinguishes this from a competent answer is using the thesis's **strongest** supporter, Galbraith, as the decisive evidence against its unbounded form — and the final sentence states the condition under which the rejected position would win, which is what converts a conclusion into a graded verdict.

**Practice question 6 [20]:** "Ideology is indispensable to politics and dangerous to truth." Discuss with reference to Marx, Mannheim and Popper. *(Discuss — answer in 250 to 300 words.)*

**Model answer:** The proposition pairs two claims that are usually argued separately, and both hold with qualifications.

Indispensability first. An ideology is action-oriented: it presents a cause and induces adherents to fight and make sacrifices for it, and it answers the four questions — who rules, how rulers are selected, on what principles, through which institutions — that any political order must answer. The chapter's own conclusion goes further: ideologies will continue as the vehicles of value-systems, oppressed classes have their own, and some have produced strong movements for the emancipation of oppressed sections. Politics without an idea-set of this kind is not an available option, since an ideology becomes political precisely by defending or seeking to change an existing system, and those options are exhaustive.

Danger to truth next. Marx supplies the structural mechanism: social existence determines consciousness, material needs advance while consciousness lags, and the resulting false consciousness is used by the dominant class to maintain its authority. Mannheim generalises it until no one is exempt — all thought is shaped by its social background, so Marxism itself is the ideology of a class — and divides false consciousness into ideology, which conserves, and utopia, which projects unrealizable principles. Popper locates the extreme case: where a regime claims to have already found the absolute truth, ideology becomes the tool of mobilisation and criticism is foreclosed.

⚠️ Verdict: both halves hold, but not symmetrically. Indispensability is a claim about politics as such; danger is a claim about a mode of holding ideas — faith rather than examination — which admits of degree. The practical conclusion is the chapter's: relevant ideas from different ideologies may be drawn and examined, which keeps the first half without conceding the second.

**Scoring note for this answer:** a Discuss stem requires both sides reconstructed fairly, the exact point of disagreement named, and a verdict. The distinguishing move here is the asymmetry in the verdict — showing that the two halves are claims of different types, so that accepting both is coherent rather than evasive, which is what a weaker answer cannot establish.

# REMEDIATION

Each entry names an error that recurs on this topic, states why it fails in source terms, and supplies the repaired formulation.

### R1 — Defining ideology by its content

**Error:** "Liberalism and socialism are ideologies; constitutionalism is not, because it is reasonable."
**Why it fails:** the definition is formal. Ideology in the first sense is a set of ideas accepted as true by a group **without further examination** and invoked to **justify or denounce** a way of social, economic or political organisation (Gauba, Ch.2, PDF p.28). Nothing turns on whether the content is reasonable.
**Repair:** apply the test — how is the doctrine held, and what work is it made to do?

### R2 — Reading "a matter of faith" as "false"

**Error:** "The chapter says ideology is faith, so ideologies are irrational."
**Why it fails:** the phrase describes the **mode of acceptance** — validity is not held to require verification. The same chapter concludes that oppressed classes also have ideologies that **cannot be set aside as 'false consciousness'** (PDF p.36).
**Repair:** "Ideology is held as faith in the sense that its claims are not held conditionally on evidence; that is a description of how it is held, not a verdict on its truth."

### R3 — Treating nationalism and communalism as good and bad ideologies

**Error:** "The chapter contrasts a positive ideology with a negative one."
**Why it fails:** both are instances of a single mechanism — an ideology presents a cause and induces adherents to fight and sacrifice for it (PDF p.29). The pair demonstrates the mechanism's indifference, not a ranking.
**Repair:** name the mechanism, then locate the divergence in what the cause does with those outside it.

### R4 — Answering Sabine by denying that theory has political origins

**Error:** "Sabine is wrong because political theory is objective."
**Why it fails:** the chapter concedes his thesis in full — theories do arise from political situations — and answers it by distinguishing the **origin** of a theory from its **validity** (PDF pp.29-30).
**Repair:** concede the origin, then open the second question and name the operation: segregating the truth embodied in a theory from the elements which are the product of political considerations.

### R5 — Treating "absolute impartiality" as a psychological claim

**Error:** "No one is absolutely impartial, so the criterion is useless."
**Why it fails:** the criterion describes an **activity** — holding goals under constant investigation, examining rightness before favouring or opposing, attending to the whole rather than to selected parts — which can be met more or less well and whose failures are identifiable, as the Aristotle and Machiavelli cases show (PDF p.30).
**Repair:** state the criterion as an activity and concede that it admits of degree.

### R6 — Missing the specified failure mode of an ideological science

**Error:** "This study is not ideological because its facts are accurate."
**Why it fails:** ideology **focuses on selected parts** of political and social reality and gives its **distorted description as well as explanation** (PDF p.30). Distortion by selection requires no false statement.
**Repair:** ask what has been chosen for examination and what has been left outside the frame.

### R7 — Treating Marx's account as conspiratorial

**Error:** "Marx says the ruling class invents ideas to deceive the workers."
**Why it fails:** the relations of production are **indispensable and independent of their will**, and forms of social consciousness **correspond to** the economic structure; falsity arises because material needs advance while consciousness **lags behind** (PDF p.32). Deployment by the dominant class is a separate, additional claim.
**Repair:** keep the structural account of formation apart from the claim about deliberate use.

### R8 — Merging Marx's and Lenin's use of "ideology"

**Error:** quoting "false consciousness" and then speaking of "proletarian ideology" without marking the shift.
**Why it fails:** Lenin held that ideology is **not necessarily** a distortion and had become a **neutral concept** covering the political consciousness of different classes (PDF p.33). Two concepts are at work under one word.
**Repair:** mark the shift, and give Lenin's reason — the class struggle continues for a very long time during the socialist phase.

### R9 — Reporting Lukács as agreeing that ideology is false consciousness

**Error:** "Lukács follows Marx in treating ideology as false consciousness."
**Why it fails:** he holds the opposite — bourgeois ideology is false **not because ideology itself is 'false consciousness'** but because the bourgeois **class situation is structurally limited** (PDF p.33).
**Repair:** relocate the charge from the genre to the standpoint, and give the mechanism: the bourgeoisie cannot stand on its own and must exploit the proletariat to maintain itself.

### R10 — Attributing a direct interest-to-thought correlation to Mannheim

**Error:** "Mannheim held that thought is determined by economic interest."
**Why it fails:** his first ground of objection is precisely that the **style of thought** of a group is only **indirectly** related to its interests, with **no direct correlation** between consciousness and economic interests (PDF p.33).
**Repair:** state all three grounds, and note that ground (b) applies the premise to Marxism itself.

### R11 — Reading utopia as the positive counterpart of ideology

**Error:** "Ideology is distorted and utopia is the forward-looking alternative."
**Why it fails:** both are forms of **false consciousness**; utopia relies on it by **projecting unrealizable principles** to muster support for change (PDF p.34).
**Repair:** present the pair as a division within false consciousness, which is what allows Mannheim to classify the Marxist vision of a classless society as utopia.

### R12 — Taking Popper to require ideology-free politics

**Error:** "Popper shows that politics should be free of ideology."
**Why it fails:** his target is ideology fused with a regime's claim to have **already found the absolute truth** and to implement it ruthlessly (PDF p.35). His claim about open societies is best read functionally — such a system does not need an ideology **to work smoothly**.
**Repair:** state the threshold, then the functional reading, then the open question of whether any actual society is coordinated by an unexamined doctrine.

### R13 — Treating ideology alone as sufficient for totalitarianism in Arendt

**Error:** "Arendt shows that strong ideology produces totalitarianism."
**Why it fails:** her definition has two constitutive elements — **ideology and terror** — and three enabling conditions, of which the third is the **dissolution of society into uprooted masses** (PDF p.35).
**Repair:** give both elements and the enabling condition, and note that her definition is narrower than Popper's.

### R14 — Quoting Bell at Milan without his qualifications

**Error:** "Bell declared that ideologies were exhausted."
**Why it fails:** the statement is bounded on five dimensions — **In the Western World**, a **rough** consensus, **among intellectuals**, on political issues, and **in that sense** (PDF p.37).
**Repair:** quote the qualifications and assess the bounded claim; note separately that the qualifications were dropped in the thesis's reception.

### R15 — Reporting Dahrendorf as claiming class conflict ended

**Error:** "Dahrendorf showed that class conflict disappeared in the West."
**Why it fails:** industrial conflict persists on his account; what ended is its **superimposition** on political conflict, since industry and industrial conflicts became **institutionally isolated** (PDF p.37).
**Repair:** use the three terms — superimposed, dissociated, institutionally isolated — and note the thesis is conditional on that separation holding.

### R16 — Treating the End of History as confirming convergence

**Error:** "1989 proved the convergence thesis right."
**Why it fails:** convergence predicted **mutual approximation** irrespective of doctrine; the End of History asserts the **victory of one** doctrine as the final form of human government (PDF pp.36, 38-39). The collapse of one converging system is awkward for the first claim.
**Repair:** present the later thesis as a replacement occupying ground the earlier one vacated.

### R17 — Merging the four critics

**Error:** "Titmuss, Mills, Macpherson and MacIntyre all said the thesis defends the status quo."
**Why it fails:** the targets differ — omitted evidence; the function the thesis performs for social scientists; the impossibility of equitable distribution **within the market society**; and the thesis's **self-exemption** from its own category (PDF p.39).
**Repair:** name the four targets separately, and note that only MacIntyre's charge survives the origin-validity reply.

### R18 — Presenting the chapter as endorsing the critics

**Error:** "Gauba concludes that the end-of-ideology thesis is simply ideological."
**Why it fails:** his own verdict is a scrutiny, not an endorsement: the debate is designed to project the supremacy of the liberal-democratic system; it **needs a close scrutiny**; the collapse of socialism **could be** the outcome of human faults in implementation; the Western democratic world is by no means an epitome of justice and morality; and **relevant ideas from different ideologies may be drawn and examined** (PDF p.39).
**Repair:** report the five moves, and note that "could be" blocks an inference without asserting a counter-fact.

### R19 — Modernising dated claims

**Error:** writing Bell, Lipset, Rostow, Galbraith or Fukuyama as present-day descriptions.
**Why it fails:** they are **mid-twentieth-century** theses and a **1989** article respectively, and even the chapter's own assessment of the debate's standing belongs to the Fifth Edition's period.
**Repair:** attach the date to the claim and say which present you are assessing.

### R20 — Upgrading supplementary vocabulary

**Error:** attributing **reification**, or the **total/particular conception** and **relationism**, to the source chapter with a page anchor.
**Why it fails:** the chapter uses none of these terms; they are secondary-literature glosses.
**Repair:** use them as glosses, flagged, with no title, year or page attached beyond the works the chapter itself names.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. The two senses, and what each is for

- **(a) Set of ideas:** accepted as true by a group, party or nation **without further examination**; invoked to **justify or denounce** a way of social, economic or political organisation; **a matter of faith; it has no scientific basis**; adherents hold that **validity need not be subjected to verification**.
- Different groups, different ideologies → differences are **inevitable** → a **love-hate relationship**, **not conducive to scientific temper**.
- The chapter's ten examples: **liberalism, capitalism, socialism, Marxism, communism, anarchism, fascism, imperialism, nationalism, internationalism**.
- **(b) Science of ideas:** examines how ideas are **formed**, how truth is **distorted**, and how distortions can be **overcome** to discover true knowledge.
- ⚠️ The two stand as **object and instrument**; ideology is a **formal** category defined by how ideas are held and used, not by content.

## 2. Ideology in politics

- Invoked to determine the **best form of government**, the **basis of the right to rule**, the **procedure of selection of rulers**.
- **Four questions:** Who should rule? How should rulers be selected? According to what principles should a government operate? What institutions should be maintained or replaced for the realisation of those principles? ⚠️ In dependency order.
- **Threshold:** becomes part of politics when used to **defend an existing system** or advocate **limited** or **radical** change — a threshold of **use**; the three options exhaust the field.
- May **lend legitimacy to the ruling class** or involve an **urge for revolution**; therefore **signifies the manipulative power of a dominant class or of a social movement**. ⚠️ Both sides named; "signifies", not "is".

## 3. Action-orientation

- Presents a **cause**; induces adherents to **fight** and to make **sacrifices**.
- **Nationalism** may inspire sacrifice of wealth or life for the nation's freedom; **communalism** may induce hatred towards another community and prompt destruction of life and property of innocent persons. ⚠️ One mechanism, two outcomes.
- 📰 One stream of **fundamentalism, based on obscurantism**, has given rise to **worldwide terrorism** — a dated Fifth-Edition claim.
- **Obscurantism:** deliberate effort at making things obscure so as to prevent people from knowing the truth.
- Conflicting ideals: **imperialism** to facilitate exploitation of colonial territories and their people; **environmentalism** to save humanity from atmospheric pollution and depletion of natural resources.

## 4. Theory against ideology

- Same terminology describes both: **liberalism, socialism, communism**. Three concessions: a theory may seem to **justify and prescribe**; its **genesis** may be explained by political stresses; a **clash of theories** may track a clash in a political situation.
- **Sabine**, Preface to *A History of Political Theory* (1937): theories of politics are **themselves a part of politics**; reflection on ends, means, possibilities and necessities, and the obligations political purposes impose, is an **intrinsic element of the whole political process**.
- **The reply:** distinguish the **origin** from the **validity** of a theory; the study of theory also includes **critical evaluation**, which **segregates the truth embodied in theories from the elements which are the product of political considerations**.
- **Worked cases:** Aristotle's defence of **slavery and subjugation of women** rejected as designed to promote the interests of **'freemen' in ancient Greek society**; his **'power and virtue cannot coexist'** retained as embodying an **eternal truth**. Machiavelli's advice to **set aside all moral considerations** rejected; his **insights regarding human behaviour** retained for **diplomacy and statecraft**.
- **The criterion:** politics pursues **partisan ends**; ideology is a **passionate search** for a better society **according to the prescribed model**; theory is a **disinterested search**, its goals under **constant investigation**.
- **Hacker (1961):** theory is **always dispassionate and disinterested** — no fascination for a particular arrangement, no prejudice, no favouring or opposing without examining rightness; **ideology is designed to defend the existing system, or to condemn it to prove the superiority of a different one**.
- **If theory is loaded by an ideology, it is bound to be distorted.** Ideology **focuses on selected parts** of reality, giving a **distorted description as well as explanation**. **Absolute impartiality is the keynote of genuine political theory.** ⚠️ The failure mode is **selection**, not fabrication.

## 5. The science of ideas and its heirs

- **Tracy (1754-1836)** first used 'ideology' during **1801-15** in writings on the **Enlightenment**: the study of the **process of forming ideas**; ideas stimulated by the **physical environment**; **empirical learning through sense-experience the only source of knowledge**; science founded on these ideas can improve social and political conditions. ⚠️ **1801-15** is the publication span of *Éléments d'idéologie*; the **coinage** of *idéologie* is independently dated to **1796**.
- **Bacon (1561-1626)**: knowledge from **careful and accurate observation and experience**; less scientific inquiry is **distorted by false impressions or 'idols'**. Boxed line: **"All colours will agree in the dark."** — Francis Bacon (1625).
- **Contemporary usage:** ideology = ideas adopted by a group to **motivate it for the achievement of predetermined goals**. The science of ideas is now **'sociology of knowledge'** (term introduced by **Mannheim**) or **'critical theory'** (popularised by the **Frankfurt School**), and it **identifies the causes of distortion in prevailing ideologies**; the systematic attempt **began with Marx**.
- **Sociology of knowledge:** how knowledge is **determined, conditioned or distorted by our social background**; introduced in ***Ideology and Utopia* (1929)**.
- **Critical theory:** society has not yet evolved a **rational form of existence**; cannot be analysed by the **paradigm of natural sciences**; institutions analysed by **deviation from a rational form**; **Frankfurt School (1923)**; **'interested' in human emancipation, not a narrow goal, hence not an ideology**. ⚠️ Two senses of "interested": a **stake** versus a **purpose**.

## 6. The six dimensions

| Axis | Set of ideas | Science of ideas |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-matter | Best form of society and government | How ideas are formed and distorted |
| Epistemic mode | A matter of faith | A matter of critical examination |
| Temper | Closed mind | Open mind |
| Stance | Interested search for better society | Disinterested search for better society |
| Institutional role | Instrument of politics | Instrument of political theory |
| Authority | Demands subordination to authority | Allows individual to question authority |

⚠️ The rows form a **derivation**, each following from the one above; treat them as **axes for placement**, not boxes for sorting.

## 7. Marx

- ***German Ideology* (1845-46)**; ***A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859)**. Ideology is a manifestation of **'false consciousness'**.
- Quoted Preface: relations of production **independent of their will**; their **sum total** is the **economic structure — the real foundation** on which **legal and political superstructures** rise and to which **definite forms of social consciousness** correspond; **"It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but, on the contrary, their social existence determines their consciousness."**
- **Mechanism:** material needs **advance**, social consciousness **lags behind** → distorted or **false consciousness** reflected in ideology; the **dominant class makes use of ideology to maintain its authority**.
- **French Revolution (1789):** the slogan **'Liberty, Equality, Fraternity'** enlisted mass support; the makers **settled for liberty**, serving the **new entrepreneurial class**; they did **not** win freedom for the common man, stopping after securing freedom for a **new dominant class to ensure inviolability of property**.
- ⚠️ 📰 Dating inconsistency inside the source: the concept is elsewhere said to be evolved "in late nineteenth century" against work-dates of 1845-46 and 1859.

## 8. Lenin and Lukács

- **Marx and Engels** (**Engels: 1820-95**; **Marx: 1818-83**): ideology is an **instrument for protecting the interests of the dominant class**; the **bourgeoisie needs** it; the **proletariat has no vested interests**, strives for conditions in which the state will **'wither away'**, and works for a **classless society**.
- **Lenin (1870-1924)**, ***What is to be Done?* (1902):** ideology is **not necessarily a distortion**; it is a **neutral concept** covering the **political consciousness of different classes, including the proletarian class**; the **class struggle continues for a very long time during the socialist phase**, so the proletariat needs **scientific socialism** lest they be **overpowered by the bourgeois ideology**.
- **Lukács (1885-1971)**, ***History and Class Consciousness* (1923):** the **dependence of thought on social life**, primarily **class relations of material production**; **consciousness was always class consciousness**; the proletariat, through **increasing estrangement**, occupies a **unique historical position** from which it could achieve **universal consciousness**.
- Ideology covers **both** bourgeois and proletarian consciousness **without a necessary negative connotation**; **Marxism itself is the ideological expression of the proletariat**; **bourgeois ideology is false not because ideology is false consciousness but because the bourgeois class situation is structurally limited** — it **cannot stand on its own** and **must exploit the proletariat**; it **dominates and contaminates the psychological consciousness of the proletariat**.
- **Warning:** **ideological struggle should not become a substitute for class struggle**.
- ⚠️ **Reification** is a supplementary gloss, not the chapter's vocabulary.

## 9. Mannheim

- ***Ideology and Utopia* (1929)**; rejects Marx on **three grounds**: (a) the **'style of thought'** of a group is only **indirectly** related to its interests, with **no direct correlation**; (b) **all thought is shaped by its social background, hence Marxism itself is the ideology of a class**; (c) other groups, such as **generations**, also influence consciousness.
- Introduced **'sociology of knowledge'** to focus on the **social determination of knowledge**, **generalizing the Marxist framework as a tool of analysis**.
- False consciousness in **two forms**: **ideology** = the **tendency of conservation**, mustering support for the **maintenance of status quo**; **utopia** = the **impetus to change**, by **projecting unrealizable principles**. A **ruling class** uses ideology; the **opposition** may project a utopia. The **Marxist vision of a classless society was nothing short of utopia**.
- **Utopia (boxed):** a vision of a perfect society where everyone is happy; **a set of fascinating but unrealizable principles**.
- The **relative character of all knowledge** makes objective truth **extremely difficult**; the **silver lining** is a **'free-floating stratum' of intellectuals**: recognise partiality → see it can be **complemented** by the opponent's view → meet **with an open mind** → **dialogue** → **synthetic common knowledge** and a **realistic assessment of actual possibilities** → a **realistic vision between ideology and utopia**.
- He identifies them as **social scientists** and recommends that they **be given authority to rule**.
- **Criticisms:** confusion between the **origin and validity** of knowledge; **extreme relativism contemplating ideas without upholders**; power to social scientists is **fraught with the danger of absolutism**; let them be **critics of power-holders** — organizers of agitations and demonstrations, journalists, writers, **'conscience-keepers' of society**.
- ⚠️ **Total/particular conceptions** and **relationism** are supplementary glosses, not the chapter's vocabulary. ⚠️ Reflexivity belongs to the **general** form of the total conception, not to the total conception as such.

## 10. Totalitarian closure

- Bridge: when ideology is conceived as an **instrument of motivating people for the achievement of predetermined goals**, it **comes close to totalitarianism**.
- **Totalitarianism (boxed):** the state **regulates and controls all aspects of life — public or private**; **directs** all political, economic, social-cultural and intellectual activities towards **state-determined aims**; **no citizen has the right or opportunity to oppose or criticize the state, or to propose any new aim**.
- **Open society (boxed):** **free flow of information**; policy as a **reconciliation of diverse interests**; **power-holders do not claim that they have found the truth**; freedom of expression; readiness to adopt new ideas; criticism of government permitted.
- **Popper (1902-94)**, ***The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945):** ideology is the **characteristic of totalitarianism**; **science and freedom flourish together** where new ideas are welcome; a totalitarian society claims it has **already found the absolute truth** and implements it **ruthlessly**; **ideology is the tool** for mobilising **manpower and other resources**; Western liberal democracies are open societies and therefore **do not need an ideology for working smoothly**, their citizens being **absolutely free** to criticise institutions and structures of power.
- **Arendt (1906-75)**, ***The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951):** total domination characterised by **ideology and terror**, made possible by (a) the **position of the Jews** giving **anti-semitism** new force, (b) **imperialism** generating **racist movements** and worldwide expansion of power, and (c) the **dissolution of European society into uprooted masses**, lonely and disoriented enough to be **mobilized behind ideologies**.
- **Popper** largely on the **communist** regime, **Arendt** on the **fascist**; totalitarianism as a concept was evolved in the **early twentieth century** for the Soviet regime to the end of the **Stalin era (1953)** and for **Italy (Mussolini)** and **Germany (Hitler)** to the end of the **Second World War (1945)**.

## 11. The chapter's defence of ideology

- **"It is not fair to look at all ideologies in this light."** ⚠️ Blocks a quantifier error.
- Ideologies continue as **vehicles of value-systems**; **motivate** people to goals cherished by their upholders; may be used to **convince others regarding their rightful claims**.
- **Ideologies do not belong exclusively to dominant classes; oppressed classes also have their own ideologies. They cannot be set aside as 'false consciousness'.**
- Four functions: a **meeting ground** instead of confinement to **tribe, caste, religion, region**; reflecting **changing social consciousness**; producing **strong social movements for the emancipation of oppressed sections**; manifesting a **deep concern with the future of humanity**.
- **Positive definition:** identified by **commitment to a cause**; **rules out personal interest, bias or submission to a particular person, group or dynasty**; a set of **coherent ideas — perception of the real and the ideal from one's own position**; may be used to **make others realize that position**.
- 📰 Illustration: in **world politics**, developing nations strive to **impress upon advanced nations to adopt humanist attitudes and policies**.

## 12. The end-of-ideology debate

- Reviewed in the **mid-1950s and 1960s**; in **Western liberal-democratic countries** it was declared that **the age of ideology had come to an end**, on **two independent grounds** — ideology as a **tool of totalitarianism** with no place in open societies, and the **convergence claim** that at an advanced stage of industrial development a country's **social-economic organization is determined by the level of its development, not by its political ideology**, so capitalist and communist countries were **bound to evolve similar characteristics**.
- **Milan, Italy, 1955**, conference on **'The Future of Freedom'**, which urged participants to **forget their minor differences and discover common grounds to face the danger of Communism**; **Shils'** report in ***Encounter* (1955)** titled **'The End of Ideology'** (⚠️ independently recorded with a question mark).
- **Bell at Milan:** "Today ideologies are exhausted... In the Western World... there is today a **rough consensus among intellectuals** on political issues: the acceptance of a **Welfare State**; the desirability of **decentralized power**; a system of **mixed economy** and of **political pluralism**. **In that sense too** the ideological age has ended." ⚠️ Five built-in limits.
- **Dahrendorf**, ***Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society* (1957):** Western societies are **'post-capitalist'**; the **coincidence of economic and political conflict** — the foundation of Marx's theory — has **ceased to exist**; formerly **superimposed**, industry and society are now **dissociated** and industrial conflict is **institutionally isolated**; hence **Marxian ideology was no longer suitable** for analysing Western societies. ⚠️ 1957 is the **German original**; the **English** edition under this title is **1959**.
- **Bell**, ***The End of Ideology* (1960):** **post-industrial** societies develop similarly **irrespective of ideology** — **fewer workers in industry than in services**, **services expanding faster than manufacturing**, **increasing dominance of technical elites**.
- **Lipset**, ***Political Man* (1960):** "Democracy is not only or even primarily a means through which different groups can attain their ends or seek the good society; **it is the good society itself in operation**"; **left-right differences no longer profound**; issues reduced to **marginal wage increases, marginal price rises, extension of old-age pensions**. ⚠️ Cross-chapter variance with "the closest approximation to"; ⚠️ the solved-problems passage is damaged in the local copy and is **not quoted**.
- **Rostow**, ***The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-communist Manifesto* (1960):** five stages — **traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, road to maturity, the age of high mass consumption**; development then under way in **Asia, Latin America, Africa and the Middle East** was **analogous** to Western **late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century** stages; **ideology played no role**.
- **Galbraith**, ***The New Industrial State* (1967):** **centralization, bureaucratization, professionalization, technocratization**, visible in the **Russian as well as the American** systems; **techno-economic structure shaped by the level of industrialization, not by political ideology**; a **new ruling class of bureaucratic and technocratic elite**, **neither working class nor capitalists**, **meritocratically** recruited and **not attached to particular capitalists**; **power vested in bureaucracy and technocracy**; **emancipation through anti-bureaucratism rather than anti-capitalism**. 📰 Message to the **new nations** of Asia, Africa and Latin America: pursue **industrial development**, not the **mirage of communism**.
- **1989** East European collapse, **1991** Soviet collapse → **Fukuyama**, 'The End of History', ***The National Interest* (1989):** failure of socialism as an **unabashed victory of economic and political liberalism**; the **end point of mankind's ideological evolution**; **universalization of Western liberal democracy as the final form of human government**; liberal democracy contains **no basic contradictions**. ⚠️ Given **wide publicity** as it **suited** the Western press's and academy's mode of thought — an assessment, explaining circulation and not validity. ⚠️ **Title variance:** the independently verified 1989 article is **"The End of History?"** (No. 16, Summer 1989, pp. 3-18); the declarative title is the **1992 book _The End of History and the Last Man_**, which the source does not cite.

## 13. The critics and the verdict

- **Titmuss:** the thesis **overlooks monopolistic concentration of economic power, social disorganization and cultural deprivation** within the capitalist system — **evidential**.
- **Mills:** upholders are **advocates of status quo**; an **ideology of political complacency**, the **only way now available for many social scientists to acquiesce in or to justify the established social structure**; it stands for a **denial of the relevance** of human and political ideas — **functional**.
- **Macpherson:** a **futile attempt to solve the problem of equitable distribution within the market society** — **structural**.
- **MacIntyre** (*Against the Self-images of the Age*; 1971): the theorists "**failed to entertain one crucial alternative possibility: namely that the end-of-ideology, far from marking the end-of-ideology, was itself a key expression of the ideology of the time and place where it arose**" — **self-exemption**, a charge of **inconsistency** rather than of origin.
- **The chapter's verdict:** the debate and its latest version are **designed to project the supremacy of the liberal-democratic system in theory as well as practice**; 📰 in the Fifth Edition's own climate of **liberalization, privatization and globalization** the idea **seems to be riding high**; it **needs a close scrutiny**; the collapse of socialism **could be the outcome of human faults in its implementation**; the **Western democratic world is by no means an epitome of justice and morality**; **human emancipation is a complex venture** with **no readymade answers**, so **relevant ideas from different ideologies may be drawn and examined** — **liberalism, Marxism, socialism, fascism, anarchism, Gandhism and feminism** named as particularly important.

## 14. Answer apparatus

- **Qualified thesis:** ideology is neither pure falsehood nor a spent category; it is a group-accepted, action-oriented idea-set that can carry emancipatory claims as readily as dominant-class distortion, and theory's task is to test it — including testing the end-of-ideology thesis against its own critics.
- **Spine:** premises → mechanism (mobilisation and distortion; threshold of closure; obligation to test claims of redundancy) → conclusion.
- **Six evidence units:** Marx, Lenin, Lukács, Mannheim, Popper with Arendt, Galbraith — each with its limitation.
- **Three chains with residuals:** false-consciousness-therefore-discard; end-of-ideology-therefore-settled; sociology-of-knowledge-therefore-relativism.
- **Two application checks:** which interests a **technical framing** makes harder to contest; and whether a claimed **neutrality** has been earned by examination rather than asserted by contrast.
- **Scaling:** 1 unit at 10 marks, 2-3 at 15, 4-5 at 20; a graded verdict at every weight; the decisive distinction is **decline versus relocation**.
- **Provenance:** quote only what the source quotes; flag supplementary vocabulary; keep the four recorded variances visible — Shils, Lipset, the Marx dating and Fukuyama's 1989 title; paraphrase the damaged passage; date every thesis.
