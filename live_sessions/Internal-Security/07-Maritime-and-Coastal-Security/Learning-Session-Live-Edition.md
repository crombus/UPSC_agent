### Definition before data
✅ **Maritime security** protects lawful use of the sea, including navigation, trade routes, maritime zones, islands, ports, vessels and offshore interests. ✅ **Coastal security** is the near-shore subset concerned with coastal approaches, territorial waters, landing points and the land-sea interface. The distinction matters because geography changes lead agency, sensor, law and response time.

### Current geography and historical qualification
📰 The Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways announced in 2025 that India's coastline had been reassessed from the long-used **7,516.6 km** to **11,098.81 km** through a modernised measurement methodology. The older number remains useful only as a historical/book-period figure. A longer measured line does not mean territory suddenly expanded; finer measurement captures coastal complexity more accurately.

| Security surface | Exposure | Required capability |
|---|---|---|
| Sea lanes | disruption of trade and energy movement | distant awareness, escort and cooperation |
| Indented coast | covert landing and small-craft anonymity | local sensors, patrol and community reporting |
| Islands | dispersed jurisdiction and logistics | persistent presence, air-sea connectivity |
| Ports/offshore assets | physical, cyber and underwater attack | layered access, continuity and recovery |
| Fishing space | identity, safety and boundary friction | registration, tracking, livelihood-sensitive policing |

### Application lens
⚠️ A good answer moves from **asset → threat → vulnerability → capability → accountable owner**. It does not begin with a shopping list of institutions. A radar may detect a track, but a patrol craft, trained crew, lawful boarding power and evidence chain are needed to convert detection into a security result.

### Revision notes
- Maritime security is broader than coastal security.
- Coastal security focuses on near-shore approaches and the land-sea interface.
- The current officially reassessed coastline is 11,098.81 km.
- The older 7,516.6 km figure must be tagged as historical.
- Coastline length depends partly on measurement scale and method.
- Islands, ports, fisheries and offshore assets create different vulnerability profiles.
- Awareness, response and recovery are separate capabilities.
- Use the asset-threat-vulnerability-capability-owner chain in Mains answers.

### Concept check
**Question:** **10 marks, 150 words.** Why does the maritime-security/coastal-security distinction improve institutional design?

**Model answer:** Maritime security covers the wider system of sea lanes, maritime zones, islands, ports, vessels and offshore interests; coastal security is its near-shore subset covering coastal approaches, territorial waters, landing points and coastal communities. The distinction improves design because geography changes the threat, response time, legal authority and capable agency. Distant shipping disruption needs naval awareness and cooperation, while an unidentified fishing craft near a creek requires Coast Guard–Marine Police coordination and local intelligence. Port disruption additionally needs access control, cyber continuity and recovery. Thus, institutions should be mapped to assets and zones rather than listed together. Yet boundaries must not become silos: common operating pictures, Joint Operations Centres, rehearsed handovers and accountable incident command must connect the layers. The correct objective is differentiated responsibility with interoperable response.

**Model-answer ceiling:** 127 words; governed ceiling 150 words.

**Rubric (10 marks exactly):** distinction 2 + geography/institution logic 3 + examples 2 + coordination qualification 2 + conclusion 1 = **10**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Coastal security is not a synonym for every naval or Indian Ocean problem.

 internal       territorial     contiguous              EEZ        high seas

 sovereignty    sovereignty     enforcement powers      resource rights

*The ladder shows why offence, location and authority must be identified before naming a law.*

### Legal foundation
The Maritime Zones Act, 1976 provides India's domestic zone framework. The territorial sea extends to 12 nautical miles; the contiguous zone to 24 nautical miles; the EEZ to 200 nautical miles. Sovereignty, specified enforcement powers and sovereign resource rights are not identical legal ideas.

### Trap-first comparison
| Incorrect shortcut | Precise position |
|---|---|
| “India owns everything to 200 nm.” | In the EEZ India has sovereign rights for resources and jurisdiction over specified matters, not territorial sovereignty equivalent to land. |
| “State police power simply ends at the beach.” | Marine Police perform close-coastal functions, while criminal jurisdiction and operational lead depend on law, location and notification. |
| “Every violent act at sea is piracy.” | Piracy has a specialised legal definition and jurisdiction; territorial-water violence may engage ordinary criminal law or the SUA framework instead. |
| “One agency owns a vessel throughout.” | Detection, pursuit, boarding, investigation and prosecution can involve different authorised bodies. |

### Incident-routing method
1. Fix the coordinates and zone.
2. Classify the conduct: safety incident, illegal fishing, smuggling, unlawful violence, piracy or security threat.
3. Identify statutory power and authorised personnel.
4. Preserve evidence and determine the investigating/prosecuting route.
5. Coordinate rather than assume that the first detector owns the entire case.

### Federal significance
⚠️ The sea has no visible administrative boundary. Federal design must turn legal lines into interoperable protocols: whom to alert, whether a unit may stop and board, how to transfer suspects, and how digital evidence is preserved.

### Revision notes
- Territorial sea extends to 12 nm.
- Contiguous zone extends to 24 nm.
- EEZ extends to 200 nm.
- Sovereignty and sovereign resource rights are different.
- Incident location helps determine applicable law and lead authority.
- Piracy is not a label for all violence at sea.
- Detection, boarding, investigation and prosecution may have different owners.
- Federal coordination must translate legal boundaries into handover protocols.
- State the offence-location-authority sequence explicitly.

### Concept check
**Question:** **10 marks, 150 words.** Apply the maritime-zone ladder to explain why coastal-security responses require legal precision.

**Model answer:** The maritime-zone ladder changes the legal character of state power. India exercises sovereignty in the territorial sea to 12 nautical miles, specified enforcement powers in the contiguous zone to 24 nautical miles, and sovereign resource rights plus defined jurisdiction in the EEZ to 200 nautical miles. Therefore, suspicious conduct cannot be routed merely by saying it occurred “at sea.” Authorities must establish coordinates, classify the offence and identify powers for stopping, boarding, arrest, evidence collection and prosecution. Piracy law should not automatically be applied to territorial-water violence; unlawful acts against navigation may invoke the SUA framework, while smuggling may require customs and criminal-law routes. Operational protocols must connect Coast Guard, Marine Police, customs, intelligence and investigators. Legal precision prevents enforcement gaps and unlawful overreach.

**Model-answer ceiling:** 124 words; governed ceiling 150 words.

**Rubric (10 marks exactly):** zone ladder 3 + legal consequence 3 + application example 2 + coordination/rightful-power conclusion 2 = **10**.

**Misconception to avoid:** The 200-nautical-mile EEZ is not equivalent to sovereign national territory.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Creek/estuary | covert small craft | clutter and multiple landing points | infiltration/smuggling | local patrol + community alert |

| Island chain | grey-zone presence | distance and logistics | delayed reinforcement | persistent MDA + forward readiness |

*The diagnostic matrix separates a threat from the condition that permits it to cause harm.*

### Causal teaching
A **threat** is an actor or event capable of harm. A **vulnerability** is the exploitable condition. A **risk** combines likelihood and consequence under existing controls. “Small boats” are not inherently a threat; anonymity, weak registration, sensor gaps and delayed interception create vulnerability. A container port is not vulnerable merely because it is busy; dependence on networked cranes, gates, manifests and power may create cascading failure.

### Historical trigger
✅ The 1993 Mumbai blasts showed how explosives could exploit a coastal landing route. ✅ The 26/11 attacks demonstrated a sea-to-shore terrorist chain. Their analytical value is to expose linked failures in intelligence fusion, vessel identity, interception, coastal landing detection and urban response.

### Prioritisation rule
⚠️ Rank risk through **probability, strategic consequence, response time and recoverability**. A low-frequency underwater attack may deserve attention because consequence and recovery time are high. Frequent fishing-boundary incidents require scalable verification and diplomatic-operational protocols rather than maximum-force responses.

### Rights and livelihood guardrail
Fishing communities live in the security space. Treating them as presumptive suspects destroys the trust needed for early warning. Security design should reduce identity ambiguity while protecting due process, safety and livelihood continuity.

### Revision notes
- Threat and vulnerability are not interchangeable.
- Risk reflects likelihood, consequence and controls.
- Coastal complexity creates landing and concealment opportunities.
- Small-craft anonymity is a systems problem, not proof of hostile intent.
- Ports combine physical, digital and supply-chain vulnerabilities.
- Offshore assets require security and environmental preparedness.
- Historical attacks should be used as failure-chain evidence.
- Prioritise by consequence, response time and recoverability.
- Community trust is an operational capability.

### Concept check
**Question:** **15 marks, 250 words.** Construct a vulnerability-based framework for prioritising India's coastal-security investments.

**Model answer:** Coastal investment should begin with assets and exploitable conditions, not a generic list of threats. First map high-consequence systems: ports, offshore energy, naval harbours, island logistics, fishing corridors, creeks and dense landing points. For each, separate threat from vulnerability. A small craft is not itself hostile; weak registration, identity mismatch, sensor clutter and delayed interception create risk. Second score risks through likelihood, consequence, warning time and recoverability. This prevents frequent but manageable incidents from crowding out low-frequency catastrophic risks such as underwater sabotage or prolonged port shutdown. Third match controls to the failure chain: community reporting and crew verification for fishing spaces; radar, AIS and anomaly analysis for awareness; patrol craft and trained boarding teams for interdiction; and redundant power, cyber continuity and emergency plans for ports. Fourth assign an accountable lead and test handovers among Coast Guard, Marine Police, ports, customs, intelligence and investigators. Finally audit detection-to-interception time, patrol availability, identity coverage, exercise defects closed and recovery time while protecting livelihood and due process. Investment then buys risk reduction, not merely equipment.

**Model-answer ceiling:** 173 words; governed ceiling 250 words.

**Rubric (15 marks exactly):** framework 3 + asset-vulnerability analysis 4 + prioritisation 3 + matched controls 3 + rights/evaluation 2 = **15**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Counting boats, radars or police stations does not by itself measure reduced vulnerability.

### Chronology with purpose
- ✅ The Customs Marine Organisation arose in 1974 against smuggling.
- ✅ The Indian Coast Guard was established in 1977 and placed on statutory footing through the Coast Guard Act, 1978.
- ✅ The Coastal Security Scheme created phased infrastructure support for State/UT coastal policing; a sanction, construction/procurement report and operational outcome are different claims.
- ✅ After 26/11, India expanded Joint Operations Centres, NC3I, radar/AIS connectivity, vessel identification, exercises and inter-agency coordination.

### Coastal Security Scheme phases — sanction, implementation and current position

| Phase | Approved implementation window | Officially recorded sanction/provision | Dated status that may safely be stated |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase I** | **2005–2011** | ₹646 crore; 73 Coastal Police Stations, 97 check posts, 58 outposts, 30 barracks, 204 interceptor boats, 153 jeeps and 312 motorcycles provided to coastal States/UTs | MHA's 2026 Annexure records Phase I as implemented during 2005–2011. This is scheme-output history, not proof of present serviceability. |
| **Phase II** | approved **1 April 2011–31 March 2016**; extended to **31 March 2020** | The **2024 MHA Annexure-VI** records the approved Phase-II **outlay as ₹1,579.91 crore** and the sanctioned package as 131 CPS, 60 jetties, 10 Marine Operational Centres, 225 boats, 131 four-wheelers and 242 motorcycles. The later **2026 MHA Annexure-VI** separately states a Phase-II **outlay of ₹131.43 crore** and says the scheme envisaged 131 CPS, 10 MPOCs, 60 jetties, 131 four-wheelers and 242 two-wheelers; it does not label ₹131.43 crore as a release, expenditure, residual allocation or revised phase total, and does not list boats in that provision sentence. | The 2026 Annexure reports 120 CPS constructed, 37 jetties constructed/upgraded, the entire allocation of four- and two-wheelers procured, and 6 MPOCs constructed. ⚠️ **Current-status discipline:** do not describe Phase II as an ongoing scheme, invent a Phase III, or convert “sanctioned/procured/constructed” into “operational and effective.” Boat availability, staffing, maintenance, sea-days, training, communications and response time require separate current evidence. Scheme closure can coexist with incomplete sanctioned outputs and continuing capability gaps.

### Architecture by function
| Function | Mechanism | What it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| Apex review | NCSMCS and inter-governmental review | policy and implementation coordination |
| Operational coordination | JOCs | continuous multi-agency watch and response |
| Information fusion | NC3I/IMAC | common maritime picture |
| State capability | CSS/Marine Police | close-coastal patrol and local enforcement |
| Readiness | Sagar Kavach/Sea Vigil-type exercises | procedural testing and defect discovery |

### Analytical judgement
⚠️ Reactive creation can overcome inertia. The weakness arises when additions remain vertical silos, assets lack maintenance and response capacity, or exercise lessons are not closed. The reform question is **integration and sustained readiness**, not merely another body.

### Current anchor
📰 MHA's order of 7 July 2026 established the National Academy of Coastal Policing at Okha as a dedicated training institution. It responds to a training-capacity gap, not automatically to command ambiguity or boat availability.

### Revision notes
- Coastal-security institutions expanded in response to demonstrated gaps.
- CMO, ICG and CSS had different original purposes.
- The Coast Guard received statutory footing in 1978.
- CSS Phase I was implemented during 2005–2011.
- CSS Phase II ran from 2011, was extended to 31 March 2020, then concluded; it is not presently under implementation.
- The 2024 Annexure records a ₹1,579.91 crore approved Phase-II outlay and a package including 225 boats; the 2026 Annexure separately states a ₹131.43 crore Phase-II outlay and an envisaged non-boat provision. Preserve both vintage-specific labels without inventing a release, expenditure, residual or revised-total relationship.
- Phase II sanctions and latest reported construction/procurement totals are not identical.
- Scheme completion and asset delivery do not prove operational readiness or security outcomes.
- Post-26/11 reform emphasised fusion, surveillance and coordination.
- JOCs are operational coordination nodes.
- NC3I/IMAC support a common picture.
- Exercises should identify and close procedural defects.
- The 2026 Academy addresses training, not every command/capacity problem.

### Concept check
**Question:** **15 marks, 250 words.** “India's coastal-security architecture has expanded reactively, but its present challenge is integration.” Examine.

**Model answer:** India's architecture shows a trigger-led sequence. The Customs Marine Organisation followed smuggling concerns; the Coast Guard emerged in 1977 and gained statutory footing in 1978; the Coastal Security Scheme from 2005 built Marine Police infrastructure; and 26/11 accelerated Joint Operations Centres, NC3I/IMAC, coastal surveillance, vessel identification and exercises. Reactive expansion mobilised resources after visible failures. Yet accumulation does not guarantee integration. A sensor track is ineffective without a reachable interceptor; state boats fail if crew, fuel and maintenance are weak; and multiple agencies can delay action if alert, pursuit, boarding, evidence and investigation handovers are unclear. Reform should focus on a common operating picture, zone- and offence-specific SOPs, interoperable communications, realistic exercises, maintenance budgets, trained Marine Police and auditable closure of exercise defects. The National Academy of Coastal Policing addresses a genuine training gap but cannot by itself resolve command or availability deficits. India now needs sustained readiness and accountable coordination rather than institution creation after every crisis.

**Model-answer ceiling:** 158 words; governed ceiling 250 words.

**Rubric (15 marks exactly):** chronology 4 + reactive thesis 2 + integration gaps 4 + reforms 4 + balanced conclusion 1 = **15**.

**Misconception to avoid:** A new institution should not be credited with solving problems outside its mandate.

### System anatomy
✅ The Coastal Surveillance Network uses coastal radar and associated sensors. ICG currently reports 46 static sensor locations; Phase II is installing 38 additional radar stations and eight mobile surveillance systems, with VTMS connectivity in the Gulfs of Kutch and Khambhat. ✅ AIS broadcasts identity/navigation data from equipped, compliant vessels. ✅ NC3I/IMAC fuse feeds for a common picture. ✅ IFC-IOR extends information exchange across the Indian Ocean Region; its diplomatic dimension belongs to IR, while its operational information-sharing value belongs here.

### Comparison after mechanism
| Layer | Question answered | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Sensor | Is something present? | range, clutter, weather, blind spot |
| Identity feed | What does it claim to be? | switched-off/spoofed/non-equipped craft |
| Fusion | Do reports refer to the same object? | poor data quality or silos |
| Analysis | Is behaviour anomalous? | false positives or missed context |
| Interdiction | Can an authorised unit reach it? | unavailable boat, crew or fuel |
| Closure | Was suspicion resolved lawfully? | weak boarding/evidence process |

### Inference guardrails
⚠️ AIS silence is a risk indicator, not proof of hostility. ⚠️ AI may prioritise tracks but must expose confidence, data limits and human override. ⚠️ More sensors can increase workload unless feeds are fused and alerts triaged.

### Regional link
IFC-IOR supports wider maritime domain awareness through partner information. It is not a supranational command centre; national agencies retain decision and enforcement authority.

### Revision notes
- Sensors detect; fusion correlates; analysis prioritises; responders act.
- CSN currently reports 46 static sensor locations.
- Phase II includes 38 radar stations and eight mobile systems.
- AIS depends on equipment and compliance.
- NC3I/IMAC support the national common picture.
- IFC-IOR supports regional information sharing.
- AIS silence is not automatic evidence of hostile intent.
- Awareness and interdiction need separate metrics.
- Alert overload can reduce effective awareness.

### Concept check
**Question:** **15 marks, 250 words.** Explain why maritime domain awareness is necessary but insufficient for coastal security.

**Model answer:** Maritime domain awareness converts fragmented radar, AIS, electro-optical, VTMS, intelligence and partner reports into a recognised picture. It helps identify vessels, correlate tracks, detect anomalies and prioritise scarce response assets. India's CSN, NC3I/IMAC and IFC-IOR therefore reduce informational blindness from the coast to the wider region. Yet awareness is not interdiction. A suspicious track produces no security outcome if no serviceable craft, trained crew, fuel, boarding authority or shore cordon can reach it. Nor does detection prove guilt: AIS silence, route deviation or identity mismatch are indicators requiring contextual verification. Fusion can generate false positives, overload operators or reproduce poor underlying data. Capability must therefore be measured end-to-end: sensor uptime, identity coverage, alert-to-tasking time, interceptor availability, detection-to-contact time, lawful boarding quality, evidence transfer and case closure. Human judgement, clear thresholds and redress are needed where algorithms prioritise vessels. The objective is not the largest screen of dots, but timely, proportionate and accountable resolution of risk.

**Model-answer ceiling:** 155 words; governed ceiling 250 words.

**Rubric (15 marks exactly):** MDA mechanism 4 + necessity 2 + insufficiency 4 + safeguards/metrics 4 + conclusion 1 = **15**.

**Misconception to avoid:** A complete radar picture does not prove that an interceptor is available or legally empowered.

Outer-area MDA identifies carrier vessels and approach patterns. Middle layers classify air/surface/underwater contacts. Inner layers protect critical berths and control systems. Response options escalate from query and geofencing to capture, diversion or defeat according to risk and law.

### Capability audit
Ask whether sensors work in local water/noise/weather; countermeasures are integrated; operators are trained; frequencies and evidence are recorded; and exercises include simultaneous conventional and autonomous threats.

### Revision notes
- Drones and autonomous craft are dual-use and attribution-challenging.
- Air, surface and underwater detection require different systems.
- Cheap systems can impose expensive defensive burdens.
- Spoofing and jamming attack the security picture itself.
- Countermeasures may create navigation and collateral risks.
- Evidence recovery can matter as much as immediate defeat.
- Layered harbour protection begins beyond the inner perimeter.
- Local environmental testing is essential.
- Exercises should combine autonomous and conventional threats.

### Concept check
**Question:** **15 marks, 250 words.** Outline a proportionate layered response to autonomous-system threats against a coastal installation.

**Model answer:** A layered response should begin with the protected service and likely approach routes. Outer maritime awareness should identify carrier vessels, launch points and unusual loitering. # COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Concepts and current geography
- Maritime security is the broader protection of lawful sea use, zones, sea lanes, islands, ports, vessels and offshore interests.
- Coastal security is its near-shore subset covering approaches, territorial waters, landing points and coastal communities.
- 📰 India's coastline was officially reassessed in 2025 from the historical 7,516.6 km to **11,098.81 km**; this is measurement revision, not territorial expansion.
- Zone ladder: territorial sea 12 nm; contiguous zone 24 nm; EEZ 200 nm; high seas beyond. Sovereignty, enforcement jurisdiction and resource rights differ.

## 2. Threat-vulnerability picture
- Threats include sea-borne terror, piracy/armed robbery, smuggling/trafficking, infiltration, illegal fishing/straying, sabotage, cyber-physical disruption and autonomous/underwater threats.
- Vulnerabilities arise from creeks and landing points, small-craft anonymity, dispersed islands, port dependencies, offshore isolation, sensor gaps and delayed response.
- Risk must be prioritised by likelihood, consequence, warning time, controls and recoverability.

## 3. Evolution and architecture
- Historical sequence: 1974 Customs Marine Organisation → 1977 Coast Guard → Coast Guard Act, 1978 → CSS Phase I (2005–2011) → CSS Phase II (2011–2020) → post-26/11 JOCs, NC3I/IMAC, radar/AIS and intensified exercises.
- Phase I provided the recorded first package of police stations, check posts/outposts, barracks, interceptor boats and vehicles. Phase II was approved for 2011–2016, extended to 31 March 2020 and has concluded; the 7 August 2024 MHA reply says it is presently not under implementation.
- MHA's 2024 Annexure records a ₹1,579.91 crore approved Phase-II outlay and sanctions of 131 CPS, 60 jetties, 10 Marine Operational Centres, 225 boats, 131 four-wheelers and 242 motorcycles. The 2026 Annexure separately states a ₹131.43 crore Phase-II outlay, describes envisaged provision of the same CPS, jetty, MPOC and vehicle counts without listing boats, and reports status of 120 CPS constructed, 37 jetties constructed/upgraded, the entire vehicle allocation procured and 6 MPOCs constructed. Neither record labels ₹131.43 crore as release, expenditure, residual allocation or a reconciled revision of ₹1,579.91 crore.
- “Sanctioned,” “constructed/procured,” “operational” and “security-effective” are separate claims; staffing, maintenance, sea-days and response outcomes need independent evidence.
- JOCs provide continuous operational coordination; NCSMCS represents apex coordination; CSS supplied state coastal-policing infrastructure.
- Since February 2009, ICG is the designated coastal-security authority in territorial waters and coordinator among Central/State agencies, according to its official page.
- 📰 MHA established the National Academy of Coastal Policing at Okha on 7 July 2026 for specialised Marine Police training; this addresses training, not every command or readiness gap.

## 4. Technology and response
- CSN currently reports 46 static sensor locations; Phase II is installing 38 radar stations and eight mobile surveillance systems.
- Radar detects presence; AIS supplies declared identity/navigation; NC3I/IMAC fuse; analysis prioritises; patrols interdict; investigators close the case.
- IFC-IOR supports regional information sharing and a coherent maritime situation picture; it does not exercise Indian enforcement command.
- Awareness must be paired with mission-capable craft, trained crew, fuel, communications, authority, boarding and evidence handover.

## 5. Communities and infrastructure
- Vessel registration, hull marking, crew identity, landing records, suitable tracking/distress devices and lawful boarding form a layered identity system.
- Fishermen are “eyes and ears” through community interaction, but safety, SAR, feedback, due process and livelihood protection sustain trust.
- Ports and offshore assets need physical, cyber, surface, air and underwater protection plus continuity, emergency, pollution and recovery plans.

## 6. Law and procedure
- The Maritime Zones Act supplies the spatial jurisdiction framework.
- SUA Act, 2002 covers unlawful acts against maritime navigation and fixed platforms.
- Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022 supplies a piracy-specific framework; not all maritime violence is piracy.
- Apply offence-location-authority-evidence discipline. Intelligence can initiate action but cannot replace admissible proof.

## 7. Readiness and cooperation
- Exercises should test ambiguity, bad weather, SAR, pollution, evidence and sea-shore handovers.
- Defects require an owner, deadline, correction and independent retest.
- Islands need persistent awareness, logistics, redundant communications, essential-service continuity and evacuation/SAR readiness.
- Regional cooperation adds information and warning; national agencies retain command and legal action.

## 8. Advanced analytical conclusions
- Reactive institutionalisation built necessary capacity but creates integration and lifecycle challenges.
- A common picture is probabilistic: dark tracks and anomaly scores are indicators, not attribution.
- Autonomous aerial, surface and underwater systems require domain-specific layered controls and proportional response.
- Evaluate readiness, process, outcome, resilience, legitimacy and learning—not inventories alone.
- Final test: can the system **detect, decide, reach, act lawfully, recover and learn under stress?**
