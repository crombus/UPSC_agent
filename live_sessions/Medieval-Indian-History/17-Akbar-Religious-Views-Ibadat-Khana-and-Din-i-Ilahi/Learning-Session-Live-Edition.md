The central question is not whether Akbar was "tolerant." That word is too broad unless we ask: tolerant in which year, through which measure, toward whom, for what political and ethical purpose, and with what evidence of implementation? The learner must keep five connected but distinct fields in view:

```text
personal belief
      ≠
public religious policy
      ≠
juristic sovereignty
      ≠
intellectual encounter
      ≠
court discipleship
```

They influenced one another, but evidence for one does not automatically prove the others.

## Lesson 1

### Reading Religious Change as a Chronology, Not a Conversion Story

## Visual first: five historical questions, not one label

```text
What did Akbar privately believe?
              |
What did the state formally change?
              |
Who could advise or challenge religious authority?
              |
How did the court learn about other traditions?
              |
How were loyalty and kingship ritualised?
              v
        RELATED, NOT IDENTICAL
```

The safest way to study Akbar is to refuse the tempting one-line stories. "He abandoned Islam," "he invented secularism," "he founded a universal religion," and "he merely manipulated religion" each take one possible strand and turn it into the whole.

### 1. Begin with sequence

Akbar's reign does not move directly from orthodoxy to Din-i Ilahi. A more secure three-phase chronology is:

| Phase | Main developments | What the phase does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| 1556-1573 | Islamic observance, Chishti attachment, political accommodation, pilgrim-tax remission in 1563 and jizya abolition in 1564 | that a complete doctrine of religious synthesis already existed |
| 1573-1580 | imperial confidence after conquest, intense inquiry, Ibadat Khana from 1575, wider debates after 1578, Mahzar in 1579, conflict over grants and legal authority | that every member of the ulama opposed Akbar or that the emperor claimed prophecy |
| 1581-1605 | public debates decline and close, private inquiry continues, translations expand, sulh-i-kul matures, selective discipleship and Tauhid-i-Ilahi become visible | that a mass new religion was proclaimed in 1582 |

Chronology changes interpretation. The removal of discriminatory taxes came before the interfaith debates. Political inclusion was therefore not simply the administrative result of a late religious experiment.

### 2. Policy is not identical with private belief

Suppose an emperor abolishes a tax imposed on non-Muslims. That is a public act with legal and symbolic consequences. It does not tell us precisely what he thought about salvation, revelation or ritual. Conversely, a private discussion about divine unity does not prove that every district officer changed policy.

```text
private conviction -> may influence policy
public order       -> may serve political integration
court ritual       -> may strengthen sovereignty

No arrow proves complete identity of motive.
```

Akbar's personal prayer, shrine visits and mystical inquiry belong to the history of belief. Jizya abolition, grant redistribution and permission for worship belong to public policy. The Mahzar belongs to legal-political authority. The translation programme belongs to controlled intellectual exchange. Initiation and the *shast* belong to court discipleship.

### 3. Context without a single-origin myth

Akbar inherited several environments:

- a Timurid and Indo-Islamic tradition in which rulers made pragmatic accommodations;
- a strong Chishti presence, including the shrine of Muinuddin Chishti at Ajmer and Akbar's association with Shaikh Salim Chishti;
- Bhakti and Sufi vocabularies of devotion, spiritual guidance and criticism of empty formalism;
- a conquest state that needed Rajput, Indian Muslim, Afghan, Turani, Irani and other elites;
- Persianate theories of justice and divinely favoured kingship;
- a court containing jurists, Sufis, poets, administrators, sceptics and competing patronage networks.

These are contexts, not a recipe. Similarity between a later Akbarian idea and an earlier devotional practice does not by itself establish direct borrowing.

### 4. Why conquest matters but does not explain everything

Expansion in Rajasthan and Gujarat enlarged the empire and strengthened Akbar's confidence. Rajput alliances made religious accommodation politically useful. Yet political utility does not make personal inquiry unreal, just as personal sincerity does not remove political advantage.

The correct causal model is cumulative:

```text
plural empire
   + personal inquiry
   + court conflict
   + need for loyal service elite
   + inherited ethical and mystical ideas
   = evolving religious-intellectual policy
```

### 5. The source problem begins immediately

Much of the evidence comes through interested observers:

| Source voice | What it illuminates | Why it needs control |
|---|---|---|
| Abu'l Fazl | imperial ideals, chronology, kingship and policy language | he legitimised Akbar and selected evidence accordingly |
| Badauni | dissent, anxiety, opposition and court conflict | hostility could turn novelty into apostasy |
| Jesuit observers | Christian missions and court encounters | missionary expectation encouraged conversion-centred readings |
| later historians | comparison, reconstruction and critique | labels such as "infallibility" or "new religion" may impose later categories |

The method is not to average the sources mechanically. Ask what each observer could know, why he wrote and which claims can be checked against institutions, orders or other testimony.

### UPSC application

- Open with chronology rather than a moral adjective.
- Separate personal belief, policy, sovereignty, encounter and discipleship.
- Use political integration as one cause, not the only cause.
- Treat contemporary writers as evidence-producing participants, not transparent cameras.
- Avoid "modern secularism" and "mass syncretic religion" as unqualified descriptions.

### Revision notes

1. Akbar's religious policy evolved in phases rather than appearing suddenly in 1582.
2. The pilgrim-tax and jizya measures preceded the Ibadat Khana.
3. Personal belief and public policy overlap but are not identical.
4. The Mahzar concerns juristic and sovereign authority.
5. Translation concerns elite knowledge and comparative inquiry.
6. Tauhid-i-Ilahi concerns restricted spiritual-political discipleship.
7. Rajput incorporation supplied political context, not a complete religious explanation.
8. Chishti and Bhakti-Sufi environments were influences and analogies, not a single blueprint.
9. Abu'l Fazl and Badauni must be read through their opposed court positions.
10. A qualified chronology is stronger than either celebratory or communal retrospective myth.

#### Answers and explanations

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** It preserves chronology and prevents evidence from one sphere being used automatically to prove another.
- **B — Incorrect:** The earlier reforms had their own political setting and cannot be made stages of a predetermined 1582 plan.
- **C — Incorrect:** Political usefulness and genuine inquiry can coexist; one does not erase the other.
- **D — Incorrect:** Contemporary proximity does not remove patronage, polemic, missionary purpose or selective reporting.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **D — Incorrect:** Rajput accommodation had already begun; the dates do not establish the sequence asserted.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain why Akbar's religious policy should be studied as an evolution rather than as the creation of a new faith.

**Model answer:**
Akbar's religious policy developed through overlapping political, intellectual and spiritual processes. In the early phase, pilgrimage tax was remitted in 1563 and jizya abolished in 1564, while Akbar still observed Islamic practices and cultivated Chishti associations. These measures show accommodation before any supposed new creed.

From 1575, the Ibadat Khana brought Muslim scholars and mystics into debate; after 1578 the discussions widened. Conflict among interpreters and disputes over clerical authority contributed to the Mahzar of 1579, which gave the emperor a bounded arbitral role when qualified jurists disagreed.

In the later phase, sulh-i-kul framed justice and non-sectarian government, translations widened courtly knowledge, and Tauhid-i-Ilahi supplied a restricted language of ethical discipleship and loyalty. It lacked the institutions of a mass religion.

Personal inquiry, plural state formation and imperial sovereignty reinforced one another, but were not identical. The best interpretation is therefore an evolving, ruler-centred programme of accommodation and authority rather than a sudden conversion or universal church.

**Scoring note:** Require the three phases, two early measures, Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, sulh-i-kul, Tauhid-i-Ilahi qualification and the distinction between belief and policy.

### Verified PYQ linkage

The 2025 GS-I question asks for the "main aspects" of Akbar's religious syncretism. This lesson supplies the chronology and category control needed to prevent a list-like or sudden-1582 answer.

### Early Accommodation: Tax, Worship, Alliance and the Limits of Equality

## Visual first: from conquered subjects to service-bearing subjects

```text
religiously marked fiscal disability
             |
pilgrim tax remitted, 1563
jizya abolished, 1564
             |
reduced formal differentiation
             |
alliance + service + patronage across communities
             |
wider imperial legitimacy
             |
still a ranked monarchy, not equal citizenship
```

The early reforms mattered because they altered how the state publicly classified subjects. They did not abolish hierarchy, but they weakened the idea that a non-Muslim loyal servant must remain fiscally marked as an inferior outsider.

### 1. Pilgrim tax in 1563

Akbar remitted the tax collected from Hindu pilgrims at sacred places such as Prayag and Banaras in **1563**. A pilgrimage tax converted religious practice into a special fiscal burden. Its removal therefore carried more than a revenue meaning.

What it proves:

- the court was willing to remove a religiously specific disability;
- accommodation preceded the Ibadat Khana;
- sacred travel and imperial revenue were being reconsidered together.

What it does not prove:

- that every local collection ceased immediately;
- that the empire became fiscally equal in all respects;
- that the measure arose from a fully articulated philosophy of sulh-i-kul.

### 2. Jizya in 1564

**Jizya** was a poll tax levied on non-Muslims within an Islamic political tradition. Akbar abolished it in **1564**. Abu'l Fazl justified the change by stressing that Hindu subjects had bound themselves to imperial service and that contemptuous differentiation was inconsistent with loyal contribution.

The precise significance was political and symbolic:

```text
service to empire
      +
removal of a confessional fiscal marker
      =
broader claim to imperial belonging
```

The tax may not have been the state's greatest source of income. That makes its symbolic removal no less important. It identified religious discrimination as an obstacle to incorporation.

### 3. A chronology dispute that must be stated carefully

Badauni associates renewed examination of jizya with 1575-1576 and places abolition differently in some readings. Satish Chandra argues that attempted orders to restore assessment quickly disappeared and retains **1564** as the abolition date. The defensible formulation is:

> Akbar abolished jizya in 1564; later court activity shows attempted reconsideration or enforcement debates, not a secure replacement of that chronology.

This is a useful example of source control: disagreement should be acknowledged without pretending all dates are equally supported.

### 4. Rajput relations: relevant mechanism, bounded coverage

Akbar's alliance with Amber and later Rajput service widened the political elite. Rajput wives at court could retain forms of worship. Rajput rulers received mansabs, governorships, honour and protection of important hereditary interests.

Yet the religious meaning must be bounded:

| What Rajput inclusion shows | What it cannot show alone |
|---|---|
| confessional identity did not bar high service | that every Rajput house accepted Mughal authority |
| political kinship could coexist with retained worship | that matrimonial alliance was purely a tolerance measure |
| a composite nobility supported imperial integration | that coercion disappeared, especially in Mewar |

The Chittor siege and massacre in 1568 sharply qualify any account of unbroken benevolence. Accommodation and coercion belonged to the same expanding monarchy.

### 5. Early Islamic observance and Chishti attachment

During this period Akbar continued prayers, supported the pilgrimage to Mecca and showed deference to figures such as Shaikh Abdun Nabi. He also visited Chishti shrines and valued mystical reflection. These facts matter because they challenge the retrospective claim that early accommodation proves immediate abandonment of Islam.

The Chishti environment helped make devotional search and spiritual guidance intelligible. It did not dictate a fixed programme. Akbar's later ideas emerged through experience, debate and sovereignty rather than simple inheritance from one Sufi order.

### 6. Enslavement and war captives

The source associates Akbar with ending forced conversion or enslavement of families connected with defeated rebels and prisoners of war. The safest conclusion is limited: the state announced a restraint on a conquest practice. It is unsafe to infer that slavery vanished from court, military or household society.

### 7. From toleration to integration

Tolerance can mean merely allowing a disliked group to remain. Akbar's policy increasingly went further by linking service, honour and loyalty across confessional lines. But participation remained selective and bestowed from above.

```text
not exclusion by religion
        |
not modern equality either
        |
selective incorporation into a ranked imperial order
```

### UPSC application

- Pair every date with its exact measure.
- Treat jizya abolition as symbolic and political, not merely fiscal.
- Use Rajput inclusion as evidence of composite state formation, while retaining coercion and unequal access.
- State that early Islamic observance coexisted with accommodation.
- Do not convert a prohibition on forced enslavement into abolition of slavery.

1. Pilgrim tax was remitted in 1563.
2. Jizya was abolished in 1564.
3. Pilgrim tax concerned sacred travel; jizya was a poll tax on non-Muslims.
4. The measures reduced selected formal religious disabilities.
5. Their symbolic importance may exceed any safely measurable revenue effect.
6. Badauni's later jizya material does not displace the canonical 1564 chronology.
7. Rajput participation gave political form to wider incorporation.
8. Mewar and Chittor prevent a purely benevolent interpretation.
9. Early accommodation coexisted with Akbar's Islamic observance.
10. Chishti association supplied a spiritual setting, not a complete causal blueprint.
11. Restraint on enslavement or forced conversion did not abolish slavery as a social institution.
12. The result was inclusive imperial hierarchy, not modern equal citizenship.

### Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why were Akbar's abolition of pilgrim tax and jizya important to imperial integration?

**Model answer:**
Akbar remitted pilgrim tax in 1563 and abolished jizya in 1564. The first removed a special burden on Hindu sacred travel; the second ended a confessional poll tax on non-Muslims. Their importance was therefore political and symbolic as well as fiscal.

The measures reduced the public marking of loyal non-Muslim subjects as an inferior category. Alongside Rajput alliances and the entry of Hindus such as Todar Mal and Birbal into high service, they helped redefine the Mughal state as an empire that could reward service across religious identities.

Their reach must not be overstated. Inclusion remained selective, social hierarchy survived and coercive expansion continued, as Chittor demonstrates. Akbar also remained personally observant within an Islamic and Chishti environment during the early phase.

The reforms created foundations for a broader imperial legitimacy; they did not establish modern equality or a fully developed doctrine of sulh-i-kul.

**Scoring note:** Require both dates and tax meanings, political-symbolic integration, one service example and explicit limits concerning hierarchy and coercion.

### The Ibadat Khana's Muslim Phase: Inquiry Under the Imperial Gaze

Progress: 3/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: foundation, setting, participants, debate dynamics and ulama conflict

## Visual first: why a debate hall could intensify disagreement

```text
emperor asks for truth
          |
selected scholars debate under royal observation
          |
doctrine + legal interpretation + access + precedence
          |
participants compete to persuade the emperor
          |
argument becomes court politics
          |
Akbar questions clerical monopoly
```

The Ibadat Khana was not initially a parliament of world religions. It began as a Muslim intellectual and devotional forum, and its political significance arose partly because disagreement unfolded before the sovereign who controlled patronage.

### 1. Foundation and location

Akbar built the **Ibadat Khana**, or Hall of Prayer, at Fatehpur Sikri in **1575**. Satish Chandra's detailed account places a large rectangular building around the cell of Shaikh Abdullah Niyazi, near the imperial palace and Anup Talao.

This is textual topography. It does not settle the archaeological identification of one surviving structure. A careful answer says "the source locates it in the court zone near Anup Talao" rather than treating a modern label as conclusive material proof.

### 2. Why 1575?

By the mid-1570s, victories in Malwa, Rajasthan and Gujarat had strengthened Akbar's confidence. He had more freedom to reflect on kingship, law and divine favour. His interest in Sufi poetry, shrine visitation, philosophy and religious questions supplied personal momentum.

The debates were not without precedent. Public disputation existed in Islamic and wider intellectual traditions, including Timurid court culture. Akbar's novelty lay less in inventing debate than in making it a sustained imperial institution whose consequences reached sovereignty.

### 3. Initial participants

The first phase admitted:

- Sufi shaikhs;
- ulama and jurists;
- learned men;
- selected companions, courtiers and attendants.

Sessions were associated with **Thursday nights**, after state business. Participants could be grouped, and larger gatherings might use the Anup Talao courtyard. Akbar also held private discussions outside the formal hall.

The category "Muslim participants" must not erase internal difference. A Sufi mystic, a jurist, a patronage-holding sadr and a court intellectual did not approach revelation, authority or royal intervention in identical ways.

### 4. Why did conflict arise?

Several incentives converged:

| Source of conflict | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| doctrinal disagreement | texts and traditions permitted competing interpretations |
| legal stakes | decisions could affect punishment, marriage and public order |
| patronage | reputation before the emperor could shape office and grants |
| precedence | proximity and speaking order signalled status |
| imperial audience | participants sought to defeat rivals and influence the sovereign |

One reported dispute concerned how many wives a ruler could legally marry. The importance is not the answer itself. The episode exposed disagreement among authorities who claimed command of the same legal tradition.

### 5. Abu'l Fazl's polemic and its limit

Abu'l Fazl depicts the theologians as arrogant, abusive and narrow. Their conduct reportedly disgusted Akbar. This testimony matters because it reveals the court's widening breach with important ulama. Yet Abu'l Fazl was also helping construct an image of the rational emperor rising above sectarian specialists.

Therefore:

```text
reported disorder = evidence of real conflict
Abu'l Fazl's language = evidence of imperial polemic
neither warrants "all ulama were ignorant"
```

### 6. Shaikh Abdun Nabi and Abdullah Sultanpuri

Akbar had earlier shown great respect to **Shaikh Abdun Nabi**, the sadr-us-sudur, and **Abdullah Sultanpuri**, also known as Makhdum-ul-Mulk. Conflict later involved:

- disputes about the learning and conduct of leading clerics;
- allegations of corruption and favouritism in revenue-free grants;
- persecution of Shia, Mahdawi or other dissenting persons;
- the Mathura Brahman case, in which legal punishment became a point of rupture;
- the broader issue of whether religious specialists or the ruler had the final voice when jurists disagreed.

These men cannot stand for all ulama. Nor was every dispute purely theological: office, land grants, legal authority and political loyalty overlapped.

### 7. What the first phase achieved

The Muslim phase did not produce settled truth. It exposed three problems:

1. juristic interpretation was plural;
2. status competition distorted inquiry;
3. religious authority could become an autonomous patronage and political power.

That experience helps explain both the widening of debate after 1578 and the later Mahzar.

### UPSC application

- Date the institution to 1575 at Fatehpur Sikri.
- Describe the first phase as Muslim, internally diverse and court-controlled.
- Explain why royal patronage altered debate incentives.
- Name Abdun Nabi and Abdullah Sultanpuri only for specific conflicts.
- Use Abu'l Fazl critically: informative insider and interested imperial advocate.

## Visual first: failure in one function, consequence in another

```text
debates widened after 1578
          |
more traditions and deeper questions
          |

          |
no common theology; bitterness increases
          |

          |

          |

```

The Ibadat Khana can fail as a machine for theological agreement and still matter historically. Its most durable result was not a common creed, but recognition that the empire required a way to govern disagreement.

### 1. Widening after 1578

After a reported mystical experience in **1578**, Akbar opened discussion beyond Muslim participants. The evidence names or associates:

- Brahman and other Hindu interlocutors;
- Jain teachers;
- Christian missionaries;
- Zoroastrian or Parsi representatives;
- sceptical or atheistic voices in hostile descriptions.

Questions extended to revelation, prophethood, resurrection, divine unity and the authority of inherited traditions. Subjects that some Muslim participants regarded as settled were now discussed comparatively.

### 2. Not a modern parliament of religions

The phrase "interfaith debate" can mislead. Attendance was selective and controlled by the court. Traditions were not represented through equal, elected or stable delegations. Surviving evidence records some named participants better than others.

| Safe proposition | Unsafe leap |
|---|---|
| Akbar received thinkers from several traditions | every tradition had equal access and speaking power |
| comparative questions were discussed | a common syncretic theology was agreed |
| named individuals influenced court inquiry | each person represented an entire religion |
| private and public encounters continued | all participants attended the same formal session |

### 3. Why widening increased bitterness

Every participant did not enter with the same aim. Some wished to teach, some to convert, some to defend inherited truth, some to secure patronage and some to satisfy the emperor. When claims about final revelation or salvation are treated as competitive propositions before a ruler, defeat can carry both theological and political humiliation.

The debates therefore rewarded:

```text
demonstrating superiority
        more than
building a shared minimum
```

Satish Chandra's formulation is that participants denounced rivals and tried to prove their own religion superior. This conclusion should not be romanticised into "dialogue inevitably produces harmony."

### 4. Closure chronology

The public forum **practically declined in 1581** and was **finally discontinued in 1582**. Both dates matter:

- 1581 captures practical decline;
- 1582 marks final closure in the standard account.

Saying only "closed in 1582" is acceptable for a short answer, but the two-stage formulation is more precise.

### 5. Closure did not end inquiry

Akbar continued private discussions, received visitors and sponsored translation. Purushottam and Devi are associated with explanations of Hindu thought. Other specialists also met the emperor outside the public forum.

This distinction prevents a common error:

```text
end of one institution
        ≠
end of intellectual engagement
```

### 6. The debate outcome paradox

The Ibadat Khana produced no stable theological synthesis. Yet it had at least two consequences in the source-based interpretation:

1. Akbar concluded that valuable insight could exist across traditions.
2. The court increasingly rejected the monopoly claims of particular clerical groups.

The response was not necessarily to merge religions. It was to seek a political ethic under which differing communities could be governed without one sect's quarrels controlling the state.

### 7. Evidence limits on named participants

Purushottam, Devi, Hiravijaya Suri, Rudolf Aquaviva, Antonio Monserrate and Meherji Rana belong to the wider history of Akbar's encounters. They should not automatically be placed together in one dramatic evening at the Ibadat Khana. The safest method is to connect each name only to the encounter securely supported and reserve broader conclusions for the cumulative pattern.

### The Mahzar of 1579: Arbitration, Public Welfare and Sovereignty

## Visual first: read the decision rule before naming the document

```text
qualified interpreters disagree
             |
             v
the emperor may select one existing interpretation
             |

             |
a royal order must not contradict explicit Quranic/Hadis command

```

The Mahzar is often remembered through the phrase "Decree of Infallibility." That phrase is dangerous because it makes Akbar sound like a pope declaring himself incapable of error. The actual document concerned how decisions should be made when qualified jurists disagreed.

### 1. What was the Mahzar?

The **Mahzar** was an attested declaration issued in **1579** and signed by leading ulama. Satish Chandra's detailed account states that seven leading figures signed, including:

- Shaikh Abdun Nabi;
- Abdullah Sultanpuri or Makhdum-ul-Mulk;
- Shaikh Mubarak, father of Abu'l Fazl and Faizi.

Some signatures may have been reluctant. That matters because formal assent does not prove intellectual agreement.

### 2. Clause-by-clause meaning

The document's logic can be separated into four claims:

1. Akbar was described in exalted Islamic political language as a just sovereign and protector.
2. His rank as ruler was placed above individual juristic claimants in matters affecting government.
3. When qualified interpreters held conflicting views, he could choose one for public welfare and proper administration.
4. He could issue an order calculated to benefit humanity provided it did not contradict an explicit Quranic or Hadis command.

The safest formula is **bounded imperial arbitration**.

### 3. What it did not claim

The Mahzar did not:

- declare Akbar a prophet;
- state that he received revelation;
- make every personal opinion religiously correct;
- abolish Islam, the sharia or the qazi establishment;
- grant unlimited power to invent doctrine;
- secure obedience to a foreign caliph by rejecting another.

The Timurids had not accepted subordination to an Ottoman or Safavid caliph. The claim was therefore not primarily emancipation from an existing external allegiance.

### 4. Why did it arise?

The Ibadat Khana had shown that jurists disagreed. The **Mathura Brahman case** sharpened the problem. A Brahman was accused of taking material collected for a mosque, using it for a temple and insulting Islam. Some jurists favoured death; others argued for a lesser punishment because he was a protected subject. Akbar preferred leniency, but Abdun Nabi had him executed.

The exact allegations come through partisan narratives, so the episode should not become an uncritical courtroom reconstruction. Its importance is institutional: disagreement over punishment revealed the cost of leaving final authority to one powerful cleric.

### 5. Grants, office and rebellion

The sadr supervised **madad-i-maash**, revenue-free grants for scholars, religious figures and dependants. Allegations of patronage abuse and persecution made reform of the office part of the conflict.

In 1580, rebellion in Bengal and Bihar received support from some ulama. Mulla Muhammad Yazdi, qazi of Jaunpur, reportedly declared rebellion lawful; the Bengal qazi linked unrest to the loss of grants. Akbar's severe response joined religious opposition to political rebellion.

This does not prove that all ulama became rebels. It shows why legal authority, patronage and state security could no longer be treated as separate questions.

### 6. Was the text more limited than its effect?

Yes. A document may impose formal conditions and still shift power greatly.

| Textual limit | Political effect |
|---|---|
| choice among conflicting qualified opinions | emperor decides which juristic voice becomes operative |
| public welfare and administrative order | ruler defines the governing necessity |
| no contradiction of explicit command | court influences what counts as explicit and applicable |

Some historians therefore accept its legal compatibility while warning that practice could support expansive autocracy. This is the strongest qualification: **not unlimited infallibility, but a real enhancement of ruler-centred sovereignty**.

### 7. International and ideological language

The document presented Hindustan as a land of security, justice and sectarian peace. This projected the realm's prestige in relation to established West Asian centres. It made just rule within a plural realm part of imperial status.

S.A.A. Rizvi's interpretation, cited by Satish Chandra, treats the Mahzar as an effective declaration of principles later associated with sulh-i-kul. This is an interpretation of significance, not a substitute for the document's clauses.

### Sulh-i-Kul: Governing Disagreement Without Merging Religions

Progress: 6/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: definition, ethical content, state mechanisms, chronology and limits

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: coexistence became a governing rule

```text
theological agreement proved unattainable
                 |
                 v
the state still needed order across communities
                 |
justice + public welfare + restraint of sectarian conflict
                 |
                 v
             SULH-I-KUL
                 |
service, patronage, worship and protection across creeds
                 |
implemented through a hierarchical emperor and uneven officials
```

**Sulh-i-kul** is commonly translated as "peace with all" or "universal peace." Its historical importance lies not in declaring every religion identical, but in making non-sectarian justice a principle of imperial rule.

### 1. The governing problem it solved

The Ibadat Khana showed that theological consensus could not be commanded. The empire nevertheless contained many religious communities and depended on their labour, revenue, military service and elite cooperation.

Sulh-i-kul answered:

> How can a ruler preserve justice and order when subjects do not share one creed?

Its answer was to restrain sectarian domination and judge service and welfare through an imperial standard.

### 2. Abu'l Fazl's ethical kingship

Abu'l Fazl described the true ruler through:

- **paternal love** toward subjects;
- attention to great and small;
- justice or **adl**;
- divine illumination, **farr-i izadi**;
- public welfare;
- refusal to let sectarian conflict disturb order.

This was normative language: how monarchy wished to appear and act. It does not prove that every official or subject experienced equal treatment.

### 3. Sulh-i-kul in action

| Mechanism | Evidence | Necessary limit |
|---|---|---|
| fiscal accommodation | pilgrim tax and jizya removed | selected disabilities, not all inequality |
| composite service | Hindus and Rajputs entered high office | entry remained selective and ranked |
| patronage reform | grants extended increasingly to Hindu, Jain and Parsi recipients; Jesuits received church support | grants depended on imperial discretion |
| worship | permission for temples, churches and synagogues appears in source-based policy | an order does not prove uniform local compliance |
| relief establishments | Dharmapura, Khairpura and later Jogipura | court-centred charity, not a universal welfare entitlement |
| education and translation | subjects beyond narrow religious learning entered court curricula | impact was mainly elite and uneven |

### 4. It was not religious indifference

The Mughal state did not withdraw from religion. Akbar:

- regulated grants;
- heard religious claims;
- used sacred and spiritual language;
- patronised institutions;
- restricted practices he judged harmful;
- fashioned court ritual around sovereign charisma.

Sulh-i-kul meant principled accommodation under imperial authority, not separation of religion and state.

### 5. It was not theological syncretism

Religions did not need to be blended for subjects to coexist. This distinction is central:

| Syncretic claim | Sulh-i-kul claim |
|---|---|
| doctrines combine or borrow | different doctrines may remain distinct |
| asks what beliefs are mixed | asks how disagreement is governed |
| may concern personal spirituality | concerns public justice and order |

Akbar did experiment with ideas and rituals, but sulh-i-kul's durability came from not requiring uniform belief.

### 6. It was not modern secularism

Modern constitutional secularism involves institutions, rights and legal equality that do not describe a sixteenth-century monarchy. Akbar's order remained:

- conquest-based;
- socially hierarchical;
- dependent on royal will;
- unequal in access;
- capable of severe coercion;
- unevenly implemented across regions.

Calling it modern secularism hides both its historical innovation and its limits.

### 7. The coercion test

Chittor, punishment after the 1580-1581 rebellion and harsh action against opponents show that inclusive policy did not make the state non-coercive. The strongest interpretation is not "tolerance was false." It is:

> Akbar widened the basis of legitimate service and protection while retaining the coercive structure of early-modern empire.

### 8. Chronology and durability

Sulh-i-kul crystallised gradually. Early fiscal reform supplied practice; debate and Mahzar supplied intellectual and sovereign conflict; later policy gave the principle clearer ideological form.

It outlasted the public Ibadat Khana and had wider relevance than the small discipleship circle because it could guide appointments, patronage and public order without demanding initiation.

- Connect it to taxes, service, grants, worship and education.
- Use coercion and uneven implementation as limits.
- Treat Abu'l Fazl as the architect of normative imperial language.

Sulh-i-kul, or peace with all, transformed religious accommodation into a language of imperial justice. It arose from the practical need to govern a plural empire and from Akbar's conclusion that theological consensus could not be produced through public debate.

In Abu'l Fazl's formulation, the just ruler displayed paternal concern, protected public welfare and restrained sectarian conflict. The principle appeared in removal of pilgrim tax and jizya, wider elite service, redistribution of religious patronage, permission for diverse worship and courtly translation.

Its innovation lay in making loyalty and merit more important than confessional monopoly within a composite ruling order. It did not require religions to merge.

Its limits were equally important. The emperor continued to regulate religion, so sulh-i-kul was not modern secular separation. Access remained selective, society hierarchical and enforcement uneven. Chittor and severe punishment of rebellion demonstrate that accommodation coexisted with coercion.

Sulh-i-kul was therefore a powerful early-modern ethic of plural governance, but one vertically guaranteed by imperial authority rather than secured through impersonal rights.

**Scoring note:** Require definition, governing problem, Abu'l Fazl's ethical language, three policy mechanisms, distinction from syncretism/secularism and two limits.

### Verified PYQ linkage

Sulh-i-kul should form the governing-principle dimension of the 2025 answer. It is analytically distinct from the Ibadat Khana and Din-i Ilahi.

### The Maktab Khana: Translation as an Imperial Technology of Understanding

## Visual first: translation changed who could read whom

```text
Sanskrit / Arabic / other learned traditions
                    |

                    |
             MAKTAB KHANA
                    |
Persian translations, adaptations and illustrations
                    |
elite comparison and imperial knowledge
                    |
reduced ignorance, but not mass linguistic equality
```

Translation under Akbar was not a decorative cultural hobby. It allowed Persian-reading elites to encounter texts that had previously required different linguistic training, and it made the emperor a patron and organiser of comparative knowledge.

### 1. The translation bureau

Akbar established a translation bureau commonly called the **Maktab Khana**. Teams could include Sanskrit-knowing pandits, oral interpreters and Persian literati. Translation was therefore collaborative and mediated.

This matters because a Persian version is not a transparent copy. Choices about vocabulary, summary, adaptation and illustration shaped how a work entered Mughal court culture.

### 2. Major works

The source-based list includes:

| Work or field | Persianate translation/adaptation significance |
|---|---|
| *Mahabharata* | translated/adapted as the *Razmnama*, or Book of War |
| *Ramayana* | made available to Persian-reading court circles |
| *Bhagavad Gita* | part of engagement with Sanskrit religious thought |
| *Atharva Veda* | included in the reported religious translation programme |
| *Panchatantra* | ethical-political fable and advice tradition |
| *Singhasana Battisi* | legendary and didactic narrative |
| *Nal-Damayanti* | poetic and narrative encounter |
| Kalhana's *Rajatarangini* | historical writing on Kashmir |
| Bhaskaracharya's *Lilavati* | mathematical knowledge |
| Christian Gospels | Christian teaching made available in Persian |
| Qur'an | the concise account reports a Persian translation, qualified as perhaps the first |

Do not turn the list into a claim that every work had identical translators, dates, method or imperial purpose.

### 3. Why translate?

Abu'l Fazl's preface to the *Razmnama* links translation to breaking the "pillars of blind following" and encouraging inquiry. The phrase reflects a court ideology that ignorance between learned communities strengthened prejudice.

Translation performed several functions:

1. **epistemic:** made other traditions intelligible;
2. **political:** displayed the emperor as arbiter and patron of knowledge;
3. **cultural:** created Persianate versions of Indic narratives;
4. **administrative-elite:** gave a heterogeneous court shared reference points;
5. **artistic:** generated illustrated manuscript production.

### 4. Translation is not fusion

Reading or translating a text does not prove acceptance of all its doctrines. Nor does Persian translation erase the source language.

```text
access -> comparison -> reinterpretation

not necessarily

conversion -> doctrinal merger -> social equality
```

The programme was mainly courtly. Most peasants and artisans did not gain direct access to expensive Persian manuscripts. Its social reach was therefore narrower than its intellectual importance.

### 5. Translation and power

The emperor decided which works deserved resources. Court teams selected passages and terminology. Translation could honour a tradition while placing it within an imperial Persian framework.

This produces a double interpretation:

| Integrative value | Power dimension |
|---|---|
| reduces learned ignorance | court selects and frames knowledge |
| enables comparison | Persian becomes language of authoritative circulation |
| supports cultural exchange | elite mediation filters voices |

Both sides are necessary. Calling translation either pure domination or pure harmony loses the historical mechanism.

### 6. Relation to sulh-i-kul

Sulh-i-kul did not require subjects to agree doctrinally. Translation made disagreement more informed. It helped the court claim that just rule should arise from inquiry rather than inherited prejudice.

## Visual first: encounter does not equal adoption

```text
named teacher or mission
          |
court conversation / petition / text / ritual observation
          |
Akbar shows interest or grants support
          |
possible influence within a wider field of ideas
          |
NO automatic proof of conversion, exclusive origin or empire-wide enforcement
```

The weakest answer says Akbar "took" one feature from each religion and mixed them. The stronger answer asks what each encounter securely establishes and which later claim goes beyond the evidence.

### 1. Hindu and Brahman interlocutors

**Purushottam** and **Devi** are associated with explanations of Hindu doctrines in private discussions. Pandits also participated in translation.

Secure inference:

- Akbar sought learned explanations of Sanskritic and Hindu traditions;
- court inquiry continued outside the public Ibadat Khana;
- translation required cross-linguistic collaboration.

Unsafe inference:

- two individuals represented one uniform Hinduism;
- Akbar accepted every doctrine explained;
- the encounter created equal social status across caste or region.

### 2. Jain contact

The Jain teacher **Hiravijaya Suri** of Gujarat spent time at Akbar's court. Jain influence is often connected with concern for animal life and restrictions on slaughter on selected days.

The safe causal language is **association and bounded influence**. Vegetarian restraint and non-killing also had wider Indian, Sufi, ethical and personal contexts. Court orders could vary by day, place and circumstance; they do not prove permanent empire-wide vegetarianism.

### 3. Jesuit missions

Akbar requested learned Christian missionaries from Goa. The first mission included **Rudolf Aquaviva** and **Antonio Monserrate**, who remained at court for an extended period and left valuable accounts. Christian images and doctrines attracted interest; Prince Salim attended some instruction.

Missionary accounts are indispensable but purpose-shaped:

```text
Akbar's courtesy and curiosity
            |
missionary hope of conversion
            |
reports may read openness as movement toward Christianity
```

No conversion followed. Christian encounter should be used as evidence of inquiry, translation and patronage, not of a Christian turn in state policy.

### 4. Zoroastrian or Parsi contact

**Meherji Rana** is associated with explaining Zoroastrian teachings. Fire and light symbolism became visible in Akbar's courtly and personal practice.

Yet respect for light existed in Islamic philosophical and mystical traditions as well as Iranian and Indic settings. It is therefore unsafe to assign every solar or fire practice to one exclusive Zoroastrian source.

### 5. Sufi and Islamic continuities

Akbar's later thought retained central Islamic and Sufi concepts:

- **tawhid**, divine unity;
- meditation and approach to God;
- criticism of **taqlid**, unreasoning imitation;
- guide-disciple language;
- the possibility of a spiritually illuminated ruler;
- concern with reconciling reason and faith.

This continuity prevents the claim that interfaith encounter amounted simply to departure from Islam.

### 6. Bhakti-Sufi environment

Kabir, Nanak and other devotional currents had widened public languages of love, devotion and criticism of empty ritual. Chishti traditions had developed service, spiritual guidance and broad social contact.

The connection is atmospheric and comparative. It is not evidence that Akbar copied one Bhakti saint, that Bhakti and Sufism were identical, or that popular devotional equality became imperial social equality.

### 7. The influence test

For every claimed influence, ask:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| contact | Did the person or text securely reach the court? |
| chronology | Did contact precede the alleged practice? |
| exclusivity | Could the idea have other genealogies? |
| adoption | Is there evidence Akbar accepted rather than merely discussed it? |
| reach | Was it personal, courtly, administrative or empire-wide? |

This five-part test prevents both denial of exchange and exaggerated syncretism.

### UPSC application

- Name Purushottam/Devi, Hiravijaya Suri, Aquaviva/Monserrate and Meherji Rana with bounded propositions.
- Never place all named figures in one unverified meeting.
- Explain missionary and courtly source incentives.
- Use "associated with" rather than "caused" when origins overlap.
- Retain Islamic-Sufi continuities alongside cross-religious encounter.

## Lesson 9

### Tauhid-i-Ilahi and Din-i Ilahi: Between Monotheism, Ethics and a Small Circle

Progress: 9/12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: terminology, institutional scale, practices, membership and the new-religion controversy

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - Satish Chandra Part II PDF pp. 158-163 and *History of Medieval India* PDF pp. 267-268
CA search: "not run - modern organised-religion categories must not be imposed on a fluid court fellowship"
CA found: none required
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: test the "new religion" claim institution by institution

```text
Was there a separate scripture?          no
Was there a priesthood?                  no
Was there a public congregation?         no
Was there a mass conversion programme?   no
Was there selective initiation?          yes
Were ethical and ritual practices noted? yes
Was loyalty to Akbar involved?           yes

Verdict: restricted spiritual-political discipleship,
not a mass organised church and not "nothing at all"
```

The strongest answer avoids two opposite errors. Din-i Ilahi was not a new mass religion imposed on India. But the court circle had enough language, initiation and conduct that it cannot be dismissed as a label without practices.

### 1. Terminology is evidence

Abu'l Fazl primarily used **Tauhid-i-Ilahi**, meaning "Divine Monotheism." Badauni used both Tauhid-i-Ilahi and **Din-i Ilahi**, or "Divine Faith."

Why does this matter? "Din-i Ilahi" sounds like a complete new religion. "Tauhid-i-Ilahi" foregrounds divine unity and a path of spiritual orientation. Later repetition of the former label can make a fluid court development look more formally established than contemporary institutional evidence supports.

Satish Chandra links Akbar's uncompromising monotheism largely to the Islamic philosophical-mystical influence of **Ibn-i-Arabi**. This supports an Islamic-Sufi genealogy for *tawhid*, meditation and the possibility of communion with God; it does not make one thinker the exclusive source of Akbar's evolving ideas.

### 2. No secure proclamation of a mass faith

A later priest, Bartoli, claimed that a council in 1582 announced a new imperial religion. No corresponding empire-wide order has been found. The first source cited by Satish Chandra for a set of ten virtues is the later *Dabistan-i-Mazahib*, written in the Shah Jahan period.

Therefore **1582** should mark the late debate/disciple phase, not the certain foundation date of a church.

### 3. The institutional test

Tauhid-i-Ilahi had:

- no separate sacred book;
- no independent priesthood;
- no exclusive place of public worship;
- no regular mass congregation;
- no systematic conversion campaign;
- no evidence of coercive empire-wide enrolment.

These absences make "mass religion" untenable.

### 4. Yet practices existed

Reported practices included:

- selective admission approved by Akbar;
- Sunday initiation;
- placing one's head or turban at the emperor's feet as a renunciation of conceit;
- receiving a **shast**, a token bearing the name of God and Akbar's favoured formula;
- greetings such as **Allah-o-Akbar** and **Jall Jalaluhu**;
- restraint from meat during specified periods;
- birthday feasts and alms;
- ethical emphasis on generosity, self-control and yearning for God in later accounts.

The exact reach and consistency of each practice are source-dependent. A list of reported observances is not proof of a complete theology.

### 5. Membership and the number problem

Blochmann reconstructed about **18 nobles** in the highest degrees of devotion; **Birbal** was the only Hindu in that list. This is not a census of everyone described as Akbar's murid or disciple.

The distinction is crucial:

```text
small group in highest devotional degrees
                 ≠
all court disciples
                 ≠
population following a new religion
```

The number eighteen should therefore be attached to the reconstruction and category, not presented as an exact total membership of Din-i Ilahi.

### 6. The four degrees of devotion

Badauni describes readiness to sacrifice:

1. property;
2. life;
3. honour;
4. religion.

"Sacrifice of religion" is best understood in the source-based interpretation as abandoning narrow inherited or imitative attachment that obstructed sulh-i-kul, not as compulsory conversion to a new mass creed.

Similar total-devotion language existed in Sufi guide-disciple relations. The analogy helps explain form, but does not make the court circle identical to a Sufi silsila.

### 7. Man Singh and the limit of coercion

A reported exchange says that Man Singh distinguished political willingness to sacrifice his life from a change of faith: he was Hindu and knew Islam, but not a third religion. Akbar did not press the matter further.

The episode is valuable because it shows ambiguity. Court actors could read discipleship as religious even if Akbar or Abu'l Fazl framed it differently. It also weighs against a programme of forced conversion.

### 8. What was it, then?

Four interpretations capture part of the phenomenon:

| Interpretation | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| new religion | explains hostile perceptions of ritual novelty | unsupported by institutions and mass reach |
| Sufi-style ethical order | fits monotheism, guide-disciple language and initiation | court sovereignty makes it unlike an ordinary silsila |
| political loyalty device | fits elite selection and heterogenous nobility | cannot erase Akbar's genuine spiritual concerns |
| personal spiritual path | respects inquiry and ethics | understates its organised court and sovereign dimensions |

The best verdict combines the middle three: **a restricted ethical-spiritual discipleship with political-integrative purpose centred on Akbar**.

**Model answer:**
The label Din-i Ilahi, used by Badauni and popularised in later writing, suggests a formally founded religion. Abu'l Fazl more often used Tauhid-i-Ilahi, or Divine Monotheism. No surviving empire-wide proclamation, separate scripture, priesthood, public congregation or conversion programme supports the mass-religion thesis.

Yet a restricted court practice existed. Sources describe selective initiation, a shast, devotional greetings, ethical restraints, almsgiving and four degrees of willingness to sacrifice property, life, honour and religion. Blochmann reconstructed only about eighteen nobles in the highest degrees, with Birbal the sole Hindu in that list; this is not a total census of imperial disciples.

Satish Chandra links the monotheistic emphasis largely to Ibn-i-Arabi. Sufi guide-disciple forms help explain initiation and discipline, while the political setting explains elite loyalty. The Man Singh episode also shows that contemporaries could perceive religious ambiguity and that enrolment was not simply coerced.

Tauhid-i-Ilahi is therefore best understood as a small, emperor-centred ethical-spiritual discipleship with an integrative political function. Calling it a universal church exaggerates its institutions; denying all practices ignores the evidence.

**Scoring note:** Require terminology, institutional absences, four reported practices, qualified membership figure, Sufi analogy, political purpose, Man Singh or coercion limit and a two-sided verdict.

### Verified PYQ linkage

For the 2025 direct PYQ, Din-i Ilahi belongs at the end of the chronology as a limited court experiment. It must not dominate the answer or be described as Akbar's principal mass achievement.

### Spiritual Kingship and Political Discipleship

## Visual first: the ruler as more than commander

```text
military conquest and appointment
              +
justice and public welfare
              +
divine illumination (farr-i izadi)
              +
spiritual guidance and discipleship
              |
              v
morally charged sovereignty
              |
elite integration, but also personality-centred power
```

Akbar did not merely ask subjects to obey force. Court ideology represented the just emperor as unusually capable of seeing beyond sectarian prejudice and guiding others toward ethical conduct.

### 1. Farr-i izadi

**Farr-i izadi** means divine radiance or illumination associated with rightful sovereignty. In Abu'l Fazl's political theory, the true ruler receives a special moral-intellectual capacity to govern.

This did not make Akbar a prophet. It gave monarchy sacred charisma:

- the ruler could claim insight above faction;
- justice became evidence of divine favour;
- loyalty to the emperor acquired ethical meaning;
- court hierarchy appeared part of a moral order.

As a court theory, it proves how sovereignty was legitimised. It does not prove universal belief in the claim.

### 2. Reason and taqlid

Akbar's later language criticised **taqlid**, blind or unreasoned imitation. He sought a faith flexible enough to consider political necessity and appeal to reason.

This did not mean modern rationalism against all religion. Reason operated within monotheistic, ethical and mystical concerns. The issue was whether inherited authority should remain immune from inquiry.

### 3. Pir-murid analogy

In Sufi traditions, a **pir** or spiritual guide directs a **murid**, or disciple. Akbarian discipleship borrowed intelligibility from this relationship:

```text
guide offers ethical-spiritual direction
disciple submits pride and practises loyalty
                |
court version joins spiritual form to imperial hierarchy
```

The analogy has a limit. A sovereign controlled office, rank, revenue and punishment in ways an ordinary Sufi guide did not. Political dependence could therefore intensify devotional submission.

### 4. Rituals of loyalty

Reported practices included prostration or **paibos**, placing the head at the emperor's feet, greetings and initiation tokens. Different rituals and terms require careful chronology and source control; not every practice was unique to Tauhid-i-Ilahi.

Ritual performed political work:

- it made hierarchy visible;
- it tested submission;
- it joined nobles of different origins to one imperial centre;
- it represented ego-renunciation as loyal service.

### 5. Rebellion and the loyalty problem

The late 1570s and early 1580s saw eastern rebellion, clerical fatwas, Mirza Hakim's challenge and wider frontier uncertainty. A heterogeneous nobility needed bonds stronger than temporary factional advantage.

S.A.A. Rizvi and J.F. Richards, as discussed by Satish Chandra, interpret the degrees of devotion and discipleship as ideological means of unifying elites around the throne.

Political timing strengthens this interpretation, but it should not erase Akbar's spiritual interest.

### 6. The autocracy objection

The strongest criticism is that Akbar used spiritual language to sacralise obedience:

```text
ruler defines reason and welfare
        +
ruler selects juristic opinion
        +
ruler becomes spiritual guide
        =
risk of personal autocracy
```

Some sources even report miraculous-healing claims associated with water breathed upon by Akbar. Satish Chandra notes the contradiction between Akbar's stated scepticism toward miracles and his willingness to use popular credulity.

### 7. The defence and residual problem

The defence is contextual. A conquest empire divided by lineage, region and confession needed a loyalty idiom wider than one sect. Spiritual kingship could support justice and non-sectarian service.

The residual problem remains: if plural order depends on the emperor's personal virtue and charisma, it lacks protection when the ruler changes.

### UPSC application

- Define farr-i izadi as courtly divine radiance, not prophecy.
- Use pir-murid as a limited analogy.
- Connect ritual to elite integration and visible hierarchy.
- Place discipleship in the rebellion and heterogeneous-nobility context.
- Balance integrative function against personality cult and autocracy.

### Social and Religious Measures: Prescription, Practice and Uneven Reach

## Visual first: an imperial order passes through many filters

```text
imperial ethical or religious prescription
                  |
official transmission and local interpretation
                  |
regional custom + elite interest + household power
                  |
selective enforcement, evasion or adaptation
                  |
historian can prove an order more easily than a uniform social outcome
```

Akbar's measures reveal the aspirations of the state. They do not automatically measure what happened in every household, village or province.

### 1. Reform of religious grants

Revenue-free grants known as **madad-i-maash** had been supervised by the sadr. Akbar investigated large grants, reduced the sadr's monopoly, appointed provincial sadrs and introduced scrutiny.

After 1575 and especially after 1580, Hindu, Jain and Parsi recipients increasingly received grants; Jesuits received support for churches. The shift widened state patronage beyond an overwhelmingly Muslim beneficiary field.

This was both religious policy and fiscal administration:

- it limited an autonomous clerical patronage network;
- widened access;
- made recipients more dependent on imperial review;
- could bring uncultivated land into use.

### 2. Dharmapura, Khairpura and Jogipura

Akbar established:

- **Dharmapura** for feeding poor Hindus;
- **Khairpura** for poor Muslims;
- **Jogipura** when yogis arrived in significant numbers.

These establishments demonstrate court charity organised across social-religious categories. They were not universal poor relief or rights enforceable throughout the empire.

### 3. Worship and reconversion

Source-based policy permitted a person forcibly converted to Islam to return to the earlier religion. It also reports no general prohibition on building Hindu temples, Christian churches or Jewish synagogues.

The safe claim is a permissive imperial policy. Do not infer that:

- every local conflict disappeared;
- all places of worship received equal patronage;
- religious freedom operated through modern legal rights;
- hostile reports of universal mosque closure are reliable.

### 4. Animal slaughter and cow protection

Akbar restricted animal slaughter on selected days and is associated with forbidding cow slaughter. Jain contact may have influenced concern for non-killing, but the measure had overlapping ethical, political and devotional contexts.

The evidence does not justify permanent empire-wide vegetarianism. Specify the difference between selected restrictions, court practice and uniform social enforcement.

### 5. Sati, widow remarriage and marriage regulation

The detailed source describes:

- restraint on sati unless the widow consented;
- permission or support for widow remarriage;
- proposed minimum marriage ages of sixteen for boys and fourteen for girls;
- restrictions on near-relative marriage;
- parental consent and marriage registration;
- a preference against multiple wives except in specified circumstances.

These measures are important as moral-political prescriptions. Satish Chandra explicitly cautions that their practical reach is uncertain. Gendered household authority and social custom could obstruct enforcement.

### 6. Slavery and trafficking

Akbar restricted sale and purchase of slaves and is credited with prohibitory measures. Yet the source notes little effect on royal and noble households. Earlier protection for war captives also did not eliminate slavery.

The same reform catalogue permitted wine when medically recommended for strengthening the body, regulated prostitution and sought to restrain trafficking of women. These are evidence of courtly moral regulation, not proof that drinking, prostitution or trafficking disappeared in practice.

The distinction is:

```text
normative restriction
        ≠
institutional disappearance
```

### 7. Education and reason

Akbar's curriculum reforms placed greater emphasis on:

- moral education;
- mathematics;
- agriculture;
- geometry;
- astronomy;
- government;
- logic;
- history.

Badauni saw this as discrimination against Arabic and traditional religious learning. The conflict illustrates how curricular change could appear as rational broadening to one observer and erosion of religious authority to another.

### 8. The implementation ladder

Use four levels:

| Level | What can be claimed |
|---|---|
| order | the emperor or court prescribed a measure |
| institution | an office, grant or establishment embodied it |
| recorded case | a specific recipient or action is known |
| social outcome | requires evidence of repeated local practice |

Never jump from the first level to the fourth.

### 9. Final social verdict

Akbar's state was comparatively liberal in religious policy and promoted forms of cultural integration. It still governed a deeply hierarchical society. Caste, patriarchy, local power, slavery and economic inequality did not dissolve through imperial ethical language.

### UPSC application

- Pair each social measure with an implementation caution.
- Use grant reform as both patronage inclusion and centralisation.
- Distinguish permission for worship from uniform local freedom.
- Treat slaughter restrictions as bounded by day, place and evidence.
- Never call regulation of sati or slavery their complete abolition.

### Historiography, Source Conflict and the Limits of Akbarian Pluralism

## Visual first: disagreement among sources is itself historical evidence

```text
Abu'l Fazl: enlightened, just, divinely favoured sovereign
                         |
Badauni: alarming novelty, apostasy and clerical displacement
                         |
Jesuits: hopeful encounter interpreted through conversion
                         |
orders and institutions: specific policy, uncertain reach
                         |
modern historians: new religion? political device? plural polity?
                         v
qualified reconstruction rather than source loyalty
```

The final lesson asks not "Which source tells the truth?" but "Which claim can each source establish, and what must be checked elsewhere?"

### 1. Abu'l Fazl

Abu'l Fazl's *Akbarnama* and *Ain-i Akbari* are indispensable for:

- chronology and imperial institutions;
- the language of sulh-i-kul;
- farr-i izadi and ethical kingship;
- court inquiry and discipleship;
- imperial explanations of policy.

His limitation is not simple dishonesty. As a close servant and intellectual architect, he arranged evidence to make Akbar's sovereignty appear rational, just and spiritually elevated.

### 2. Badauni

Badauni's *Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh* supplies:

- internal dissent;
- hostility to religious innovation;
- anxieties about ulama, ritual and education;
- details that a panegyrist might minimise.

His opposition is historically valuable because it reveals perceived threat. But dramatic charges of apostasy, universal religious decline or copied ritual require corroboration.

### 3. Jesuit and encounter sources

Monserrate and other Jesuit writers observed court life and Christian discussion. Their mission sought conversion. Akbar's hospitality, use of Christian images or request for explanation could therefore be read as progress toward baptism.

The accounts are strongest for presence, conversation and missionary expectation; weakest when courtesy becomes proof of conversion.

### 4. Orders, grants and material setting

An order can prove that a ruler prescribed something. A grant can prove a named beneficiary. Fatehpur Sikri demonstrates the planned court environment. None alone proves empire-wide social acceptance.

The exact physical identification of the Ibadat Khana remains uncertain. Textual location near Anup Talao and the palace should not be turned into an unqualified archaeological label.

### 5. Major interpretations

| Interpretation | Contribution | Problem if used alone |
|---|---|---|
| Vincent Smith: "monument of Akbar's folly" | highlights limited survival of Din-i Ilahi | assumes a failed organised religion |
| S.A.A. Rizvi | sees Mahzar and devotion as ideological elements of a new polity | political reading may underplay private spirituality |
| J.F. Richards | discipleship integrated a heterogeneous nobility | elite mechanism does not explain every belief |
| Satish Chandra | stresses liberal composite polity and political device, with criticism of religio-political confusion | "liberal" must remain early-modern and hierarchical |
| communal reading | notices real orthodox opposition | turns conflict into timeless Hindu-Muslim binaries |
| hagiographic reading | recognises unusual accommodation | erases conquest, hierarchy and implementation failure |

### 6. A balanced interpretation

Akbar's policy combined:

- genuine spiritual inquiry;
- removal of selected religious disabilities;
- political incorporation of diverse elites;
- reduction of clerical monopoly;
- translation and comparative knowledge;
- non-sectarian imperial justice;
- restricted discipleship and charismatic kingship.

Its limits included:

- coercive conquest and punishment;
- dependence on imperial personality;
- elite and courtly reach;
- uncertain local enforcement;
- social hierarchy;
- spiritualisation of political submission;
- contradictory and partisan evidence.

### 7. Jahangir as a bounded transition

Jahangir broadly continued the composite nobility and much of Akbar's accommodation. Imperial murid or banda language survived among nobles. Satish Chandra states that Jahangir gave up formal distribution of the shast and enrolment of disciples, although the concise account describes some continuation for a time.

The safe transition is:

> The organised fellowship did not survive as a religion; courtly discipleship language and the broader political inheritance continued in altered form.

Detailed Jahangir policy, Guru Arjan, Mewar and later religious choices belong to Topic 20.

### 8. Avoid the Akbar-Aurangzeb morality play

Aurangzeb's reimposition of jizya in 1679 is a valid contrast, but not a sufficient explanation of either reign. Different wars, factions, regional pressures and political choices require their own chronology.

Use comparison to show that Mughal religious policy was contingent, not to construct timeless heroes and villains.

### 9. Final verdict

Akbar did not create secular democracy or a universal religion. He did something historically significant within monarchy: he weakened confessional monopoly over imperial belonging, made comparative inquiry part of court culture and articulated a ruler-centred ethic for governing plurality.

The achievement was real; so were its limits. Sulh-i-kul had wider political life than the Ibadat Khana or Tauhid-i-Ilahi because it did not require common theology or initiation. Yet it depended heavily on the sovereign's judgment.

- Present at least two historiographical interpretations.
- Keep Jahangir and Aurangzeb bounded.
- End with an innovative-but-ruler-centred verdict.

Akbar's religious policy is known through sources embedded in court and missionary conflict. Abu'l Fazl presents the emperor as rational, just and divinely illuminated. His works are indispensable for institutions, sulh-i-kul and kingship, but also legitimise sovereign power.

Badauni preserves dissent, anxiety over ulama displacement and allegations of ritual novelty. His hostility reveals contemporary opposition, yet cannot make every charge of apostasy a settled fact. Jesuit accounts verify missions and Christian encounter, but their expectation of conversion encouraged them to misread courtesy as theological movement.

Orders, grants, translations and court institutions can corroborate specific acts, though prescription does not prove uniform implementation. Even the textual location of the Ibadat Khana does not settle one archaeological identification.

Modern interpretations consequently diverge. Vincent Smith treated Din-i Ilahi as failed folly; Rizvi and Richards emphasise sovereignty and elite integration; Satish Chandra stresses a composite polity while recognising religio-political ambiguity.

A defensible history triangulates these sources. Akbar's policy was neither apostasy nor modern secularism, but a significant, hierarchical and ruler-centred attempt to govern plurality.

**Scoring note:** Require Abu'l Fazl, Badauni and Jesuit positions, one institutional corroboration limit, three modern interpretations and a triangulated final verdict.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish the Ibadat Khana, Mahzar and sulh-i-kul in Akbar's religious policy.

**Model answer:**
The Ibadat Khana was a debate institution founded at Fatehpur Sikri in 1575. It first hosted Muslim scholars and mystics and widened after 1578. Its public discussions exposed doctrinal conflict but failed to create consensus.

The Mahzar of 1579 addressed a different problem: final authority when qualified Muslim jurists disagreed. It allowed Akbar to select an interpretation for public welfare within stated scriptural limits. It was bounded imperial arbitration, not universal infallibility.

Sulh-i-kul was the broader governing ethic of peace and justice across creeds. It informed service, patronage and protection without requiring theological merger.

Thus the Ibadat Khana was a forum, the Mahzar a sovereignty mechanism and sulh-i-kul a principle of plural government. Confusing them turns a chronology of institutional development into one vague claim of tolerance.

**Scoring note:** Require three separate definitions, dates for the first two, distinct functions and one sentence explaining their chronological connection.

## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Critically examine whether Tauhid-i-Ilahi was principally a political device.

**Model answer:**
Tauhid-i-Ilahi, rather than the later-sounding Din-i Ilahi, was Abu'l Fazl's principal term for Akbar's Divine Monotheism. Its political character is visible in selective elite initiation and the four degrees of devotion involving property, life, honour and religion. In a period of rebellion, clerical opposition and a heterogeneous nobility, discipleship could bind nobles personally to the emperor. Rizvi and Richards consequently stress its integrative function.

It lacked a separate scripture, priesthood, public congregation and mass conversion programme. Blochmann's list of about eighteen nobles concerns the highest devotional degrees, not an empire-wide membership census. This supports the interpretation of a small court fellowship rather than a popular religion.

However, "political device" is insufficient if it implies insincerity. Akbar's monotheism, which Satish Chandra links largely to Ibn-i-Arabi, meditation, criticism of taqlid, ethical conduct and long religious inquiry indicate genuine spiritual concerns. Sufi pir-murid forms helped shape the relationship.

Tauhid-i-Ilahi was therefore a restricted ethical-spiritual discipleship whose political effect was to sacralise loyalty and integrate selected elites. Its spiritual content and political purpose were mutually reinforcing, while its tiny scale and dependence on Akbar limited survival.

**Scoring note:** Require terminology, political timing, devotional degrees, institutional absences, qualified membership, spiritual evidence, historiographical view and a combined verdict.

## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Akbar's religious policy created an inclusive imperial ethic without creating an equal or non-coercive state. Discuss.

**Model answer:**
Akbar's policy evolved from practical accommodation to an explicit theory of plural rule. Pilgrim tax was remitted in 1563 and jizya abolished in 1564. Rajput and Hindu participation widened the service elite, though conquest and ranked dependence continued.

The Ibadat Khana, founded in 1575 and widened after 1578, exposed both comparative religious knowledge and the failure of public theological consensus. The Mahzar of 1579 gave Akbar bounded authority to select among conflicting juristic opinions for welfare and order. Sulh-i-kul then framed justice, patronage and service across confessional lines.

Translations such as the *Razmnama* widened Persianate intellectual exchange. Hindu, Jain, Jesuit and Zoroastrian encounters informed inquiry without producing one merged creed. Tauhid-i-Ilahi remained a small ethical-political discipleship rather than a mass religion.

The limits were substantial. Akbar continued to regulate religion; Chittor and rebellion punishment reveal coercion; grants and reform orders were unevenly implemented; patriarchy, caste and slavery persisted. Spiritual kingship could convert plural ethics into personal autocracy.

Akbar's achievement was therefore an innovative, composite and non-sectarian imperial vocabulary. It widened belonging within monarchy, but did not create modern secular equality or institutions independent of the ruler.

**Scoring note:** Require chronological evolution, four mechanisms, encounter/translation, Tauhid qualification, coercion and social limits, autocratic risk and a graded early-modern verdict.

# REMEDIATION

## Misconception 1: Akbar's religious policy began in 1582

**Correction:** Pilgrim tax was remitted in 1563 and jizya abolished in 1564. The Ibadat Khana began in 1575, widened after 1578 and ended in 1581-1582. The later discipleship phase cannot absorb the earlier chronology.

## Misconception 2: Personal belief proves public implementation

**Correction:** Prayer, shrine visits or interest in another doctrine concern personal inquiry. A farman, grant, office change or tax abolition is different evidence. Each needs its own scale.

## Misconception 3: Ibadat Khana was interfaith from the start

**Correction:** The initial phase involved Muslim Sufis, ulama, learned men and selected companions. Wider participation followed after 1578.

## Misconception 4: Debate created religious harmony

**Correction:** Public contest increased bitterness and closed. Its indirect contribution was to expose the need for governing disagreement.

## Misconception 5: Mahzar made Akbar infallible

**Correction:** It allowed selection among conflicting qualified opinions for welfare and order, within stated scriptural limits. Its effect enhanced sovereignty, but the text did not declare universal correctness or prophecy.

## Misconception 6: Sulh-i-kul meant religions became identical

**Correction:** Sulh-i-kul governed coexistence without requiring doctrinal merger.

## Misconception 7: Sulh-i-kul was modern secularism

**Correction:** The emperor continued to regulate grants, law, ritual and worship. It was a ruler-centred ethic in a hierarchical monarchy.

## Misconception 8: Translation equals conversion

**Correction:** Translation enabled access and comparison. It did not prove acceptance of every doctrine or mass social fusion.

## Misconception 9: One interlocutor represented an entire religion

**Correction:** Named teachers and missionaries establish specific encounters, not equal or exhaustive representation.

## Misconception 10: Every solar or fire practice came from Zoroastrianism

**Correction:** Light symbolism had Islamic, Iranian and Indic genealogies. Contact supports possible influence, not exclusive origin.

## Misconception 11: Din-i Ilahi was a mass church

**Correction:** No separate scripture, clergy, congregation or conversion programme is established.

## Misconception 12: Nothing existed under the Din-i Ilahi label

**Correction:** Selective initiation, the shast, greetings, ethical conduct and devotional degrees are reported.

## Misconception 13: Eighteen was the exact total membership

**Correction:** Blochmann's approximate figure concerns nobles in the highest degrees, not every imperial disciple.

## Misconception 14: Social orders transformed all society

**Correction:** Orders reveal prescription. Uniform outcomes require local evidence, which is often unavailable.

## Misconception 15: Abu'l Fazl is propaganda and Badauni is truth

**Correction:** Both are interested witnesses. Their opposed purposes must be triangulated with institutions, chronology and other testimony.

## Misconception 16: Akbar and Aurangzeb form a complete tolerance-bigotry binary

**Correction:** Comparison requires separate political, regional and chronological analysis. One policy contrast cannot explain either reign.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Chronology

- 1563: pilgrim tax remitted.
- 1564: jizya abolished.
- 1575: Ibadat Khana built at Fatehpur Sikri.
- After 1578: debates widened beyond Muslim participants.
- 1579: Mahzar issued and signed by leading ulama.
- 1580: eastern rebellion and supporting fatwas sharpen conflict.
- 1581: public Ibadat Khana debates practically decline.
- 1582: debates finally discontinued; late discipleship phase associated with this period.
- 1580s-1605: sulh-i-kul, translation and selective Tauhid-i-Ilahi practices develop.
- 1605: Akbar dies; broader accommodation continues in altered form under Jahangir.

## Method

- Separate personal belief, public policy, juristic sovereignty, encounter and discipleship.
- Do not narrate the reign backward from Din-i Ilahi.
- Contact does not equal adoption.
- Court adoption does not equal empire-wide change.
- Orders prove prescription more readily than implementation.
- Contemporary sources are positioned witnesses, not transparent records.

## Early accommodation

- Pilgrim tax burdened sacred travel; its removal had symbolic significance.
- Jizya was a poll tax differentiating non-Muslims.
- Abolition linked loyal service to wider imperial belonging.
- Rajput and Hindu high service made inclusion politically concrete.
- Chittor and Mewar qualify a pure-tolerance account.
- Akbar's Islamic observance and Chishti devotion continued in the early phase.
- Restraints on forced conversion or enslavement did not abolish slavery.

## Ibadat Khana

- Founded in 1575 at Fatehpur Sikri.
- Textually located near the palace and Anup Talao; exact archaeological identification uncertain.
- Thursday-night discussions initially involved Muslim Sufis, ulama, learned men and selected companions.
- Muslim participants were internally diverse.
- Doctrine, legal interpretation, patronage and status converged.
- Abu'l Fazl's disorder narrative is informative and polemical.
- Widening after 1578 included Hindu, Jain, Christian, Zoroastrian and other voices.
- Public debate increased bitterness rather than producing agreement.
- Practical decline occurred in 1581; final closure in 1582.
- Private discussions and translations continued.

## Mahzar

- Issued in 1579 as an attested statement.
- Leading signatories included Abdun Nabi, Abdullah Sultanpuri and Shaikh Mubarak.
- Applied when qualified jurists disagreed.
- Allowed Akbar to select an interpretation for welfare and administrative order.
- Royal orders remained formally bounded by explicit Quranic and Hadis commands.
- It did not declare prophecy or universal infallibility.
- The Mathura Brahman case exposed disagreement over punishment.
- Sadr patronage, grants and rebellion formed the political context.
- The text was bounded, but final selection strengthened ruler-centred sovereignty.
- Its justice-and-peace language had international and imperial significance.

## Sulh-i-kul

- Means universal peace or peace with all.
- Governed disagreement rather than merging doctrines.
- Joined justice, welfare and restraint of sectarian conflict.
- Abu'l Fazl connected it to paternal kingship and farr-i izadi.
- Appeared through fiscal accommodation, composite service, patronage and worship policy.
- Did not withdraw the state from religion.
- Was not modern constitutional secularism.
- Remained hierarchical, coercive and ruler-dependent.
- Had wider political durability than the Ibadat Khana or small discipleship circle.

## Translation and encounter

- Maktab Khana coordinated collaborative translation.
- *Mahabharata* became the *Razmnama*.
- Other texts included *Ramayana*, *Bhagavad Gita*, *Atharva Veda*, *Panchatantra*, *Singhasana Battisi*, *Nal-Damayanti*, *Rajatarangini*, *Lilavati* and Christian Gospels.
- The concise account also reports a Persian Qur'an, cautiously described there as perhaps the first such translation.
- Translation challenged learned ignorance but remained elite and mediated.
- Purushottam and Devi explained Hindu doctrines.
- Hiravijaya Suri represents Jain contact and bounded non-killing influence.
- Aquaviva and Monserrate represent Jesuit encounter and missionary expectation.
- Meherji Rana represents Zoroastrian instruction.
- Light and fire symbolism had multiple genealogies.
- Islamic-Sufi continuities included tawhid, meditation, reason and pir-murid language.

## Tauhid-i-Ilahi / Din-i Ilahi

- Abu'l Fazl mainly used Tauhid-i-Ilahi; Badauni used both terms.
- Satish Chandra links Akbar's uncompromising monotheism largely to Ibn-i-Arabi, without making that influence exclusive.
- No secure empire-wide founding decree survives.
- No separate scripture, priesthood, church or public congregation existed.
- No mass conversion programme is established.
- Selective initiation and ethical conduct did exist.
- Sunday initiation, the shast and devotional greetings are reported.
- Four degrees involved property, life, honour and religion.
- Blochmann's roughly eighteen nobles concern the highest degrees, not total membership.
- Birbal was the sole Hindu in that reconstructed list.
- Man Singh's reported response demonstrates ambiguity and lack of straightforward compulsion.
- Best verdict: small spiritual-political discipleship with elite-integrative purpose.

## Spiritual kingship

- Farr-i izadi means divine illumination associated with just sovereignty.
- It did not make Akbar a prophet.
- Criticism of taqlid elevated inquiry and reason within a religious frame.
- Pir-murid forms helped make discipleship intelligible.
- Court ritual made hierarchy and loyalty visible.
- Rebellion and a heterogeneous nobility sharpened the loyalty problem.
- Spiritual kingship could support plural order and personal autocracy simultaneously.

## Social measures and limits

- Madad-i-maash patronage was scrutinised and widened.
- Dharmapura, Khairpura and Jogipura were court charity establishments.
- Reconversion and diverse worship received broad permission in policy.
- Animal-slaughter restrictions were selected, not proof of universal vegetarianism.
- Sati, widow remarriage, marriage age and registration measures were prescriptions with uncertain reach.
- Wine, prostitution and trafficking measures likewise show regulatory intent rather than demonstrated social elimination.
- Slavery restrictions had limited effect in elite households.
- Education widened moral and secular subjects.
- Caste, patriarchy, local power and hierarchy persisted.

## Historiography

- Abu'l Fazl: indispensable insider and imperial legitimiser.
- Badauni: indispensable dissenter and polemical opponent.
- Jesuits: valuable observers shaped by conversion hopes.
- Vincent Smith's folly thesis assumes a failed new religion.
- Rizvi and Richards emphasise sovereignty and elite integration.
- Satish Chandra emphasises composite polity while recognising religio-political confusion.
- Communal readings exaggerate apostasy and timeless conflict.
- Hagiographic readings erase coercion and unevenness.
- Jahangir broadly retained accommodation while formal discipleship changed.
- The final judgment is innovative plural statecraft within hierarchical personal monarchy.
