# The Khaljis: Alauddin Khalji & Market Reforms — Live Session Edition

This session asks one governing question: **how did a ruler with contested accession convert recurrent frontier danger and elite insecurity into an unusually coercive military-fiscal-market system—and why did that system prove both effective and fragile?** The answer requires chronology, territorial distinctions, institutional mechanisms, social incidence and source criticism rather than a list of ordinances.

## Roadmap

1. Khalji transition, chronology and the “revolution” problem
2. Jalaluddin Khalji: conciliation, limits and the 1296 rupture
3. Alauddin’s legitimacy problem and control of the nobility
4. Mongol pressure, Siri and frontier defence
5. Gujarat, Rajasthan and Malwa: different modes of expansion
6. Malik Kafur and the Deccan: raid, tribute, vassalage and annexation
7. Military-fiscal design: cavalry, cash salary, dagh and chehra
8. Agrarian measures: khalisa, measurement and rural power
9. Why market regulation followed from army finance
10. Grain market: assessment, transport, storage and rationing
11. Cloth, costly goods, horses, slaves and cattle
12. Effects, reach and limits of the market system
13. State, religion and medieval reason of state
14. Architecture, urbanism, culture and source memory
15. Historiographical debates and integrated policy assessment
16. Succession, reversal and the end of Khalji rule

The dependency is deliberate: political transition and accession precede frontier pressure; frontier pressure creates the cavalry problem; the cavalry problem leads to agrarian finance and market regulation; only after those mechanisms are clear can their social effects, ideological claims, source limits and dynastic fragility be assessed. Substantial coverage within a lesson is taught through internal Parts rather than by changing this sequence.

### Drawing the boundary of the Khalji story

#### Put the period on a clock before interpreting it

```text
1290: Khalji accession ── 1296: Alauddin seizes power ── 1316: Alauddin dies ── 1320: Tughlaq takeover
```

This is a bounded thirty-year political sequence. Ask what a Delhi-centred state could enforce, through which institutions, and how securely the sources let us know it.

#### Two tests for every claim

| Test | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Source criticism | Who reports it, how close was the writer, and what purpose shaped the account? |
| State-capacity analysis | Was it enforceable in the core, an incorporated province, or only through tribute and campaign reach? |

> **Opening line:** Read Alauddin through source criticism and state-capacity analysis within the 1290-1320 Khalji arc, not through anachronistic labels.

**Evidence anchor:** The Khalji arc begins with the **1290 accession** and ends with the **1320 Tughlaq takeover**.

**Historical inference:** The best framing is **source criticism plus state-capacity analysis**. Alauddin Khalji's regime was neither a welfare price-control state nor a perfectly centralised modern command economy. It was a coercive, Delhi-centred **military-fiscal experiment** responding to frontier danger, elite conspiracy and imperial ambition.

**Boundary:** The same caution applies to opposite caricatures. "First socialist", "anti-Hindu fanatic", "proto-modern dictator", "uniform national market regulator" and "Padmavati's conqueror" are all exam-unsafe simplifications.

#### Governing thesis

- [ANALYSIS] The Khalji phase combined **wider recruitment to the ruling class**, **harder coercive monarchy**, **major northern and Deccan expansion**, and the most famous **revenue-market-army linkage** of the Sultanate.
- [ANALYSIS] Its success depended on making Delhi provisioned, cavalry affordable and nobles politically isolated.
- [LIMIT] Its institutions were impressive, but not equally deep everywhere. The most intense evidence concerns **Delhi and the upper doab**, not every province.

#### From a date range to a defensible thesis

**Claim:** Alauddin Khalji's regime is best understood as a coercive, Delhi-centred military-fiscal experiment rather than a welfare state or a uniform empire-wide command economy. **Named evidence:** its defining institutions - the three regulated Delhi markets, the doab's half-produce revenue demand and the standing cavalry raised against repeated Mongol invasions between 1299 and 1305 - survive mainly through Ziauddin Barani's later, aristocratic chronicle. **Analysis:** because these institutions cluster around the army's and the capital's needs rather than around subject welfare, and because the main source carries its own moralising bias, the safest exam frame combines source criticism (weighing what Barani could know and why he wrote) with state-capacity analysis (asking what a Delhi-centred medieval state could actually enforce, and where). **Qualification:** this rules out both the "medieval welfare state" caricature and the "uniform totalitarian empire" caricature; the correct register is a bounded, evidence-led description of a coercive military-fiscal system, strongest in Delhi and the upper doab and weaker at the frontier of Khalji power.

### 1290 changed the ruling coalition, not the social order

#### Test “revolution” against change and continuity

| Changed after 1290 | Continued after 1290 |
|---|---|
| Wider access to high office for Khaljis, Indian Muslims and other service groups | Delhi-centred monarchy, iqta routines and Persianate court practice |
| End of an exclusive old-Turkish claim to the highest offices | Aristocratic hierarchy and coercive extraction |
| A new ruling coalition | No mass social emancipation |

The Khaljis were a Turkic-origin group long associated with Afghan regions; that layered history cannot be flattened into rigid modern ethnic boxes.

> **Judgement:** 1290 widened the ruling class without revolutionising the lives of cultivators, artisans or women.

**Inherited institutional base:** The Mamluks left behind Delhi, iqta routines, coinage, court idiom and a frontier problem. They did **not** leave behind a stable succession or a permanently closed Turkish monopoly.

**Evidence of elite widening:** Satish Chandra's concise OCR states that many **non-Turks**, including Khaljis and Indian Muslims, had long served in the Sultanate, especially in frontier and eastern spaces, but faced blocked advancement under old Turkish exclusivism.

**Interpretive dispute:** The phrase **"Khalji revolution"** is useful only if used carefully. It refers most safely to a **broadening of the social base of the ruling class** and the ending of the old Turkish monopoly of high office.

**Social boundary:** It does **not** mean a social revolution among cultivators, artisans, women or the subcontinent's population at large.



#### Interpretation table

| Interpretation | What it gets right | What must be resisted |
|---|---|---|
| Pure continuity | Khaljis inherited a functioning Delhi-centred state, frontier anxieties and iqta routines | It understates the change in who could rise to high office |
| Pure rupture | Khalji accession clearly broke old Turkish monopoly and widened elite access | It overstates total institutional novelty |
| Exam-safe synthesis | Mamluk institutions made Khalji expansion possible; Khalji accession widened access to power and intensified central coercion | Avoid both "nothing changed" and "everything changed" |

#### Must-know distinctions

- [FACT] The concise source uses the older phrase **"mixed Turkish-Afghan origin"**; the exam-safer formulation is that the Khaljis were a **Turkic-origin group long associated with Afghan regions**, whose political identity was not reducible to a fixed modern ethnicity.
- [FACT] They did **not** exclude Turks from office.
- [ANALYSIS] Their rise showed that the Delhi Sultanate was becoming more Indian in recruitment without becoming socially egalitarian.

#### Continuity-and-change map for 1290

| Political opening | Institutional continuity | Social limit |
|---|---|---|
| Wider service-elite recruitment | Monarchy, iqta and Persianate administration endured | No mass redistribution or equality |

*“Recomposition” captures all three columns more accurately than an unqualified revolution.*

### Revision notes

1. The Khalji dynasty ruled Delhi from 1290 to 1320.
2. The 1290 transition widened elite access without abolishing Sultanate institutions.
3. “Khalji revolution” is safest as elite recomposition, not social revolution.
4. Jalaluddin’s accession and Alauddin’s 1296 seizure are distinct political ruptures.
5. Barani is indispensable but retrospective, moralising and selective.
6. Chronology must separate contemporary evidence from later literary memory.
7. State capacity should be judged by reach, enforcement and durability—not royal claims alone.
8. A strong answer combines continuity, change and source qualification.

### Mains practice

**Question:** “The Khalji revolution represented elite recomposition within institutional continuity.” Explain. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer:** **Claim:** The "Khalji revolution" names a change in who could reach high Sultanate office, not a change in the structure of the state or in the condition of ordinary subjects. **Named evidence:** Jalaluddin Khalji's 1290 accession ended the old Turkish nobility's monopoly of the throne, yet Delhi remained the capital, iqta continued to fund the army, and Persianate court idiom carried on unbroken. **Analysis:** because the institutions that a genuine social or administrative revolution would have to change - land tenure, army financing, court language, urban hierarchy - stayed in place, while only the ethnic/factional composition of the ruling elite shifted, the safest reading is "elite recomposition inside a continuing Sultanate framework." **Qualification:** this does not make 1290 trivial; a monopoly ending is still a real and examinable political event, but it must never be inflated into a claim about mass social change among cultivators, artisans or women.

### How Devagiri treasure turned provincial command into a coup

#### Follow the resources that made the coup possible

```text
Kara/Awadh command + Devagiri treasure
                 ↓
troops, gifts and independent following
                 ↓
Jalaluddin murdered at Kara (1296)
                 ↓
Delhi secured through patronage, fear and coalition-building
```

Jalaluddin sought legitimacy through relative conciliation and the rhetoric of willing support. Alauddin used provincial resources, assassination and purchased allegiance. That contrast explains why independent wealth and private alliances later looked dangerous.

#### Source control

Barani's moral contrast between a lenient uncle and severe nephew is useful, but it cannot replace the causal sequence of command, treasure, violence and coalition.

**Chronological anchor:** Jalaluddin Khalji came to power in **1290** after overthrowing Balban's weak successors.

**Political contrast:** The concise OCR describes him as the first Sultan clearly to argue that the state in India should rest on the **willing support of the governed**, and that a truly Islamic state was impossible in a land with a Hindu majority.

**Causal turning point:** This made Jalaluddin's kingship **conciliatory by Sultanate standards**, but not pacifist. He remained a frontier commander shaped by Mongol pressure and elite insecurity.

**Source qualification:** Alauddin, then positioned at **Kara/Awadh**, raided **Devagiri/Deogir**, accumulated vast portable wealth, lured Jalaluddin into a vulnerable meeting and had him killed in **1296**.


*Accession flow. Conciliation, Devagiri loot, the Kara murder and coerced legitimacy together explain the later politics of surveillance and confiscation.*

#### Jalaluddin versus Alauddin

| Dimension | Jalaluddin Firuz Khalji | Alauddin Khalji |
|---|---|---|
| Tone of kingship | Conciliatory, less harsh than Balban | Severe, suspicious and confiscatory |
| Legitimacy style | Military support plus softening rhetoric | Seizure by assassination plus wealth redistribution and terror |
| Frontier context | Continuation of Mongol anxiety | Mongol crises become sharper and more system-shaping |
| Relation with nobles | Sought support through tolerance and leniency | Sought submission through surveillance, confiscation and bans |

#### Why Barani’s contrast needs control

- [LIMIT] Jalaluddin's idealised language survives through later chroniclers, above all Barani, who writes with moral and political agendas.
- [ANALYSIS] Yet even if stylised, the contrast between Jalaluddin's relative leniency and Alauddin's harsher turn is strongly supported by the narrative structure of the local sources.

#### Coup sequence from Kara to Delhi

```text
Provincial command → Devagiri treasure → financed following → murder at Kara → insecure throne
                                                                    ↓
                                                   surveillance and confiscation
```
*The manner of accession helps explain the political fears of the reign.*

### Revision notes

1. Jalaluddin used conciliation and support-building rather than Alauddin’s later coercive style.
2. Conciliation was a political strategy, not proof of military incapacity.
3. Alauddin’s Devagiri expedition supplied wealth and an independent following.
4. The murder at Kara in 1296 converted provincial ambition into a succession crisis.
5. Alauddin still had to secure Delhi and build a workable ruling coalition.
6. Barani’s moral contrast should not replace causal analysis.
7. The rupture joined personal violence, resource mobilisation and elite realignment.
8. That transition creates the legitimacy problem: how could a usurper prevent other nobles from repeating his path?

### Closing the routes by which nobles could rebel

#### Model a conspiracy before listing the ordinances

| Capacity needed by rebels | Alauddin's countermeasure |
|---|---|
| Independent finance | Confiscation of grants and suspicious accumulations |
| Private meeting spaces | Restrictions on banquets, festivities, wine and intoxicants |
| Durable family blocs | Permission controls over elite marriages |
| Secrecy | Barids, munhiyan and overlapping intelligence |

The ordinances were not random moral prohibitions. Together they reduced the money, meeting places, kinship ties and secrecy through which nobles could coordinate rebellion.

> **Political reading:** a usurper who had used wealth and alliances now denied those instruments to rivals.

**Reported measure:** After facing rebellions, Alauddin forbade nobles to hold **banquets or festivities**, to form **marriage alliances without permission**, and he restricted **wine and intoxicants**.

**Reported measure:** He also expanded **spying and information networks** and confiscated large accumulations of wealth and grants.

**Institutional inference:** These rules form one coherent political mechanism: **break noble solidarity, reduce resources for conspiracy, increase surveillance, and preserve royal awe**.



#### Ordinance-purpose table

| Reported restriction | Anti-rebellion function |
|---|---|
| Confiscation of wealth, grants or suspicious accumulation | Nobles lose independent funding power |
| Ban on convivial gatherings and wine | Plotting spaces shrink |
| Permission for elite marriage alliances | Family blocs cannot easily harden into political factions |
| Spy networks and reports | Conspiracy becomes riskier and costlier |
| Punitive severity | Makes royal disobedience visibly dangerous |

#### Limits

- [LIMIT] The detail is heavily Barani-shaped and should be treated as **reported royal policy**.
- [ANALYSIS] Even if enforcement varied, the political logic is strong and fits Alauddin's wider behaviour.

#### Four levers of anti-conspiracy rule

```text
MONEY ─ confiscation          MEETINGS ─ banquet/wine restrictions
KINSHIP ─ marriage permission SECRECY ─ barid/munhiyan intelligence
                 \             /
                  reduced coordination capacity
```
*The ordinances converge on the resources and networks required for collective rebellion.*

### Revision notes

1. Alauddin’s accession created a persistent fear of noble conspiracy.
2. Confiscation reduced independent material resources available for rebellion.
3. Restrictions on feasts and wine reduced unsupervised political gatherings.
4. Marriage-permission rules constrained alliance-building among elite households.
5. Barids and munhiyan strengthened formal and secret information channels.
6. The ordinances targeted coordination capacity rather than morality alone.
7. These controls increased compliance but also depended on intrusive coercion.
8. Elite control did not remove the external Mongol danger that required a permanent army.

```text
        ↺ renewed attempts to evade
```


**Evidence:** **Khusrau Khan** then seized power, and **Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq** defeated him in **1320**.

- [FACT] Topic 05 owns Ghiyasuddin's settled policy, Muhammad bin Tughlaq's large-scale annexation and Firuz's later departures.

## 15 marks · 250 words

**Question:** Compare Alauddin Khalji’s policies toward rural intermediaries and urban merchants.

**Model answer:** **Thesis:** Both groups were disciplined to reduce autonomous control over resources, but the mechanisms reflected their different positions in the fiscal-supply chain. **Rural intermediaries:** Khuts, muqaddams and chaudhuris faced measurement-based assessment, loss of exemptions and collection privileges, and payment of reported levies such as ghari and charai. The aim was to prevent them from shifting burdens to weaker cultivators and withholding crown revenue. They were curtailed, not universally abolished, because the state still depended on local knowledge and village organisation. **Urban and long-distance merchants:** Traders were registered, bonded to maintain supply and compelled to sell at official rates. Multani merchants could receive treasury advances, while costly-cloth permits restricted arbitrage. Horse brokers faced grading, inspection and limits on collusion. **Common logic:** Surveillance, records and punishment redirected bargaining power toward the crown and supported army finance and Delhi provisioning. **Difference:** Rural policy extracted revenue and grain at origin; market policy controlled movement, quality and sale. Incentives were more visible in commerce through advances, whereas agrarian compliance rested heavily on assessment and collection. **Social effect:** Consumers and soldiers gained, but cultivators and merchants bore coercive costs. **Conclusion:** Alauddin integrated village and market discipline into one centralising system while tailoring instruments to each economic actor.

**Why this earns marks:** It fulfils “compare” through parallel rural and urban sections, identifies a common centralising logic and a mechanism-specific difference, converts examples into distributional analysis, qualifies abolition and benefit claims, and ends with a balanced comparative conclusion.

In Rajasthan, Delhi combined occupation of forts such as Ranthambhor and Chittor with submission and uneven local continuity. **South:** Ramachandra's Devagiri became a subordinate ally and logistical base; Warangal and Dvarasamudra retained rulers while surrendering treasure and promising tribute; Mabar yielded extraction without stable administration. This spectrum reduced the cost of ruling distant regions while extending Delhi's claims. **Military-market link:** Cash salaries, *dagh*, *chehra*, horse regulation and controlled prices sustained cavalry under Mongol pressure. **Limits:** tribute could lapse, distance weakened enforcement, core extraction burdened cultivators and traders, and the system depended heavily on Alauddin. Its weakening after 1316 distinguishes acquiring submission from institutionalising routine obedience. **Conclusion:** Khalji power joined a directly managed core to strategic forts and tributary rulers; later Tughlaq annexation must not be projected backward.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “discuss” through a spatial hierarchy from core to tributary south, attaches named evidence to each level, analyses administrative cost and military-fiscal integration, qualifies reach and later annexation, and concludes with differentiated rather than uniform sovereignty.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Chronology and transition

- Khalji dynasty: 1290–1320.
- Jalaluddin Firuz Khalji: 1290–1296.
- Alauddin Khalji: 1296–1316.
- Mubarak Shah and Khusrau Khan belong to the 1316–1320 succession phase.
- Ghazi Malik became Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq in 1320.
- “Khalji revolution” means widening/recomposition of the ruling elite, not mass revolution.

## 2. Jalaluddin and accession

- Jalaluddin retained several earlier officers and preferred comparative conciliation.
- His policy was neither modern liberalism nor proof that coercion disappeared.
- Alauddin raided Devagiri from Kara in 1296.
- Devagiri treasure financed troops, gifts and coalition-building.
- Alauddin murdered Jalaluddin at Kara and took power.
- The violent accession made legitimacy and noble control central problems.

## 3. Noble control

- Confiscation attacked autonomous aristocratic wealth.
- Spies monitored private political activity.
- Feasts, wine and unsupervised gatherings were restricted.
- Marriage alliances needed royal permission.
- Punishment could extend to rebels’ families.
- Aim: atomise nobles and redirect dependence vertically to the Sultan.
- Limit: fear-based compliance was highly ruler-dependent.

## 4. Mongol defence

- 1297–98 Jullundhar: Ulugh Khan.
- 1298 Siwistan: Zafar Khan.
- 1299 Kili: Qutlugh Khwaja; Zafar Khan died.
- 1303: Targhi’s pressure reached Delhi and exposed supply weakness.
- 1305 Amroha: Malik Nayak defeated Ali Beg and Tartaq.
- Siri, frontier forts, commanders, cavalry and supplies formed one system.
- Mongol pressure was the strategic link between army size and market control.

## 5. Expansion

- Gujarat, 1299: revenue, textiles, Cambay trade and imported horses; stronger incorporation.
- Ranthambhor, 1301: Hammir Deva; prolonged siege.
- Chittor, 1303: conquest secure; Padmini story is later literary memory.
- Malwa, 1305: Ain-ul-Mulk; route and plateau value.
- Siwana, 1308; Jalor, 1311.
- Rajasthan combined key-fort occupation, submission and local continuity.

## 6. Deccan

- Devagiri: 1296 raid; 1307 submission; Ramachandra restored as Rai Rayan.
- Devagiri became a logistical bridge.
- Warangal, 1309–10: Prataparudra retained kingdom after treasure and tribute.
- Dvarasamudra, 1310–11: Ballala accepted tributary terms.
- Mabar: deep raid and plunder without stable Alai administration.
- Later resistance moved Devagiri toward direct control.
- Broad Tughlaq annexation belongs to Topic 05.

## 7. Army

- Large standing cavalry relied substantially on cash salaries.
- Dagh: horse branding.
- Chehra: descriptive soldier roll.
- Muster matched claim, person and mount.
- Barani reports about 238 tankas for a one-horse cavalryman; use with source caution.
- Price control preserved real purchasing power.

## 8. Agrarian system

- Core lands from the north-west through the doab were brought into closer crown control.
- Khalisa: crown-revenue domain.
- Masahat: measurement.
- Demand associated with roughly half the produce.
- Cash and kind collection varied by place and provisioning need.
- Ghari and charai: reported house and cattle/grazing levies.
- Khuts, muqaddams and chaudhuris lost privileges and evasion opportunities but were not universally abolished.
- Patwari records assisted auditing.
- Peasants could gain from checked intermediary exaction yet lose through heavy state demand.

## 9. Market objectives and administration

- Primary aim: sustain army purchasing power at controlled fiscal cost.
- Strong secondary aim: provision Delhi during siege and scarcity.
- Diwan-i-Riyasat supervised the regulated system.
- Shahna-i-mandi enforced market orders.
- Barids and munhiyans supplied official and secret intelligence.
- Merchants were registered and bonded.
- Three named markets: grain; cloth/costly goods; horses/slaves/cattle.
- A fourth market is only analytical shorthand.

## 10. Grain system

- Revenue in kind supplied state stocks.
- Cultivators and dealers faced limits on withholding marketable grain.
- Banjaras transported grain under registration and surety.
- Royal granaries countered artificial scarcity.
- Supervised mandis enforced price and weight.
- Scarcity triggered rationing and ward-based distribution.
- Barani’s reported prices are schedule markers, not audited empire-wide data.

## 11. Other markets

- Sarai-i-Adl: cloth and costly-goods market, not a court.
- Multani merchants reportedly received 20 lakh tankas in advances.
- Costly-cloth permits limited arbitrage.
- Horse dealers and dallals faced price grading and inspection.
- Horse-market control complemented dagh and muster.
- Slaves and cattle also had price schedules; this evidence records coercive commodification.

## 12. Effects and limits

- Army and many Delhi consumers benefited from cheap prices.
- Crown reduced pressure to raise nominal salaries.
- Cultivators bore heavy demand and compelled sale.
- Merchants and carriers faced narrow margins, bonds and surveillance.
- Trade continued; it was regulated, not abolished.
- Evidence is strongest for Delhi and the doab-supported core.
- Information problems, corruption and geography limited uniformity.
- Mubarak Shah’s reversal revealed low durability.

## 13. State and religion

- Alauddin reportedly prioritised what he considered necessary for the state.
- Zawabit/sultanic regulations were distinguishable from shara.
- Barani’s dialogue is retrospective, not verbatim.
- Best label: medieval reason of state.
- Avoid both modern secularism and pure-theocracy claims.
- Formal Islamic kingship coexisted with practical dependence on diverse subjects.

## 14. Architecture and sources

- Siri: defended royal-military centre.
- Alai Darwaza: completed 1311; mature arch-and-dome construction.
- Do not call it India’s first true arch.
- Alai Minar: ambitious and unfinished.
- Hauz-i-Alai: urban reservoir.
- UNESCO includes the Alai structures within the Qutb complex; ASI manages the property.
- Khusrau: near-contemporary but panegyrical.
- Barani: administratively rich but later and moralising.
- Isami: useful comparative voice with its own context.
- *Padmavat*: sixteenth-century literary memory, not 1303 eyewitness evidence.

## 15. Decline

- Alauddin died in 1316.
- Malik Kafur briefly controlled a minor-heir arrangement.
- Kafur was killed.
- Mubarak Shah relaxed many controls.
- Khusrau Khan killed Mubarak and seized power.
- Ghazi Malik defeated Khusrau in 1320.
- High state capacity did not produce resilient succession.
- Institutional memory survived even as the Alai configuration collapsed.

## 16. Final answer verdicts

- Political: widened elite recruitment but concentrated monarchy.
- Military: successfully defended the core during peak Mongol pressure.
- Territorial: expanded through differentiated control, not uniform annexation.
- Fiscal: deepened crown access to revenue and grain.
- Market: innovative integrated regulation with unequal burdens.
- Social: benefits and costs varied sharply by stakeholder.
- Institutional: strong under Alauddin, fragile across succession.
- Historiographical: rank military-fiscal need first, then political discipline, while retaining ideological and welfare dimensions as qualified factors.
