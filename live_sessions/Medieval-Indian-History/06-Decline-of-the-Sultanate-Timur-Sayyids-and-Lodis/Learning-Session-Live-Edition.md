## Lesson 1 — The centre contracts before Timur

### Visual: a reinforcing contraction, not one sudden collapse

```text
Firuz's short-term bargains
  ├─ offices and assignments become more hereditary
  ├─ nobles and local intermediaries gain durable claims
  └─ a large corps of Firuzi slaves acquires organised interests
                         ↓
weak succession after 1388 → court factions choose rival claimants
                         ↓
provinces retain more revenue → centre pays and disciplines fewer troops
                         ↓
local rulers and governors assert autonomy
                         ↓
Delhi's coercive reach shrinks further
                         ↺
```

*The key is feedback: every loss of revenue reduced military reach, and every loss of military reach made further revenue loss easier.*

### Intuition: a state can weaken before it disappears

Imagine a firm whose headquarters still carries a famous name but can no longer appoint reliable managers, collect income from its branches or pay a common security force. The firm has not legally vanished, yet its commands increasingly depend on negotiation. Late Tughlaq Delhi faced a comparable political problem.

The analogy has a limit: a medieval sultanate was not a modern corporation. Authority depended on military service, tribute, personal loyalty, agrarian surplus and public symbols of sovereignty, not impersonal corporate law.

### 1. Succession was a political mechanism

The Delhi Sultanate had no consistently binding rule of primogeniture, meaning automatic succession by the eldest son. A ruler's death therefore reopened the question of who could command the army, nobles, treasury and capital.

After Firuz Shah's death in 1388:

1. competing princes sought the throne;
2. nobles backed candidates who might protect their interests;
3. the Firuzi slaves intervened as an organised faction;
4. provincial governors and local rulers exploited the distraction;
5. rival claimants appeared around Delhi and Firuzabad.

Succession conflict mattered because it split the very coalition needed to collect revenue and field troops. It was not merely palace drama.

### 2. Firuz's settlement: stability bought with future constraints

Firuz had restored a measure of peace after Muhammad bin Tughlaq's turbulent reign. Yet several policies that reduced immediate conflict weakened later central control:

- hereditary concessions made offices and revenue assignments harder to resume;
- nobles acquired entrenched local interests;
- military service could become less effectively supervised;
- revenue claims were dispersed among intermediaries;
- a very large slave establishment depended personally on Firuz and later became a faction.

The **Firuzi slaves** were enslaved dependants collected and trained under Firuz, some for crafts and royal workshops and others for military service. Their expected personal loyalty to Firuz did not automatically transfer to his successors. Satish Chandra notes that such slaves could develop a separate corporate interest.

### 3. Fiscal-military contraction

**Fiscal-military capacity** means the state's ability to obtain revenue and convert it into dependable armed force. The late Tughlaq problem can be followed step by step:

```text
provincial autonomy
      ↓
less revenue reaches Delhi
      ↓
smaller or less reliable central military capacity
      ↓
weaker enforcement against governors, chiefs and zamindars
      ↓
still greater provincial autonomy
```

This formulation is stronger than saying merely “weak rulers caused decline.” Personal ability mattered, but rulers operated inside a narrowing resource base and a divided elite structure.

### 4. Provincial assertion was active politics

Governors in Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa and Jaunpur did not simply wait for Delhi to collapse. They transformed provincial offices, military commands, agrarian resources and urban centres into regional states. Rajput and other local powers also widened their autonomy.

Thus, “decline of Delhi” and “rise of regions” describe the same redistribution of power from different vantage points.

### 5. The Delhi-to-Palam saying: useful rhetoric, unsafe cartography

A famous witticism said that the dominion of the “Lord of the Universe” extended only from Delhi to Palam. It captures the contrast between an expansive royal title and severely reduced effective authority.

**Source qualification:** it is a literary exaggeration, not a surveyed boundary. Use it to illustrate shrinking reach, never to claim that every village beyond Palam was outside all Delhi influence.

### Strongest qualification and reply

**Objection:** If the Sultanate survived until 1412 and later dynasties ruled Delhi, “collapse” is exaggerated.

**Reply:** Correctly qualified, the claim is not that all institutions vanished in 1388. The imperial centre lost revenue, territory and command cohesion, while chancery practices, revenue assignments, offices, urban life and the symbolic value of Delhi continued. Political contraction and institutional persistence can coexist.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** distinguish internal decline before 1398 from Timur's later shock.
- **Mains use:** explain decline through a chain—succession conflict → elite autonomy → revenue loss → military weakness → provincial assertion.
- **Trap:** do not treat hereditary concessions as automatically benevolent decentralisation; they reduced the centre's capacity to rotate and discipline holders.
- **Answer line:** *Timur struck a polity already weakened by a self-reinforcing fiscal-military and succession crisis.*

### Revision notes

1. Firuz Shah died in 1388.
2. Absence of a settled succession rule repeatedly invited factional intervention.
3. Nobles supported rival princes to protect offices and assignments.
4. Firuzi slaves became an organised post-Firuz political interest.
5. Personal dependence on one ruler did not guarantee dynastic loyalty.
6. Hereditary concessions reduced later rulers' freedom to resume offices and revenue claims.
7. Provincial revenue retention weakened central military capacity.
8. Local rulers, zamindars and governors exploited court conflict.
9. Delhi's contraction preceded Timur's invasion.
10. The Delhi-to-Palam phrase is political rhetoric, not exact territorial measurement.
11. Institutional continuity prevents us from describing the period as instant administrative extinction.
12. The best causal vocabulary is “reinforcing fiscal-military contraction.”

### Original Mains application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how political and fiscal mechanisms reinforced each other in the decline of the late Tughlaq Sultanate before Timur's invasion.

**Model answer**

“Explain” requires a causal chain rather than a list of weak rulers. After Firuz Shah's death in 1388, the absence of settled succession allowed princes, nobles and Firuzi slaves to promote rival claimants. This divided the coalition through which Delhi appointed officers, collected revenue and mobilised troops.

Firuz's hereditary concessions deepened the problem. **Claim:** durable local claims reduced central flexibility. **Evidence:** offices and revenue assignments became harder for successors to resume or rotate. **Analysis:** provincial governors, chiefs and zamindars could retain resources that formerly supported the central army. With weaker coercive capacity, Delhi found it harder to recover tribute or discipline provinces, producing further revenue loss.

The Delhi-to-Palam witticism captures the resulting gap between royal title and effective reach, but it is rhetorical rather than a surveyed border. Institutions and Delhi's prestige survived.

Thus, elite fragmentation and fiscal-military contraction formed a feedback loop before Timur's external shock of 1398.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes mechanism from chronology, uses named institutional evidence, explains the feedback loop and qualifies both “collapse” and the Palam phrase.

## Lesson 2 — Timur's raid: setting, route, violence and limits

### Visual: motive, opportunity, campaign and exit

```text
TIMUR'S CENTRAL ASIAN POWER
Transoxiana/Samarqand + conquests across Iran and western Asia
                         |
        motives ---------------- opportunity
        plunder                  divided Tughlaq centre
        prestige                 weak north-western resistance
        army reward              rich urban target
        frontier strategy
        religious self-justification
                         |
                         v
Indus/Punjab route → Delhi battle → sack, killing and enslavement
                         |
                         v
loot + captives + artisans taken toward Samarqand → withdrawal
                         |
             no permanent conquest regime in Delhi
```

*Timur's invasion joined material, strategic and ideological motives, but it remained a devastating raid rather than a durable Indian administration.*

### Intuition: a catalyst acts on existing vulnerability

A spark can cause enormous damage when a structure is already dry and fractured. The spark matters, but it does not explain why the structure was combustible. Timur's invasion was such a catalyst: a major external shock acting on a pre-existing internal contraction.

The analogy must not minimise agency. The violence, enslavement and extraction were deliberate features of conquest, not impersonal natural events.

### 1. Central Asian setting

Timur emerged in **Transoxiana**, the region beyond the Oxus/Amu Darya, and made Samarqand the centre of an expanding conquest empire. By the late fourteenth century his power stretched across much of Central and West Asia toward the Indus.

He used Chinggisid political prestige but was not himself a direct reigning Chinggisid khan. Timurid legitimacy combined conquest, dynastic marriage connections, steppe-imperial forms and Islamic claims.

### 2. Motives: a bundle, not a single slogan

| Motive | Historical logic | Required qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Plunder | Delhi and north Indian towns held movable wealth accumulated over generations | Wealth was not the only consideration |
| Prestige | A victorious expedition enhanced Timur's imperial reputation | Court histories magnify the ruler's glory |
| Army reward | Booty and captives helped sustain a conquest coalition | It does not make every campaign economically identical |
| Frontier strategy | Punjab and the Indus zone connected Central Asian and north Indian politics | Timur did not seek routine government over all India |
| Religious self-justification | Timurid narratives framed violence through claims of holy war | Self-description is evidence of legitimation, not a transparent report of motive |

The safe conclusion is multi-causal: plunder, power, military reward, frontier calculation and religious rhetoric interacted.

### 3. Route and campaign

The campaign moved in a broad sequence:

```text
Indus crossing
      ↓
Tulamba and Dipalpur, with other north-western towns on the Punjab route
      ↓
advance toward Delhi
      ↓
battle and sack
      ↓
withdrawal through the north-west with booty, captives and artisans
```

This is a route framework, not a reconstructed day-by-day itinerary. The route phase, the battle for Delhi, the sack and the return must be kept analytically separate. Towns and populations along the north-western advance suffered assault, plunder and captivity, while the divided Tughlaq regime could not organise resistance equal to the threat.

Near Delhi in December 1398, Timurid forces defeated the Tughlaq army. Before or around the battle, large numbers of captives were killed under Timurid orders because they were considered a security burden. Delhi was then subjected to sack, mass violence and enslavement.

**Evidence caution:** chronicles agree on extreme destruction but casualty totals vary and may be rhetorical. State the character and scale of violence firmly; treat exact totals cautiously.

### 4. Extraction and deportation

The invaders removed:

- precious metals, jewellery and other movable wealth;
- captives who could be enslaved;
- skilled artisans, including masons, stone-cutters and carpenters.

Some deported artisans were used in building activity associated with Samarqand. Artisan transfer reveals that conquest extracted skill as well as treasure.

### 5. Withdrawal and the consequence test

Timur withdrew rather than founding a directly governed Indian empire. His relationship with Khizr Khan mattered later, but this did not make the post-1414 Sayyid state a simple modern province ruled from Samarqand.

Separate the consequences:

| Consequence | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Delhi's population, wealth and urban confidence | devastating immediate damage |
| Tughlaq legitimacy and coercive capacity | further shattered |
| Provincial independence | encouraged by the centre's weakness |
| Timurid rule over India | no durable all-India administration followed |
| Long-run cause of Sultanate decline | accelerator, not sole origin |

### Sources, objection and reply

Timurid memoir traditions and the *Zafarnama* accounts associated with Nizam al-Din Shami and Sharaf al-Din Yazdi illuminate route, imperial claims and victory. Their courtly purpose also favours triumph, exemplary punishment and ruler-centred explanation.

**Objection:** Because Timur left, his invasion had only a small effect.

**Reply:** Its direct administrative effect was limited, but the demographic, fiscal, military and symbolic blow to Delhi was severe. “No permanent regime” does not mean “no major consequence.”

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Timur invaded in 1398, long after Muhammad bin Tughlaq; he did not found the Sayyid dynasty himself.
- **Mains use:** separate cause, catalyst, immediate impact and long-term limit.
- **Trap:** religious justification in a victory narrative cannot be treated as the only motive.
- **Answer line:** *Timur's raid magnified Delhi's crisis through extraction and terror, while its rapid withdrawal limited direct political replacement.*

### Revision notes

1. Timur's power base lay in Transoxiana with Samarqand as his capital.
2. His India campaign belongs to 1398.
3. Plunder, prestige, army reward and frontier strategy all mattered.
4. Religious language was also a mode of self-legitimation.
5. The Tughlaq centre was already divided before the invasion.
6. Timurid forces moved through Punjab toward Delhi.
7. The Delhi battle was followed by sack, killing, enslavement and extraction.
8. Exact casualty numbers require caution.
9. Artisans as well as treasure were taken toward Samarqand.
10. Timur withdrew and did not create a durable Indian administration.
11. His attack accelerated rather than originated the Sultanate's decline.
12. Direct political replacement was limited; fiscal and symbolic damage was profound.

### Original Mains application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** “Timur's invasion was a catalyst of the Delhi Sultanate's disintegration, not its original cause.” Explain.

**Model answer**

The directive “explain” requires a causal distinction between pre-existing weakness and the additional effect of 1398. Before Timur, succession struggles after Firuz Shah, intervention by nobles and Firuzi slaves, hereditary concessions and provincial revenue retention had already contracted Delhi's fiscal-military reach. Satish Chandra's discussion of the Delhi-to-Palam witticism captures this restricted authority, though the phrase is rhetorical rather than cartographic.

Timur exploited this divided field. His forces defeated the Tughlaq army, sacked Delhi, killed and enslaved inhabitants, removed treasure and deported artisans toward Samarqand. These acts damaged revenue, manpower, urban confidence and dynastic prestige, encouraging further provincial assertion. Yet Timur withdrew and did not build a permanent Indian administration; the Tughlaq line lingered until 1412 and inherited institutions survived under later states.

Thus, the invasion intensified a structural crisis generated within the Sultanate. It was a devastating accelerator, but neither the sole cause of decline nor a complete political replacement.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the causal proposition directly, links named evidence to effects, and qualifies both the Palam rhetoric and the limits of Timurid rule.

### Visual: Delhi's contraction redistributes power

```text
                       WEAKENED DELHI
                             |
       +----------+----------+-----------+-----------+
       |          |          |           |           |
    Gujarat     Malwa      Jaunpur     Bengal      Mewar
       \          /           |            \          /
        \        /       upper/eastern       \        /
      western balance      Ganga field      eastern checks
            \                  |                  /
             +------ competitive balance -------+
                             |
             war + diplomacy + tribute + patronage
```

*Regionalisation created several centres that governed, fought, negotiated and patronised culture; it was not an empty interval between two empires.*

### Intuition: the fall of one centre can multiply centres

If a single umbrella breaks, people do not necessarily remain exposed and inactive; they may build several smaller shelters. Likewise, Delhi's contraction allowed regional governors and warrior houses to convert local resources into states.

The limit is important: these states were not peaceful substitutes. They imposed taxes, fought wars and competed for forts, routes and fertile tracts.

### 1. From “decline” to state formation

The fifteenth century saw:

- independent or effectively independent sultanates in Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa and Jaunpur;
- stronger Rajput powers, especially Mewar;
- competition over the Ganga-Yamuna doab, Rajasthan and Malwa;
- continuing urban, agrarian and cultural development outside Delhi.

Satish Chandra describes a regional balance of power: Gujarat, Malwa and Mewar checked one another in the west; Bengal faced Orissa and Jaunpur in the east; Lodis and Sharqis struggled for the Ganga valley.

This does not mean equilibrium was exact or permanent. “Balance of power” means that no single actor could expand without provoking resistance from others.

### 2. Malik Sarwar and the Sharqi origin

Malik Sarwar, a prominent late Tughlaq noble and former wazir, received responsibility for eastern territories with the title **Malik-us-Sharq**, “Lord of the East.” His successors were called **Sharqis** from this title.

The sequence matters:

```text
imperial office under Delhi
          ↓
regional command and resources
          ↓
autonomous dynasty centred at Jaunpur
```

This is a common pattern of medieval regional state formation: a provincial office became the institutional bridge to sovereignty.

### 3. Jaunpur's political role

At its height the Sharqi state controlled a broad tract of eastern Uttar Pradesh and adjoining Bihar. It:

- maintained order after Delhi's retreat;
- checked Bengal's westward expansion;
- contested the upper and middle Ganga plain;
- repeatedly challenged Delhi;
- forced the Lodis into a long struggle before annexation in 1484.

Calling Jaunpur merely a cultural city misses its fiscal and military base. Calling it a replacement empire over all north India exaggerates its reach.

### 4. Architecture and the “Shiraz of the East”

Sharqi rulers patronised scholars, poets, saints, palaces, mosques and mausoleums. Jaunpur architecture developed a recognisable regional idiom, especially monumental gateways and broad, high arches rather than simple copying of Delhi forms. The Atala Masjid is commonly used as an architectural anchor.

The label **“Shiraz of the East”** signifies Jaunpur's reputation for learning and culture. It is a prestige comparison, not proof that Jaunpur duplicated every institution, language community or artistic form of Iranian Shiraz.

### 5. Dark-age thesis: objection and reply

**Objection:** Frequent warfare, Delhi's devastation and dynastic instability justify calling the fifteenth century a dark age.

**Reply:** The violence and insecurity were real, but the label hides positive historical processes: regional taxation, military organisation, urban patronage, architecture, literary culture and inter-state diplomacy. The sound verdict is differentiated—imperial fragmentation at Delhi alongside active state formation elsewhere.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Malik-us-Sharq → Sharqis; Jaunpur → “Shiraz of the East”; Bahlul annexed Jaunpur in 1484.
- **Mains use:** convert “decline” into a two-sided argument—central contraction and regional reconstruction.
- **Architecture trap:** regional style means adaptation and innovation, not isolation from Delhi's broader arcuate building tradition.
- **Answer line:** *The fifteenth century decentralised sovereignty without extinguishing state capacity or cultural production.*

### Revision notes

1. Regionalisation redistributed rather than erased political power.
2. Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa and Jaunpur became important regional sultanates.
3. Mewar was a major Rajput power in the western contest.
4. Balance of power means reciprocal checking, not a stable treaty system.
5. Malik Sarwar had served the Tughlaq order before establishing eastern autonomy.
6. Malik-us-Sharq means “Lord of the East.”
7. The Sharqi name derived from that title.
8. Jaunpur checked Bengal and challenged Delhi.
9. Sharqi rule maintained order across a substantial eastern tract.
10. Jaunpur's style featured lofty gateways and large arches.
11. “Shiraz of the East” is a cultural-prestige label requiring qualification.
12. Bahlul Lodi annexed Jaunpur in 1484.
13. Regional vitality refutes a simple civilisational-dark-age narrative.

## Lesson 4 — The Sayyids: restricted sovereignty, not non-rule

Progress: 4/8 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Khizr Khan to Alam Shah, legitimacy, tribute and pressure

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Topic 06 canonical ruler ladder and Yahya Sirhindi source note; Satish Chandra regional context
CA search: "not required: Sayyid legitimacy and fifteenth-century tribute politics need no current-affairs anchor"
CA found: none used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: four rulers, one narrowing problem

| Ruler | Reign | Central political problem | Safe verdict |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Khizr Khan | 1414–1421 | taking Delhi while using Timurid association for legitimacy | founded a real but restricted regime |
| Mubarak Shah | 1421–1434 | campaigns against Khokhars, Mewatis, nobles and other challengers | energetic recovery remained insecure |
| Muhammad Shah | 1434–1445 | factional pressure and shrinking reach | authority survived through negotiation and expeditions |
| Ala al-Din Alam Shah | 1445–1451 | withdrawal to Badaun and loss of Delhi initiative | yielded Delhi's field to Bahlul |

*The dynasty's history is neither “strong empire” nor “nothing happened”: it is the politics of maintaining a capital and extracting obedience in a crowded regional field.*

### Intuition: sovereignty can be real without being unlimited

A ruler need not control every claimed district uniformly to exercise sovereignty. If he holds a capital, appoints officers, receives tribute, leads campaigns and uses public symbols of rule, he possesses political authority—even when that authority is contested.

The limit: this does not make restricted sovereignty equivalent to the territorial and bureaucratic reach of a modern nation-state.

### 1. Khizr Khan's path to Delhi

The canonical Advanced account records that Timur **assigned or entrusted Delhi, Multan and Dipalpur to Khizr Khan**. This statement identifies a Timurid basis for Khizr's later legitimacy, but it must not be read as proof that he immediately exercised equal control over all three places.

Khizr's effective power was first rooted in **Multan and Punjab**. From that north-western base he survived the post-invasion contest and later entered Delhi in 1414, after the Tughlaq centre had become untenable. The sequence is therefore:

```text
Timurid assignment or entrustment
             ↓
Punjab-Multan base, with Dipalpur in the claimed north-western sphere
             ↓
later entry into Delhi
             ↓
restricted Delhi-centred sovereignty
```

His Timurid connection:

- supplied prestige and a language of legitimate succession after 1398;
- was reflected for a period in the use of Timurid names in the **khutba**, the formal Friday sermon in which naming a sovereign signalled political legitimacy;
- did not establish continuous direct supervision, regular provincial administration or unquestioned subordination from Samarqand.

Satish Chandra's account of the post-Timurid field stresses the rise of the Sayyids amid Punjab-based Afghan and regional competition. Read together with the canonical assignment statement, this supports a graded conclusion: Timurid recognition mattered, but Khizr's actual state rested on his north-western resource base, conquest of Delhi and repeated local bargaining.

His claimed descent as a Sayyid—one tracing lineage to the Prophet—functioned as dynastic legitimation, but the genealogy is not beyond historical dispute.

### 2. Instruments of restricted rule

Sayyid governance depended on:

```text
Delhi and its immediate resource core
            +
older revenue and assignment practices
            +
noble cooperation
            +
military expeditions for tribute
            =
real authority with uneven territorial depth
```

**Tribute** is a payment acknowledging superior power without necessarily implying continuous local administration. Repeated tribute campaigns reveal both authority and weakness: the ruler could compel payment, but payment often had to be compelled again.

### 3. Pressure zones

- **Khokhars:** a powerful Punjab/Salt Range community whose resistance affected the north-western frontier.
- **Mewatis:** chiefs and armed groups in Mewat close to Delhi, able to disrupt routes and challenge the capital's neighbourhood.
- **Sharqis:** rulers of Jaunpur who competed for the Ganga plain and threatened Delhi from the east.
- **nobles and local chiefs:** essential collaborators who could become rivals when central leverage weakened.

Mubarak Shah campaigned energetically but was murdered. Muhammad Shah faced continued factional pressure. Alam Shah withdrew to Badaun; Bahlul Lodi then occupied Delhi in 1451.

### 4. What the source can and cannot show

Yahya bin Ahmad Sirhindi's *Tarikh-i Mubarak Shahi* is central for the late Tughlaq and Sayyid political sequence. Its Delhi-centred court perspective is especially useful for rulers, campaigns and factions, but it does not provide a complete village-level picture of administration.

Coins and titulature can show claims to authority; they cannot alone measure how regularly revenue was collected in every locality.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Frequent tribute expeditions prove that the Sayyids were merely ceremonial rulers.

**Reply:** The need for repeated expeditions proves limited consolidation, not absence of state action. The dynasty held Delhi for nearly four decades, mobilised forces, negotiated nobles and imposed payments. Its capacity was restricted and uneven, but historically consequential.

- **Trap:** Timurid association did not equal direct provincial administration from Central Asia.
- **Source use:** pair *Tarikh-i Mubarak Shahi* with coins, inscriptions and regional evidence.
- **Answer line:** *Sayyid sovereignty was negotiated and tribute-oriented: genuine in Delhi, uneven beyond it and persistently contested.*

### Visual: Bahlul's coalition-building machine

```text
Sirhind/Punjab base
       |
check Khokhar power
       |
invite/recruit Afghans from Roh
       |
kinship + grants + personal bargaining + shared campaigns
       |
enter Delhi political field → accession in 1451

       |

```

*Bahlul did not inherit a ready-made centralised empire; he assembled one through an Afghan military-political network.*

### Intuition: leadership among strong partners

Bahlul's monarchy resembled a coalition leader dealing with armed partners who expected honour, consultation and shares of resources. He could command, but command worked through relationships as well as hierarchy.

The analogy stops at constitutional democracy. These nobles were not representatives of equal citizens; they were armed lineage elites competing over rank, land and service.

### 1. From Punjab to Delhi

Bahlul Lodi rose through control of Sirhind and a widening Punjab base. By checking Khokhar power and recruiting Afghan supporters, including men associated with **Roh**—the hill and frontier zone linked to Afghan homelands—he acquired the military coalition required to intervene in Delhi.

When the Sayyid ruler Alam Shah remained at Badaun, Bahlul took Delhi and was crowned in 1451. Accession was therefore both seizure and negotiated transfer within a weakened political field.

### 2. The Afghan political idiom

Afghan chiefs often understood honour and authority through lineage standing and negotiated equality among leading warriors. For Bahlul, durable rule required:

- personal accessibility and consultation;
- grants and territorial shares;
- recognition of kin and chiefs;
- joint campaigning;
- coercion when bargaining failed.

This is **negotiated kingship**: royal authority created through repeated bargains with militarily significant elites.

### 3. What “tribal equality” does not mean

The phrase can mislead in three ways:

| Misreading | Correction |
|---|---|
| democratic government | political voice centred on warrior-nobles, not universal participation |
| absence of hierarchy | sultan, senior chiefs, retainers and cultivators remained unequal |
| automatic unity | lineages and chiefs could defect, partition resources or pursue rival ambitions |

Use “noble-lineage bargaining” as the safer analytical description.

### 4. The Jaunpur struggle

The Sharqis possessed a rich territorial and cultural base and repeatedly contested Delhi. Bahlul's struggle against them was prolonged rather than a single decisive march.

His methods combined:

1. campaigning and pressure in the Ganga plain;
2. alliances and defections;
3. accommodation of Afghan supporters;
4. gradual erosion of Sharqi positions;
5. occupation and annexation of Jaunpur in 1484.

The annexation enlarged Lodi resources and revived Delhi's influence from Punjab toward the Bengal border. Yet Bahlul distributed territories among sons and relations, leaving a later problem of partition and autonomous power.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** If Bahlul relied on bargaining, he was a weak monarch.

**Reply:** Bargaining was not the opposite of power; it was the method by which power was assembled in an Afghan elite environment. His long reign and annexation of Jaunpur demonstrate effectiveness. The limitation was that negotiated grants strengthened partners whom successors might struggle to discipline.

- **Trap:** Afghan elite equality cannot be projected onto subjects generally.
- **Answer line:** *Bahlul expanded the state by treating Afghan chiefs as partners in conquest, but that partnership limited the crown's later hierarchical ambitions.*

## Lesson 6 — Sikandar Lodi: administrative recovery and its boundaries

### Visual: Sikandar's three-sided consolidation

| Crown over nobles | Revenue and market | Space and communication |
|---|---|---|
| attendance and standing before the sultan | rent or revenue rolls and account scrutiny | safer roads and communication |
| respect for royal *farmans* | *gazz-i-Sikandari* measurement | Agra as strategic centre |
| jagir accounts examined | grain-octroi abolition | doab, Rajasthan and Malwa access |

```text
discipline + information + mobility
                ↓
       stronger royal supervision
                ↓
   recovery without total centralisation
```

*Sikandar strengthened the crown by making nobles more visible, revenue more measurable and the eastern-southern routes more governable.*

### Intuition: authority grows when rulers can see

A ruler governs better when he can know who holds land, what revenue is claimed, whether orders are obeyed and how quickly officials or grain can move. Sikandar's reforms increased this “visibility.”

The analogy's limit is documentary survival. Prescriptive orders and court praise show intended control more clearly than uniform implementation in every locality.

### 1. Disciplining nobles

Sikandar tried to raise monarchical distance above the more collegial style associated with Bahlul. Nobles were expected to:

- stand respectfully before the sultan;
- receive and honour royal **farmans**, meaning formal royal orders;
- submit accounts connected with their **jagirs**, revenue assignments supporting service;
- accept closer scrutiny of rank and obligation.

This did not eliminate Afghan bargaining. It shifted the balance toward the crown while strong chiefs retained military and territorial leverage.

### 2. Revenue, measurement and agriculture

The **gazz-i-Sikandari** was a standard of length associated with land measurement and revenue administration. Standardisation could improve comparability in assessment, but evidence does not justify assuming perfectly uniform empire-wide application.

Revenue or rent rolls strengthened the state's informational claim over agrarian resources. Later administrative regimes could use and refine inherited records; this is one bridge toward Sur and Mughal practice, not proof of an unchanged system.

Sikandar is also associated with concern for agriculture, cheap prices and grain supply.

### 3. Grain octroi, roads and justice

**Octroi** is a duty levied on goods entering a town or market. Abolishing octroi on grain could:

```text
reduce transaction cost
        ↓
ease grain movement
        ↓
support urban supply and lower prices
```

This mechanism is plausible and historically associated with Sikandar's policy. It cannot by itself prove low prices in every season or region; harvests, transport security and local exactions also mattered.

Accounts praise justice and safer highways. These claims support an image of administrative attention but should be checked against the courtly tendency to depict an able ruler as universally effective.

### 4. Why Agra mattered

Sikandar established or developed Agra as a major centre around 1504–06, conventionally dated to 1506. Its position improved access to:

- the doab and eastern Ganga routes;
- Rajasthan;
- the Malwa-Gujarat direction;
- contested zones beyond Delhi's immediate surroundings.

Agra did not instantly replace Delhi's prestige. It gave the Lodi state a strategically useful second centre and later became central to Mughal power.

### 5. Persian learning and religious orthodoxy

Sikandar gave grants to Persian scholars, philosophers, poets and men of letters, and his court attracted learned people from India and beyond. His cultural patronage also crossed linguistic boundaries:

- Sanskrit works were translated into Persian;
- Satish Chandra specifically reports the translation of rare Sanskrit works on music;
- more Hindus learned Persian and entered Persianate administrative service.

These developments belong to three related but distinct categories. **Patronage** supported scholars and translation. **Administrative culture** widened participation in Persian literacy and office. Neither category by itself establishes religious toleration.

Narrative sources also associate Sikandar with restrictive Sunni orthodoxy:

- restrictions on certain popular Muslim practices, including women's visits to saints' graves and processions commemorating saints;
- a reported restoration of **jizya**, the poll tax imposed on non-Muslim subjects;
- the execution of a Brahman reported to have maintained that Hindu and Muslim scriptures were equally sacred;
- destruction of some temples during campaigns, with Nagarkot named in Satish Chandra's account.

These are source-reported acts and should be stated with event-level attribution. They demonstrate coercive orthodoxy, but should not be inflated into proof that every locality experienced an identical policy at all times.

Avoid a false choice:

| Incomplete label | Fuller assessment |
|---|---|
| “only a patron of Persian culture” | ignores restrictions, the reported jizya restoration and coercive episodes |
| “only a religious persecutor” | erases administration, translation, Persian learning and Hindu entry into Persianate service |

A ruler can sponsor translation and administrative participation while also enforcing religiously restrictive policies. The evidence requires coexistence, not a one-word verdict.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Sikandar's reforms prove that the Sultanate's decline had ended.

**Reply:** They demonstrate recovery in the Lodi heartland, not restoration of the earlier Sultanate at its widest reach. Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal, Mewar and other powers remained autonomous, while Afghan noble expectations still limited hierarchical monarchy.

- **Prelims:** match Sikandar with Agra, *gazz-i-Sikandari*, grain-octroi abolition and noble discipline.
- **Mains use:** frame reform as visibility—accounts, measures, roads and command rituals.
- **Trap:** administrative ability and orthodoxy are not mutually exclusive descriptions.
- **Answer line:** *Sikandar made the Lodi state more legible and disciplined, but not territorially universal or socially uncontentious.*

Therefore, administrative consolidation and cultural exchange coexisted with coercive orthodoxy. Sikandar was an effective regional state-builder, not a uniformly tolerant ruler or restorer of the Sultanate's widest empire.

*Technology mattered because it was organised with mobility, barriers, discipline and command; political fragmentation shaped how effectively each army used its resources.*

### Intuition: new equipment does not fight by itself

A tool changes outcomes only when people know where to place it, how to protect it and how to coordinate it with movement. Babur's artillery was important, but guns without carts, cavalry, field arrangement, intelligence and command would not explain victory.

The analogy's limit is that exact battlefield numbers and formations come heavily through Babur's own self-representing memoir and later narratives.

### 1. Ibrahim's centralising project

Ibrahim Lodi succeeded Sikandar in 1517. He sought stronger monarchical hierarchy and attempted to discipline or replace powerful Afghan chiefs. The problem was not centralisation in the abstract; it was centralisation pursued against an elite culture accustomed to lineage status, consultation and territorial leverage.

```text
Ibrahim demands sharper subordination
              ↓
chiefs fear loss of honour, office and territory
              ↓
punishment, distrust and rebellion
              ↓
opponents seek alternative princes or external support
              ↓
the army facing Babur is politically less cohesive
```

### 2. Daulat Khan and Alam Khan

Daulat Khan Lodi, the Punjab governor, operated with substantial autonomy. Alam Khan, Ibrahim's uncle and a rival claimant, also opposed him. Both became part of the context in which Babur was invited or encouraged to intervene.

Their calculations were not necessarily identical to Babur's. They could hope to use an outside conqueror to alter the Delhi succession; Babur increasingly aimed to retain the territory for himself.

### 3. Rana Sanga and the regional field

Rana Sanga of Mewar had expanded influence in Rajasthan and Malwa. Ibrahim suffered pressure in this zone, including a reverse associated with Khatoli. This made the Lodi-Mewar rivalry another axis of north Indian politics.

Babur later alleged that Rana Sanga had invited him or promised cooperation against Ibrahim. The precise communication and promises are debated. Treat Babur's claim as evidence of his understanding and self-justification, not an independently complete transcript of an alliance.

### 4. Babur's Central Asian predicament and Indian opportunity

Babur, a Timurid prince, lost repeated contests for Samarqand and established himself at Kabul in 1504. Uzbek pressure and Kabul's limited resources encouraged attention toward Punjab and Hindustan. He also invoked Timurid claims to Punjab territories.

Earlier probes, control of frontier forts and negotiations preceded the final campaign. Babur therefore did not appear suddenly in 1526, nor did he enter an empty political space.

### 5. The Panipat system

| Element | Function in Babur's force | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| field artillery and firearms | shock, fire and disruption | important, not independently decisive |
| carts or *araba* with barriers | protected frontage and channelled attack | exact arrangement relies strongly on Babur's account |
| *tulughma* | flanking or divisional manoeuvre | term requires context; it was not a magical single tactic |
| cavalry and mounted archery | mobility, encirclement and repeated attack | depended on training and command |
| narrow field and prepared position | reduced advantage of a larger host | terrain worked with deployment |
| intelligence and discipline | coordinated response | contrasted with Lodi political strain |

Ibrahim was killed and his army defeated at the First Battle of Panipat on 21 April 1526.

### 6. Why “artillery won” is inadequate

The technology monocause fails because it cannot explain:

- why Babur's guns were tactically effective;
- why a numerically larger army could not redeploy well;
- why Afghan defections and elite hostility mattered before battle;
- why terrain and carts shaped the engagement;
- why command experience and cavalry manoeuvre multiplied firepower.

The stronger explanation is **combined arms plus political cohesion**.

### 7. What ended—and what did not

Panipat ended Lodi dynastic rule at Delhi and conventionally closes the Delhi Sultanate phase. It did not instantly create a stable Mughal empire. Babur still had to confront Rana Sanga and Afghan resistance; after Babur, Humayun faced a powerful Afghan revival culminating in Sher Shah.

### Visual: triangulation rather than source obedience

```text
Narrative texts -------- rulers, campaigns, claims, sequence
      |                  limit: patronage, genre, hindsight
      |
Coins ------------------ titles, mints, monetary assertion
      |                  limit: circulation ≠ full territorial control
      |

      |                  limit: selective survival and formula
      |
Architecture ----------- resources, urban patronage, regional style
                         limit: dating, rebuilding, survival bias

                 CROSS-CHECK
                     ↓
          graded historical conclusion
```

*No single source measures the entire state; agreement and tension among source types create the best reconstruction.*

### Intuition: each source is a window with a frame

A window reveals part of a landscape while its frame excludes other parts. A victory chronicle reveals political language and campaign sequence, but usually not the experience of every cultivator. A coin reveals a claim and minting capacity, but not routine obedience in every village.

The solution is not cynicism. Historians compare windows.

### 1. Timurid and Delhi chronicles

| Source family | What it helps establish | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Timurid memoir traditions | Timur's route, commands and self-presentation | textual history and conqueror-centred rhetoric |
| Shami and Yazdi *Zafarnama* traditions | campaign narrative and Timurid legitimacy | court-sponsored victory history |
| Afif and other Indian chronicles | Tughlaq structures and remembered disruption | patronage, chronology and elite focus |
| Yahya Sirhindi, *Tarikh-i Mubarak Shahi* | late Tughlaq and Sayyid sequence | Delhi-centred field of vision |
| Babur's memoir | motives, negotiations and Panipat deployment | royal self-fashioning and uncertain numbers |
| later chronicles | retrospective memory and comparison | hindsight and possible inflation |

The method is to distinguish event, interpretation and rhetorical purpose.

### 2. Coins and inscriptions

Coins can establish:

- ruler names and titles;
- minting claims;
- monetary continuity or change;
- the symbolic reach of sovereignty.

They cannot automatically establish:

- uniform circulation;
- uninterrupted control over the mint's surrounding countryside;
- social acceptance of every royal claim.

Inscriptions can date buildings, identify patrons and preserve titles or religious formulas. Survival is patchy, reuse is possible and formulaic praise may exceed effective capacity.

### 3. Architecture as political evidence

Sayyid-Lodi architecture belongs to a transitional Delhi landscape. Tombs became especially visible expressions of lineage, legitimacy and elite commemoration. Lodi-period monuments are associated with compact domed forms, both square and octagonal plans, and garden or landscaped settings that anticipate later developments.

Sharqi architecture at Jaunpur used monumental façades, lofty portals and broad arches to create a regional language of authority. Difference from Delhi was neither total isolation nor simple imitation.

Architecture proves mobilisation of artisans, materials and patronage. It does not by itself reveal the complete tax system or popular response.

### 4. Continuity across dynastic labels

The 1526 transition changed the ruling house and military balance, but several institutions continued in altered forms:

- Persian chancery and documentary culture;
- revenue assignments often described through iqta/jagir-like arrangements;
- qazis and other judicial or administrative officers;
- local revenue personnel and intermediaries;
- urban mints and strategic capitals;
- Afghan military networks.

Babur's regime had to work through this inherited terrain. Afghan power did not vanish: it reassembled under later leaders, most successfully Sher Shah Sur. Agra and Delhi continued as major centres.

### 5. Periodisation without inevitability

It is convenient to say:

```text
Delhi Sultanate ends in 1526 → Mughal period begins
```

But historical process is less abrupt:

```text
late Sultanate institutions + regional states + Afghan networks
                         ↓
Babur's conquest and new military-political coalition
                         ↓
continued contest under Humayun and the Surs
                         ↓
later Mughal consolidation
```

Nothing in 1398 made Babur's victory inevitable. The path passed through Sayyid survival, Sharqi state formation, Bahlul's coalition, Sikandar's recovery, Ibrahim's conflict and contingent alliances.

### Original Mains application

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** How can material and narrative sources be combined to reconstruct the decline and transition of the Delhi Sultanate?

**Model answer**

The question asks for method, so the answer must show what each source establishes and how cross-checking controls bias. Narrative sources provide the political sequence. Yahya Sirhindi's *Tarikh-i Mubarak Shahi* records late Tughlaq and Sayyid rulers, factions and campaigns; Timurid *Zafarnama* traditions narrate the 1398 invasion; Babur's memoir describes negotiations and Panipat. These texts reveal elite vocabulary and intentions. Their courtly genres, self-justification and uncertain numbers, however, limit direct acceptance.

Material evidence supplies different tests. **Claim:** coins express sovereignty. **Evidence:** ruler names, titles and mints. **Analysis:** they trace monetary and dynastic assertion. **Qualification:** circulation does not prove uniform territorial control. **Claim:** inscriptions and buildings reveal local patronage. **Evidence:** dated foundations, Sayyid-Lodi tombs and Jaunpur's monumental mosque façades. **Analysis:** they demonstrate urban resources, artisan traditions and regional political languages. **Qualification:** survival and attribution are selective.

Triangulation resolves apparent contradictions. Delhi-to-Palam rhetoric can be balanced against coins and monuments showing continued authority; Timurid claims of total devastation can be placed beside the survival of chancery, revenue and urban institutions. Similarly, Babur's firearms must be read with battlefield organisation and Lodi political fracture.

The transition is therefore reconstructed as severe imperial contraction, regional state formation and institutional continuity—not either uninterrupted empire or complete civilisational rupture.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks | 150 words

**Question:** Explain why the fifteenth century in north India should be understood as regionalisation rather than a political dark age.

**Directive:** “Explain” requires the mechanism and supporting evidence, not a list of kingdoms.

**Model answer**

Delhi's decline redistributed rather than extinguished sovereignty. After Firuz Shah, succession conflict, entrenched nobles, weaker revenue flows and provincial assertion reduced the capital's fiscal-military reach; Timur's 1398 sack accelerated this contraction.

Regional authorities then converted provincial offices, agrarian resources and military followings into states. Malik Sarwar's eastern command, expressed in the title Malik-us-Sharq, became the base of Sharqi Jaunpur. The Sharqis maintained order, checked Bengal and contested the Ganga plain until Bahlul's annexation in 1484. Gujarat, Malwa and Mewar formed a western balance, while Bengal and Orissa checked each other in the east.

Cultural evidence reinforces this political vitality. Jaunpur's monumental gateways and scholarly patronage produced its “Shiraz of the East” reputation, though the phrase is an honorific rather than literal equivalence.

The period remained violent and fragmented, but fragmentation generated competing state formation, diplomacy and patronage. “Regionalisation” therefore captures both Delhi's decline and the active reconstruction of power elsewhere.

**Why this earns marks:** It gives a causal thesis, names Malik Sarwar, Jaunpur and the western balance, and qualifies both violence and the Shiraz label.

## 15 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate the achievements and limitations of Sikandar Lodi as a state-builder.

**Directive:** “Evaluate” requires criteria, evidence on both sides and a graded verdict.

**Model answer**

Sikandar Lodi was the strongest administrative consolidator of the Lodi dynasty, but his state-building remained regionally and politically bounded.

**Royal authority:** He required nobles to stand before him, respect royal farmans and submit jagir accounts. These rituals and records made service obligations more visible and shifted Bahlul's collegial Afghan balance toward monarchy. Yet powerful chiefs retained lineage prestige and territorial leverage.

**Fiscal-agrarian supervision:** The *gazz-i-Sikandari* standardised measurement relevant to assessment, while revenue rolls improved information. Such records provided an institutional bridge later regimes could refine. However, normative measurement does not prove uniform implementation.

**Market and communication:** Abolition of grain octroi could reduce entry costs and ease urban supply; attention to roads, security and justice supported movement and trade. Courtly praise of cheap prices must nevertheless be qualified by harvest and local variation.

**Strategic geography:** Agra, founded or developed around 1504–06 and conventionally dated to 1506, improved access to the doab, Rajasthan and Malwa routes. It complemented rather than instantly erased Delhi.

**Culture and religion:** Sikandar patronised Persian learning and poetry while also enforcing Sunni orthodoxy and restrictions against some practices and institutions. Administrative ability cannot cancel exclusion, nor should orthodoxy erase governance.

Sikandar strengthened a north Indian heartland, not a restored pan-Indian Sultanate: Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal and Mewar remained autonomous. His achievement was substantial recovery through discipline, information and location; his limit was the enduring regional balance and unresolved Afghan crown-chief bargain.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates through explicit criteria, attaches a qualification to each evidence unit and offers a graded rather than binary verdict.

## 20 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Trace the transition from late Tughlaq contraction to Mughal victory in 1526 without reducing it to a story of inevitable dynastic decline.

**Directive:** “Trace” requires chronological causation; the final clause demands contingency rather than hindsight.

**Model answer**

The transition began with structural contraction, not a predetermined Mughal ascent. After Firuz Shah's death in 1388, succession disputes, noble and slave factions, hereditary concessions and provincial revenue retention weakened Delhi's fiscal-military capacity. Timur exploited this vulnerability in 1398, devastating Delhi and deporting wealth, captives and artisans; his withdrawal, however, left no permanent Timurid Indian administration.

The next stage was political multiplication. Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal, Mewar and Sharqi Jaunpur built active regional states. Khizr Khan established Sayyid rule at Delhi in 1414 through a Punjab base and Timurid legitimacy, but tribute campaigns against Khokhars, Mewatis and other challengers reveal restricted sovereignty.

Bahlul Lodi rebuilt power through Afghan recruitment from Roh, kinship bargains and military pressure. His 1484 annexation of Jaunpur enlarged the Lodi resource base, though grants to chiefs entrenched autonomy. Sikandar then tightened noble discipline, audited jagirs, used the *gazz-i-Sikandari*, abolished grain octroi and developed Agra around 1506.

Contingency sharpened under Ibrahim. His hierarchical centralisation alienated Afghan chiefs; Daulat Khan and Alam Khan engaged Babur, while Rana Sanga pressed from the Mewar-Malwa field. Babur's own loss of Samarqand, Kabul base and Punjab ambitions brought an experienced claimant into this coalition struggle.

At Panipat, artillery mattered only within carts, cavalry, flanking, terrain and disciplined command. Lodi political fracture amplified the tactical disadvantage. The battle ended Lodi rule, but continued Afghan and Rajput resistance shows that neither 1398 nor 1526 made Mughal consolidation inevitable.

**Why this earns marks:** It preserves chronology, integrates structures with choices, uses named evidence at every phase and ends by disproving teleology.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Chronology spine

- **1388:** death of Firuz Shah; succession and factional conflict intensify.
- **1398:** Timur invades and sacks Delhi.
- **1412:** effective Tughlaq line ends.
- **1414:** Khizr Khan establishes Sayyid rule at Delhi.
- **1414–1451:** Sayyid dynasty.
- **1451:** Bahlul Lodi takes Delhi; Lodi dynasty begins.
- **1484:** Bahlul annexes Sharqi Jaunpur.
- **1489–1517:** Sikandar Lodi.
- **c. 1504–06/1506:** Agra established or developed as a strategic Lodi centre.
- **1517:** Ibrahim Lodi succeeds.
- **21 April 1526:** Babur defeats Ibrahim at the First Battle of Panipat.

## B. Structural decline before Timur

- No fixed succession rule converted royal death into competition among princes and nobles.
- Firuzi slaves formed a separate faction after the ruler on whom they depended died.
- Hereditary concessions and entrenched assignments reduced the centre's freedom to rotate and discipline officeholders.
- Provincial revenue retention reduced central military capacity.
- Governors, zamindars and regional rulers widened autonomy.
- “Delhi to Palam” captures the rhetoric of contracted reach; it is not a measured boundary.
- Best chain: elite fragmentation → revenue contraction → weaker coercion → provincial assertion → further contraction.

## C. Timur in 1398

- Central Asian conqueror based in Transoxiana and Samarqand.
- Motives: plunder, prestige, army reward, frontier strategy and religious self-justification.
- Religious language is evidence of legitimation, not proof of a single motive.
- Route passed through the Indus-Punjab corridor toward Delhi.
- Delhi battle and sack involved mass killing, enslavement and extraction.
- Artisans such as masons, stone-cutters and carpenters were deported toward Samarqand.
- Exact casualty figures remain uncertain.
- Timur withdrew; no durable all-India Timurid administration followed.
- Verdict: devastating catalyst, not original or sole cause.

## D. Regionalisation and Jaunpur

- Delhi's retreat generated multiple state centres rather than a political void.
- Gujarat, Malwa and Mewar formed a western competitive balance.
- Bengal, Orissa and Jaunpur checked one another in the east.
- Malik Sarwar's title **Malik-us-Sharq** produced the name Sharqi.
- Jaunpur maintained order, challenged Delhi and checked Bengal.
- Sharqi architecture developed monumental gateways and broad arches.
- “Shiraz of the East” denotes cultural prestige, not literal equivalence.
- Bahlul annexed Jaunpur in 1484.

## E. Sayyid ruler ladder and political form

1. **Khizr Khan, 1414–1421:** Punjab-Multan base; Timurid association; limited sovereign projection.
2. **Mubarak Shah, 1421–1434:** campaigns against Khokhars, Mewatis, nobles; murdered.
3. **Muhammad Shah, 1434–1445:** factional pressure and shrinking effective reach.
4. **Ala al-Din Alam Shah, 1445–1451:** withdrew to Badaun; Bahlul took Delhi.

- Timurid legitimacy did not make Delhi a simple governed province.
- Sayyid descent functioned as legitimating genealogy but is not beyond dispute.
- Tribute acknowledged superiority without proving routine administration.
- Repeated tribute expeditions show both coercive capacity and limited consolidation.

## F. Bahlul and Afghan political culture

- Power base: Sirhind and Punjab.
- Checked Khokhars and mobilised Afghans, including from Roh.
- Took Delhi in 1451 after Alam Shah's withdrawal.
- Used kinship, consultation, grants, honour and force.
- Afghan “equality” means noble-lineage bargaining, not democracy.
- Prolonged struggle with Jaunpur culminated in annexation in 1484.
- Grants and partition strengthened the coalition while entrenching future rivals.

## G. Sikandar Lodi

- Tightened noble discipline: standing before sultan, honouring farmans, submitting jagir accounts.
- *Gazz-i-Sikandari*: measurement standard linked to revenue.
- Rent/revenue rolls improved fiscal information.
- Grain-octroi abolition could ease movement and reduce transaction cost.
- Justice, safer roads and agricultural concern feature in his administrative reputation.
- Agra developed around 1504–06, conventionally 1506, for doab-Rajasthan-Malwa strategy.
- Gave grants to Persian scholars, philosophers, poets and men of letters.
- Sponsored Sanskrit-to-Persian translation, including reported music-related works.
- More Hindus learned Persian and entered Persianate administrative posts.
- Restricted certain popular Muslim practices, including women's visits to saints' graves and commemorative processions.
- Satish Chandra reports restoration of jizya, the Brahman execution episode and temple destruction at Nagarkot.
- These religious episodes require source attribution and do not prove identical enforcement in every locality.
- Cultural patronage, administrative participation and restrictive orthodoxy are distinct dimensions that coexisted.
- Strong recovery did not restore control over every regional state.

## H. Ibrahim and Panipat

- Ibrahim's sharper hierarchy collided with Afghan chief expectations.
- Daulat Khan in Punjab and Alam Khan opposed him and engaged Babur.
- Rana Sanga pressured the Lodi position from Mewar-Malwa.
- Precise Sanga-Babur communications require qualification.
- Babur held Kabul from 1504 and entered after repeated probes.
- Panipat system: artillery/firearms + carts/barriers + cavalry + *tulughma* + terrain + discipline.
- Political cohesion and command converted equipment into battlefield effect.
- Artillery-alone explanation is inadequate.
- Panipat ended Lodi rule but not Afghan or Rajput resistance.

## I. Evidence and source limits

- Shami and Yazdi *Zafarnama* traditions: campaign and Timurid legitimacy; court-victory bias.
- Yahya Sirhindi's *Tarikh-i Mubarak Shahi*: late Tughlaq-Sayyid sequence; Delhi-centred.
- Afif and other chronicles: Tughlaq structures; elite and patronage limits.
- Babur's memoir: close political-military evidence; self-representation and uncertain numbers.
- Coins: titles and minting claims; not uniform territorial control.
- Inscriptions: patrons, dates and formulas; patchy survival.
- Architecture: resources, legitimacy and regional style; attribution and survival limits.
- Best method: triangulate texts and material evidence.

## J. Ten answer-grabbing lines

1. The Sultanate contracted before Timur because elite fragmentation and revenue loss reinforced one another.
2. Timur was a devastating catalyst whose withdrawal limited direct political replacement.
3. Delhi's decline redistributed sovereignty to regional states rather than creating a civilisational vacuum.
4. Sayyid sovereignty was real in claim and action but uneven in territorial depth.
5. Tribute campaigns expressed overlordship while revealing incomplete consolidation.
6. Bahlul's negotiated kingship converted Afghan lineage networks into expansion.
7. Afghan noble parity must not be confused with equality of subjects.
8. Sikandar strengthened state visibility through discipline, accounts, measurement, roads and Agra.
9. Ibrahim's centralising project failed politically before Babur exploited it militarily.
10. At Panipat, technology became effective through organisation, terrain, mobility and command.
