# Decline of the Mughal Empire and the Eighteenth Century — Live Session Edition

The central question is not, “Why did India suddenly collapse after 1707?” Nothing so simple happened. Mughal command weakened unevenly, provincial and local powers reorganised authority, trade and culture continued in many regions, and the imperial name remained politically useful long after the emperor lost effective control.

The word **decline** is useful only after identifying what is declining. Mughal central fiscal-military command contracted sharply. The dynasty, however, survived. Regional administration did not disappear; it shifted toward governors, zamindars, chiefs and military entrepreneurs. Economic and cultural activity also changed location rather than vanishing everywhere.

### 1. The date frame

Aurangzeb died at Ahmadnagar in **1707**. His death opened a succession contest and exposed strains accumulated during the long Deccan war. The core sequence then runs through:

- Bahadur Shah I and renewed court faction;
- Jahandar Shah, Farrukhsiyar and the struggle over the wizarat;
- Muhammad Shah and provincial consolidation;
- Nadir Shah's invasion in 1739;
- Abdali's invasions after 1748; and
- the Third Battle of Panipat in **1761**.

Why stop at 1761? Panipat ended the Peshwa's immediate bid for supremacy in northern India and reveals the political order created by Mughal contraction. It did **not** end the Mughal dynasty or Maratha power. Shah Alam II later returned to Delhi with Maratha support, and the dynasty formally survived until 1857. Those later events prove the difference between effective command and symbolic legitimacy.

### 2. Source ladder

| Evidence | Best use | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Persian chronicles and newsletters | court politics, appointments, campaigns | patronage, factional blame and elite silence |
| farmans, sanads and revenue records | legal claims, offices, jagirs and assessments | an order does not prove implementation |
| regional Persian, Marathi and Sikh materials | non-Delhi politics and memory | genre, chronology and retrospective claims |
| company records | trade, credit, ports and company interests | company-centred and commercially selective |
| coins, seals, khutba and titles | public claims to legitimacy | symbolism does not measure coercive reach |
| urban, architectural and artistic evidence | patronage and cultural movement | cannot alone measure general prosperity |

The key method is to separate **representation**, **legal claim** and **implemented power**. A coin in an emperor's name proves that his legitimacy remained useful; it does not prove that he collected the province's revenue.

### 3. Decline and transformation

The older “dark age” picture treats imperial fragmentation as general decay. A regionalisation interpretation points instead to Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, Maratha domains, Jat Bharatpur and Sikh misls as centres of new authority. Neither extreme is sufficient.

The strongest frame is two-level:

```text
decline of Delhi's command
            +
regional redistribution of revenue, armies and patronage
            +
uneven violence, extraction and commercial continuity
```

Regional vitality does not cancel crisis. A prosperous textile zone could coexist with coercive revenue collection. Cultural florescence at Lucknow or Hyderabad does not restore Mughal military coordination. Conversely, court disorder at Delhi does not prove that all Indian agriculture and trade collapsed.

### UPSC application

Open an answer by defining decline as the contraction of enforceable imperial command. Then separate centre, regions, economy and legitimacy. This prevents both instant-collapse and uniform-prosperity errors.

### Original Mains application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish the decline of Mughal imperial power from the condition of eighteenth-century India.

**Model answer**

Mughal decline means the contraction of Delhi's ability to command revenue, appointments, armies and provincial obedience. It does not mean that the dynasty vanished in 1707 or that India entered uniform anarchy. Mughal emperors remained sources of titles and legitimacy, while Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad converted provincial office into substantial autonomy. Marathas, Jats, Sikhs and Rohillas created other political formations.

The economy also followed regional paths. Warfare and multiple fiscal claims damaged some routes and agrarian zones, yet Bengal's commerce, merchant credit, internal trade and new courts remained active. Artists and scholars moved from Delhi to regional patrons rather than ceasing production.

Thus, the eighteenth century combined central imperial decline, regional state-building and uneven social costs. “Transformation” corrects a total-collapse thesis, but it must not conceal coercion, fiscal strain or the loss of coordinated defence.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the level of decline, supplies contrasting regional evidence, and ends with a qualified two-level verdict.

### Visual: why succession was a state-wide contest

```text
NO FIXED PRIMOGENITURE
        |
princes need provincial bases + treasuries + armies + noble coalitions
        |
rapid marches and field battle decide succession
        |
victors reward supporters with mansab, office and jagir
        |
resources and appointments are redistributed after every contest
```

The succession conflict was not simply a family quarrel. Because no rule of primogeniture automatically made the eldest son emperor, each prince had to turn provincial command, money and patronage into armed victory.

### 1. From Aurangzeb to Bahadur Shah

Aurangzeb's sons included Muazzam, Azam and Kam Bakhsh. Muazzam, who took the title **Bahadur Shah I** or Shah Alam I, reached the north with the stronger logistical position. He defeated Azam at **Jajau in June 1707** and Kam Bakhsh near Hyderabad in **January 1709**.

The empire remained territorially united during Bahadur Shah's short reign, 1707-12. Yet its political centre became less coherent. Experienced nobles expected reward after decades of Deccan service, while the emperor also needed to reconcile former opponents.

### 2. Conciliation with limits

Bahadur Shah tried accommodation in several directions:

- he restored Rajput rulers after initial coercive moves;
- he treated Shahu as a possible Maratha negotiating partner;
- he reduced some of Aurangzeb's religious-political tensions;
- he confronted Banda Bahadur's Sikh rising during his final years.

These measures were not a settled liberal programme. Policy oscillated because the emperor, wazir, princes and leading nobles did not agree on the terms of conciliation. Restoring a ruler's homeland while posting him far away, for example, preserved service but did not rebuild confidence.

### 3. Wizarat becomes the hinge

Bahadur Shah appointed Munim Khan wazir while giving Asad Khan and Zulfiqar Khan high standing. The important institutional change was that the wazir was increasingly imagined not merely as a financial specialist but as the leading noble who might coordinate the whole political system.

That solution contained a danger:

```text
weak or overextended emperor
        -> stronger coordinating wazir
        -> rival nobles fear monopoly of patronage
        -> factions form around office and succession
        -> emperor fears the wazir
```

### 4. Fiscal inheritance

Bahadur Shah found substantial treasure at Agra, but expenditure, grants and campaigns rapidly consumed resources. Liberal mansab and inam grants widened the pressure on suitable jagirs. The problem was not generosity alone: an emperor seeking loyalty after civil war had to reward followers. Political survival therefore intensified fiscal strain.

### 5. Banda Bahadur boundary

Banda Bahadur's movement challenged Mughal power in Punjab after Guru Gobind Singh. Bahadur Shah spent much of his final period pursuing it. This topic uses the conflict to show northern military pressure and the rise of a durable Sikh political tradition; detailed Sikh religious development belongs its own historical sequence.

### UPSC application

Use 1707 to connect institutional design with political behaviour: no primogeniture made provincial resources and noble coalitions decisive, while the reward cycle deepened jagir pressure.

### Original Mains application

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Explain how the Mughal succession system converted dynastic conflict into an institutional crisis after 1707.

**Model answer**

The Mughals lacked an accepted law of primogeniture. A prince's dynastic status was therefore insufficient; he needed provincial revenue, a mobile army and noble allies. In 1707 Muazzam's access to northern resources and rapid movement helped him defeat Azam at Jajau and become Bahadur Shah I.

Every succession war disrupted administration and destroyed men and money. The victor then had to confirm old nobles, reward new supporters and neutralise defeated factions through mansabs, offices and jagirs. This widened claims upon a revenue system already strained by the Deccan war and jama-hasil mismatch. Nobles consequently treated control of the throne and wizarat as routes to scarce assignments.

Bahadur Shah's conciliation of Rajputs and Shahu illustrates the political need for compromise, while his campaigns against Kam Bakhsh and Banda Bahadur show continuing military demands. The empire remained united, but the resources needed to sustain unity became more contested.

Succession conflict was therefore neither the sole cause nor mere court drama. It was the mechanism through which fiscal scarcity, provincial power and noble patronage repeatedly entered the heart of imperial government.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the institutional mechanism linking succession, resources, patronage and fiscal strain rather than listing rulers.

### 1. Jahandar Shah and Zulfiqar Khan

Bahadur Shah's death in 1712 caused another succession war. **Jahandar Shah**, who ruled only in 1712-13, depended heavily on **Zulfiqar Khan**. The wazir tried to restrain new appointments, enforce musters and restore fiscal discipline, but strong rivals resented one noble controlling patronage.

Revenue farming, or **ijara**, spread as officials sought predictable contracted payments when ordinary collection weakened. It could supply cash to the state, but a farmer who had prepaid or guaranteed revenue had strong incentives to extract rapidly. It was a response to administrative breakdown as well as a source of further pressure.

Lal Kunwar's influence has often supplied moralising stories about Jahandar Shah. The stronger explanation is political: old nobles disliked the redistribution of honour and access, the army lacked pay, and competing coalitions undermined command. Lal Kunwar was not a second Nur Jahan directing government.

### 2. Farrukhsiyar and the Sayyid Brothers

Farrukhsiyar defeated Jahandar Shah in January 1713 with the decisive support of **Sayyid Abdullah Khan Barha** and **Sayyid Husain Ali Khan Barha**. Abdullah became wazir and Husain Ali mir bakhshi. Their power came from military following, high office, control of patronage and alliances—not from a fixed ethnic community acting as one party.

Farrukhsiyar wanted ministers to execute the emperor's will. The Sayyids wanted major appointments and decisions to pass through the wizarat. Their conflict was therefore constitutional in the pre-modern sense: who controlled the practical levers of monarchy?

### 3. Policy was not simply paralysis

The Sayyid-led order:

- abolished jizyah;
- sought Rajput support;
- negotiated with Shahu;
- accepted Maratha collection of chauth and sardeshmukhi in the Deccan in return for payment and military assistance.

These measures reveal political adaptation. They also reveal weakness: the centre recognised claims it could not suppress and needed Maratha troops in court conflict.

### 4. Deposition and “king-making”

Farrukhsiyar repeatedly conspired against the brothers. Husain Ali returned from the Deccan with Maratha support. Farrukhsiyar was deposed in **February 1719** and later killed. The Sayyids then installed **Rafi-ud-Darajat** and **Rafi-ud-Daulah, titled Shah Jahan II**, both of whom died during 1719, before selecting Muhammad Shah.

“King-maker” should not mean that the Sayyids possessed unlimited power. They depended on their military following and negotiated alliances. Rival nobles, including Nizam-ul-Mulk and Muhammad Amin Khan, mobilised against them. Husain Ali was assassinated and Abdullah defeated in **1720**.

### 5. No ethnic determinism

Labels such as Turani, Irani, Hindustani, Afghan and Barha Sayyid described origin, kinship or service networks. They did not create disciplined parties with permanent policies. Nizam-ul-Mulk could cooperate with Marathas despite anti-Maratha rhetoric; Rajput and Mughal nobles changed sides according to office, honour, locality and survival.

### UPSC application

Explain faction as competition over scarce patronage conducted through networks. Do not write “Iranis versus Turanis” as if two modern political parties controlled every decision.

### Original Mains application

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Was the rise of the Sayyid Brothers merely evidence of weak emperors?

**Model answer**

Weak royal authority enabled the Sayyid Brothers, but their rise reveals a deeper struggle over the organisation of government. Farrukhsiyar owed his 1713 victory to Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali Khan Barha, who received the wizarat and mir bakhshi's office. They controlled military support, access and appointments; the emperor attempted to bypass them through favourites.

The conflict concerned who exercised monarchy in practice. The Sayyids abolished jizyah, sought Rajput support and negotiated Maratha chauth and sardeshmukhi in exchange for service. These were policy choices, not mere palace intrigue, but they also showed dependence on forces outside the court.

Their deposition and killing of Farrukhsiyar in 1719 and installation of brief successor emperors justify “king-makers.” Yet the brothers were not sovereign. Rival nobles mobilised their own networks, Husain Ali was assassinated, and Abdullah was defeated in 1720.

The episode therefore combined personal weakness with scarce patronage, militarised noble coalitions and disagreement over the wizarat. It was an institutional crisis expressed through personalities, not simply the consequence of an incapable emperor.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates the label through offices, policies, coalition dependence and the brothers' eventual defeat.

## Lesson 4 — Power shrinks, legitimacy travels

```text
revenue remittance falls           imperial titles remain valuable
provincial armies localise         appointments still seek sanction
frontier defence weakens           coins/khutba retain the royal name
          \                                  /
           \                                /
            SUCCESSOR STATES USE IMPERIAL FORM
            WHILE CONTROLLING REGIONAL SUBSTANCE
```

### 1. Muhammad Shah's long reign

**Muhammad Shah** ruled from 1719 to 1748. The Sayyids were overthrown in 1720, but their fall did not restore the old concentration of authority. Muhammad Amin Khan briefly served as wazir. Nizam-ul-Mulk then attempted reforms, including scrutiny of jagirs and opposition to revenue farming, but encountered resistance from entrenched interests.

Nizam-ul-Mulk left Delhi politics and defeated Mubariz Khan at **Shakar Kheda in October 1724**, establishing de facto autonomy in Hyderabad. Bengal and Awadh also consolidated regional control. The imperial court retained ritual and appointments, but could not reliably coordinate provinces or defend the frontier.

Muhammad Shah's pleasure-loving image and the title “Rangila” are not explanations. He did fail to provide sustained administrative and military leadership, but institutional scarcity, faction and provincial resources shaped the choices available to any ruler.

### 2. Later emperors

| Emperor | Reign | Causal relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Ahmad Shah Bahadur | 1748-54 | Abdali pressure and court conflict intensified |
| Alamgir II | 1754-59 | dominated by Imad-ul-Mulk; killed in 1759 |
| Shah Jahan III | 1759-60 | brief claimant supported in Delhi politics |
| Shah Alam II | from 1759/60 | imperial name survived while he lacked stable control of Delhi |

Shah Alam II's later participation at Buxar in 1764 and the 1765 Diwani settlement belong to Modern Indian History. Here his position demonstrates that imperial legitimacy survived the loss of an imperial territorial machine.

### 3. Why legitimacy survived

Mughal sovereignty supplied a shared political vocabulary:

- titles and mansabs;
- farmans and sanads;
- provincial subedari;
- coin and khutba;
- courtly rank and Persianate ceremony.

A regional ruler could therefore gain practical autonomy without declaring a modern national independence. Retaining imperial form reduced the cost of inventing a new legal order and helped negotiate with rival elites.

### 4. The strongest objection

**Objection:** If governors ignored Delhi, Mughal legitimacy was empty.

**Reply:** It was weaker than coercion, but not empty. Competitors sought imperial titles; Marathas negotiated grants; regional dynasties used Mughal offices; and later powers treated the emperor's sanction as politically useful. Symbolic authority could not command armies by itself, but it shaped how new power was represented.

### UPSC application

Use the phrase **continuity of form, localisation of substance**. It explains why imperial institutions survived inside autonomous regimes.

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Topics 16, 21 and 23 plus Satish Chandra's decline chapters
CA search: not required
CA found: none used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: from imperial promise to military weakness

```text
MANSAB gives rank, pay claim and service obligation
                    |
JAGIR assigns a transferable revenue claim
                    |
JAMA records expected revenue
                    |
HASIL is what can actually be collected
                    |
low hasil + scarce suitable PAIBAQI
                    |
BE-JAGIRI or inadequate assignment
                    |
weak contingent / debt / extraction / faction
```

### 1. The terms

| Term | Exact meaning | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| mansab | graded imperial rank linking status, pay and service | treating it as a hereditary estate |
| jagir | transferable claim to collect assigned state revenue | treating it as ownership of land |
| jama | assessed revenue on paper | assuming it was realised cash |
| hasil | revenue actually realised | treating shortfall as bookkeeping only |
| paibaqi | revenue area available or awaiting assignment | assuming every listed area was productive |
| be-jagiri | absence or delay of a suitable assignment | claiming that no land existed |

### 2. Why the gap widened

Under Shah Jahan the number and rank claims of nobles had already grown faster than assessed revenue. Month-scale adjustments and reduced muster requirements recognised that a nominal jagir could not always support full rank.

The late Deccan war sharpened the problem:

- Bijapur and Golconda added territory but also garrisons, officials and claimants;
- Deccani and Maratha entrants required mansabs and assignments;
- warfare and parallel chauth collection reduced actual hasil;
- long communications raised military costs;
- northern assignments were already heavily claimed.

The result was not a literal disappearance of land. It was a shortage of **reliably collectible revenue** relative to authorised claims.

### 3. The feedback loop

```text
poor or delayed jagir
      -> noble cannot finance expected contingent
      -> presses local collectors, zamindars and cultivators
      -> resistance, concealment, flight or private bargain
      -> hasil falls further below jama
      -> competition for better jagirs intensifies
      -> patronage and faction become more valuable
```

Frequent transfers could prevent hereditary entrenchment, but they also shortened the holder's time horizon. A jagirdar unsure of next year's assignment had less incentive to protect long-term cultivation.

### 4. Debate and limits

The jagirdari thesis does not claim:

- every mansabdar was without a jagir;
- all agriculture stopped;
- corruption alone caused decline;
- the same crisis intensity existed in every province;
- fiscal strain automatically produced British rule.

It explains how the imperial service bargain weakened. Nobles expected rank and resources; the centre increasingly offered paper claims whose realised income was uncertain.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Regional states retained jagirs and revenue systems, so jagirdari cannot explain decline.

**Reply:** The crisis concerned the transferable imperial allocation system. Regional regimes often made assignments more locally anchored or hereditary, reducing Delhi's transfer power. Institutional survival at a different level can therefore demonstrate imperial devolution rather than refute it.

The thesis does not imply that all jagirs or agriculture disappeared. It explains a self-reinforcing breakdown in the imperial bargain linking revenue, service and loyalty. War, rural power and regional variation complete the mechanism.

### Visual: revenue was a negotiated chain

```text
IMPERIAL ASSESSMENT
       |
provincial officials / jagirdars
       |
zamindars, village leaders, revenue farmers
       |
cultivators and local production
       |
actual collection depends on harvest + force + bargain + legitimacy
```

The Mughal state did not reach each cultivator without intermediaries. Zamindars possessed local knowledge, social authority, armed retainers and claims over revenue. They could serve the empire, bargain with it, resist it or use its crisis to enlarge local power.

### 1. Agrarian pressure was uneven

Revenue demand did not mechanically cause one empire-wide peasant revolution. Outcomes varied:

- cultivators could conceal produce, flee or shift allegiance;
- zamindars could withhold payment or fortify themselves;
- revenue farmers could intensify collection;
- local chiefs could turn resistance into state formation;
- war could destroy one zone while military demand stimulated another.

Irfan Habib's agrarian-crisis approach directs attention to extraction and resistance. Its strength is to move beyond court biography. Its limit appears when regional commercial expansion, cooperation between zamindars and states, and varying ecological conditions are treated as secondary.

### 2. Ijara and intermediary power

Under **ijara**, the state contracted revenue collection to a farmer for a fixed amount. This could convert uncertain collection into a promised payment. Yet the contractor needed to recover his advance and profit, creating incentives for intense extraction. Merchants and bankers could enter revenue administration through credit, but this did not mean that all revenue farmers behaved identically.

### 3. Military capacity

Mughal military weakness was not simply technological backwardness. The empire still possessed cavalry, artillery, fortresses and experienced commanders. It defeated Ahmad Shah Abdali at **Manupur in 1748**, showing retained capacity under effective leadership.

The deeper weakness was coordination:

```text
uncertain revenue -> unpaid or under-equipped troops
localised armies -> loyalty to commander or province
slow intelligence -> frontier surprise
faction -> fragmented command
heavy camp -> reduced mobility
```

European drill and naval power mattered increasingly in coastal and Company warfare, but no single weapon explains Mughal contraction. Military systems depend on regular finance, supply, command and political trust.

### 4. Administrative erosion

The centre's information problem grew as provinces withheld remittance and appointments became contested. A farman could name a governor, but the successful office-holder needed local military and fiscal support. This gap between appointment and enforcement made sovereignty negotiable.

### 5. From pressure to regionalisation

Agrarian and zamindar resistance could weaken imperial extraction, yet local power did not remain anti-state. Jat, Sikh and other leaders built forts, collected revenue and disciplined territory. Former resistance could become a new state apparatus.

### UPSC application

### 1. Shahu's release did not settle the Maratha polity

Azam Shah released **Shahu** in 1707. Shahu claimed the legacy of Shivaji against **Tarabai**, who represented the Kolhapur line. His success required the support of chiefs and the political work of **Balaji Vishwanath**, appointed Peshwa in 1713.

There is no secure basis for saying that Azam granted Shahu all later fiscal rights at release. The important imperial recognition came through the Sayyid-Maratha negotiations of 1718 and formal rescripts associated with the 1719 Delhi intervention.

### 2. Chauth and sardeshmukhi

| Claim | Simplified meaning | What it did not automatically mean |
|---|---|---|
| chauth | claim commonly framed as one-fourth of assessed revenue | direct administration or ownership of the territory |
| sardeshmukhi | additional claim commonly framed as ten per cent | replacement of all local officials by Maratha officers |

These claims could represent tribute, protection payment, negotiated fiscal sharing or the prelude to deeper control. Their political meaning depended on who collected, who enforced and whether Maratha officers established a durable local presence.

### 3. Baji Rao and northward expansion

**Baji Rao I**, Peshwa from 1720 to 1740, argued that Maratha power should strike at the weakening Mughal centre and extend fiscal and military influence northward. Malwa, Gujarat and Bundelkhand became major arenas. Rapid cavalry movement, negotiated alliances and revenue agreements mattered as much as battlefield victory.

Expansion was not a single centrally administered empire. Leading houses developed regional bases:

- Holkars in Indore/Malwa;
- Scindias in Ujjain and later Gwalior-north India;
- Gaekwads in Gujarat;
- Bhonsles in Nagpur and eastern expansion.

The Peshwa supplied coordination, but chiefs retained armies and fiscal interests. This was a **confederacy**, not a unified nation-state.

### 4. Limits of expansion

Maratha collections could overlap Mughal, provincial and zamindari claims, increasing rural burdens. Raiding and tribute did not always create stable administration. North Indian success depended on alliances with Jats, Rajputs, Sikhs and others, which the Peshwa's leadership did not consistently secure.

### 5. Relationship to Mughal decline

Maratha expansion was both effect and cause:

```text
Mughal weakness allows fiscal entry
        -> Maratha claims reduce imperial/provincial hasil
        -> Maratha chiefs acquire resources and allies
        -> confederacy becomes another centre of competition
```

It was not an uninterrupted national war between “Hindus” and “Muslims.” Maratha chiefs fought one another, cooperated with Mughal nobles and sought imperial grants when useful.

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Explain how Maratha expansion transformed rather than simply replaced Mughal power.

**Model answer**

Expansion thus appropriated imperial resources and political language rather than immediately replacing every institution.

### 1. A successor state defined

A successor state emerged when a Mughal provincial governor or high noble converted office into durable regional authority while retaining imperial forms. This differs from a rebel state whose leadership arose mainly outside provincial office.

### 2. Bengal

**Murshid Quli Khan** became the central fiscal authority in Bengal from about 1703, gained Bengal and Orissa by 1710 and later incorporated Bihar into the provincial complex. He shifted the capital to Murshidabad, tightened revenue control and worked with bankers and merchants.

His death in 1727 opened succession changes that must be narrated carefully. The secure analytical point is continuity of a regional nawabi state, followed by **Alivardi Khan's reign, 1740-56**, and severe Maratha **bargi** incursions. Bengal's wealth strengthened autonomy but also attracted military and Company competition.

### 3. Awadh

**Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk** became governor around 1722-23. He built a regional military-fiscal base, disciplined zamindars and used imperial office while resisting transfer from Awadh. Some sources mark **1726** as the point of de facto independence; the process was gradual rather than a single declaration.

**Safdar Jang** succeeded in 1739 and also served as imperial wazir. This combination shows that regional and imperial office were not mutually exclusive.

### 4. Hyderabad

**Nizam-ul-Mulk**, also known as Chin Qilich Khan and later Asaf Jah, withdrew from ineffective Delhi politics. At **Shakar Kheda in October 1724**, he defeated Mubariz Khan and established de facto control of Hyderabad.

He faced Maratha fiscal pressure and powerful Deccan elites. Hyderabad was not a fully centralised breakaway kingdom from its first day. It retained Mughal ranks and forms while bargaining with local military actors.

```text
imperial office + regional revenue + local armed coalition
                   |
hereditary succession and local appointment
                   |
formal Mughal allegiance with practical autonomy
```

The three states were not equally stable or prosperous. Their trajectories depended on agrarian structure, bankers, military competition, succession and external intervention.

Jat resistance around Agra and Mathura predated Aurangzeb's death. In the eighteenth century **Churaman** consolidated forts and bargaining power. **Badan Singh** gained recognition, and **Suraj Mal** built Bharatpur into a major regional state.

After Guru Gobind Singh, **Banda Bahadur** led an armed challenge beginning in 1708. His forces attacked Mughal centres and asserted agrarian claims. He was captured and executed in **1716**.

Repression did not end Sikh political mobilisation. Afghan soldiers and migrants established local authority in the region later called **Rohilkhand**. **Ali Muhammad Khan** emerged as an important organiser. Rohilla power grew through military service, settlement, alliances and revenue command.

They were neither remnants of one invading army nor a single tribe operating outside Indian politics. They participated in Mughal, Awadh, Maratha and Abdali contests.

```text
- negotiate with bankers and zamindars
```

The typology explains origin, not permanent behaviour. Jat and Sikh rulers could later govern like territorial states; successor rulers could rebel against Delhi.

## Lesson 10 — Nadir Shah: invasion as shock and diagnosis

Progress: 10/14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Frontier failure, Karnal and the sack of Delhi

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Satish Chandra's Nadir Shah narrative and canonical Topic 25 chronology
CA search: not required
CA found: none used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: a short campaign exposed a long crisis

```text

              +

              +

              |
        KARNAL, February 1739
              |
      emperor taken under control
              |
      occupation and sack of Delhi
              |
treasury loss + prestige collapse + frontier cession
```

### 1. The invading power

**Nadir Shah** had reunited Iran through military conquest and then turned east. Disputes over Afghan fugitives and frontier communication formed the immediate diplomatic setting, but opportunity mattered: Kabul and the north-western line were poorly defended.

Claims that Nizam-ul-Mulk and Saadat Khan invited Nadir Shah lack secure documentary proof. They should not be repeated as fact.

### 2. Karnal

At **Karnal in February 1739**, Mughal forces were numerically substantial but poorly coordinated. Commanders acted competitively, intelligence failed, and the imperial army could not combine its contingents effectively. Saadat Khan was captured. Muhammad Shah entered Nadir's camp and lost freedom of action.

This was not proof that every Mughal soldier was ineffective. It was a command-system defeat: armies, finance and political trust could not be coordinated.

### 3. Delhi and massacre

Nadir occupied Delhi. A rumour that he had been killed triggered attacks on Iranian soldiers; he ordered a massacre and the city was plundered. The **Peacock Throne** and **Koh-i-Noor** are traditionally associated with the booty.

Contemporary and later accounts offer enormous monetary estimates. Satish Chandra's OCR text reproduces an estimate of seventy crores, but such figures cannot be treated as audited accounts. The secure conclusion is vast transfer of treasure and a severe fiscal-prestige shock.

### 4. Cause or accelerator?

Nadir did not remain to administer north India. He withdrew after taking wealth and securing frontier concessions. The invasion therefore did not create the jagirdari crisis, faction or provincial autonomy. It accelerated them:

- treasury and stored wealth were depleted;
- military reputation collapsed;
- Punjab and frontier defence weakened;
- regional powers revised expectations about Delhi;
- court conflict intensified after the shock.

### 5. No instant economic death

Delhi suffered terrible violence, but urban and commercial life did not disappear at once. The OCR narrative notes continuing commercial significance into the later eighteenth century. Recovery does not diminish the massacre; it prevents a false equation between catastrophic event and permanent total extinction.

### UPSC application

## Lesson 11 — Abdali, alliances and Panipat

CA found: none used
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: Panipat was not a two-state duel

```text
MARATHA SIDE                         ABDALI COALITION
Peshwa's northern army               Ahmad Shah Durrani
Sadashivrao Bhau                     Rohilla support
Vishwasrao                           Najib-ud-Daula
some regional allies                 Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh
        \                                  /
         \                                /
       JATS, RAJPUTS, SIKHS AND MUGHAL ELITES
       made separate calculations; several stayed outside
                         |
              PANIPAT, 14 JANUARY 1761
```

### 1. Abdali's repeated intervention

**Ahmad Shah Abdali**, also called Ahmad Shah Durrani, invaded India repeatedly from 1748. The exact count varies by how campaigns are grouped; the secure range extends from 1748 into the 1760s. The Mughals defeated him at Manupur in 1748, but later invasions established his leverage in Punjab and Delhi politics.

His object was not to recreate the Mughal Empire from Kabul. He sought frontier provinces, tribute, plunder, allies and a favourable north Indian balance.

### 2. Why the Marathas moved north

By the 1750s Maratha chiefs had expanded fiscal and military influence toward Delhi and Punjab. They could protect the emperor, collect revenue and challenge Afghan-backed groups. Yet such reach required:

- long supply lines from the Deccan;
- reliable local allies;
- payment for a large army;
- a political settlement acceptable to regional powers.

### 3. Coalition failure

The Maratha army at Panipat was led by **Sadashivrao Bhau**, with the Peshwa's son **Vishwasrao**. The force included artillery and a large camp. Abdali worked with Rohilla leaders, especially **Najib-ud-Daula**, and won the support of **Shuja-ud-Daula** of Awadh.

The Marathas did not secure durable cooperation from Suraj Mal's Jats, major Rajput rulers or Sikh forces. Responsibility cannot be assigned to one act of “betrayal.” Political mistrust, past Maratha extraction, disagreement over command and different regional interests limited coalition-building.

### 4. Logistics and battle

Abdali cut supplies and used mobile forces to contain the Maratha camp. The Marathas faced hunger, a burdened camp and diminishing options. On **14 January 1761**, they fought a major set battle. Initial pressure did not produce victory; coordination faltered, Vishwasrao and Bhau were killed, and the army suffered catastrophic losses.

### 5. Consequences with limits

Panipat:

- destroyed the Peshwa's immediate northern army and leadership;
- checked rapid Maratha expansion toward Punjab;
- intensified political fragmentation around Delhi;
- did not create stable Abdali rule in India;
- did not restore independent Mughal command;
- did not permanently end Maratha power.

Maratha houses recovered. Mahadji Scindia later returned Shah Alam II to Delhi in **1772**. This bounded later fact proves recovery without extending the lesson into a separate post-Panipat narrative.

### UPSC application

Frame the battle through logistics, coalition and political legitimacy. Treat battlefield decisions as the final stage of a wider strategic failure, not as the sole cause.

The battle therefore rearranged a multipolar struggle rather than deciding India's political future in one morning. Coalition-building, logistics and legitimacy explain both defeat and its limited long-term finality.

```text
        |

        |

        |

        |
```

### 1. What the 1717 farman did

Farrukhsiyar's **1717 farman** confirmed important commercial privileges for the English East India Company. It reduced some customs burdens and strengthened company claims in Bengal. It was not a transfer of sovereignty, a grant of Bengal's revenue or permission to depose nawabs.

Company servants also engaged in private trade. Disputes over passes, customs and fortification made commercial privilege politically contentious.

### 2. Why companies gained leverage

European companies combined several capacities:

- oceanic shipping and armed trade;
- fortified coastal settlements;
- corporate credit and access to merchants;
- trained infantry and artillery;
- diplomacy with Indian rulers and factions;
- ability to convert commercial disputes into military action.

These advantages mattered only through Indian political conditions. Companies required bankers, suppliers, soldiers, informants and allies. Mughal fragmentation created opportunities but did not predetermine British victory.

### 3. Plassey as a boundary link

The **Battle of Plassey on 23 June 1757** marks the Company's decisive intervention in Bengal's nawabi politics. It involved military action, elite conspiracy, credit networks and succession. Topic 25 uses it only to show how a regional successor state's internal conflict opened a new route to Company power.

The battle's detailed causes, Siraj-ud-Daulah, Mir Jafar, Company officials and post-battle settlements belong to Modern Indian History.

### 4. Why Buxar is the stronger fiscal turning point

The **Battle of Buxar on 22 October 1764** and the **Diwani grant of 1765** connected Company military power to legal rights of revenue collection in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. This occurred after the core 1761 endpoint and must not be projected backward onto the 1717 farman or Plassey.

### 5. No inevitability

```text
Mughal fragmentation = opportunity
Company organisation = capacity
Indian bankers/allies = enabling coalition
military victories = contingent decisions
revenue rights = later institutional consolidation
```

Change any element and the sequence could differ. Company rise was historically produced, not technologically or culturally automatic.

### UPSC application

Never write “the Mughals gave Bengal to the British in 1717.” Use a staged transition: commercial privilege, political intervention, military victory, then revenue sovereignty.

## Lesson 13 — Political fragmentation without civilisational silence

### Visual: redistribution, not one-direction collapse

```text

           |

           |

           |
```

### 1. Economy: continuity with shocks

India remained a major producer of textiles and agricultural goods. Bengal's textile exports, merchant credit and revenue made it one of the richest regional economies. Bankers such as the **Jagat Seths** connected nawabi finance, Company trade and elite politics.

Internal trade and urban life continued, but not uniformly. Bargi raids damaged parts of Bengal. Nadir Shah devastated Delhi. Afghan-Maratha conflict disrupted north-western routes. Multiple fiscal demands could burden cultivators and merchants. The correct conclusion is uneven resilience, not untouched prosperity.

### 2. Why regional courts mattered

As imperial patronage contracted, elites moved:

- musicians and painters found support at regional courts;
- Persian remained an administrative and literary language;
- Urdu literature expanded in Delhi and beyond;
- local languages gained courtly and devotional patronage;
- architectural and material cultures were adapted regionally.

Political fragmentation could therefore disperse creativity.

### 3. Muhammad Shah's cultural court

Muhammad Shah's reign saw important musical development. **Sadarang** and **Adarang** are associated with the growth and refinement of **khyal**. This cultural vitality does not prove administrative strength; it shows that courtly creativity and political weakness can coexist.

**Wali Dakhani's** arrival in Delhi, commonly dated to **1721**, helped demonstrate the literary potential of Rekhta/Urdu. Later eighteenth-century poets including **Mir**, **Dard** and **Sauda** reflect a flourishing literary world amid political upheaval.

### 4. Culture is not an excuse for coercion

A transformation thesis becomes misleading if it uses poetry and trade to minimise massacre, extraction, slavery, famine or peasant displacement. A collapse thesis becomes misleading if it treats those crises as proof that every market, craft and artistic tradition vanished.

### 5. A better regional test

For any region, ask:

| Dimension | Evidence question |
|---|---|
| production | did cultivation or craft output continue, contract or relocate? |
| exchange | were routes and credit functioning, and for whom? |
| fiscal pressure | how many authorities claimed revenue? |
| urban change | which towns declined and which grew? |
| patronage | where did artists and scholars move? |
| distribution | who paid for resilience and who benefited? |

### UPSC application

Use evidence of continuity to challenge “dark age,” then qualify it with distributional and regional costs. Do not substitute cultural achievement for political analysis.

## Lesson 14 — Building the best explanation

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: rival explanations answer different levels

```text
PERSONAL FAILURE ---------- agency and leadership
        |
AURANGZEB/DECCAN ---------- strategic overreach and policy
        |
JAGIRDARI CRISIS ---------- revenue-service mismatch
        |
AGRARIAN CRISIS ----------- extraction and resistance
        |
REGIONALISATION ----------- provincial and local state formation
        |
MILITARY-FISCAL/GLOBAL ---- command, credit, frontier and companies
        |
MULTI-CAUSAL SYNTHESIS ---- interaction, chronology and regional variation
```

### 1. Weak-successor explanation

This approach emphasises Jahandar Shah, Farrukhsiyar, Muhammad Shah and later rulers. Leadership clearly mattered: indecision, favourite-based politics and failure to coordinate armies changed outcomes.

Its weakness is circularity. A ruler looks weak partly because nobles, provinces and armies no longer respond. Biography cannot explain why the same institutions repeatedly produced fragile authority.

### 2. Aurangzeb-centred explanation

Jadunath Sarkar and related interpretations stress Aurangzeb's Deccan expansion, long war, Rajput and Maratha conflicts, and religious-political choices. The thesis rightly identifies burdens inherited in 1707.

Its limit is over-personalisation. Administrative strain preceded some late policies, and post-1707 actors made new decisions. Religious policy cannot by itself explain Bengal's banking, Awadh's autonomy, Nadir Shah's command advantage or Company coalitions.

### 3. Jagirdari explanation

Satish Chandra emphasises the mismatch between expanding mansab claims and adequate jagirs, jama and hasil, paibaqi scarcity, and faction around assignments. This offers a powerful institutional mechanism linking finance, army and politics.

Its limit is regional variation. Not all provinces experienced the same shortage, and regional regimes could adapt the same revenue forms.

### 4. Agrarian explanation

Irfan Habib directs attention to high extraction, peasant resistance and zamindar assertion. The approach explains why paper assessment failed and why rural power mattered.

Its limit appears when cooperation, commerce and state-building are read only as symptoms of agrarian antagonism.

### 5. Regionalisation approach

Muzaffar Alam, C. A. Bayly and wider revisionist scholarship emphasise negotiated regional states, commercial networks and continuity. This corrects the Delhi-centred “dark age.”

Its limit is the risk of underplaying violence, unequal extraction and the real loss of coordinated imperial defence.

### 6. Military-fiscal and global approaches

These approaches compare revenue mobilisation, credit, infantry-artillery organisation, naval capacity, frontiers and international competition. They illuminate Company growth and invasions.

Their limit is technological determinism if social alliances, legitimacy and agrarian resources disappear from the explanation.

### 7. Causal synthesis

```text
INHERITED STRAIN
mansab-jagir mismatch + Deccan costs
          |
POST-1707 POLITICS
succession war + faction + contested wizarat
          |
LOCAL AND REGIONAL RESPONSES
zamindar assertion + successor states + Maratha fiscal expansion
          |
EXTERNAL SHOCKS
Nadir Shah + Abdali
          |
NEW COMPETITORS
companies using credit, arms and Indian coalitions
          |
OUTCOME
imperial contraction + regional state formation + uneven economic-cultural continuity
```

No arrow is sufficient alone. Chronology matters: jagir pressure did not wait for Nadir Shah; Company revenue sovereignty did not exist in 1717; Panipat did not cause the first provincial autonomy.

### 8. The final verdict

The Mughal Empire declined as a centralised fiscal-military system because it could no longer convert assessed resources and symbolic authority into coordinated force. The eighteenth century transformed because those resources, institutions and elites did not disappear; they were redistributed among regional states, military confederacies, merchant-credit networks and eventually companies.

### UPSC application

Structure a 20-marker as: definition, six explanations with one strength and limit each, interaction model, regional variation, verdict. Weight causes rather than listing them.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Model 1 — 10 marks

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why did regional autonomy not immediately destroy Mughal legitimacy?

**Model answer**

Regional autonomy reduced Delhi's control over revenue, armies and appointments, but Mughal legitimacy remained a valuable shared language. Provincial leaders had risen through subedari, mansab and imperial service. Farmans, titles, coins and khutba could therefore sanction authority already exercised locally.

Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad retained Mughal administrative forms while making office hereditary and remittance uncertain. Maratha leaders also sought imperial recognition for chauth and sardeshmukhi. Such sanction helped rulers negotiate with nobles, zamindars and rivals without inventing an entirely new political order.

Symbolic legitimacy was not equivalent to coercive power: Delhi often could not remove governors or collect revenue. Yet it lowered the political cost of autonomy and allowed new regimes to present change as lawful continuity. The eighteenth century consequently combined localisation of fiscal-military substance with survival of imperial form.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer states the apparent contradiction, resolves it institutionally and preserves the difference between sanction and enforcement.

## Model 2 — 15 marks

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** How did fiscal stress alter the relationship between emperor, nobles and rural society after 1707?

**Model answer**

The Mughal service system linked the emperor to nobles through mansabs and transferable jagirs. Its stability depended on assignments whose hasil could support the rank and military contingent recorded on paper. By 1707, expanding mansab claims, Deccan costs and weak collection had widened the mismatch between jama and hasil.

The emperor and wazir therefore controlled a scarce political resource: access to suitable paibaqi and transfer to productive jagirs. Nobles joined factions and succession coalitions partly to secure these resources. Poor or delayed assignments created be-jagiri and indebtedness, weakening the maintenance of troops.

Pressure then moved downward. A short-tenure jagirdar or revenue farmer had incentives to maximise immediate collection. Zamindars could cooperate, bargain or resist using local armed and social power. Cultivators might conceal produce, flee or join resistance. Lower realisation then further weakened the assignee and the centre.

This cycle was regionally uneven. Commercial zones and effective bargains could sustain revenue, while some regional states adapted Mughal institutions successfully. Nevertheless, the imperial ability to transfer nobles, finance armies and enforce assessments contracted.

Fiscal stress therefore changed all three relationships: it made imperial patronage less credible, noble service more factional and rural collection more negotiated or coercive. It was not merely an empty treasury but a crisis of the political bargain sustaining empire.

**Why this earns marks:** It follows one mechanism across court, service and countryside and ends with a qualified institutional judgement.

## Model 3 — 20 marks

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** “The eighteenth century was an age of imperial decline, regional state formation and commercial-cultural continuity.” Critically examine.

**Model answer**

After 1707, Mughal central command declined sharply. Succession wars, contested wizarat, jagir scarcity and jama-hasil gaps weakened the connection between revenue and military service. Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad retained provincial resources; Maratha fiscal expansion redirected revenue; Nadir Shah and Abdali exposed failures of frontier defence.

This decline did not create a political vacuum. Successor states converted Mughal office into hereditary regional authority. Jats, Sikhs and Rohillas followed different paths from local, religious-military or soldier-settlement mobilisation. These regimes retained titles, revenue practices and imperial sanction while controlling practical force.

Nor did political fragmentation produce uniform economic collapse. Bengal's textiles, merchant credit and banking remained powerful, though bargi raids and Company intervention caused severe disruption. Internal trade and cultivation persisted unevenly. Patronage moved from Delhi to Awadh, Hyderabad, Rajasthan, Punjab, Bengal and Maratha courts. Khyal under Muhammad Shah and the growth of Urdu literature show cultural vitality amid political weakness.

Continuity must not romanticise the period. Multiple fiscal demands, war, massacre and displacement imposed heavy costs. Regional states did not automatically improve peasant welfare, and Company power converted commercial advantage into coercive revenue authority.

The proposition is therefore valid if its three terms are held together: effective imperial decline, creative but unequal redistribution of power, and regionally differentiated economic-cultural survival.

**Why this earns marks:** It tests each limb of the proposition, includes counter-evidence and integrates politics, economy and culture.

# REMEDIATION

## Error clinic 1 — “Weak kings caused everything”

**Misconception:** After Aurangzeb, incapable rulers alone destroyed the empire.

**Repair chain**

```text
personal choice matters
        +
choices operate through scarce revenue, noble networks and provincial armies
        +
the same institutions repeatedly reduce royal options
        =
agency inside structure
```

**Check:** Could a better emperor have improved outcomes? Yes. Could personality alone create productive jagirs, reconcile all regional claims and repair frontier coordination? No.

## Error clinic 2 — “There were no jagirs”

**Misconception:** Be-jagiri means the empire ran out of land.

**Repair:** It means shortage or delay of **suitable collectible assignments** relative to rank claims. A territory could exist on paper but have low hasil, competing claims, disturbed cultivation or weak enforcement.

## Error clinic 3 — “Every regional state declared independence”

**Misconception:** Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad rejected the Mughal emperor in one legal act.

**Repair:** Autonomy accumulated through local appointment, revenue retention, hereditary succession and military command while titles and formal allegiance continued.

## Error clinic 4 — “All eighteenth-century powers were successor states”

**Misconception:** Marathas, Jats, Sikhs and Rohillas inherited Mughal subedaris in the same way.

**Repair:** Their origins lay in resistance, local chieftaincy, religious-military organisation or soldier-settlement, although they later borrowed Mughal practices.

## Error clinic 5 — “Chauth equals direct rule”

**Misconception:** Payment of chauth proves Maratha officers governed each paying village.

**Repair:** A fiscal claim could function as tribute or protection payment. Direct rule requires evidence of durable appointments, enforcement and administration.

## Error clinic 6 — “Nadir Shah caused the decline”

**Misconception:** The empire was stable until 1739.

**Repair timeline**

```text
1707 succession strain
1712-20 emperor-wazir conflict
1720s provincial autonomy
1739 Nadir Shah accelerates an existing crisis
```

## Error clinic 7 — “Panipat ended the Marathas”

**Misconception:** The 1761 defeat permanently eliminated Maratha power.

**Repair:** It destroyed the immediate northern army and checked the Peshwa's project. Confederate houses recovered; Scindia influence returned to Delhi.

## Error clinic 8 — “The 1717 farman gave Bengal to the Company”

**Misconception:** Commercial privilege and territorial sovereignty are identical.

**Repair:** The sequence is 1717 commercial privilege, 1757 political-military intervention, 1764 Buxar and 1765 Diwani.

## Error clinic 9 — “Fragmentation means a dark age”

**Misconception:** If Delhi declined, all Indian trade, towns and culture declined equally.

**Repair:** Test each region. Bengal commerce and regional courts flourished in some respects, while warfare and extraction caused severe local crises.

## Error clinic 10 — “Transformation means there was no decline”

**Misconception:** Regional vitality disproves loss of imperial power.

**Repair:** Delhi's capacity to coordinate revenue, appointments and frontier defence clearly declined. Transformation describes where resources and authority went.

1. **jama / hasil:** paper assessment / actual realisation.

3. **successor / new polity:** provincial localisation / mobilisation outside inherited governorship.
4. **legitimacy / power:** recognised claim / ability to enforce.
5. **chauth / sovereignty:** fiscal extraction / durable governing authority.
6. **Plassey / Buxar-Diwani:** political entry / military-fiscal consolidation.

- **Not decline:** instant disappearance of dynasty, administration, economy or culture.
- **Transformation:** localisation and redistribution of revenue, armies, office and patronage.
- **Core period:** 1707-1761.
- **Long continuity:** Mughal symbolic legitimacy survives beyond the core period.

## C. Court and wizarat

- Zulfiqar Khan sought coordination and fiscal restraint under Jahandar Shah.
- Farrukhsiyar owed accession to Sayyid Abdullah Khan and Husain Ali Khan.
- Abdullah was wazir; Husain Ali was mir bakhshi.
- The conflict concerned control of appointment, patronage and policy.
- Farrukhsiyar was deposed in February 1719.
- The Sayyids installed two brief emperors and Muhammad Shah.
- Their power depended on offices, troops and alliances.
- Rival nobles defeated the Sayyid order in 1720.
- Irani, Turani, Hindustani, Afghan and Barha were shifting networks, not fixed parties.

## D. Fiscal-administrative mechanism

| Concept | Recall |
|---|---|
| mansab | rank linking status, salary claim and service |
| jagir | transferable claim to collect assigned state revenue |
| jama | assessed paper revenue |
| hasil | actual realised revenue |
| paibaqi | revenue territory available or awaiting assignment |
| be-jagiri | delay or shortage of suitable assignment |

### Qualifications

- The empire did not literally run out of land.
- Not every region had the same crisis.
- Jagirs survived in regional regimes.
- Regional survival of an institution does not prove continued Delhi control.

## E. Agrarian and military pressure

- Revenue passed through officials, jagirdars, zamindars and village leaders.
- Cultivators could bargain, conceal, flee or resist.
- Zamindars could collect for the state or create local power.
- Ijara could promise cash but intensify short-horizon extraction.
- Military weakness involved finance, supply, intelligence and coordination.
- Mughal victory at Manupur in 1748 proves residual military capacity.
- Technology mattered through organisation rather than as an isolated explanation.

## F. Maratha expansion

- Shahu released in 1707; Tarabai remained a rival.
- Balaji Vishwanath became Peshwa in 1713.
- 1718-19 negotiations and rescripts recognised fiscal claims.
- Baji Rao I became Peshwa in 1720.
- Chauth: commonly one-fourth fiscal claim.
- Sardeshmukhi: commonly an additional ten-per-cent claim.
- Neither automatically proved territorial administration.
- Maratha mobility, revenue bargains and alliances drove expansion.
- Holkar, Scindia, Gaekwad and Bhonsle developed regional bases.
- Confederacy meant uneven Peshwa coordination, not a unitary bureaucracy.
- Expansion was effect and accelerator of Mughal contraction.

- Murshidabad became the political-fiscal centre.
- Death in 1727; succession details require source caution.

- Bargi raids brought major disruption.

**Awadh**

- Saadat Khan appointed around 1722-23.
- Regional base built through military-fiscal control and zamindar management.
- 1726 is often used as a de facto autonomy marker.
- Safdar Jang succeeded in 1739 and also became imperial wazir.

**Hyderabad**

- Nizam-ul-Mulk = Chin Qilich Khan = Asaf Jah.
- Defeated Mubariz Khan at Shakar Kheda in October 1724.
- Retained Mughal titles while exercising Deccan autonomy.
- Faced Maratha claims and powerful local elites.

### New and rebel formations

**Jats**

- Agrarian-zamindari and fortified base around Agra-Mathura.
- Churaman -> Badan Singh -> Suraj Mal.
- Bharatpur became a major state.

**Sikhs**

- Banda Bahadur's challenge after 1708.
- Captured and executed in 1716.
- Later jathas, misls and Dal Khalsa.
- Ranjit Singh belongs a later phase.

**Rohillas**

- Afghan migration, service and soldier-settlement.
- Ali Muhammad Khan was a key organiser.
- Rohilkhand entered Mughal, Awadh, Maratha and Abdali politics.

## H. External shocks

### Nadir Shah

- Invaded in 1738-39.
- Karnal: February 1739.
- Mughal defeat resulted from divided command and weak intelligence.
- Saadat Khan captured; Muhammad Shah lost freedom of action.
- Delhi experienced massacre and plunder.
- Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Noor traditionally linked to booty.
- Huge monetary totals are estimates.
- Invitation allegations against Nizam and Saadat are unproven.
- Nadir withdrew; he did not establish permanent Indian rule.
- He exposed and accelerated an existing crisis.

### Abdali and Panipat

- Ahmad Shah Abdali = Ahmad Shah Durrani.
- Repeated invasions began in 1748.
- Mughal victory at Manupur in 1748.
- Abdali later gained Punjab and north Indian leverage.
- Maratha northern expansion faced long supply lines.
- Sadashivrao Bhau commanded; Vishwasrao accompanied him.
- Najib-ud-Daula and Shuja-ud-Daula supported Abdali.
- Jat, Rajput and Sikh cooperation failed for multiple political reasons.
- Third Battle of Panipat: 14 January 1761.
- Maratha army suffered catastrophic defeat.
- Abdali did not remain to rule India.
- Maratha power recovered; Scindia restored Shah Alam II to Delhi in 1772.

## I. Company boundary

- Farrukhsiyar's 1717 farman granted commercial privilege.
- It did not transfer Bengal's sovereignty or Diwani.
- Company power combined shipping, fortification, credit, soldiers and Indian allies.
- Plassey: 23 June 1757; bounded political-entry link.
- Buxar: 22 October 1764.
- Diwani: 1765.
- Detailed Buxar-Diwani history belongs Modern Indian History.
- British victory was contingent, not an automatic product of “decay.”

## J. Economy and culture

- Political fragmentation did not cause uniform economic collapse.
- Bengal retained textiles, trade and banking strength.
- Jagat Seths linked credit with nawabi and Company politics.
- Warfare, bargi raids and layered claims caused real disruption.
- Patronage dispersed to Awadh, Hyderabad, Rajasthan, Punjab, Bengal and Maratha courts.
- Muhammad Shah patronised music.
- Sadarang and Adarang are associated with khyal.
- Wali Dakhani reached Delhi around 1721.
- Mir, Dard and Sauda represent Urdu literary vitality.
- Persian remained important alongside vernacular growth.
- Cultural resilience cannot erase coercion and inequality.

## K. Historiography

| Interpretation | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| weak successors | restores agency | can rename rather than explain weakness |
| Aurangzeb-centred | identifies Deccan and policy inheritance | may over-personalise |
| jagirdari crisis | explains fiscal-service-faction mechanism | needs regional qualification |
| agrarian crisis | reveals extraction and rural resistance | can understate cooperation and commerce |
| regionalisation | recovers state-building and continuity | can soften coercion and defence failure |
| military-fiscal/global | explains command, credit and competition | can become technological determinism |

### Final synthesis

```text
inherited Deccan and jagir strain
+ succession and noble conflict
+ agrarian and zamindar assertion
+ regional state formation and Maratha expansion
+ Nadir-Abdali shocks
+ later Company coalitions and fiscal power
= decline of central command with uneven regional transformation
```

## L. High-yield traps

1. **Wrong:** The empire disappeared in 1707.
   **Correct:** Central command contracted through a prolonged process.
2. **Wrong:** Be-jagiri means no land existed.
   **Correct:** Suitable collectible assignments were inadequate relative to claims.
3. **Wrong:** Coin in the emperor's name proves obedience.
   **Correct:** It proves continuing legitimacy, which must be tested against revenue and force.
4. **Wrong:** All regional states were provincial successors.
   **Correct:** Maratha, Jat, Sikh and Rohilla origins differed.
5. **Wrong:** Chauth is direct territorial government.
   **Correct:** It could remain a layered fiscal claim.
6. **Wrong:** Nadir Shah initiated Mughal weakness.
   **Correct:** He exposed and accelerated prior crisis.
7. **Wrong:** Panipat ended Maratha power.
   **Correct:** It ended the immediate northern project, not later recovery.
8. **Wrong:** The 1717 farman created Company sovereignty.
   **Correct:** Political and fiscal sovereignty emerged through later war and Diwani.
9. **Wrong:** “Rangila” explains Muhammad Shah's reign.
   **Correct:** Biography must be joined to institutional and regional mechanisms.
10. **Wrong:** Cultural vitality disproves decline.
    **Correct:** Cultural and political trajectories can diverge.

2. Exact treasure totals from Nadir Shah's plunder are estimates.

4. Shahu's release in 1707 must not be confused with the later 1718-19 recognition of fiscal claims.
5. Awadh's 1722/23 appointment dating and 1726 autonomy marker reflect a gradual process.
6. Bengal succession details after Murshid Quli Khan's death require care; apparent OCR name errors were not used.
7. The number of Abdali invasions varies with campaign grouping; the chronology rather than a rigid count is used.
8. Maratha, Jat, Sikh and Rohilla formations are not collapsed into one category.
9. Plassey, Buxar and Diwani are used only to define the transition to the Modern-history owner.
