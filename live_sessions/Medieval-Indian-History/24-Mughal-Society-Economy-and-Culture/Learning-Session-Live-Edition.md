### Visual first

```text
Ain/revenue records → assessed norms and categories ─┐
chronicles/memoirs  → court ideals and rivalry ─────┤
travellers          → observed trade and society ───┤→ triangulated history
factory records     → company transactions ─────────┤
coins/buildings/art → material capacity/representation ─┘
```

### Intuition before terminology

No source is a transparent window. The *Ain-i Akbari* is exceptionally rich on offices, crops, rates and provinces, yet it is an imperial project of ordering. Its figures may record a norm or assessment, not one simultaneous census. Persian chronicles reveal political ideas, patronage and faction; their court proximity also creates praise and silence about ordinary labour.

European travellers widen the view. François Bernier compared Mughal land and society with Europe; Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, a jeweller and repeated visitor, is especially useful for high-value trade and diamond mines; Pelsaert observed Agra's commerce and inequality; Manucci left a long outsider narrative. Each selected what interested him. Factory records are precise about purchases and credit because companies required accounts, but they remain company-centred. Paintings show atelier technique and desired hierarchy, not population shares. Coins show minting, not equal cash access. Buildings prove organised labour, not solitary authorship or universal welfare.

The practical method is: **producer → purpose → scale → silence → corroboration**. Distinguish elite description from social typicality and representation from measurement. Bias does not make evidence useless; genre tells us which question it can answer.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A broad Mughal pattern can be stated without repeated qualifications.
**Reply:** The empire varied by region, class, source and decade. Qualification is not evasion; it fixes the claim's scale and prevents an elite norm from becoming a false social average.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **B — Incorrect:** Chronicle placement does not turn a commissioned image into a neutral chronological record.

### Original local Mains model

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why no single source can represent Mughal society as a whole.

**Model answer**

A source answers the questions its makers enabled. The *Ain-i Akbari* classifies imperial offices, crops and assessed revenue, but its ordered norms are not a simultaneous census. Court chronicles disclose ideology, patronage and conflict while limiting ordinary labour. Travellers such as Bernier and Tavernier widen observation toward inequality, routes and luxury trade, yet select through European interests. Factory records document purchases and credit but show a company-centred slice. Coins, paintings and monuments establish material capacity and representation without measuring welfare. Historians therefore triangulate genres: compare jama with hasil, rhetoric with documents, traveller claims with vernacular and material evidence, and images with patronage. Bias defines a claim's safe scale; it does not erase evidentiary value.

**Unique scoring note — Lesson 1:** Award the top band only for a clear claim about evidentiary scale, comparison of at least three source genres, and one explicit triangulation move; mere source listing is insufficient.

### Visual first

```text
monsoon/river/well/tank + soil + seed + cattle + labour
                         ↓
food grains and market crops → subsistence, sale and revenue
                         ↓
shock: drought/flood/war/cattle loss → access and famine crisis
```

### Production as a resource chain

Land alone produced nothing. Expansion came through forest clearance, migration, settlement concessions, recovery of abandoned land and irrigation. Rice, wheat, millets, pulses and barley coexisted with cotton, indigo, sugarcane, oilseeds, tobacco and silk-related production. Calling the latter simply “cash crops” can mislead because households combined subsistence and sale, while revenue, debt and merchant demand shaped choice.

Irrigation included wells and lifting devices, tanks, canals and inundation channels, but much cultivation remained rain-dependent. Access was unequal. Total output could rise because cultivated area increased even if yield per acre did not. Sources rarely permit a secure empire-wide productivity series.

Famine joined production shock with entitlement failure. Drought, flood, warfare or locusts could reduce supply; landless labourers, poor peasants and artisans then lost work and purchasing power. Stocks, transport, merchant movement, relief and remission shaped severity. Commercialisation could create opportunity and risk, so **output growth is not welfare**.

**Objection:** A broad Mughal pattern can be stated without repeated qualifications.
**Reply:** The empire varied by region, class, source and decade. Qualification is not evasion; it fixes the claim's scale and prevents an elite norm from becoming a false social average.

### Original local Mains model

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish agrarian expansion from agricultural welfare under the Mughals.

**Model answer**

Agrarian expansion meant more land, labour or crops entering cultivation through clearance, migration, irrigation and settlement. It could raise total output without proving higher yield per acre. Diversified crops and urban demand show productive capacity. Welfare depended on distribution. Cultivators needed cattle, seed, labour, water and credit; poorer households were more exposed to debt and harvest loss. Cash revenue could stimulate market sale but also force distress sales. Famine joined production shocks with lost wages, purchasing power, transport and uneven relief. Official remissions indicate awareness, not uniform implementation. Thus output growth and commercialisation were real economic processes, but welfare requires evidence about subsistence, effective burden, bargaining power and resilience.

**Unique scoring note — Lesson 2:** Credit a linked ecology-to-output explanation, separate extensification from intensification, and test any growth claim against famine and distribution; cap crop catalogues lacking mechanism.

### Visual first

| Position | Typical strength | Typical vulnerability |
|---|---|---|
| khud-kasht/riyayati | resident custom, cattle, village influence | revenue and crop shock |
| raiyati/muzarian | recognised cultivation/tenancy | negotiated or heavier dues |
| pahi-kasht | incoming cultivator, possible concessions | weaker local roots |
| landless/service labour | access through work | little control over land or stocks |

### “The peasant” was not one class

A holding required a working bundle: seed, cattle, tools, family or hired labour, water and credit until harvest. **Khud-kasht** generally meant resident cultivators with stronger local standing; *riyayati* could denote privileged customary assessment. **Muzarian/raiyati** described ordinary cultivators or tenants in varying usage. **Pahi-kasht** marked an incoming or non-resident cultivator. These were regionally flexible categories, not empire-wide castes.

**Mirasidar**, especially in south Indian usage, denoted a holder of hereditary occupancy or proprietary claims. It was a regional agrarian-rights term, not a synonym for a transferable Mughal jagir or for every cultivator.

A newcomer could receive concessions to bring land under the plough yet lack durable protection. A richer resident might rent cattle or seed to poorer neighbours. Credit could sustain cultivation and also bind future produce. Caste, lineage and gender cut across every category.

Movement also needs classification. Migration could seek new land; seasonal movement could supplement income; flight could follow famine, war, debt or excessive demand. Because rulers and zamindars needed cultivators, flight sometimes became bargaining. It did not make peasants fully free modern labourers.

**Reply:** The empire varied by region, class, source and decade. Qualification is not evasion; it fixes the claim's scale and prevents an elite norm from becoming a false social average.

### Original local Mains model

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse differentiation within the Mughal peasantry.

**Model answer**

Mughal rural society was not an undifferentiated peasant mass. Khud-kasht cultivators were generally resident and possessed stronger customary standing; riyayati could indicate privileged assessment. Raiyati/muzarian denoted ordinary cultivators or tenants in varying usages, while pahi-kasht marked incoming or non-resident cultivators who might receive concessions yet lack deep local protection. The underlying differentiation arose from control over land claim, cattle, seed, labour, irrigation, storage and credit. Better-resourced households could withstand a bad season; poor tenants and landless service groups faced greater dependence. Caste, gender and lineage shaped access across categories. Migration could open new cultivation, while flight could signal famine, war, debt or excessive demand and sometimes create bargaining leverage because states needed cultivators. Credit sustained sowing but could tie future produce to lenders. These labels were regionally flexible rather than fixed castes. The countryside was mobile but hierarchical, with residence, custom and productive assets determining opportunity and vulnerability.

```text
village claimant → lineage cluster → taluq holder → armed chief/raja

                 all could be called zamindar in context
```

### Why local power mattered

The centre could appoint collectors, but village boundaries, crop histories, caste relations and cultivator capacity were local knowledge. **Zamindar** therefore covered a spectrum from village-level claimants to large rajas. Recurrent features included hereditary or customary revenue shares, social authority, forts or **garhis**, armed retainers and a capacity to assist collection, policing and cultivation. These rights were not modern absolute ownership of every field or peasant.

Cooperation and resistance emerged from the same capacity. Recognition, acceptable demand, honour and imperial service could align a zamindar with the state. High demand, succession disputes, official coercion or local rivalry could generate concealment, arrears, fortified resistance or rebellion. Entry into mansab service could overlay rather than erase *watan* interests.

A **jagirdar** held a transferable imperial assignment to collect a revenue claim as remuneration. A zamindar's position was locally rooted. The two could interact or overlap in one person's career, but they were not synonyms. This is the central distinction behind the routed 2019 Prelims question; its official key is unavailable locally.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** The category seems too varied to explain a common mechanism.
**Reply:** Variation is part of the explanation. State the spectrum, identify the recurrent function, and then show how ecology, power or market position changed the outcome.

### UPSC application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why were zamindars simultaneously collaborators and constraints in Mughal rule?

Incorporation into mansab service could align interests but did not erase watan ties. A zamindar also differed from a jagirdar: the latter held a transferable imperial revenue assignment, while the former's claim was locally rooted. Zamindars were thus neither mere tax clerks nor permanently autonomous chiefs, but negotiated rural powers through whom the empire governed and against whom it sometimes fought.

## Lesson 5 — Revenue, commercialisation and welfare

### Visual first

```text
measured/estimated cultivation + rate schedule → JAMA (paper demand)
                         ↓ harvest, price, officials, remissions, coercion
                         → HASIL (actually realised)
```

### Assessment meets society

Topic 16 owns full **zabt**, **dahsala**, *batai*, *kankut* and *nasaq* mechanics. Here the issue is consequence. Money-denominated schedules could make claims calculable and support fiscal accounting, but neither a one-third norm nor a large jama proves what was collected from each field. **Jama** meant assessed demand; **hasil** meant realised revenue. Harvest, prices, resistance, remission, record quality and official coercion shaped the gap.

Cash demand connected villages to markets because cultivators sold produce or borrowed. Commercialisation could arise through profitable choice, obligatory sale for revenue, merchant advance, urban demand or distress sale. Better-resourced cultivators might gain; those forced to sell just after harvest or borrow through failure might lose bargaining power. Cesses, intermediary shares and conversion rates could raise the effective burden.

Bernier's critique of insecure rights and extraction rightly raises incentives, but his European comparison flattened peasant, zamindari and mirasidari claims and understated merchant and middle strata. Predictability could restrain arbitrariness without ensuring a low burden. **Output growth is not welfare; jama is not hasil.** Leave the later acute jagirdari crisis to Topic 23.

**Objection:** The category seems too varied to explain a common mechanism.
**Reply:** Variation is part of the explanation. State the spectrum, identify the recurrent function, and then show how ecology, power or market position changed the outcome.

### Follow one textile order

A merchant or broker estimated demand, advanced money or yarn, specified quality and collected cloth through agents. Spinning and preparation could occur in households, weaving in specialised communities, and bleaching, dyeing or printing in further stages. Goods then moved to a qasba, **aurang** or port. Craft knowledge often passed through family, jati and apprenticeship. High skill did not automatically produce mechanisation. Merchant advances widened markets but could bind artisans to price, quality and delivery terms. “Commercially organised craft production” is safer than an unqualified proto-industrial claim.

### Towns formed a functional network

Capitals such as Agra, Delhi and Lahore concentrated court, offices, soldiers, servants, builders and luxury demand. Provincial centres joined administration to regional exchange. Ports connected inland production to shipping. **Qasbas** were smaller market-service towns tying villages to larger centres through trade, learning and local administration. Pilgrimage towns generated recurring demand; mobile imperial and army camps could behave like moving cities.

Urban growth did not detach towns from agriculture. Grain, fodder, fuel and raw materials moved inward; money, credit, orders and manufactures moved outward. Revenue-funded court consumption could concentrate surplus while stimulating building, textiles, arms, transport and food services.

Bernier's two-class picture is therefore too stark, though urban inequality was real.

**Banjaras** and other carriers moved bulk grain and army supplies with pack bullocks. Ox-carts, river craft, coastal vessels and human or animal portage formed a multimodal system. ### A crowded ocean before companies

Indian textiles moved to the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Southeast Asia and other markets. Indigo, pepper, rice, sugar and raw silk travelled on particular routes; horses, metals, bullion and luxuries entered India. Commodity mixes changed by region and decade. Surat linked Gujarat and inland north India to western seas; Masulipatam and the Coromandel ports connected textile zones eastward; Bengal's river-port system supported silk and cotton trade.

**Virji Vohra** demonstrates the leverage of a major Surat merchant house. **Abdul Ghaffur** demonstrates large-scale Indian shipping and commerce. They counter a company-only story but cannot represent every merchant. Gujarati, Bohra, Jain, Multani/Khatri, Armenian, Chetti and south Indian Muslim networks had varied interests.

Mughal **farmans** granted specified facilities; they did not surrender sovereignty. Companies sought low duties, secure factories and sometimes fortification. Mughal authorities could limit forts and retaliate against maritime coercion by seizing factories or agents on land. European naval advantages mattered, but colonial predominance depended on later military-fiscal changes. The seventeenth-century frame is **entrants into established networks**, not inevitable conquest.

### Money connected unequal circuits

The **silver rupee**, **copper dam** and **gold mohur** were major denominations, but money of account, coin in hand and local practice were not identical. Mints converted bullion into coin; a comparatively reliable silver currency helped state accounting and long-distance trade. Bullion inflow reflected strong manufactured exports, especially textiles. Bernier's “sink of gold and silver” image captured direction but exaggerated if treated literally.

More silver did not mean equal household access. Bullion could be coined, hoarded, remitted or concentrated in fiscal and commercial networks. Copper, gold, barter and payment in kind continued. Cash revenue might widen exchange and also expose cultivators when crop prices fell.

Price and wage evidence demands caution. A nominal wage must be compared with food, cloth, housing, employment regularity and payment form. One local quotation cannot establish empire-wide living standards. **Bullion inflow is not universal prosperity; monetisation is not uniform cash access.**

Quarry workers, masons, calligraphers, inlay artists, engineers and transport labour made monuments collectively, so single-designer claims require caution. Shahjahani architecture culminated earlier experimentation through imperial resources and skilled labour, while its grandeur coexisted with an unequal agrarian funding base.

**Unique scoring note — Lesson 17:** Require correct Akbar-Jahangir-Shah Jahan sequencing, two formal transitions, and collective labour/patronage analysis; unsupported single-designer claims should be penalised.

### Visual first

```text
Akbar: collaborative atelier/Hamza Nama
  ↓ Jahangir: portraiture, naturalism, Ustad Mansur
  ↓ Shah Jahan: refined court formality
  ↓ reduced central support: dispersal to regional patrons

music: court ↔ nobles/royal women ↔ devotional and regional centres
```

### Painting as institutional production

Akbar organised a large imperial painting **karkhana**. The **Hamza Nama** required collaborative work: design, colouring, finishing and supervision could involve different hands. Persian manuscript conventions interacted with Indian artists and observation. Jahangir valued portraiture, individual likeness, animals, birds and flowers. **Ustad Mansur** is associated with close natural studies, but patron selection means a painting is not a neutral scientific photograph. Under Shah Jahan, painting often became more formal and sumptuous. Reduced personal patronage under Aurangzeb encouraged dispersal to noble and regional courts; it did not end painting.

### Music beyond one emperor

**Tansen**, associated with Akbar's court, symbolises imperial patronage, but Hindustani music developed through court, devotional and regional settings. Under Aurangzeb, singing was restricted or removed from the imperial court in important accounts, while instrumental practice, theory, noble and royal-women patronage and regional centres continued. The “burial of music” anecdote is moralising rhetoric, not proof of extinction.

### Daily material life and final synthesis

Food varied by ecology, class and community: grains, pulses, vegetables, dairy, meat, fruits, sweets and spices appeared in unequal combinations. Cotton clothed many; muslin, silk, brocade and jewellery marked wealth. Houses ranged from village mud or thatch to urban brick, mansions and gardens. Fairs, festivals, markets and shrines created encounter but not identical meanings or access.

Political integration, agrarian surplus, merchant networks and skilled labour supported trade and culture. Revenue pressure, famine risk, caste, gender, servitude and weak institutional diffusion qualified achievement. Court culture is not all society; bullion is not universal prosperity; synthesis is not absence of conflict.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Imperial patronage alone explains the cultural achievement.
**Reply:** Patronage supplied resources and direction, but translation, construction, painting and performance depended on multilingual specialists, artisans, labourers, regional traditions and audiences. Attribution must include institutions and limits.

### UPSC application

Use chronology as argument, name the work or monument precisely, explain the institution and labour behind it, then qualify courtly reach and source certainty.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Final 10-mark model

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how commercial institutions connected Mughal villages to overseas markets.

**Model answer**

Mughal commerce connected dispersed production through specialised actors. Merchants or agents advanced money and raw material to artisans and specified cloth quality. Qasbas and manufacturing towns aggregated output; an **aurang** served as a warehouse. Banjaras and carts moved inland goods, while rivers and coastal shipping reduced some bulk costs. **Banians** and **dalals** brokered purchases and supplied local information. **Sarrafs** assayed coins, extended credit and settled **hundis**, enabling remittance without moving specie for every transaction. Ports such as Surat and Masulipatam joined these chains to Indian, Armenian, Arab and European merchants. Companies entered rather than created the networks and depended on Indian suppliers. The system was sophisticated but uneven: monsoon, tolls, insecurity, debt and intermediary power affected producers. Integration arose from logistics, credit and brokerage, not one company or a uniformly free market.

**Final scoring note — 10 marks:** Require a field-to-port chain, four accurately defined institutions and one distributional limit; cap a ports-only catalogue below the top band.

## Final 15-mark model

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “Mughal agrarian prosperity was real, but its social meaning was unequal.” Discuss.

**Model answer**

Mughal agriculture sustained a large population, imperial revenue, towns and craft production. Cultivation widened through migration, forest clearance, wells, tanks, canals and settlement incentives. Food crops coexisted with cotton, indigo, sugarcane, oilseeds, tobacco and silk-related production. This diversity and market sale support substantial productive capacity.

Its social meaning depended on unequal rights and resources. Resident **khud-kasht** cultivators often possessed stronger customary standing than ordinary *muzarian/raiyati* or incoming **pahi-kasht** peasants. Cattle, seed, labour, irrigation, storage and credit determined whether a household could exploit opportunity or survive failure. Zamindars mediated revenue and local authority; cooperation could extend cultivation, while coercion intensified burden.

Fiscal evidence also needs care. **Jama** was assessed demand, not **hasil**, the amount realised. Cash claims encouraged markets but could force early sale or borrowing. Commercial crops might raise income in good years and deepen debt or food-price vulnerability in bad ones. Famine arose from ecological shock together with lost work, purchasing power, transport and uneven relief.

Therefore prosperity meant growth in output, exchange and surplus, not universal welfare. Mughal agrarian dynamism coexisted with differentiated peasantry, high claims and unequal resilience.

**Final scoring note — 15 marks:** Reward output-versus-welfare reasoning, peasant differentiation, jama-hasil and famine/credit qualification; a generic “golden age versus poverty” answer lacks mechanism.

## Final 20-mark model

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Evaluate Mughal culture as an institutional and composite achievement rather than merely an expression of imperial taste.

**Model answer**

Imperial taste mattered, but Mughal culture was produced through institutions, multilingual encounter and collective skilled labour. Akbar's karkhana organised collaborative manuscript painting; the **Hamza Nama** joined narrative ambition to teams of designers and colourists. Jahangir's connoisseurship encouraged portraiture and natural studies associated with **Ustad Mansur**, while Shahjahani painting developed court formality. Reduced patronage under Aurangzeb dispersed rather than extinguished practice.

Architecture accumulated forms. Fatehpur Sikri combined trabeate and arcuate systems; Itimad-ud-Daula advanced marble and pietra dura; the Taj Mahal integrated garden planning, symmetry, calligraphy and inlay. Red Fort and Jama Masjid expressed imperial urbanism. Quarry workers, masons, calligraphers, inlay artists, engineers and transport labour make solitary-designer narratives inadequate.

Literary culture crossed languages. Persian chronicles shaped imperial memory; Akbar's *Razmnama* and other translations joined pandits with Persian literati; regional authors such as Tulsidas, Rahim, Eknath and Tukaram addressed devotional worlds. Music circulated through court, nobles, royal women, shrines and regional patrons.

Yet “composite” does not mean conflict-free. Court projects had elite reach, while caste, gender and unequal patronage persisted. Mughal culture was an institutional synthesis—creative through encounter and labour, but socially bounded.

**Final scoring note — 20 marks:** Demand visual arts, literature/translation and music, plus institutional labour and a non-romantic composite-culture qualification; monuments alone cannot earn a high score.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Evidence and method

- Focus on sixteenth- and seventeenth-century structures; post-1707 political economy belongs to Topic 25.
- *Ain-i Akbari*: categories, regions and assessment; norm is not automatic practice.
- Chronicles reveal court, ideology and rivalry; travellers reveal selected observations; factory records remain company-centred.
- Bernier raises land and inequality debates; Tavernier is especially useful for diamond mines and luxury trade; Pelsaert for Agra commerce and inequality; Manucci for a long outsider narrative.
- Paintings and architecture reveal technique and patronage, not population or welfare statistics.
- Method: producer, purpose, scale, silence and corroboration.

## Agrarian order

- Expansion: clearance, migration, settlement and irrigation; ecology remained decisive.
- Food grains coexisted with cotton, indigo, sugarcane, oilseeds, tobacco and silk-related production.
- Output growth ≠ yield growth ≠ welfare.
- Khud-kasht/riyayati: resident or stronger customary position in context.
- Raiyati/muzarian: ordinary cultivator or tenant; terminology varied.
- Pahi-kasht: incoming/non-resident; concessions could coexist with weaker roots.
- Working bundle: claim + cattle + seed + labour + water + credit.
- Migration/flight: opportunity, seasonal strategy, distress or bargaining.
- Zamindar spectrum: village claimant to armed chief; local roots, shares, garhi, retainers and negotiation.
- Jagirdar: transferable imperial revenue assignment; not zamindar and not owner of all soil.
- Jama = assessment; hasil = realisation.
- Famine joined harvest shock with entitlement, transport, employment and relief failure.

## Crafts, towns and commerce

- Production forms: household, workshop, merchant advance and imperial/noble karkhana.
- Karkhana ≠ mechanised factory; aurang = warehouse/depot in commercial usage.
- Gujarat, Coromandel and Bengal were major textile regions.
- Towns: capital, provincial centre, port, manufacturing town, qasba, pilgrimage and camp town.
- Middle strata included merchants, clerks, bankers, professionals and master artisans; not a unified modern middle class.
- Banjaras carried bulk grain; sarrafs assayed coins and banked; hundis moved credit/remittance; dalals and banians brokered exchange.
- Europeans entered established Indian Ocean networks.
- Virji Vohra and Abdul Ghaffur demonstrate Indian commercial scale, not universal merchant conditions.
- Farman granted specified privileges within sovereignty; fortification remained contested.
- Mughal land power and company maritime power produced bargaining.

## Money, hierarchy and mobility

- Rupee—silver; dam—copper; mohur—gold.
- Bullion supported minting and exchange but did not produce universal prosperity.
- Price and wage claims require exact place, date, occupation and consumption context.
- Nobility was a diverse service aristocracy; its consumption concentrated surplus and stimulated crafts.
- Merchant wealth did not equal noble rank; artisans and peasants were internally differentiated.
- Caste/jati persisted but varied regionally.
- Mobility came through service, wealth, settlement, migration and patronage.
- Peasantisation joined groups to cultivation and revenue, sometimes reducing autonomy.
- Verdict: mobility within hierarchy.

## Gender, servitude and knowledge

- Women worked in agriculture, animal care, processing, spinning and embroidery.
- Household records understate female labour.
- Nur Jahan and Jahanara prove bounded elite agency, not general equality.
- Property entitlement differed from control; purdah varied; sati was not universal.
- Slavery/servitude included domestic, military, court and labour forms.
- Avoid Atlantic equivalence and benign-household romanticism.
- Education used maktabs, madrasas, mosques, khanqahs, pathshalas, temples, maths, households and apprenticeship.
- Persian served administration; Arabic and Sanskrit retained learned roles; regional literatures expanded.
- Technical skill was high in several sectors; elite-artisan linkage, mechanisation and diffusion were limited.

## Sources, literature and arts

- *Akbarnama*: imperial narrative; *Ain*: classificatory administration.
- *Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh*: dissent through Badauni's lens.
- *Tuzuk-i Jahangiri*: memoir, observation and self-fashioning.
- *Padshahnama*: Shahjahani ceremony, conquest and magnificence.
- 2020 GS-I: Persian texts refract as well as reflect the age.
- *Razmnama*: Persian Mahabharata; the *Yogavasistha* PYQ names Nizamuddin Panipati, and no option is selected without a locally official 2022 key.
- Tulsidas—Awadhi *Ramcharitmanas*; Rahim—Hindi verse; Eknath and Tukaram—Marathi bhakti in different periods.
- Composite culture = interaction and new forms, not absence of conflict.
- Architecture: Fatehpur Sikri/Buland Darwaza → Itimad-ud-Daula → Taj Mahal/Red Fort/Jama Masjid.
- Pietra dura is coloured-stone inlay; monuments were collective productions.
- Painting: Akbar—Hamza Nama/collaboration; Jahangir—naturalism/Ustad Mansur; Shah Jahan—formality; later dispersal.
- Music: Tansen and multiple patronage sites; Aurangzeb did not end music everywhere.
- Food, clothing, housing, festivals and consumption varied by class, region, occupation and ecology.

> **Final verdict:** Mughal India combined agrarian expansion, commercial sophistication and institutional cultural creativity with unequal rights, high extraction, caste-gender hierarchy, famine vulnerability and limits of evidence and technological diffusion.
