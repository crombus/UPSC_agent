# Administration, Economy and Society under the Delhi Sultanate — Complete Live Session Edition

This learner-first edition studies 1206–1526 as a changing military-fiscal order: centralising at the apex, dependent on provincial and village intermediaries, commercially dynamic in selected corridors, and sharply unequal in social effect. Institutions are periodised; legal ideals are not confused with practice; one ruler's measure is never projected across three centuries.

### Evidence triangulation table

| Evidence | Secure use | Necessary limit |
|---|---|---|
| Court chronicles: Minhaj, Barani, Afif | regulations, political ideals, elite conflict | patronage, moral judgement and capital bias |
| Ibn Battuta and travellers | routes, products, towns and observed practice | itinerary, hearsay and literary marvels |
| Coins and mint names | titulature, metal, denomination and issuing centres | find-spots are not complete borders or circulation censuses |
| Inscriptions and buildings | dated claims, patronage, labour and material capacity | survival is uneven and form does not prove motive |
| Normative law | sharia and fiscal categories | prescription is not uniform implementation |

*No single source sees the whole state; reliable reconstruction comes from comparison.*

### From conquest to routine rule

Winning a fort is not the same as governing a countryside. State formation began when armed command was joined to revenue, information, appointments and cooperation from people who knew local society.

### Reading a changing state step by step

1. **Set the boundary.** This session covers 1206–1526 thematically. Topics 04–06 supply only bounded reign-specific examples; they do not displace the institutional, economic and social focus here.
2. **Periodise the state.** Mamluk consolidation, Khalji tightening, Muhammad bin Tughlaq's ambitious centralisation, Firuz's conciliation, Sayyid contraction and Lodi bargaining produced different administrative balances.
3. **Distinguish claim from capacity.** Khutba, titles and coins advertised sovereignty. Regular revenue, troop inspection, communications and remittance show power operating.
4. **Name the evidence.** Satish Chandra reads Barani for regulation, Ibn Battuta for towns and routes, coins for monetary activity and buildings for state-supported labour.
5. **Explain the mechanism.** Conquest secured nodes; iqtas financed commanders; barids reported; local chiefs and village officials translated demands into collection. Each link could transmit or obstruct.
6. **Qualify “centralised”.** It fits the apex and selected strategic cores better than everyday rule in every locality. Distance, ecology, succession and intermediary resistance repeatedly weakened enforcement.
7. **Historical payoff.** The Sultanate created durable administrative languages and practices, yet later regional and Mughal states adapted rather than mechanically inherited them.

### Evidence discipline for UPSC

Use **centralising military-fiscal apex with negotiated local operation**. The trap is inferring a modern uniform bureaucracy from a court chart.

### Revision notes

1. The Sultanate changed across five dynastic phases.
2. Conquest of nodes preceded routine countryside administration.
3. Chronicles are rich but elite and normative.
4. Travellers are observers, not statistical surveys.
5. Coins reveal issues and mints, not exact political borders.
6. Legal categories must be separated from practice.
7. Local intermediaries were structural to rule.
8. Centralisation was strongest in strategic cores.
9. Evidence should be triangulated across text, coin and material remains.
10. Continuity with later states was adaptive, not automatic.

### Lesson-local practice

#### Question 1

Which description best follows the evidence for Sultanate state formation?

**A.** A centralising military-fiscal order mediated locally.
**B.** A territorially uniform administration whose village officers followed one fixed code.
**C.** A reconstruction based on court chronicles without comparison with coins or buildings.
**D.** A sequence of conquests that treated occupied forts as complete countryside control.

#### Question 2

Why must a chronicler's description of an institution be checked against other evidence?

**A.** Chronicles chiefly record court ceremony and therefore add little to administrative history.
**B.** Because courtly norms could diverge sharply from regional administrative practice.
**C.** Coins provide more precise village-administration records than literary sources.
**D.** Buildings identify the exact legal jurisdiction exercised by each commissioning ruler.

#### Answers and option-by-option explanations

**MCQ 1: A**
- **A — Correct:** Delhi concentrated military command and fiscal claims, but local chiefs and village officials made everyday enforcement possible.
- **B — Incorrect:** Provincial and village practice varied by reign, distance and bargaining; no fixed code operated uniformly.
- **C — Incorrect:** Court chronicles are selective and capital-centred; coins and buildings test their institutional claims against material evidence.
- **D — Incorrect:** Taking forts secured strategic nodes, but routine rule also required revenue, information, appointments and local cooperation.

**MCQ 2: B**
- **A — Incorrect:** Chronicles preserve indispensable policy and elite evidence; their limitation is selectivity, not total uselessness.
- **B — Correct:** Court chronicles often describe intended rules at the capital; coins, inscriptions and regional evidence test whether those rules operated elsewhere.
- **C — Incorrect:** Coins show denominations, rulers and mints, not complete village administration.
- **D — Incorrect:** Architecture shows resources and patronage but cannot by itself define an office's legal jurisdiction.

### Lesson-local Mains model

**Question (10 marks; ceiling: 150 words):** Explain why the sources do not support a picture of uniformly centralised Sultanate rule.

**Model answer:** Sultanate sources reveal strong central claims but uneven implementation. Barani describes regulations, offices and Alauddin Khalji's surveillance; this establishes ambition, yet his normative Delhi-centred account cannot prove identical enforcement elsewhere. Ibn Battuta's observations of roads, Delhi and Daulatabad show mobility and urban scale, but his itinerary is selective. Coins attest rulers, metals and mints, while inscriptions and buildings reveal patronage and material capacity; neither maps everyday village compliance.

The governing mechanism itself required mediation. Muqtis collected assigned revenue, while amils, khuts, muqaddams and local record-keepers supplied information and coercion. Distant provinces could retain surplus or become autonomous when audit weakened. The Sultanate therefore possessed a centralising military-fiscal apex, particularly effective in strategic cores, but effective sovereignty varied with ruler, region, communications and intermediary cooperation.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses four named evidence classes, explains mediation and reaches a qualified rather than binary verdict.

### Four tests of sovereignty

| Dimension | Instrument | Political work | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recognition | caliphal investiture, titles | wider Islamic legitimacy | no direct command over Indian officials |
| Public sovereignty | khutba and sikka | proclaimed lawful rule | did not guarantee territorial obedience |
| Coercion | army, forts, punishment | enforced command | depended on loyalty and revenue |
| Justice | appointed qazis and royal appeal | protective image of kingship | custom and community forums persisted |

*Legitimacy made rule intelligible; fiscal-military power made it effective.*

### Why symbols need resources

A caliph could strengthen a ruler's title, but he could not make a provincial governor remit revenue. Delhi sovereignty needed symbols, armed support and an accepted distribution of office.

### Building sovereignty from recognition and command

1. **Sultan's authority.** The sultan was supreme political and military authority, commander-in-chief and final appellate figure against official oppression.
2. **Caliphal recognition.** Iltutmish's investiture and the caliph's name in the khutba linked Delhi to the Islamic world. Satish Chandra calls the caliph's position moral, not an operating Indian government.
3. **Khutba and sikka.** Friday prayer and coinage publicly named authority. They are evidence of claim and recognition, not proof of equal control wherever a coin travelled.
4. **Succession without primogeniture.** Sons might claim, rulers might nominate—including Iltutmish's preference for Razia—but nobles, household networks and military strength made nomination effective or futile.
5. **Sharia and zawabit.** Sharia supplied norms interpreted by ulema; sultans also framed regulations for political necessity. Alauddin's reported exchange with Qazi Mughis demonstrates discretion, not modern secularism.
6. **Ceremony as technology.** Balban's controlled access, precedence and awe made hierarchy visible and sought to discipline nobles.
7. **Regional qualification.** A Sayyid ruler could hold sovereign style while exercising narrow reach; Lodi kings had to negotiate Afghan expectations of consultation.

### The legitimacy trap

Distinguish symbolic legitimacy from effective sovereignty. Do not say either that Baghdad ruled Delhi or that Islamic idioms were irrelevant.

### Revision notes

1. The sultan combined political, military and high judicial authority.
2. He remained commander-in-chief.
3. Caliphal recognition added moral legitimacy.
4. It did not create direct Baghdad administration.
5. Khutba and sikka were public signs of rule.
6. Succession lacked fixed primogeniture.
7. Nomination needed elite and military acceptance.
8. Sharia coexisted with sultanic zawabit.
9. Court ceremony disciplined noble rank.
10. Effective sovereignty required revenue, troops and obedience.

### Lesson-local Mains model

**Question (10 marks; ceiling: 150 words):** Distinguish legitimacy from effective sovereignty under the Delhi Sultanate.

**Model answer:** Legitimacy made authority intelligible; effective sovereignty made it operative. Iltutmish's caliphal investiture, khutba and coinage linked Delhi to the Islamic world and publicly announced rulership. Balban's ceremony similarly converted hierarchy into awe. Yet the caliph did not appoint Indian collectors or command Delhi's army.

Effective sovereignty depended on troops, forts, revenue, appointments, intelligence and the capacity to punish or transfer officials. Succession exposes the distinction: a nominated heir could possess dynastic legitimacy but fail without noble and military backing. Sharia supplied a normative vocabulary, while sultans used zawabit when governance required measures not reducible to juristic prescription. Sovereignty was therefore neither pure symbolism nor naked force; it joined public legitimacy to fiscal-military capacity, whose reach varied sharply by ruler and region.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both concepts, uses Iltutmish, Balban, succession and zawabit, and avoids two opposite caricatures.

### Administrative nerve-centres

| Office | Core function | Political dependence |
|---|---|---|
| Wazir / Diwan-i-Wizarat | income, expenditure and fiscal oversight | accountants, provincial returns and royal confidence |
| Ariz-i-mamalik / Diwan-i-Arz | recruitment, muster, equipment and pay | treasury, inspectors and horse supply |
| Diwan-i-Insha | royal and diplomatic correspondence | scribes, paper and couriers |
| Diwan-i-Risalat / sadr | religious matters and grants in Chandra's reconstruction | learned networks; portfolio is debated |
| Barid | intelligence and reporting | roads and trusted agents |
| Wakil-i-dar | household, stores, karkhanas and court order | servants, artisans and precedence |

*Functional differentiation expanded capacity, but offices remained embedded in court politics.*

### Why specialisation creates new risks

A ruler cannot personally inspect every horse, account and letter. Departments solved a scale problem, then created another: officials controlled information and resources.

### Following information through the court

1. **Finance.** By the fourteenth century the wazir presided over income and expenditure. Under Muhammad bin Tughlaq, audit and account functions helped manage an exceptionally large realm.
2. **Military administration.** The ariz recruited, equipped, mustered and paid troops. Supreme command remained with the sultan because control of armed force was the basis of survival.
3. **Chancery.** Diwan-i-Insha converted decisions into written orders and managed correspondence with subordinates and other rulers. Wider paper use supported this documentary world.
4. **Religion and justice.** Chandra associates Risalat with religious matters and pious grants under the sadr, often connected to the chief qazi. Other reconstructions differ, so state the source usage.
5. **Information.** Barids reduced dependence on a governor's own report. Alauddin's surveillance shows information serving anti-rebellion and market policy.
6. **Court and household.** Wakil-i-dar oversaw royal needs and precedence. Robes, gifts, seating and access distributed rank; court culture was political administration.
7. **Nobility.** Early Turkish exclusivity broadened under Khaljis and Tughlaqs to Afghans, Iranians, Indian Muslims and other recruits, yet lineage prejudice and faction remained.
8. **Risk.** High salary and assignment attracted service but also financed households and followings. Strong rulers audited; weak rulers bargained.

### Department-chart caution

A departmental chart proves specialisation, not an impersonal modern bureaucracy. Portfolios, tenure and influence changed.

### Revision notes

1. Wazir supervised income and expenditure.
2. Fiscal specialisation deepened over time.
3. Ariz handled recruitment, equipment, muster and pay.
4. The sultan remained commander-in-chief.
5. Insha handled state correspondence.
6. Risalat's exact portfolio is debated.
7. Sadr and qazi linked grants, learning and justice.
8. Barids supplied independent reports.
9. Wakil-i-dar managed household and precedence.
10. Nobility broadened but remained hierarchical.
11. Court ritual allocated political status.
12. Offices could become factional power bases.

### Lesson-local Mains model

**Question (15 marks; ceiling: 250 words):** Examine how departmental differentiation strengthened the Sultanate while generating political risks.

**Model answer:** Departmental growth enabled government beyond the ruler's personal presence. The Wizarat organised income and expenditure; under Muhammad bin Tughlaq, audit and accounting helped manage a vast realm. Diwan-i-Arz recruited, equipped, mustered and paid troops, while the sultan retained command. Diwan-i-Insha turned royal intention into correspondence, and barids supplied reports independent of governors. The sadr-qazi sphere connected grants, learning and justice; Satish Chandra's placement of Diwan-i-Risalat here should be disclosed as a source-specific reconstruction.

Specialisation also created intermediaries who controlled information and resources. A powerful wazir could become kingmaker; an ariz or governor could cultivate a military following; court access converted household rank into factional advantage. High salaries and iqtas supported service but fortified noble households. Alauddin answered with intelligence and inspection, whereas Firuz's heredity and conciliation enlarged elite autonomy.

Departments therefore made centralisation technically possible without depersonalising rule. Their effectiveness depended on the sultan's ability to compare reports, audit income and prevent office from becoming an independent power base.

**Why this earns marks:** It links named departments to governing tasks, then demonstrates the information and faction problem through contrasting reigns.

### Two devices for loyalty and finance

| State-building device | Resource supplied | Service expected | Failure risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elite bandagan / mamluk household | training, patronage, office and possible manumission | personal military-administrative loyalty | household faction or autonomous successor |
| Iqta | authorised claim on designated revenue | troops, order, administration and remittance | local entrenchment or concealed surplus |

*Both devices transformed resources into service, but neither made loyalty permanent.*

### Why dependence could become power

Early rulers needed commanders who depended on them and a way to maintain those commanders without paying every cost from Delhi's treasury.

### Unpacking household service and assignment

1. **Bandagan defined.** The term covered enslaved or dependent persons attached to households. Elite military slaves received specialised training and could reach command; they were only one form within slavery.
2. **Mobility and coercion.** Aibak and Iltutmish show exceptional elite ascent. Their careers cannot represent domestic, female, artisanal or mass war captives.
3. **Why household recruitment worked.** Initial separation from independent kin bases could strengthen patron dependence; education and proximity created capability and opportunity.
4. **Iqta defined.** An iqta assigned revenue with service or administrative obligations. It did not grant absolute ownership of soil or cultivators.
5. **Operating sequence.** A muqti or wali collected authorised revenue, maintained troops and order, met sanctioned expenditure and in principle remitted the balance.
6. **Controls.** Transfer, recall and audit sought to stop assignment becoming a private principality.
7. **Named evidence.** Satish Chandra notes broad early autonomy and a later effort to ascertain actual income, fix costs and demand surplus.
8. **Comparison limit.** “Feudal” is useful only if ownership, transfer, service and audit are specified; European hereditary estates cannot simply be imported as a model.
9. **Combined function.** Bandagan supplied trained command; iqta supplied territorial finance under conditions of limited cash administration.

### The property and slavery traps

Never equate mamluk with every slave or iqta with private landed property.

### Revision notes

1. Bandagan covered varied dependent and slave relationships.
2. Elite military slaves received specialised training.
3. Elite ascent did not erase general coercion.
4. Aibak and Iltutmish illustrate exceptional mobility.
5. Iqta was a revenue-service assignment.
6. Muqti and wali were common holder terms.
7. The holder met authorised troop and administrative costs.
8. Expected surplus belonged to the centre in principle.
9. Transfer, recall and audit limited entrenchment.
10. Distant enforcement was often weak.
11. Iqta was not ownership of cultivators.
12. Both devices solved early loyalty and finance problems.

### Lesson-local Mains model

**Question (15 marks; ceiling: 250 words):** Analyse bandagan and iqta as instruments of early Sultanate state formation.

**Model answer:** Bandagan and iqta addressed connected problems: creating dependable commanders and financing territorial rule. Elite military slaves were trained within a ruler's household and rewarded through patronage and office. Aibak and Iltutmish show remarkable upward mobility. This evidence must be qualified: domestic, female, artisanal and mass war captives experienced different coercive conditions, so elite mamluk ascent cannot represent slavery generally.

Iqta converted a designated revenue claim into service. A muqti or wali collected revenue, maintained troops and order, met authorised expenditure and was expected to remit the balance. Transfer, recall and audit preserved the centre's superior claim and distinguish iqta from absolute landownership. Satish Chandra's description of increasingly close scrutiny also shows institutional learning after initially autonomous governors.

The devices reinforced each other: trained household elites supplied command, while assignments maintained them where a large cash bureaucracy was unavailable. Their weakness was parallel—dependants could form factions and muqtis could entrench locally. They strengthened conquest and consolidation but required continual information, rotation and coercion to remain instruments of the crown.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains both mechanisms, uses named rulers, separates elite slavery from other forms and identifies a shared political risk.

| Village | khut, muqaddam, chaudhuri, patwari | headship, mediation and records | privilege and burden-shifting |
| Cultivating base | peasants and labourers | production and payment | ecology and coercion |

*Administration was a chain of translation; every link could transmit, alter or obstruct a demand.*

### Why local knowledge could resist Delhi

Delhi needed local information more than it needed labels. The same intermediary who made collection possible could hide income or shift the burden onto weaker cultivators.

### Periodising iqta and local administration

1. **Early Mamluks.** Leading commanders held tracts and distant muqtis could act autonomously because routine accounts and communications were weak.
2. **Iltutmish and Balban.** Transfer and noble discipline sought to prevent permanent provincial bases without abolishing assignment.
3. **Khalji tightening.** Alauddin expanded khalisa control in the core, measured revenue, pressured privileged intermediaries and funded a cash-paid army.
4. **Muhammad bin Tughlaq.** Wider cash calculations and central expectations exceeded reliable verification in several regions.
5. **Firuz's shift.** Softer audits and hereditary tendencies purchased elite peace but reduced mobility and scrutiny; they did not instantly create absolute private property.
6. **Sayyid-Lodi context.** Narrow fiscal reach encouraged bargaining. Afghan chiefs expected consultation, while Sikandar still demanded attendance and accounts.
7. **Local continuity.** Satish Chandra says initial local administration changed little. Pargana is associated with the amil; khuts, muqaddams and patwaris remained indispensable.
8. **Conflict over surplus.** Alauddin's attack on privileges shows centralisation as a struggle over who kept agrarian revenue.
9. **Chronology caution.** Shiq, pargana, chaudhuri and record offices become clearer at different dates; a standard late hierarchy should not be projected onto 1206.
10. **PYQ foundation.** Amil belongs to revenue collection; iqta was not an ancient indigenous institution; Mir Bakshi is Mughal, not a Khalji office.

### The fiscal-military-legal mesh

| State need | Institution or category | Operating mechanism | Variation |
|---|---|---|---|
| troops | central cavalry, household troops, iqta/chief contingents, infantry, elephants | recruitment, pay/assignment, muster and command | composition shifted by dynasty and region |

*The fiscal, military and legal systems intersected, but they were not one uniform code.*

### What an army needs beyond soldiers

An army had to be recruited, mounted, paid, inspected, supplied and coordinated. Revenue categories mattered only when people and institutions could turn them into grain, cash and service.

### Assembling troops, taxes and jurisdictions

1. **Army composition.** Sultanate forces included household or central troops, free cavalry, mamluk retainers, contingents maintained by muqtis and chiefs, infantry, elephants, sappers, garrison forces and changing regional recruits. Turks and Afghans were prominent in prestigious cavalry; Hindus served in infantry and also cavalry.
2. **Recruitment routes.** The centre recruited and paid some troops directly. Iqta holders and subordinate chiefs supplied contingents. War captives, household networks, frontier groups and mercenary/service markets widened manpower. No single ethnic group formed the entire army.
3. **Central versus assigned forces.** Central troops gave the sultan a counterweight to provincial commanders. Iqta and chief contingents reduced immediate treasury costs but created problems of loyalty, quality and delayed mobilisation.
4. **Payment.** Soldiers could receive cash salaries, revenue assignments or maintenance through a commander's iqta. Earlier doab village assignments became hereditary claims; Alauddin resumed them and paid central cavalry in cash. Under Firuz, hereditary village grants called **wajh** stabilised military households but weakened inspection and mobility.
5. **Horse supply.** Effective cavalry required imported and locally bred horses, remounts, fodder, roads, stables, dealers and veterinary care. Horses arrived through north-western and maritime trade; technology alone could not replace supply.
6. **Dagh and chehra.** Alauddin's horse branding and descriptive rolls checked substitution, fictitious musters and poor-quality mounts. They were inspection controls, not inventions of the Mughals; later rulers adapted them.
7. **Revenue categories.** Kharaj broadly denoted agrarian revenue; ushr a tithe-like legal category; zakat an alms levy on qualifying Muslim wealth; jizya a poll tax on eligible non-Muslims; khams the state's specified share of booty/resources. Customs, house, grazing, irrigation and other cesses also operated.
8. **Legal-practice warning.** Juristic labels did not map neatly onto every Indian locality. Alauddin's half-produce core demand and Firuz's separate jizya policy were reign-specific, not timeless percentages.
9. **Kotwal.** In major towns the kotwal handled policing, security, surveillance and aspects of market/urban order.
10. **Muhtasib.** The muhtasib supervised weights, market conduct and public morality in Islamic administrative theory and practice, with reach varying by centre and reign.
11. **Qazi and plural law.** Qazis dispensed Islamic civil law especially where Muslim populations were substantial. Criminal regulation could depend on sultanic rules; Hindu civil matters continued through panchayats, caste leaders, Dharmashastra and custom.
12. **Dynastic variation.** Alauddin tightened central cash cavalry and inspection; Muhammad enlarged and diversified forces but faced fiscal overreach; Firuz relied more on wajh and heredity; Lodi kings negotiated Afghan chief contingents.

*Monetisation was a layered ecology of denominations, mints, regions and cash-kind coexistence.*

### Why confidence gives money value

Money works because people recognise an issuer, metal, weight and accepted value. More coins do not mean every tax and wage became cash.

### Following coin, mint, region and trust

1. **Tanka and jital.** Iltutmish is associated with the silver tanka and copper jital system that anchored Sultanate monetary discussion. Chandra's concise text sometimes renders copper currency as dirham; source-specific terminology must be disclosed rather than flattened.
2. **Dirham caution.** “Dirham” could function as a broad monetary/accounting expression in chronicles or translations. Identify the source's usage before equating it mechanically with one denomination or metal.
3. **Mint plurality.** Delhi was central, but provincial mints issued coins as territory and administration expanded. Mint names show issuing activity, not automatically an independent kingdom.
4. **Delhi continuity during Deogir policy.** Coins continued to be minted at Delhi while Muhammad bin Tughlaq developed Daulatabad; this material fact contradicts the claim that Delhi was wholly emptied and permanently abandoned.
5. **Regional circulation.** Bengal, Gujarat, the Deccan and southern coasts possessed distinct monetary mixes. Bullion availability, trade and political authority shaped which coins circulated.
6. **Cash and kind.** Cash salaries, revenue valuation and merchant exchange expanded, yet grain payments, stores, assignments and barter persisted. Monetisation means wider money use, not universal cash.
7. **Token currency.** Muhammad declared copper/brass tokens equivalent to precious-metal value. The problem was not the abstract idea of token money but weak control of issue and authentication.
8. **Trust mechanism.** Easy imitation let counterfeit coins enter tax and exchange; inability to distinguish valid tokens destroyed confidence. Withdrawal and redemption imposed treasury cost.
9. **Fanam context.** Fanam referred to coins—typically small gold coins in southern/coastal contexts, especially Vijayanagara-Malabar trade. It should not be turned into a Delhi office or standard northern silver unit.
10. **Analytical result.** Coins linked taxation, salary, market pricing and long-distance exchange, but monetary depth remained unequal across regions and transactions.

### Monetisation traps

The trap is “cash economy = cash everywhere.” Also distinguish exact coin types from a chronicler's generic dirham vocabulary.

### Lesson-local Mains model

**Question (15 marks; ceiling: 250 words):** Explain why Sultanate monetisation should be understood as expansion without uniformity.

**Model answer:** Sultanate monetisation expanded because taxation, salaries, urban demand and trade increasingly used recognised coin. ### Why a garrison can become a city

A town grows when people, surplus and demand meet. Garrisons may start the concentration, but merchants, artisans, clerks and shrines make it more than a camp.

### Testing the urban-growth thesis

1. **Scale evidence.** Ibn Battuta called Delhi the largest city in the eastern Islamic world and compared Daulatabad's size with it. The testimony proves perceived scale, not a reliable census.

### Production chains and differentiated effects

| Production field | Sequence or institution | Named output | Social consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| textiles | cotton cleaning → spinning → weaving → dyeing/printing/embroidery | coarse cloth, calico, muslin, patola, zardozi | more specialised labour; women concentrated in low-paid spinning |

### Why a device changes labour relations

A spinning wheel matters only when cotton is cleaned, yarn reaches a loom, cloth is finished and merchants or the court create demand. Technology changes relationships as well as output.

### Following cloth, workshops, paper, buildings and metal

1. **Textile chain.** Cotton-carder's bow cleaned fibre; spinning wheel increased yarn preparation; looms turned yarn into cloth; dyeing, block printing and embroidery added value. Named cloth included coarse cotton, calico, Bengal muslin and fine cotton, Gujarat patola and zardozi.
2. **Organisation of artisans.** Production occurred in households, villages, specialised settlements, urban workshops and merchant-linked networks. “Guild” should not be assumed wherever occupational clustering appears.
3. **Gendered labour.** Women often spun and processed fibre in household production. Greater output could expand employment while keeping women in low-paid, less visible stages.
4. **Karkhana functions.** Royal karkhanas were stores, procurement units and supervised workshops. They supplied robes of honour, textiles, jewellery, metalware, weapons, furniture, household needs and construction materials.
5. **Karkhana labour.** Skilled artisans, servants and slaves worked under court demand. Firuz trained some slaves as craftsmen; the chronicler's huge slave total is not an audited labour census.
6. **Paper and bureaucracy.** Wider paper manufacture lowered the practical cost of correspondence, accounts and manuscripts. It increased demand for scribes and scholars without creating universal literacy.
7. **Building technology.** Lime mortar, arch-and-dome construction, stone and brick expertise emerged through interaction between transregional forms and Indian artisans. Large projects required quarrying, transport, scaffolding and thousands of workers; chronicler totals require caution.
8. **Hydraulic and urban construction.** Reservoirs, canals, wells, bridges and sarais joined engineering to revenue, movement and settlement; benefits depended on upkeep and locality.
9. **Metallurgy.** Armour, swords, tools, vessels, stirrups and horse equipment stimulated metal crafts. Cavalry superiority depended equally on horses, training, fodder and command.
10. **2023 GS-I social effects.** Technologies supported urbanisation, bureaucracy, military capacity, trade and craft specialisation. Effects were differentiated: merchants and courts captured high value; artisans faced discipline; slaves were coerced; peasants funded demand; women's employment often remained low-status.
11. **Dating caution.** Many techniques were diffused, adapted or expanded rather than introduced on one exact date by one foreign inventor.

For the 2023 demand, pair every technology with a mechanism and a differentiated social consequence; a mere device list cannot answer “how”.

| nobles and military elites | office, iqta, household and lineage | conquest, patronage and faction | ruler's suspicion and status rivalry |

*Mobility existed within a steep hierarchy; opportunity and equality are not synonyms.*

### Why opportunity did not equal equality

A slave could become sultan and a merchant could lend to nobles, yet these exceptional openings did not dissolve everyday inequality.

### Mapping rank, wealth, skill and exclusion

1. **Elite composition.** Turks, Tajiks/Iranians, Khaljis, Afghans, Indian Muslims and local chiefs entered ruling coalitions in changing proportions. Early Turkish exclusivity weakened without disappearing as a status ideal.
2. **Noble wealth.** Salaries, iqtas, gifts, robes and access to court supported ostentatious households, gardens and markets. Chronicler figures display perceived scale but are not audited accounts.
3. **Merchant position.** Wealthy Hindu and Muslim merchants lived conspicuously and financed elites, yet commercial wealth did not automatically grant the highest court rank.
4. **Rural hierarchy.** Rais, rawats, khuts, muqaddams, chaudhuris, owner-cultivators, tenants and labourers occupied unequal positions. “Peasant” is not a homogeneous category.
5. **Artisans and clerks.** Skills created demand in towns and karkhanas; literacy opened lower administration. Low wages and dependence limited mobility.
6. **Caste and occupation.** Varna-jati norms persisted, while migration, new crafts, military service, towns and patronage opened selective movement. Normative texts reveal prescriptions, not a complete social census.
7. **Muslim differentiation.** Ethnic, lineage and conversion-status prejudice divided Muslim society. Later labels such as ashraf-ajlaf can illuminate hierarchy only when used with chronological caution, not projected as one frozen Sultanate scheme.
8. **Interdependence.** Hindu managers, soldiers, merchants and village officials worked within Sultanate institutions; Muslim artisans, peasants and clerks occupied non-elite positions. The society cannot be divided into two sealed blocs.
9. **Analytical verdict.** Mobility was real but selective, usually through service, skill, patronage or wealth; hierarchy remained the governing structure.

### The exceptional-career trap

The unique trap is using one spectacular upward career to claim broad social openness, or using caste prescriptions as a literal census.

Opportunity widened within, rather than abolished, hierarchy.

### Different lives hidden inside broad categories

| Problem | Evidence-led range | Unsafe simplification |
|---|---|---|
| community interaction | conflict, shared work, patronage and neighbourhood exchange | permanent harmony or permanent separation |
| conversion | conviction, patronage, local networks, status, marriage and occasional coercion | one universal “sword” or “lower-caste escape” cause |
| slavery | elite mamluks, captives, domestic servants, artisans and royal bandagan | one uniform condition or Atlantic-racial analogy |
| women | elite household politics, property claims, agricultural/craft labour and servitude | Razia proves equality or purdah describes every woman |

*Each category contains different experiences; method begins by refusing a single cause.*

### Why social categories must be disaggregated

Words like “women,” “slaves,” “Hindus” and “Muslims” are too large to explain a life. Ask class, region, occupation, legal status and source visibility.

### Reconstructing conversion, bondage and gender

1. **Shared institutions and boundaries.** Hindu and Muslim officials, soldiers, merchants, artisans and peasants interacted in administration and markets while retaining religious and social distinctions.
2. **Conflict in context.** Temple damage, reuse, mosque construction and punitive action varied by conquest, war, ruler and locality. Neither a conflict-free composite society nor uninterrupted religious war fits the evidence.
3. **Conversion.** Motives could include conviction, Sufi or local networks, patronage, political advantage, marriage, occupational setting, agrarian expansion and occasional coercion. Punjab, Kashmir and east Bengal followed distinct regional processes.
4. **Negative evidence.** Satish Chandra rejects large-scale conversion by the sword as a sufficient account and finds no proof for one universal mass lower-caste conversion mechanism.
5. **Slavery's forms.** War captives, purchased persons, domestic workers, elite mamluks, royal bandagan and karkhana artisans had different training, rights, vulnerability and mobility.
6. **Firuz's establishment.** Afif's report of about 180,000 slaves and 12,000 trained artisans indicates a claimed large institution, not an audited census. The corps later contributed to faction.
7. **Women and work.** Common women worked in agriculture, spinning, food processing, craft and service, though male elite sources under-record them.
8. **Elite women.** Household and succession influence, patronage and Razia's rule show agency within exceptional positions, not general equality.
9. **Purdah and status.** Seclusion widened among some elites and became a prestige marker; occupation and poverty made uniform seclusion impossible.
10. **Property and marriage.** Multiple legal and customary systems shaped dower, inheritance, widow rights, marriage and remarriage. Textual rules do not prove uniform practice.
11. **Methodological caution.** Women and non-elite communities often appear through prescriptive or courtly sources; silence must not be converted into absence of labour or agency.

### The one-cause trap

Separate form, status and region before making a claim. The exam trap is a monocausal conversion narrative or treating Firuz's number as audited fact.

### Institutions of cultural exchange

| Cultural actor or field | Main role | Relationship to power | Necessary caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| ulema | law, teaching, grants and moral authority | advisers, office-holders and critics | never one united bloc |
| Sufis and khanqahs | discipline, hospitality, teaching and devotional networks | patronage, distance or tension with rulers | orders and individual saints differed |
| Persian | chancery, court and high literature | language of administration and status | “Persianate” is cultural, not ethnic |
| Arabic | theology, law and scholarship | scriptural and learned authority | less dominant in everyday administration |
| Sanskrit and regional languages | learning, ritual and literary production | local courts, temples and scholars | continued rather than disappearing |
| Hindavi | urban, military, devotional and everyday contact | cross-community communication | not one standard modern language |

*Interaction occurred through institutions and languages, not by erasing difference.*

### Why interaction did not erase difference

Ulema did not simply command the sultan, and Sufis did not simply oppose him. Both depended on specific people, orders, patrons and political moments.

### Locating authority in institutions and languages

1. **Ulema's roles.** Learned men taught, interpreted law, served as qazis or sadrs and received grants. Their access could legitimise rule, yet rulers resisted attempts to make juristic prescription the sole policy.
2. **Internal diversity.** Court-linked scholars, independent teachers and critics had different interests. Barani's ideals cannot be treated as the collective programme of all ulema.
3. **Sufi institutions.** Khanqahs offered teaching, hospitality and devotional community. Chishti distance from courts is a tendency, not an absolute; Suhrawardi and individual relations with power differed.
4. **Nizamuddin Auliya evidence.** Malfuzat such as *Fawaid-ul-Fuad*, recorded by Amir Hasan Sijzi, provide a non-court but hagiographic window into ethics and urban social contact.
5. **State interaction.** Rulers granted land or gifts, sought blessing, feared independent popularity or punished dissent. Patronage and autonomy coexisted.
6. **Persianate field.** Persian became the principal language of chancery and court literature. Persianate denotes an administrative-cultural sphere, not Persian ethnicity.
7. **Arabic, Sanskrit and vernaculars.** Arabic remained important for theology and law; Sanskrit learning persisted; regional languages grew through courts and devotional expression.
8. **Hindavi.** Military camps, towns, markets and devotional environments encouraged shared vernacular communication. Amir Khusrau's multilingual cultural position is evidence of interaction, though panegyric shapes his court writing.
9. **Causal synthesis.** Paper, chancery demand, migration, patronage and urban encounter widened textual and linguistic exchange. Unequal prestige did not eliminate multilingual practice.
10. **Advanced caution.** “Composite culture” is an analytical outcome to demonstrate with institutions and texts, not a slogan that erases conflict or hierarchy.

### Using culture without slogans

Use named texts and institutions: Barani, *Fawaid-ul-Fuad*, khanqah, Persian chancery and Hindavi contact. Avoid treating ulema or Sufis as homogeneous camps.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Dynastic change matrix

| Phase | Dominant institutional tendency | Named evidence | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mamluk | household elites, iqta consolidation and Delhi core | Iltutmish, Balban, transfer and court awe | uneven frontier and succession |
| Khalji | army-revenue-intelligence tightening | cash cavalry, dagh, chehra and market supply | coercive, capital-centred and ruler-dependent |
| Muhammad Tughlaq | widest ambition, cash calculation and experiments | Daulatabad, token currency and Doab assessment | communication, trust and regional overreach |
| Firuz Tughlaq | conciliation, canals, karkhanas, heredity and slaves | wajh, public works and Diwan-i-Bandagan | weaker audit and military discipline |
| Sayyid | restricted Delhi-centred tribute state | narrow territorial reach after Timur | thin resources and strong regional powers |
| Lodi | Afghan mobilisation, Agra and renewed revenue discipline | Bahlul's alliances; Sikandar's accounts | crown-chief tension under Ibrahim |

*The Sultanate was a sequence of changing bargains, not a single institutional photograph.*

### One recurring problem, five changing bargains

Every dynasty inherited the same basic problem—how to turn surplus into obedience—but solved it with a different mix of coercion, audit, patronage and negotiation.

### Comparing mechanisms across dynasties

1. **Mamluk foundation.** Iltutmish consolidated Delhi rule through iqtas, household networks and public legitimacy. Balban strengthened awe, frontier defence and noble discipline but did not solve succession.
2. **Khalji intensification.** Mongol pressure encouraged a large standing cavalry. Alauddin linked core revenue, cash salaries, dagh-chehra inspection, intelligence and Delhi market supply in the tightest fiscal-military nexus.
3. **Muhammad's expansion.** Wider recruitment, multiple mints, cash calculations, Daulatabad and token currency reveal extraordinary ambition. Counterfeiting, Doab distress, rebellion and logistics reveal institutional limits.
4. **Firuz's bargain.** Canals, towns, karkhanas, grants and slave training supported a benevolent image and selected growth. Hereditary office, wajh and softer audit secured peace while weakening central military-fiscal discipline.
5. **Fragmentation.** Post-1388 faction and Timur's 1398 sack accelerated an existing crisis. Regional states possessed their own agrarian and commercial resources; decline of Delhi was not societal emptiness.
6. **Sayyid restriction.** Delhi survived as a limited tribute and campaign state amid Khokhar, Mewati, Sharqi and other pressures.
7. **Lodi recovery and tension.** Bahlul mobilised Afghan networks and absorbed Jaunpur; Sikandar strengthened accounts, roads, grain movement and Agra. Ibrahim's stronger monarchy conflicted with chief expectations.
8. **Economic continuity.** Agrarian extraction, local intermediaries, coinage, crafts, roads and Persian administration survived in changing forms across contraction.
9. **Social continuity and change.** Hierarchy persisted while migration, urbanisation, service and language created selective mobility and interaction.
10. **Non-teleological conclusion.** Sher Shah and the Mughals adapted Sultanate precedents, but the Sultanate did not exist merely as their preliminary stage.

### How to synthesise rather than list rulers

A cross-dynastic answer must compare mechanisms, not list rulers. Use pressure → instrument → effect → limit for every phase.

- **B — Incorrect:** Wajh is especially associated with Firuz, token currency with Muhammad bin Tughlaq and the stated chronology conflates different reigns.
- **C — Correct:** Mongol danger encouraged a large cavalry whose cash maintenance required reliable core revenue, verified horses and soldiers, and controlled provisioning at Delhi.
- **D — Incorrect:** Fanam was southern/coastal coinage, field artillery is later in this form, and Delhi's mint continued during the Daulatabad policy.

#### Question 44

Which synthesis best characterises the Sultanate across 1206–1526?

**A.** A juristic state whose legal categories produced broadly similar practice across its localities.
**B.** A hereditary landed order in which iqta holders were immune from transfer, audit and royal claims.
**C.** A self-sufficient urban economy largely detached from agrarian surplus, horse imports and merchant credit.
**D.** A changing Persianate military-fiscal order: centralising in ambition, locally negotiated and socially unequal.

**MCQ 44: D**
- **A — Incorrect:** Sharia categories mattered, but zawabit, custom, regional conditions and political bargaining altered actual practice.
- **B — Incorrect:** Iqta was an assignment subject in principle to superior claims, transfer and scrutiny, although heredity could strengthen.
- **C — Incorrect:** Towns relied on rural food and raw material, imported horses, routes, revenue and credit networks.
- **D — Correct:** Across five dynastic phases, Persianate institutions and military-fiscal ambitions persisted, but local bargaining, regional variation and unequal social effects repeatedly reshaped them.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original model — 10 marks; ceiling: 150 words

**Question (10 marks; ceiling: 150 words):** Explain why the iqta was an instrument of centralisation as well as a source of decentralising pressure.

**Model answer:** The iqta converted a territorial revenue claim into service. A muqti collected authorised revenue, maintained troops and order, met sanctioned costs and was expected to submit accounts and remit surplus. Transfer and recall prevented a permanent local base; Iltutmish and Balban used these powers to discipline commanders. Thus assignment extended rule without a fully cash-paid provincial bureaucracy.

The same mechanism generated decentralising pressure. A distant muqti controlled revenue, soldiers and local information, enabling concealment or entrenchment when Delhi weakened. Alauddin therefore tightened audit and khalisa claims, whereas Firuz's hereditary concessions and softer scrutiny strengthened service households at the centre's expense. Sayyid contraction and Lodi chief bargaining further exposed the problem.

Iqta was consequently neither private property nor simple decentralisation. It centralised by delegating revocable authority, but its success depended on information, transfer and coercive capacity.

**Why this earns marks:** The answer defines the institution, uses named dynastic contrasts, explains both causal directions and closes with a precise qualified verdict.

## Original model — 15 marks; ceiling: 250 words

**Question (15 marks; ceiling: 250 words):** Examine monetisation, urbanisation and agrarian extraction as connected processes under the Sultanate.

**Model answer:** Agrarian extraction supplied the surplus that sustained courts, cavalry and towns. Khalisa officers, muqtis, amils and village intermediaries transferred grain or cash value from cultivators. Alauddin's upper-Doab assessment and cash-paid cavalry show how fiscal pressure could create predictable urban military demand.

Silver tankas, copper jitals, provincial mints and regional currencies facilitated salary, pricing and exchange. Muhammad bin Tughlaq's token issue reveals both monetary ambition and the institutional need for authentication: counterfeiting destroyed trust. Cash nevertheless coexisted with grain, stores, assignments and barter. Hundis, sarrafs and brokers reduced the risk of moving coin, while banjaras carried grain.

These mechanisms supported Delhi, Multan, Daulatabad, Cambay, Lakhnauti and Sonargaon. Towns concentrated clerks, merchants, artisans, soldiers and service workers. Textile chains, karkhanas, paper, construction and metal crafts expanded specialised labour.

The process was unequal and regional. Towns depended on village food and raw material; local elites could shift revenue burdens; merchant advances and slavery constrained labour. Firuz's canals enlarged output in selected tracts without ensuring universal welfare.

Monetisation, urbanisation and extraction therefore formed one political economy: rural surplus sustained towns, money widened circulation and urban demand stimulated production, while costs and gains remained uneven.

**Why this earns marks:** It builds a three-way causal chain with named coins, credit, cities and fiscal actors, then qualifies cash, geography and distribution.

## Original model — 20 marks; ceiling: 250 words

**Question (20 marks; ceiling: 250 words):** “The Delhi Sultanate was neither a uniform bureaucracy nor a timeless Islamic state.” Discuss.

**Model answer:** The Sultanate developed specialised institutions, but neither their functions nor their reach remained uniform. Wizarat, Diwan-i-Arz, Diwan-i-Insha, barids and iqtas gave the centre fiscal, military and communicative capacity. Yet portfolios changed, muqtis bargained, and village collection depended on amils, khuts, muqaddams and customary record-keepers. Alauddin's cash cavalry, audit and market controls differed sharply from Firuz's heredity and wajh; Lodi rulers negotiated Afghan chief expectations.

Nor can the state be reduced to a timeless theocracy. Khutba, sikka, caliphal recognition, qazis and ulema supplied Islamic legitimacy. Iltutmish's investiture and Firuz's juristic posture show its importance. But the caliph did not command armies or collect Indian revenue. Sultans issued zawabit for political necessity; Alauddin's reported dialogue with Qazi Mughis asserts discretion. Qazi adjudication coexisted with panchayats, caste forums and custom, while Hindu chiefs, accountants, soldiers and merchants remained embedded in governance.

The state was Persianate and Islamic in significant idioms, military-fiscal and pragmatic in operation. Its central apex could be formidable, but enforcement varied by ruler, region, resources and intermediary cooperation. “Changing centralisation under negotiated sovereignty” is therefore more accurate than either uniform bureaucracy or unchanging religious state.

**Why this earns marks:** It addresses both halves of the quotation, compares dynasties, integrates law and locality, and delivers a reasoned conceptual verdict.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Evidence and state formation

- Read chronicles, travellers, coins, inscriptions and material remains together.
- Court prescription is not proof of uniform provincial practice.
- The state was a centralising military-fiscal apex resting on mediated local operation.
- Mamluk, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid and Lodi phases require separate treatment.

## Sovereignty and central offices

- Sultan: supreme political-military authority, commander-in-chief and appellate justice.
- Caliphal recognition: moral and Islamic-world legitimacy, not direct Indian government.
- Khutba + sikka: public sovereignty; open succession still required noble and military support.
- Wazir/Wizarat: finance; Ariz/Arz: recruitment, muster, equipment and pay; Insha: correspondence.
- Risalat is linked by Satish Chandra to religious matters/grants, but the portfolio is debated.
- Barids supplied reports; wakil-i-dar connected household, stores, karkhanas and court precedence.

## Bandagan, iqta and locality

- Elite mamluks could rise through training and patronage; this was not the condition of all slaves.
- Iqta: revenue-service assignment, not absolute ownership.
- Muqti/wali: collect, maintain order/troops, meet authorised costs, account and remit surplus.
- Transfer, recall and audit were central controls; heredity and distance weakened them.
- Amil, khut, muqaddam, chaudhuri and patwari connected demand to cultivation.
- Alauddin tightened the core; Firuz encouraged heredity; Lodis bargained with Afghan chiefs.

## Army, revenue, police and law

- Army: central cavalry, household forces, iqta/chief contingents, infantry, elephants, sappers and garrisons.
- Payment: cash, assignments and maintenance through commanders; wajh was prominent under Firuz.
- Horses required imports/breeding, remounts, fodder, stables and inspection.
- Dagh = horse branding; chehra = descriptive roll; both have Sultanate roots under Alauddin.
- Kharaj, ushr, zakat, jizya, khams, customs and cesses are distinct; practice varied.
- Kotwal policed towns; muhtasib supervised market/public conduct; qazi adjudicated Islamic law.
- Panchayats, caste forums, custom and sultanic criminal regulations created plural jurisdiction.

## Agrarian system

- Crops included wheat, rice, millets, pulses, oilseeds, sugarcane, cotton and indigo.
- Wells, tanks, rivers, rahat devices and Firuz's canals supplied uneven irrigation.
- Haqq-i-sharb linked irrigation benefit to a water charge.
- Alauddin's half-share and measurement belong to a core setting, not every region.
- Muhammad's standard yield/price assessment plus famine and harsh collection deepened distress.
- Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi and sondhar loans attempted cultivation extension but faced corruption and ecology.
- Peasants were differentiated; intermediaries could shift burdens downward.

## Money, towns and exchange

- Silver tanka and copper jital anchor Delhi monetary history.
- Dirham vocabulary must be interpreted by source and metal context.
- Delhi and provincial mints coexisted; Delhi mint continuity contradicts complete abandonment during Daulatabad policy.
- Cash salaries and valuation expanded beside kind, stores, assignments and barter.
- Token currency failed through weak issue control, authentication and confidence.
- Fanam: small southern/coastal gold coin, especially Vijayanagara-Malabar contexts.
- Delhi, Daulatabad, Lahore, Multan, Kara, Lakhnauti, Cambay and Sonargaon were major nodes.
- Urbanisation drew on garrisons, courts, crafts, administration, religion and trade, but depended on villages.
- Banjaras moved grain; hundis, sahs, sarrafs and brokers supported credit and transfer.
- Gujarati, Marwari, Jain, Bohra, Multani, Khurasani and other merchants formed overlapping networks.

## Crafts and technology

- Textile chain: cotton cleaning → spinning → weaving → dyeing/printing/embroidery.
- Named outputs: coarse cloth, calico, muslin, patola and zardozi.
- Production occurred in households, villages, specialised settlements and towns.
- Karkhanas were stores, procurement centres and workshops for court and military goods.
- Paper supported chancery, accounts and manuscripts without mass literacy.
- Lime mortar, arch-dome adaptation and Indian craft skill enabled large construction.
- Metallurgy supplied armour, weapons, stirrups, tools and vessels.
- Technology was diffused and adapted; benefits differed by class, gender and region.

## Society and culture

- Nobles, merchants, rural elites, artisans, peasants, servants and slaves occupied unequal positions.
- Selective mobility through service, skill, literacy and wealth did not abolish hierarchy.
- Hindu and Muslim populations were internally differentiated and worked in shared institutions.
- Conversion was multi-causal and region-specific; no universal sword or lower-caste thesis suffices.
- Slavery included elite mamluks, captives, domestic servants and workshop labour.
- Afif's Firuz slave figures are chronicler claims, not audited census totals.
- Elite women's agency, common women's labour, purdah and property must be analysed by class and source.
- Ulema and Sufis were internally diverse; court relations ranged from patronage to tension.
- Persian dominated chancery; Arabic remained important in law/theology; Sanskrit and regional languages continued.
- Hindavi grew through urban, military, devotional and everyday contact.
