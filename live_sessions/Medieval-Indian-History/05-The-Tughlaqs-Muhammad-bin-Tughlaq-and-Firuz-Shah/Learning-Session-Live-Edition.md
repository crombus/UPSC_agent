# The Tughlaqs: Muhammad bin Tughlaq and Firuz Shah - Live Session Edition

> **Subject:** Medieval Indian History
> **Primary examination route:** UPSC Prelims GS-I; contextual support for GS-I culture, state formation and historical analysis
> **Central question:** Why did an exceptionally ambitious Sultanate under Muhammad bin Tughlaq contract, while Firuz Shah's quieter and more conciliatory state survived his lifetime but weakened after him?

## Learner roadmap

1. Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq: recovery, campaigns, Tughlaqabad and a disputed death
2. Muhammad bin Tughlaq: sources, personality, all-India ambition, elites and justice
3. Daulatabad: a phased second-capital project, not an emptied-Delhi legend
4. Token currency: design, precedents, authentication, redemption and trust
5. The doab crisis: assessment, famine, rebellion, relief and agricultural repair
6. Khurasan, Qarachil, plague, rebellions and regional breakaways
7. Firuz Shah's accession, governing contrast and campaigns
8. Revenue settlement, canals, towns, hospitals, workshops and public works
9. Heredity, military weakening, slaves, orthodoxy and the welfare contradiction
10. Succession, decline, architecture, material evidence, historiography and comparison

The roadmap follows historical dependency. First establish the sources and inherited state, then
test each policy as an objective-instrument-capacity-outcome chain, and only afterward compare the
two rulers or explain decline.

The Tughlaq story does not begin with an eccentric experiment. It begins with a frontier
commander rebuilding authority after a succession crisis.

### 1. How Ghiyasuddin came to power

Ghazi Malik emerged from the north-western military frontier and defeated Khusrau Khan in 1320.
As Sultan Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq, he had to solve three linked problems: restore dynastic legitimacy,
reassure officials and cultivators after political disorder, and keep a very large military state
solvent. His accession therefore matters because Muhammad inherited not a collapsing fragment but
a recently stabilised and expanding Sultanate.

### 2. Moderation was a governing instrument

Ghiyasuddin is associated with moderation in revenue demand, protection of cultivation and a less
disruptive relationship with rural society than the most coercive phase of Alauddin Khalji's rule.
The term **moderation** does not mean a modern welfare state. It means that the ruler recognised a
basic fiscal mechanism:

```text
cultivator survives and extends cultivation
                  ->
larger and more dependable taxable produce
                  ->
stronger treasury and army
```

This was prudential kingship. Its limit is equally important: collection still served a
military-fiscal monarchy, and chroniclers usually reveal policy more clearly than village-level
outcomes.

### 3. Three campaigns, three different forms of control

Ghiyasuddin's campaigns must not be compressed into a list because their objectives, commanders
and political results differed.

| Theatre | Objective and leadership | Result | Control qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warangal and the Deccan | Prince Ulugh Khan, the future Muhammad bin Tughlaq, was sent to restore tribute and then replace uncertain Kakatiya submission with firmer Delhi control | A first siege failed after a false report of Ghiyasuddin's death disrupted the army; a renewed expedition defeated Prataparudra, renamed Warangal Sultanpur and divided the conquered territory for administration | This was an annexation project, not merely another raid for treasure; its durability remained vulnerable to distance and later rebellion |
| Jajnagar/Orissa | Ulugh Khan's forces operated eastward after the Warangal success to assert Delhi's military superiority and obtain submission, booty or elephants | The operation demonstrated reach and produced an overlordship or tribute claim | Orissa was not incorporated into routine Delhi administration; describe this as a raid or tributary assertion, not permanent annexation |
| Bengal | Ghiyasuddin personally marched east to intervene in a contested regional order and reassert Delhi's superior authority | The expedition was militarily successful and restored Delhi's leverage through a political settlement before the Sultan returned toward Delhi | A successful royal expedition did not make all Bengal a uniformly administered province; negotiated allegiance and local rule remained important |

The distinction is therefore:

```text
Warangal -> conquest and attempted direct administration
Jajnagar -> raid/tribute/overlordship without annexation
Bengal   -> royal intervention and settlement, not uniform absorption
```

All three displayed dynastic capacity, but only Warangal represented the clearest move from
tribute toward annexation. This is the larger shift from Alauddin Khalji's preference for
southern submission toward the more ambitious Tughlaq attempt at direct control.

### 4. Tughlaqabad as evidence

Tughlaqabad was a fortified capital complex associated with Ghiyasuddin. Its massive enclosure,
battered or inward-sloping walls, rubble masonry, tomb-causeway relationship and water landscape
project authority and defence.

| What the site can show | What it cannot prove alone |
|---|---|
| large labour and material mobilisation | one single motive for every design choice |
| fortified dynastic kingship | that the entire dynasty had one uniform style |
| a planned political landscape | exact prosperity or poverty of all subjects |
| the technical use of sloping walls | that insecurity alone caused the architecture |

The strongest method is to move from visible fabric to bounded interpretation. Architecture is a
source, not a transparent photograph of motive.

### 5. The death controversy

Ghiyasuddin died in 1325 when a wooden pavilion at Afghanpur collapsed after his return from
Bengal. Some narratives implicated his son Juna Khan, the future Muhammad bin Tughlaq. Barani,
Ibn Battuta and later traditions do not produce a single independently verifiable verdict.

The responsible formulation is:

> The pavilion collapse is established; deliberate patricidal conspiracy remains disputed.

The strongest objection says that suspicious construction and the beneficiary's rapid accession
make conspiracy plausible. The reply is that political suspicion, retrospective narrative and
later literary shaping are not equivalent to forensic proof. Keep accident, culpable negligence
and conspiracy analytically separate.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Ghiyasuddin founded the Tughlaq dynasty in 1320; Tughlaqabad belongs to his phase.
- **Mains use:** open an answer by showing that the dynasty began with recovery and moderation,
  which sharpens the contrast with Muhammad's high-risk centralisation.
- **Trap:** do not state Ghiyasuddin's murder by Muhammad as uncontested fact.
- **Source skill:** distinguish event, accusation, motive and later stereotype.

### Revision notes

1. Ghazi Malik became Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq in 1320 after defeating Khusrau Khan.
2. His accession restored authority after the Khalji succession crisis.
3. Revenue moderation aimed to protect cultivation and stabilise receipts.
4. Ulugh Khan's renewed Warangal expedition defeated Prataparudra and attempted direct annexation.
5. The Jajnagar operation asserted tribute or overlordship but did not annex Orissa.
6. Ghiyasuddin personally led the successful Bengal expedition, whose settlement preserved local mediation.
7. Campaign success could mean annexation, tribute or negotiated allegiance; these are not interchangeable.
8. Tughlaqabad expressed fortified dynastic kingship.
9. Battered walls slope inward and visually increase solidity.
10. Architecture proves resources and choices more securely than a single political motive.
11. Ghiyasuddin died in the Afghanpur pavilion collapse in 1325.
12. Muhammad's responsibility is a source dispute, not a settled fact.

### Lesson 1 Mains application - differentiated campaigning

**Question:** Examine how Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq's campaigns combined dynastic recovery with different
forms of regional control. Answer in 150 words.

**Directive:** "Examine" requires a claim, differentiated evidence, consequences and a qualified
judgment; a conquest list is insufficient.

**Model answer (138 words):**

Ghiyasuddin's campaigns restored the authority of a new dynasty, but they did not produce one
uniform system of rule. At Warangal, Prince Ulugh Khan first retreated after a false report of the
Sultan's death, then returned, defeated Prataparudra and organised the renamed Sultanpur for direct
administration. This marked a shift from periodic Kakatiya tribute toward annexation. The
Jajnagar operation in Orissa had a narrower purpose: it displayed military reach and sought
submission, elephants or tribute, without incorporating the region into routine Delhi government.
Ghiyasuddin personally led the Bengal expedition, where military success enabled a political
settlement and renewed Delhi's superior claim, but local rulers and negotiated allegiance remained
important. Thus the campaigns rebuilt prestige and routes after Khalji instability while exposing
different layers of sovereignty. Chronicle claims of victory must not be equated automatically
with durable provincial administration.

**Why this earns marks:** It differentiates three theatres by named commander, objective, result
and control type, then qualifies campaign narrative through the annexation-overlordship distinction.

The useful question is not "Was he mad?" It is "How did ambition, instruments, information,
ecology and coercion interact?"

### 1. The source problem

| Source | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Ziya Barani | policies, political ideals, elite conflict | aristocratic and normative; hostile to socially low appointees |
| Ibn Battuta's *Rihla* | eyewitness court, gifts, roads, punishments and officials | traveller's taste for the remarkable; later composition |
| Isami | Deccan displacement and anti-Delhi critique | grievance, exile and retrospective memory |
| coins and inscriptions | titles, minting, dates and sovereign claims | rarely explain motive or social reception |
| architecture and archaeology | cities, forts and construction phases | repair, reuse and incomplete survival complicate attribution |

A vivid story can be true without being typical. A royal order can be documented without proving
uniform execution. Historical method therefore separates **intention**, **instrument**,
**implementation**, **regional effect** and **memory**.

### 2. Personality without diagnosis

Muhammad was learned in religious studies, philosophy, logic, medicine and the sciences. He
engaged with Muslim scholars and mystics, Hindu yogis and Jain teachers. Barani criticised his
rationalist disposition because he did not accept authority without inquiry. Yet intellectual
curiosity coexisted with severe punishment, impulsive coercion and political suspicion.

This combination is not a clinical diagnosis. Medieval chroniclers wrote moral and political
history, not modern psychiatry. Labels such as "mad king", "mad genius" and "ahead of his time"
hide the mechanisms that UPSC expects.

### 3. The all-India-state ambition

Muhammad inherited a Sultanate extending from the Punjab and Ganga plain through Gujarat and
Malwa toward the Deccan. But a map of maximum claim concealed different degrees of control.

```text
wide territorial claim
        +
slow communication and seasonal routes
        +
tribute-based or recently conquered provinces
        +
plague, famine and mobile armies
        =
high cost of uniform central control
```

His regulations, capital strategy, monetary experiment, agrarian interventions and recruitment
plans form one state-building project: make a vast empire more mobile, standardised and fiscally
usable. Their repeated weakness was not identical irrationality but inadequate administrative
verification and coercive overreach.

### 4. Nobility and appointments

Muhammad widened recruitment beyond old Turkish aristocratic circles. Foreigners, Indian Muslims,
some Hindus, artisans and men of comparatively low social origin could receive favour or office.
This could enlarge the talent pool and weaken inherited oligarchy. It also provoked elite
resentment, which Barani's social hierarchy makes especially visible.

The policy's limit was unstable patronage. Rapid elevation tied officials closely to the Sultan,
but rapid punishment made loyalty insecure. Diversity by itself did not create an impersonal
bureaucracy.

### 5. Ulema, Sufis, Jains, yogis and justice

Muhammad did not simply reject religion. Caliphal recognition and Islamic legitimacy remained
politically useful. He nevertheless resisted automatic clerical control and interacted across
religious traditions. Conflict with particular ulema or Sufis should not become the claim that he
was uniformly anti-Islamic.

His justice is similarly contradictory. The Sultan could hear complaints and punish powerful
people, yet Ibn Battuta and Barani also describe spectacular severity. Equal susceptibility to
royal punishment is not the same as predictable rule of law. The central problem was
discretion: a ruler personally active in justice could also be personally arbitrary.

### Criticism and reply

**Objection:** Rational intentions are an excuse; policies that repeatedly produced suffering
should be called irrational.

**Reply:** Consequences must remain central, but explanation improves when we distinguish a
rational objective from a defective instrument. Calling token currency or a central Deccan base
conceptually absurd prevents us from identifying why mint control, consent, logistics and trust
failed.

**Residual:** Rational objective does not absolve coercive design. State capacity includes the
capacity to anticipate social response, not merely the ability to issue orders.

### UPSC integration

- Use the formula **objective -> instrument -> capacity -> context -> outcome -> source limit**.
- The 2021 Prelims ruler-event question tests the danger of attaching every dramatic invasion to
  Muhammad's reign.
- For Mains, present personality only after analysing institutions.

### Revision notes

1. Barani is indispensable but aristocratic, moralising and hostile to low-status advancement.
2. Ibn Battuta is an eyewitness, not a statistically representative survey.
3. Isami is important for Deccan displacement but wrote from grievance.
4. Coins and inscriptions securely show dates, mints and titles more than motive.
5. Muhammad combined intellectual range with harsh political violence.
6. Rationalism did not make him secular in the modern sense.
7. His central concern was governing an unusually extensive empire.
8. Direct rule, tribute, garrison control and campaign claims must be distinguished.
9. Broad recruitment challenged old aristocratic monopoly.
10. Personal justice could coexist with arbitrary punishment.
11. "Madness" is a stereotype, not an explanatory category.

### Lesson 2 Mains application - personality through institutions

**Question:** Analyse why the label "mad king" is inadequate for explaining Muhammad bin
Tughlaq's rule. Answer in 150 words.

**Directive:** "Analyse" requires causal decomposition and source criticism, not either praise or
diagnosis.

**Model answer (129 words):**

The "mad king" label converts a difficult reign into personality and therefore obscures
institutions. Muhammad's learning in philosophy, medicine and religious debate, noted alongside
his contact with Jain teachers, yogis, Sufis and ulema, contradicts simple intellectual incapacity.
His projects also had identifiable aims: Daulatabad addressed Deccan supervision, token currency
sought monetary flexibility, and wider recruitment challenged aristocratic monopoly. Failure
arose when these aims met coercive design, weak verification, slow communication and unstable
patronage. Barani's aristocratic hostility helps explain his criticism of low-status appointees;
Ibn Battuta supplies eyewitness detail but favours remarkable court episodes; Isami writes from
Deccan grievance. These limitations do not excuse severe punishments or policy suffering.
Muhammad is best understood as an intellectually ambitious ruler whose personal discretion and
administrative reach were unequal to his centralising programme.

**Why this earns marks:** It replaces diagnosis with named policies and source positions, explains
causal mechanisms, preserves moral responsibility and ends with a qualified institutional thesis.

### Visual: one project, several phases

```text
Deogir chosen for central position and Deccan supervision
                         |
renamed/developed as Daulatabad
                         |
court, officials, nobles, scholars, Sufis,
merchants and artisans pressed to move
                         |
roads, posts and state support cannot remove
distance, coercion, heat, disease and livelihood costs
                         |
Delhi still populated and minting
                         |
southern rebellions remove strategic purpose
                         |
return permitted; Daulatabad remains a major Deccan centre
```

The right mental model is not a switch that moved one capital and emptied another. It is a
coercive second-capital project whose purpose changed as the empire contracted.

### 1. Why Deogir looked attractive

Deogir lay closer than Delhi to the newly conquered Deccan and was positioned within north-south
routes. A southern political centre could supervise distant provinces, reduce the psychological
distance between core and frontier, and protect authority from north-western danger.

This strategic logic resembles a firm opening a second headquarters near its fastest-growing
region. The analogy stops where medieval coercion begins: officials and social groups could not
simply choose whether relocation suited them.

### 2. Who moved?

The pressured groups included the royal household, nobles, officials, ulema, Sufis, merchants,
artisans and other leading inhabitants. Isami's account conveys severe dislocation. Yet evidence
that Delhi continued to mint coins and remain inhabited disproves the literal claim that every
resident was deported and the city left wholly empty.

Use **selective in social composition but coercive in execution**. "Selective" does not mean mild.
Moving administrators, religious networks, productive specialists and elite households could
reshape both cities even without deporting every labourer.

### 3. The logistics problem

The state reportedly improved roads, rest arrangements and communication. Still, infrastructure
could not eliminate:

- the distance travelled by households, dependants and animals;
- disruption of property, clientele and urban livelihoods;
- climate and water stress;
- surveillance and force;
- the continuing need to administer Delhi and the north.

The project therefore multiplied administrative commitments before it had secured consent.

### 4. Reversal and consequence

When Mabar, Warangal and other southern regions broke away, Daulatabad lost much of its purpose as
the command point of an all-India Sultanate. Return became possible. This was not simply an
admission that geography had always been irrational; changing political geography altered the
cost-benefit balance.

The project nevertheless had durable consequences. Daulatabad remained an important Deccan urban
and cultural centre, and migration strengthened the movement of Persianate, religious and
administrative networks southward. A failed imperial instrument can produce unintended historical
integration.

### Comparison: Delhi, Daulatabad and later capitals

| Case | Central problem | Instrument | Crucial difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daulatabad | supervise a far-flung Deccan empire | second capital plus coerced elite migration | fragile communications and changing territorial control |
| Sher Shah | integrate north Indian rule | roads, sarais, currency and administrative circuits | stronger route-centred consolidation rather than mass elite transfer |
| Akbar at Fatehpur Sikri | courtly-imperial centre | planned capital and mobile court | Mughal state retained multiple political centres and later moved without the same migration legend |

The comparison is analytical, not a claim that later states copied Daulatabad directly.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims trap:** Daulatabad was Deogir; it did not permanently replace an empty Delhi.
- **Mains thesis:** rational geopolitical aim + coercive social engineering + changing regional
  context = failure of political durability.
- **Evidence qualification:** Isami's suffering narrative is valuable but politically situated.

### Revision notes

1. Deogir was renamed and developed as Daulatabad.
2. Its central location and Deccan proximity gave the policy strategic logic.
3. It functioned as a second capital rather than the sole permanent replacement of Delhi.
4. Delhi remained inhabited and continued mint activity.
5. Court and leading administrative, religious and productive groups faced pressure to migrate.
6. Selective migration can still be large-scale and coercive.
7. Roads and facilities did not cancel social and ecological costs.
8. Southern breakaways removed much of the project's political purpose.
9. Return did not erase Daulatabad's later Deccan importance.
10. Failure should be explained through logistics, consent and political change, not geography alone.

### Lesson 3 Mains application - a rational aim with coercive costs

**Question:** Assess the Daulatabad project as both a strategic policy and a social failure.
Answer in 150 words.

**Directive:** "Assess" requires criteria, evidence on both dimensions and a reasoned degree of
success or failure.

**Model answer (138 words):**

Daulatabad had a rational strategic premise. Deogir stood closer to Delhi's recently conquered
Deccan provinces, and Muhammad bin Tughlaq developed it as a second capital with roads, posts,
quarters and court presence. Yet the instrument relied on pressure upon nobles, officials,
ulema, Sufis, merchants and artisans. Isami's account records severe dislocation, although his
grievance and retrospective voice require caution; continued Delhi minting independently disproves
the legend of a completely deserted city. The policy also imposed dual administrative and
livelihood costs that facilities could not remove. When Mabar, Warangal and other southern areas
broke away, the centre lost much of the territory it was designed to supervise, and return became
possible. The project therefore failed as a durable imperial mechanism and inflicted real social
costs, while migration still helped Daulatabad develop as a major Deccan urban and cultural
centre.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the strategic rationale, names affected groups and material

## Lesson 4 - Token Currency: A Trust System without Sufficient Control

Progress: 4/10 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Design, precedents, mint monopoly, authentication, redemption and failure

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - canonical mechanism and OCR book discussion
CA search: "India currency authentication counterfeiting trust legal tender 2026 RBI"
CA found: no direct historical linkage used; modern currency is only a bounded analogy
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: where the monetary circuit broke

```text
fiscal and monetary need
        |
copper/brass token assigned high face value
        |
success requires:
state monopoly + recognisable design + acceptance + enforcement + redemption
        |
weak authentication and easy private imitation
        |
forged coins enter taxes and exchange
        |
good money/trust withdraws from circulation
        |
state cancels issue and redeems accepted tokens
        |
treasury loss and heaps of rejected metal in chronicler imagery
```

Token money is a promise embedded in an institution. The metal need not equal the face value, but
users must trust who issued it and whether others will accept it.

### 1. What Muhammad attempted

Muhammad issued copper or brass token coins assigned a value associated with precious-metal
currency. This was not paper money. Nor was token currency unknown elsewhere: Chinese precedents
and monetary experiments in the wider Asian world show that the idea itself was not unimaginable.

A possible shortage of silver may form part of the fiscal context, but it cannot independently
explain the policy or its collapse.

### 2. Five conditions of workable token money

| Condition | Why it matters | Tughlaq weakness |
|---|---|---|
| controlled issue | limits supply | private imitation was easy |
| authentication | separates official from counterfeit | verification was weak |
| tax acceptance | creates demand for the token | forged coins could be tendered |
| enforcement | raises the cost of forgery | state reach was uneven |
| redemption/confidence | stabilises expectations | cancellation imposed treasury cost |

The central failure was not that base metal can never carry high value. Modern fiat currency also
depends on authority rather than intrinsic metal value. The crucial difference is institutional:
secure printing or minting, legal tender rules, centralised issue, banking systems, anti-forgery
technology and durable confidence.

### 3. Why counterfeiting became systemic

If an official token worth one silver unit can be privately produced for a fraction of that value,
the gain from forgery is enormous. When officials cannot distinguish genuine and counterfeit
issues, refusing all suspicious coins undermines legitimate users, while accepting them transfers
loss to the treasury.

```text
cheap imitation -> excess supply -> uncertain quality
               -> sellers demand better money
               -> taxes paid in doubtful tokens
               -> confidence collapses
```

The analogy with Gresham-type behaviour is useful only in a loose sense: the historical system and
coin standards were more complex than the slogan "bad money drives out good."

### 4. Withdrawal and redemption

The Sultan withdrew the scheme and reportedly redeemed token coins for precious metal. Redemption
prevented the state from simply repudiating every holder, but it socialised the cost of private
forgery. Chronicler descriptions of heaps of copper should be treated as imagery of scale, not as
audited monetary statistics.

### Criticism, defence and verdict

**Criticism:** The state should have anticipated forgery; weak design was part of the policy, not
an accidental later mishap.

**Defence:** Monetary innovation can be rational when precious metal is constrained and the state
needs liquidity.

**Verdict:** Rational concept, inadequate mint monopoly and authentication, unstable political
trust, and costly redemption. "Good idea, bad implementation" is still too shallow unless the
implementation requirements are named.

### Revision notes

1. The token issue used copper/brass metal, not paper.
2. Face value exceeded intrinsic metal value.
3. Token-money precedents existed outside India.
4. Silver shortage is possible context, not a complete causal explanation.
5. A state mint monopoly is essential when face value is conventional.
6. Weak authentication made official and forged coins hard to separate.
7. Counterfeit tokens could enter exchange and tax payment.
8. Confidence failure, not metal composition alone, destroyed circulation.
9. Redemption shifted losses toward the treasury.
10. Chronicler quantities and heaps are not audited statistics.
11. Modern fiat analogy requires strong institutional qualification.

### Lesson 4 Mains application - credibility before declaration

**Question:** Explain why Muhammad bin Tughlaq's token currency was not conceptually impossible

above a coin's metal content. Muhammad bin Tughlaq's copper and brass issue also had wider Asian
precedents. Its fragility arose from institutions rather than from copper itself. The Sultanate

could imitate official tokens at low cost. Doubtful coins then entered markets and land-revenue
payments, making sellers and officials unable to separate valid issue from forgery. Confidence
contracted, better money was preferred, and withdrawal followed. Redemption in precious metal
protected some holders but shifted counterfeit losses to the treasury. Barani's heaps of copper
are evidence of remembered scale, not audited monetary data. Modern fiat money is therefore only
a qualified analogy: both need trust, but contemporary systems add controlled issue, legal

The same state first deepened the crisis and then tried to repair it. Both halves are essential.

### 1. Why the doab mattered

The Ganga-Yamuna doab was fertile, accessible to Delhi and fiscally important. Raising revenue
there promised a large return. The danger was that a high paper assessment could become
uncollectable when official assumptions ignored actual harvest, price and household resilience.

### 2. The assessment mechanism

The state's share remained severe and assessment was associated with standard yields and official
prices rather than actual produce and local market conditions. This made the system legible to the
centre but brittle on the ground.

Example: if officials calculate revenue from a normal harvest of 100 units while drought produces
60, an unchanged demand consumes a much larger share of the real crop. The numbers are only an
illustration; they do not claim a documented village account.

### 3. Famine, collection and rebellion

Famine or climatic scarcity overlapped with over-assessment and harsh collection. Avoid choosing
between "natural disaster" and "bad policy" when the evidence supports interaction:

```text
scarcity reduces output
      +
rigid demand raises effective burden
      +
coercion destroys coping capacity
      =
flight, concealment, rebellion and further production loss
```

Chronology also matters. The tax measure, famine effects, rebellion and relief did not necessarily
begin on one day.

### 4. Relief and Swargadwari

The Sultan undertook relief and is associated with a camp at Swargadwari during the crisis. Relief
does not erase responsibility for earlier pressure. It shows that the ruler could recognise
failure and mobilise resources, while the timing and reach of assistance remained limited.

### 5. Diwan-i Amir-i Kohi and sondhar

The **Diwan-i Amir-i Kohi** was an agricultural-development department. **Sondhar** refers to
advances or loans for cultivation, including seeds, wells and inputs. The scheme sought to bring
land under cultivation and encourage superior crops.

This was more than famine charity. It attempted state-directed agricultural investment:

| Development need | Proposed instrument | Failure risk |
|---|---|---|
| uncultivated land | supervised cultivation | unsuitable land selection |
| lack of inputs | sondhar advances | diversion and corruption |
| water constraint | wells and investment | ecological mismatch |
| crop improvement | superior crops | time, skill and market limits |
| accountability | official supervision | weak monitoring and recovery |

### Comparison with Alauddin, Sher Shah and Akbar

Alauddin's high extraction worked more effectively in the core because measurement, intelligence,
price control and coercion formed a connected apparatus. Muhammad retained high ambition but
lacked equally dependable local compliance during crisis. Sher Shah and Akbar later developed
more durable measurement, rates, records and administrative routines. This is a comparison of
institutional capacity, not a claim that later systems were humane or perfectly uniform.

### UPSC integration

- Separate **revenue crisis** from **agricultural-development experiment**.
- Define Diwan-i Amir-i Kohi and sondhar precisely.
- Use famine as an interacting cause, not an excuse that removes policy responsibility.
- A strong conclusion: state legibility without ecological feedback can destroy the revenue base
  it seeks to enlarge.

### 1. The Khurasan project

Muhammad considered a western campaign when political conditions beyond the north-west appeared
favourable. He recruited and paid a large army, but the campaign was not launched as envisaged.
The important distinction is between strategic planning, force recruitment and actual invasion.

The fiscal cost arose even without battle: salaries, horses, equipment and eventual disbandment
consume revenue. Calling it a "world-conquest fantasy" skips the contemporary frontier context;
calling it harmless planning skips the mobilisation cost.

### 2. Qarachil was a different operation

The Qarachil expedition was an actual Himalayan campaign connected with hill or frontier
objectives. Severe losses followed terrain, weather, supply difficulty and command problems. It
should not be described simply as an invasion of China.

```text
plains army enters mountains
        ->
narrow routes and dispersed supplies
        ->
weather and disease magnify delay
        ->
communication breaks
        ->
retreat becomes more destructive than advance
```

The lesson is ecological state capacity: numbers and royal orders lose value when terrain prevents
food, information and reinforcement from moving.

### 3. Expansion and the plague

Muhammad's empire reached an exceptional scale, but campaigning in distant regions exposed armies
to disease and attrition. Plague during southern operations weakened forces and leadership.
Disease should not become a universal explanation; it interacted with prolonged campaigning,
revenue demands and unstable provincial alliances.

### 4. Rebellions and regional breakaways

| Region | Tughlaq-era transition | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Mabar/Madurai | far south asserted independence | direct Delhi control was shallow and expensive |
| Warangal | incorporation followed by loss and local assertion | not reducible to one capital policy |
| Kampili/Karnataka | conquest fed a wider political reconfiguration | do not equate one locality with all Karnataka |
| Vijayanagara | emerged conventionally in 1336 amid southern reordering | its full history belongs to a separate topic |
| Bahmani state | Deccan breakaway in 1347 under Ala al-Din Bahman Shah | Daulatabad and Deccan elite politics supplied context |
| Bengal, Gujarat, Sindh | recurring rebellion or autonomy | distance, governors and local resources mattered |

Mabar, Vijayanagara and Bahmani developments were not one coordinated revolt. They were distinct
regional processes accelerated by Delhi's weakening grip.

### 5. A multicausal model

```text
maximum territorial reach
      +
communication delay
      +
costly mobilisation
      +
fiscal experiments and agrarian distress
      +
plague and ecological limits
      +
provincial elites with local resources

repeated rebellion and durable regional states
```

The strongest objection says a more decisive ruler could have crushed the rebellions. The reply
is that repeated reconquest itself drains the resources needed to prevent the next revolt.
Individual decisions mattered, but the problem had become structural.

Firuz did not inherit Muhammad's full empire. His first achievement was political extraction: get

### 1. Accession and legitimacy

Muhammad died in 1351 during the Thatta campaign. Firuz's accession depended on selection by
nobles and military leaders, Tughlaq kinship claims, religious support and the practical need for
command. There was no fixed law of primogeniture. In the Sultanate, dynastic relation helped, but

### 2. The governing contrast

| Axis | Muhammad | Firuz |
|---|---|---|
| imperial aim | wide centralisation and experimentation | core stability and conciliation |
| elite method | broad recruitment, rapid favour and punishment | high assignments, reassurance and heredity |
| fiscal style | demanding experiments | stable settlement and development |

| religious posture | wide intellectual contact plus violence | stronger juristic orthodoxy plus welfare |

Firuz's model was not simple weakness. It was a calculated bargain: accept less control over
distant regions and elites in exchange for peace in the north Indian core.

### 3. Bengal

Firuz campaigned in Bengal but failed to restore durable Delhi control. Bengal's riverine
geography, established ruling resources and distance limited conquest. Campaigning could secure
recognition or temporary advantage without producing lasting annexation.

### 4. Jajnagar and Nagarkot

Campaigns toward Jajnagar in Odisha and Nagarkot in the Himalayan foothills displayed continuing
military activity and royal claims. Temple action, tribute and plunder in such narratives require
case-specific treatment rather than a single universal label. Firuz was not militarily inactive,
but these expeditions did not rebuild Muhammad's all-India empire.

### 5. Thatta

The Thatta campaign was difficult because of distance, terrain, supply and famine-like stress.
Firuz eventually secured a settlement, but the experience discouraged further high-risk
expansion. Thatta demonstrates continuity with Muhammad's logistical problem: an army can be
large in the core and still become fragile at the end of a long supply line.

### Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Firuz's peace resulted merely from abandoning imperial responsibility.

**Reply:** Restraint can be rational after overextension, and the northern core retained
considerable scale. The deeper criticism is not that he avoided every distant war, but that his
domestic concessions weakened mechanisms needed to survive a succession crisis.

2. Succession required elite and military acceptance; primogeniture was not fixed.
3. Firuz prioritised conciliation and northern-core stability.
4. He did not simply become a pacifist.
5. Bengal campaigns failed to restore durable Delhi authority.
6. Riverine geography and regional resources limited conquest.
7. Jajnagar and Nagarkot show continuing military and religious-political assertion.

9. The post-Thatta turn toward peace was strategic as well as temperamental.
10. Short-term stability and long-term institutional strength are different tests.

Firuz's works were not charity detached from politics. Irrigation, settlement, revenue and
legitimacy formed one governing cycle.

### 1. Revenue settlement

Firuz sought a stable assessment and reduced the violent audit practices associated with periods
of intense central pressure. Stable **jama**, the assessed revenue demand, reassured assignment
holders and cultivators where implemented. But freezing or softening assessment could also hide
gains captured by intermediaries and weaken the centre's knowledge of actual resources.

He emphasised taxes represented as authorised by Islamic law and removed some cesses in his own
account. Royal self-description must be compared with local practice; abolition on paper does not
prove uniform disappearance of every levy.

### 2. Canals and haqq-i sharb

Firuz is associated with canals serving Hissar-Firuzah and Haryana tracts, linking water sources
with cultivation and settlement. **Haqq-i sharb** was a water charge levied on benefiting
villages, reported in the advanced source as ten per cent.

| Canal effect | Political-economic meaning | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| more reliable irrigation | reduces some rainfall risk | only within reachable gradients |
| cultivation extension | enlarges taxable output | maintenance and silt control required |
| new settlements | strengthens core-region presence | benefits were regionally concentrated |
| water charge | captures part of state-created gain | burden and incidence could vary |

Do not call the canals modern national irrigation policy. They were substantial medieval works
whose effectiveness depended on local labour, maintenance and terrain.

### 3. Towns and urban landscapes

Firuz founded or developed Hissar-Firuzah, Firuzabad and the palace-fort now called Firoz Shah
Kotla. Urban foundations placed court, market, military and water infrastructure within a visible
political geography. Firuzabad's river orientation and Kotla's mosque, palace remains and Ashokan
pillar show that a capital was both administrative machinery and sovereign theatre.

### 4. Hospitals, education and charity

The **Dar al-Shifa** was a hospital or healing institution. **Diwan-i Khairat** is associated with
charity and marriage assistance for needy Muslim women. Madrasas, stipends and repairs to water
works supported learning and welfare.

Narrative sources also report that **kotwals**, urban police and administrative officers, were
ordered to compile registers of unemployed persons, and that an employment bureau was attempted.
This should not be modernised into a labour ministry or read as proof of universal job placement.
The same accounts indicate that grants and marriage assistance were directed largely toward
respectable Muslim families, especially in and around Delhi. By contrast, the reported
**Dar al-Shifa** offered free treatment more widely. These were different measures with different
social reach:

| Measure | Reported function | Necessary qualification |
|---|---|---|
| unemployment registers/employment bureau | identify unemployed persons and attempt assistance | narrative-source evidence, not a modern employment guarantee |
| grants and marriage assistance | support selected poor or distressed households | concentrated among respectable, Delhi-centred Muslim groups |
| Dar al-Shifa | provide free medical treatment | described as wider in access than the targeted grants |

These measures justify the term benevolence only with qualification:

- beneficiaries were not socially universal;
- charity reinforced the ruler's moral legitimacy;
- surviving descriptions often come from celebratory sources;
- institutional names should not be inflated into modern ministries with modern rights.

### 5. Karkhanas and public works

**Karkhanas** were royal workshops and store establishments producing, repairing or maintaining
goods for court and state. They could employ skilled artisans and slaves. Firuz also created
a separate public-works department, identified in the detailed narrative as the
**Diwan-i Imarat**; Malik Ghazi, bearing the title **Mir-i Imarat**, supervised building activity.
This was a medieval office for construction and repair, not a modern infrastructure ministry.
Its works included canals, gardens, reservoirs, bridges, mosques, madrasas, towns and restoration.

### 6. Ashokan pillars

Firuz transported ancient monolithic pillars, including the Topra pillar placed at Firuz Shah
Kotla. The operation demonstrates engineering organisation, labour command and appropriation of an
ancient object into Sultanate sovereignty. The Brahmi inscriptions were not necessarily fully
understood at court. Conservation, curiosity and political display can coexist.

**Criticism:** Celebrated public works merely mask elite appeasement and orthodoxy.

**Reply:** Canals, hospitals, towns and repairs had real material effects even when politically
useful and socially limited.

**Balanced assessment:** Welfare and hierarchy were not opposites in a medieval monarchy.

### 1. Iqta, office and heredity

An **iqta** was a revenue assignment linked to service and administration; it was not private
ownership of the land or cultivators. Firuz allowed stronger hereditary claims in iqtas and
offices. This reassured nobles and reduced destructive transfer struggles.

The cost was institutional. Transfer and audit are ways by which a centre prevents an assignment
holder from becoming locally entrenched. Heredity shifted bargaining power toward families and
away from the Sultan.

### 2. Wajh, dagh and military review

**Wajh** refers here to village or revenue assignments used to support soldiers. Hereditary
transmission stabilised military households but weakened cash payment, mobility and review.

**Dagh** means horse-branding, and **chehra** means the descriptive roll of a soldier. Alauddin
used these controls to reduce false musters and substitution. Under Firuz, rigorous inspection and
audit weakened. A hereditary soldier on an assignment could become a local claimant rather than a
regularly tested cavalryman.

Comparison with Sher Shah and Akbar is justified only at mechanism level: both later rulers
revived or systematised inspection and revenue-accounting controls. They did not invent the idea
that cavalry and assignments require verification.

### 3. The slave establishment

Firuz maintained a large body of **bandagan**, meaning slaves or dependants in coerced service,
under a separate Diwan-i Bandagan. They served in households, workshops, administration and
armed/personal roles. Afif reports about 180,000, including approximately 12,000 trained as
artisans.

#### Optional advanced depth - interpreting Afif's number

Treat both 180,000 and the approximately 12,000 artisan-trained subset as chronicler figures, not
audited administrative totals. Do not romanticise training: skill formation occurred within
unfree status. Nor should every Sultanate slave be equated with modern Atlantic racial chattel
slavery; legal status, military opportunity, household position and coercion varied.

The corps could serve as a counterweight to nobles, but after Firuz it became another organised
faction in succession politics.

### 4. Orthodoxy and jizya

Firuz presented rule in closer conformity with Islamic law, strengthened ulema influence and
treated **jizya**, the poll tax on eligible non-Muslim subjects, as a distinct levy. Sources
associate his reign with extending it to Brahmans who had claimed exemption.

This does not justify either extreme:

- "uniform persecution" ignores negotiation, local variation, translations and public works;
- "complete tolerance" ignores jizya differentiation, temple actions and punishments.

Case, chronology, source and administrative reach must be specified.

### 5. Punishment, translation and welfare contradiction

Firuz's own *Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi* presents preferred religious and public acts; it is royal
self-representation. Satish Chandra reports the chronicle account that a Brahman was publicly
burnt after being accused of preaching to people, including Muslims. This is significant evidence
of punitive orthodoxy, but one reported prosecution cannot establish a uniform policy applied
identically across all regions and communities.

The same reign is associated with abolishing mutilatory punishments such as cutting hands, feet
or noses, establishing hospitals, translating Sanskrit works into Persian, supporting education,
building canals and transporting ancient pillars. The contrast is not resolved by discarding one
side of the evidence:

```text
reported public burning for prohibited preaching
                    +
distinct jizya and Brahman liability
                    |
          sharper juristic hierarchy

abolition of mutilatory penalties
                    +
hospitals, translations and public works
                    |
          bounded humanitarian policy
```

#### Optional advanced depth - reading the contradiction

The chronicle report identifies a specific accusation and punishment, not a statistical pattern.
Firuz's *Futuhat* is also self-presentation, while later narratives organise memory around both
orthodoxy and benevolence. Source genre, political purpose and regional reach must therefore
qualify any general verdict.

The advanced narrative also reports death penalties imposed on some Muslims described as acting
"in a sufistic manner" against orthodox belief. This is evidence of punishment directed at
particular heterodox practices, not a basis for claiming persecution of Sufis as a whole.
Indeed, the same reign supported or repaired a number of Sufi establishments. The historical unit
is a specific reported prosecution within a wider, internally varied relationship.

The contradiction disappears only if we wrongly assume that a ruler must be either culturally
curious or orthodox. Medieval sovereignty could appropriate knowledge while enforcing hierarchy.

**Defence:** After Muhammad's upheavals, heredity and conciliation reduced fear, stabilised
expectations and supported decades of peace and construction.

**Criticism:** Peace was purchased by surrendering audit, mobility and merit; welfare was selective;
slave patronage and orthodoxy created new dependencies.

**Verdict:** Firuz strengthened the lived stability of his reign but weakened the impersonal
capacity of the office he would leave behind.

### 1. Succession after 1388

Firuz's long reign did not create a stable succession mechanism. Rival princes, nobles, military

### 2. Timur as catalyst, not sole cause

Timur crossed into north India and sacked Delhi in 1398, long after Muhammad bin Tughlaq and a
decade after Firuz's death. The invasion caused enormous human, economic and symbolic damage and
removed artisans and wealth. But Timur did not create the preceding succession crisis or establish
a continuously administered Indian empire.

The correct verdict is catalyst: an external shock exploited and intensified internal
fragmentation.

### 3. Architecture across three rulers

Tughlaq architecture is not uniformly austere. Rubble cores, plaster, stone facing, battered walls,

### 4. Inscriptions and coins

Coins establish rulers, titles, mints, denominations and continued urban activity. Delhi minting
during the Daulatabad phase helps challenge the emptied-city legend. Token coins illuminate design
and circulation but cannot alone quantify forgery or popular confidence.

Inscriptions can date construction, patronage and titulature. Their survival is uneven, and a
commemorative inscription presents authority rather than an impartial social survey.

### 5. Historiographical stereotypes

| Stereotype | What it notices | What it hides |
|---|---|---|
| Muhammad the mad king | volatility and disastrous outcomes | rational aims, source prejudice and distinct mechanisms |
| Muhammad ahead of his time | innovative policy imagination | coercion, design defects and contemporary constraints |
| Firuz the benevolent ruler | canals, hospitals, charity and peace | hierarchy, jizya, slavery and institutional weakening |
| Firuz the fanatic | orthodoxy and punishments | translations, repairs and developmental works |

A balanced answer need not split the difference mechanically. It should preserve contradictions
and rank causes.

#### Optional advanced depth - how evidence creates reputations

Barani's normative criticism, Ibn Battuta's taste for exceptional court conduct, Isami's Deccan
grievance, Afif's celebration of Firuz and each ruler's own monumental self-presentation explain
why later memory hardens into opposites. Historiography must compare genre and purpose before
combining claims; it should not average incompatible sources into a falsely neutral personality.

### 6. Comparisons with Alauddin, Sher Shah and Akbar

**Alauddin and Muhammad:** both pursued centralisation and heavy extraction. Alauddin connected
revenue, market, intelligence and military inspection more tightly in the core; Muhammad attempted
wider and more varied interventions across a larger, less stable geography.

**Muhammad and Sher Shah:** road, currency and revenue concerns can be compared, but Sher Shah's
strength lay in more routinised instruments and a narrower consolidation problem. There is no need
to call every later reform a direct copy.

**Firuz and Akbar:** both used public works and elite management, but Akbar's mansab-jagir order
combined hierarchy, transfer, assessed revenue and military obligations more systematically.
Akbar's system also had serious later strains; comparison is not a progress myth.

### 7. Final causal verdict

The Tughlaq experience reveals two failed routes to durable centralisation:

```text
Muhammad:
high ambition + coercive mobility + weak verification + ecological overreach
                         -> rapid contraction

Firuz:
conciliation + works + heredity + softer audit + personal patronage
                         -> lifetime stability, posthumous fragmentation
```

The dynasty did not fail because one ruler was insane and the other kind. It exposed the problem
of turning conquest into institutions that could survive distance, crisis and succession.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Model 1 - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Examine why Muhammad bin Tughlaq's token-currency experiment failed despite having a
rational monetary objective. Answer in 150 words.

**Directive:** "Examine" requires the objective, operating mechanism, causes of failure and a
qualified verdict. Do not merely narrate issue and withdrawal.

**Model answer (149 words):**

Muhammad bin Tughlaq's copper/brass token currency sought monetary flexibility by assigning base
metal a face value associated with precious-metal coin. The objective was not inherently absurd:
token money had external precedents and could enlarge circulation where silver was constrained.

Failure lay in the credibility mechanism. The state lacked an effective mint monopoly and a
reliable method to distinguish official tokens from private imitations. Counterfeit production
therefore expanded, doubtful coins entered exchange and tax payment, and holders preferred more
trusted money. When the Sultan withdrew and redeemed accepted tokens in precious metal, the
treasury absorbed part of the loss created by forgery.

Barani's imagery of metal heaps conveys collapse but is not an audited quantity. Thus the scheme
was rational in broad intent yet defective in design, enforcement and political trust. Its lesson
is that conventional money requires controlled issue, authentication, acceptance and credible
redemption, not royal declaration alone.

**Why this earns marks:** It follows claim -> named evidence -> mechanism -> source qualification
-> verdict and explains failure rather than labelling the ruler.

## Model 2 - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Compare Muhammad bin Tughlaq and Firuz Shah Tughlaq as two responses to the problem
of governing a large Sultanate. Answer in 250 words.

**Directive:** "Compare" requires common problem, paired axes, reasons for difference and a graded
judgment, not two disconnected biographies.

**Model answer (223 words):**

Muhammad bin Tughlaq and Firuz Shah faced the same structural problem: how could Delhi convert
wide conquest into durable revenue, military obedience and provincial loyalty? They chose nearly
opposite instruments.

Muhammad pursued mobile centralisation. Daulatabad sought closer Deccan supervision, token
currency aimed at monetary flexibility, doab assessment enlarged fiscal claims, Diwan-i Amir-i
Kohi attempted cultivation extension, and the Khurasan army projected strategic initiative.
Each had a rational objective, but coercive migration, weak mint authentication, rigid assessment,
corrupt supervision and difficult logistics exposed a gap between decree and implementation.
Plague, distance and regional resources enabled Mabar to break away, while Vijayanagara emerged
amid southern reordering and Deccan elites founded the Bahmani state in 1347. Barani's hostility
and Isami's grievance require caution, but contraction is clear.

Firuz accepted a smaller effective empire and substituted conciliation. Canals around
Hissar-Firuzah, towns, hospitals, charity, repairs and karkhanas strengthened the northern core.
High iqta incomes, hereditary office and wajh, softer audit and noble reassurance reduced immediate
conflict. Yet Afif's large slave establishment, weakened military review, sharper jizya and
entrenched family claims reveal the costs of stability.

Therefore Muhammad overstrained institutions through high-risk centralisation, whereas Firuz
stabilised his lifetime by devolving discipline into patronage. The first produced rapid
contraction; the second postponed conflict until succession after 1388. Neither model created an
impersonal state able to survive distance and dynastic transition.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares on common axes, uses named policies and sources, and ends
with an institutional rather than moral contrast.

## Model 3 - 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** "The decline of Tughlaq power was a crisis of state capacity, not a story of one
eccentric ruler or one external invasion." Discuss. Answer in 250 words.

**Directive:** "Discuss" requires evidence for the proposition, counter-qualification and a
reasoned final assessment.

**Model answer (235 words):**

Tughlaq decline began at the moment when territorial ambition exceeded the institutions that
could transmit information, revenue and force. Muhammad bin Tughlaq's personality affected
decisions, but it cannot alone explain the pattern.

First, distance weakened centralisation. Daulatabad had strategic logic as a second capital, yet
coercive migration and the loss of southern provinces removed its purpose. Second, fiscal
instruments lacked verification. Token currency collapsed under counterfeiting, while doab
assessment based on official yields and prices intensified famine distress and rebellion. Third,
developmental correction remained institutionally weak: sondhar loans and Diwan-i Amir-i Kohi
were undermined by land choice, corruption and monitoring. Fourth, mobilisation exceeded
logistics. The unlaunched Khurasan project burdened the treasury; Qarachil exposed mountain supply
limits; plague weakened southern armies. Mabar's independence, Vijayanagara's emergence and the
Bahmani breakaway of 1347 show regional agency, not merely royal error.

Firuz restored peace through canals, towns, welfare and elite reassurance. However, hereditary
iqtas, offices and wajh, weaker dagh and audit, and the large slave establishment converted
personal conciliation into organised posthumous factions. After 1388, rival heirs and nobles
fragmented the core.

Timur's sack of Delhi in 1398 was devastating, but it struck an already weakened polity and did
not establish a durable Indian administration. Barani, Ibn Battuta, Isami, Afif, coins and
architecture each illuminate only part of this process.

Thus individual choices mattered within a deeper crisis: conquest had outgrown communications,
fiscal credibility, military logistics and succession institutions.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the quotation directly, ranks multicausal mechanisms, names
regional and material evidence, qualifies sources and integrates the counter-role of Timur.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Dynastic recovery and evidence

- 1320: Ghazi Malik became Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq after Khusrau Khan.
- Moderation aimed to rebuild cultivation, revenue and legitimacy.
- Ulugh Khan's renewed Warangal campaign defeated Prataparudra and attempted direct administration.
- The Jajnagar/Orissa operation asserted military superiority and tribute without annexation.
- Ghiyasuddin personally led the successful Bengal intervention, but its settlement retained local mediation.
- Campaign outcomes must be classified as annexation, tribute/overlordship or negotiated allegiance.
- Tughlaqabad: fortified city, battered walls, tomb-causeway-water landscape.
- 1325 Afghanpur pavilion collapse: event certain; Muhammad's conspiracy disputed.
- Source rule: separate policy intention, implementation, regional effect and later memory.

## Muhammad's state-capacity problem

- Reign: 1325-51.
- Intellectual range: religion, philosophy, logic, medicine/science; contact with Sufis, ulema,
  Jain teachers and yogis.
- Violence and curiosity coexisted; avoid psychiatric labels.
- Empire combined direct rule, tribute, garrisons and claims across a vast geography.
- Nobility included foreigners, Indian Muslims, some Hindus, artisans and socially low new men.
- Broad recruitment weakened monopoly but retained unstable personal patronage.
- Justice could reach powerful men yet remained discretionary and severe.

## Daulatabad

- Deogir renamed/developed as Daulatabad.
- Strategic aim: a second centre closer to the Deccan.
- Pressured migration affected court, nobles, officials, religious groups, merchants and artisans.
- Delhi was not wholly emptied; population and minting continued.
- Logistics, coercion and disrupted livelihoods produced hardship.
- Mabar/Warangal/southern losses removed the centre's imperial purpose.
- Return was permitted; Daulatabad remained an important Deccan centre.

## Token currency

- Copper/brass tokens received a face value associated with precious-metal currency.
- Not paper money; external precedents existed.
- Possible silver constraint is context, not sole cause.
- Failure chain: weak mint monopoly -> forgery -> indistinguishable issues -> distrust -> withdrawal.
- Redemption burdened the treasury.
- Modern fiat analogy requires controlled issue, authentication, institutions and confidence.

## Doab and agricultural policy

- Standard yields and official prices made assessment insensitive to actual harvest and markets.
- Harsh collection interacted with famine, flight and rebellion.
- Relief and Swargadwari followed severe crisis.
- Diwan-i Amir-i Kohi = agricultural-development department.
- Sondhar = cultivation advances for inputs such as seeds and wells.
- Aim: extension and superior crops.
- Limits: corruption, monitoring, land suitability, ecology, time and scale.

## Khurasan, Qarachil and contraction

- Khurasan: western campaign project; large army recruited and paid; plan not launched as envisaged.
- Qarachil: actual Himalayan expedition; terrain, climate, supply and command caused losses.
- Do not call Qarachil a straightforward invasion of China.
- Plague weakened southern campaigning.
- Mabar became independent; Warangal and Kampili moved outside durable control.
- Vijayanagara emerged conventionally c. 1336 amid southern reconfiguration.
- Bahmani state broke away in 1347.
- Bengal, Gujarat, Sindh and the Deccan reflected different combinations of distance and local power.

## Firuz's accession and campaigns

- Reign: 1351-88.
- Selected after Muhammad died during the Thatta campaign.
- Legitimacy joined kinship, noble-military acceptance and religious support.
- Bengal campaigns failed to restore durable control.
- Jajnagar and Nagarkot showed continued campaigning.
- Thatta exposed logistics and encouraged later restraint.
- Governing shift: coercive centralisation -> conciliation and northern-core development.

## Revenue, irrigation and welfare

- Stable jama and softer audit reassured elites and producers but reduced fiscal scrutiny.
- Canals supported Hissar-Haryana cultivation and settlement.
- Haqq-i sharb = water charge; advanced source reports ten per cent.
- Towns: Hissar-Firuzah, Firuzabad/Firuz Shah Kotla.
- Dar al-Shifa = hospital reported to provide free treatment with comparatively wider reach.
- Diwan-i Khairat = charity and marriage assistance, largely for needy Muslim women.
- Narrative sources report unemployment registers and an attempted employment bureau, but not
  a modern labour ministry or universal employment guarantee.
- The reported grants and assistance were limited mainly to respectable Muslim groups around
  Delhi, unlike the hospital's wider/free-treatment claim.
- Diwan-i Imarat = separate public-works department; Mir-i Imarat Malik Ghazi supervised works.
- Karkhanas = royal workshops/stores.
- Public works included canals, gardens, bridges, reservoirs, mosques, madrasas and repairs.
- Ashokan pillar transport shows engineering and sovereign appropriation, not assured decipherment.

## Heredity, army and slavery

- Iqta = revenue/service assignment, not private sovereignty.
- Hereditary iqta and office bought peace but weakened transfer and audit.
- Wajh = revenue/village support assignment to soldiers.
- Hereditary wajh weakened cash review, mobility and military readiness.
- Dagh = horse-branding; chehra = descriptive roll.
- Firuz softened rigorous inspection associated with Alauddin's model.
- Diwan-i Bandagan administered slaves/bandagan.
- Afif's roughly 180,000 total, including approximately 12,000 trained artisans, consists of
  chronicler figures rather than audited census totals.
- Slaves worked in household, workshop, administrative and armed roles.
- The corps became a faction after Firuz.

## Orthodoxy and contradiction

- Firuz stressed taxes represented as authorised by Islamic law.
- Jizya became a distinct levy; sources associate its extension with Brahmans.
- Ulema influence and punishments for heterodoxy sharpened.
- A chronicle report, reproduced by Satish Chandra, records that a Brahman accused of preaching,
  including to Muslims, was publicly burnt; it is a specific punitive case, not proof of a
  uniform empire-wide persecution programme.
- The advanced narrative reports punishment of particular Muslims accused of heterodox
  Sufi-style practice, while other Sufi establishments received support; this is not evidence of
  a general campaign against Sufis.
- Firuz also abolished mutilatory penalties such as cutting hands, feet and noses.
- Temple episodes require case-specific chronology.
- Sanskrit translation, hospitals, education, welfare and ancient-pillar transport coexisted with orthodoxy.
- Neither "complete tolerance" nor "uniform persecution" is sufficient.

## Decline and historical verdict

- Firuz died in 1388 without a durable succession system.
- Princes, nobles, hereditary interests and Firuzi slaves backed rival claimants.
- Fiscal and military fragmentation preceded Timur.
- 1398: Timur sacked Delhi, removed wealth and artisans and accelerated decline.
- Timur did not invade under Muhammad and did not alone cause disintegration.
- Architecture sequence: Ghiyasuddin-Tughlaqabad; Muhammad-Jahanpanah/Adilabad; Firuz-Kotla and
  Hauz Khas additions.
- Muhammad = rational aims plus coercive and weakly verified instruments.
- Firuz = real works and welfare plus elite entrenchment, slavery and orthodoxy.
- Final answer line: the Tughlaqs reveal the difficulty of converting conquest into institutions
  that survive distance, ecological crisis, elite bargaining and succession.
