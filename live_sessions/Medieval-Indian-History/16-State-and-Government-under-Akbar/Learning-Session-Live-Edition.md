## Lesson 1

### Sovereignty and Padshahship: The Ruler as the Moving Centre

Progress: 1/12 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: kingship, authority and the logic of personal government

## Visual first: a personal centre could operate through institutions

```text
                         PADSHAH
                            |
          +-----------------+-----------------+
          |                 |                 |
     appoints rank      hears reports     dispenses justice
          |                 |                 |
     nobles/officers    news writers       petitions/audience
          |                 |                 |
          +----------- imperial decision ----+
                            |
                 departments and provinces
```

The emperor did not stand outside the system like a ceremonial head. Appointment, promotion, transfer, access, punishment and reward made him the point at which different branches of government met.

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Imagine a large organisation whose rules are written, but whose senior appointments, transfers and disputes still depend on one chief executive. The organisation can be sophisticated without becoming impersonal. Akbar's state worked in this way.

**Padshah** meant sovereign ruler or emperor. Akbar's authority rested on conquest and dynasty, but also on the visible performance of rule: public audience, court hierarchy, campaigns, appointments, justice and distribution of favour.

### 2. What the emperor actually controlled

Akbar's centrality operated through concrete powers:

- appointing, promoting and dismissing senior officials;
- granting or transferring mansabs and revenue assignments;
- deciding major military and diplomatic questions;
- receiving departmental reports and petitions;
- arbitrating among competing nobles and offices;
- presenting royal justice as protection against oppression;
- making hierarchy visible through court ritual and access.

This was not arbitrary action without procedure. Records, departmental scrutiny and established custom constrained choices. Yet these constraints did not form a constitution above the ruler.

### 3. Court ritual as government

Daily appearance, audience and ceremonial ranking communicated who could approach the emperor and on what terms. **Jharoka darshan**, the ruler's appearance before assembled subjects, created a daily visual bond. Public and private audiences enabled petitions, presentation of officials and review of business.

```text
visibility of ruler
        ->
access and petition
        ->
recognition of rank
        ->
reward or correction
        ->
renewed personal loyalty
```

The mechanism mattered because distant service-holders did not belong to a permanent modern civil service. Their careers depended on recognised standing at court.

### 4. Legitimacy without importing the whole religious-policy topic

Abu'l Fazl described kingship through ideas such as **farr-i izadi**, divine radiance associated with the just sovereign. The relevant administrative point is limited: imperial ideology made obedience to the ruler appear morally ordered, not merely militarily compelled.

This does not mean all subjects accepted the court's theory. A court historian's formulation proves how monarchy wished to justify itself, not what every village believed.

### 5. Centralisation and its paradox

Akbar reduced the chance that one minister could become another Bairam Khan. At the same time, he needed capable ministers, provincial governors and local intermediaries. Centralisation therefore meant:

```text
not: emperor personally performs every task

but: authority is delegated
     + duties are recorded
     + officers countercheck one another
     + careers remain tied to the emperor
```

The strength of this design was coordination. Its weakness was dependence on sustained imperial attention and a functioning court.

### UPSC application

- Define Akbar's state as ruler-centred, not institution-free.
- Distinguish personal monarchy from random despotism.
- Use court ritual as a mechanism of hierarchy and access.
- Treat Abu'l Fazl's theory as normative political language, not measured popular consent.
- Link strong central authority to delegated and negotiated implementation.

3. Audience and court hierarchy were administrative instruments.
4. Jharoka darshan made sovereign visibility part of governance.
5. Rank and access linked noble careers to the imperial centre.
6. Farr-i izadi expressed a theory of legitimate monarchy.
7. Court ideology does not prove universal social acceptance.
8. Centralisation meant controlled delegation, not direct performance of every task.
9. Administrative checks remained dependent on imperial enforcement.
10. Akbar's state was hierarchical rather than modern egalitarian government.

### Local practice

Which description best captures Akbar's padshah-centred government?

A. The emperor retained appointment, transfer and final review while specialised offices and records organised delegated government.
B. Specialised departments possessed settled spheres whose joint decisions normally limited the emperor's personal intervention.
C. Provincial officers exercised broad discretion while the centre relied mainly on periodic remittances rather than continuous documentary scrutiny.
D. Court audience preserved personal access while routine administration rested on departments operating beyond regular imperial review.

Why should jharoka darshan be studied as more than a private religious act?

A. It chiefly sacralised kingship through daily appearance, while petitionary access remained secondary to ritual display.
B. It combined controlled sovereign visibility with ritual legitimacy, petitionary access and direct political communication.
C. It functioned mainly as a surveillance channel through which officials observed crowds and communicated orders.
D. It allowed controlled public visibility and occasional petitions while keeping routine access centred on departmental channels.

#### Answers and explanations

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** It combines imperial control over careers and final decisions with specialised delegation and documentary government.
- **B — Incorrect:** Departments had differentiated functions, but their collective decisions did not bind or displace the emperor.
- **C — Incorrect:** Provincial discretion existed, yet continuous records and reporting were central to imperial supervision.
- **D — Incorrect:** Audience and departments operated inside regular imperial review rather than beyond it.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Ritual legitimation mattered, but jharoka also created a controlled channel of access and petition.
- **B — Correct:** It joins the ceremony's legitimising function to visibility, access and communication in government.
- **C — Incorrect:** Observation and communication were relevant, but the practice was not principally an official surveillance station.
- **D — Incorrect:** Departmental channels continued, yet the regular appearance itself provided a meaningful route of access.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain how Akbar's government could be both highly personal and institutionally elaborate.

**Model answer:**
Akbar's government was personal because the padshah remained the source of senior appointment, promotion, transfer, honour, punishment and military command. Noble careers depended on imperial recognition, while audience and court ritual made hierarchy visible.

Personal rule did not mean administrative simplicity. Finance, military presentation, household supply, religious grants, justice and provincial government were handled by specialised officers. Records, news reports and overlapping appointments enabled the emperor to compare information and restrain any single subordinate. Provincial diwans and bakhshis, for example, were not merely servants of the governor.

The system nevertheless lacked a modern separation between political authority and administration. Departments did not possess sovereignty against the ruler, and formal checks often depended on his attention. Court ideology, including Abu'l Fazl's language of divinely illuminated kingship, justified hierarchy but cannot be read as proof of universal consent.

Akbar's achievement was therefore a ruler-centred system of controlled delegation. Its sophistication lay in distributing work while keeping office, status and access tied to the imperial centre; its vulnerability lay in dependence on personal supervision and negotiated obedience.

**Scoring note:** Require padshahship, two concrete royal powers, specialised departments, overlapping checks, court ritual and one limitation of personal centralisation.

### Verified PYQ linkage

No direct PYQ tests Akbar's theory of kingship alone. The 2021 hierarchy question becomes intelligible only after distinguishing sovereign control from the territorial chain taught in Lesson 3.

## Visual first: overlapping responsibility created checks

```text
                           EMPEROR
                              |
       +-----------+----------+----------+-----------+
       |           |                     |           |
     WAKIL       DIWAN              MIR BAKHSHI   MIR SAMAN
   prestige/   income, accounts,     ranks, muster, household,
   general     expenditure,          presentation, stores,
   authority   khalisa, jagirs       intelligence   karkhanas
       |
       +--------------------+-----------------------+
                            |
                     SADR + QAZI
              grants, learned offices, law
```

These were not modern ministers with watertight portfolios. Their work intersected, and that intersection allowed the emperor to receive more than one account of the same official or transaction.

### 1. The wakil after Bairam Khan

The **wakil** or **vakil** could exercise broad general authority. Bairam Khan's regency showed the danger of concentrating military, political and patronage power in one intermediary. After taking personal control, Akbar retained the office but reduced its capacity to dominate the whole administration.

The lesson is not that the wakil disappeared. Prestige could survive after operational authority had been distributed.

### 2. The diwan

The **diwan-i ala**, often described as the chief finance officer, supervised:

- income and expenditure;
- revenue accounts and scrutiny;
- khalisa lands reserved for the central treasury;
- jagir-related financial calculation;
- payments, balances and departmental accounts.

The term **wazir** could overlap in usage, and titles evolved. It is safer to explain function and chronology than to force a permanently fixed title chart.

### 3. The mir bakhshi

The **mir bakhshi** connected the nobility, army and court:

- presented candidates and mansabdars;
- supervised or certified military musters;
- checked cavalry-related obligations;
- participated in pay and appointment verification;
- received or coordinated news and intelligence reports.

Calling him merely a "defence minister" hides the personnel and court dimensions of his work.

### 4. Sadr and qazi

The **sadr-us-sudur** supervised religious grants, learned appointments and charitable endowments. Akbar later curtailed and reorganised the office's power because grant distribution could create patronage monopolies.

The **qazi-ul-quzat** stood at the head of the qazi establishment. Qazis administered legal and religious-law functions, but actual justice was plural:

```text
imperial justice
    + qazi courts
    + official adjudication
    + local custom
    + caste/community/village forums
```

This was not one uniform code applied identically across the empire.

### 5. Mir saman and the material state

The **mir saman** or **khan-i-saman** supervised the imperial household, stores and **karkhanas**, meaning royal workshops and establishments. The **diwan-i buyutat** scrutinised the accounts and expenditure of these establishments; the exact division of household functions changed over time.

Karkhanas produced, stored or supplied textiles, arms, tents, furniture, luxury goods and campaign materials. The household was therefore not merely domestic:

```text
court consumption
      +
craft production
      +
stores and transport
      +
campaign supply
      =
material infrastructure of monarchy
```

### 6. What "checks and balances" means here

The phrase should be used cautiously. It means that financial, military and provincial authority was distributed among officers with separate reporting links. It does not mean independent branches capable of invalidating imperial policy.

**Strongest objection:** If the emperor appointed everyone, were the checks real?

**Reply:** They were operational rather than constitutional. Separate records and rival reporting could expose fraud or overreach, but effectiveness varied with access, faction and imperial attention.

### UPSC application

- Match offices to functions without modern-ministry labels.
- Explain why the wakil's decline followed the experience of regency.
- Connect the mir bakhshi to personnel, muster and intelligence.
- Distinguish sadr grants from qazi adjudication.
- Use karkhanas to show the fiscal-logistical basis of court power.

### Revision notes

1. Bairam Khan's dominance encouraged reduction of the wakil's concentrated authority.
2. The diwan supervised finance, revenue and accounts.
3. Wazir and diwan terminology changed; functions matter more than a frozen title.
4. The mir bakhshi linked appointments, mansabs, musters and intelligence.
5. The sadr supervised grants and learned or charitable patronage.
6. The qazi performed judicial and religious-law functions.
7. Justice also operated through customary and local forums.
8. The mir saman supervised household stores and karkhanas.
9. Karkhanas were material and logistical institutions, not merely luxury workshops.
10. Mughal checks were overlapping administrative counterweights, not separation of powers.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Judicial appeals and charitable grants belonged primarily to qazi and sadr functions, not this pairing.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Examine the organisation of Akbar's central administration as a system of specialisation and counterbalance.

**Model answer:**
Akbar inherited Sultanate and Sur offices but reorganised them after Bairam Khan's regency demonstrated the danger of an over-mighty wakil. The wakil retained prestige, while operational authority was distributed.

The diwan supervised revenue, accounts, khalisa, jagir calculations and expenditure. The mir bakhshi presented mansabdars, checked musters and linked military personnel with intelligence. The mir saman managed the imperial household, stores and karkhanas, with accounts subject to scrutiny. The sadr supervised religious and charitable grants, while qazis performed judicial and religious-law functions.

These departments were specialised but not sealed. Rank generated salary claims checked through finance; military obligations required muster; provincial reports reached more than one central channel. Such overlap limited the capacity of one officer to monopolise information.

The arrangement should not be called a modern separation of powers. All senior authority remained derived from the emperor, titles evolved, and faction or access could weaken formal checks. Justice also remained legally plural rather than uniform.

Akbar's achievement was thus to divide administrative labour while retaining sovereign coordination. Counterbalance was practical and documentary, not constitutional.

**Scoring note:** Require the wakil context, functions of at least four offices, one example of overlap, rejection of modern separation and a qualified verdict.

## Visual first: territorial scale and officer networks

```text
EMPIRE
  |
  +-- SUBA
  |     subadar/sipahsalar + provincial diwan
  |     bakhshi + sadr/qazi + news writer
  |
  +-- SARKAR
  |     faujdar + amalguzar/amil + record staff
  |
  +-- PARGANA
  |     shiqdar + amil/amin + qanungo
  |     chaudhuri + fotedar + karkuns
  |
  +-- VILLAGE
        muqaddam/headman + patwari + panchayat
        cultivators, artisans and local right-holders
```

The hierarchy tells us the relative scale of units. It does not mean every officer's practical jurisdiction fitted those boxes perfectly.

### 1. Suba

Akbar divided the empire into **twelve subas in 1580**. Later conquest and reorganisation changed the count; fifteen is commonly associated with the end of the reign after Deccan additions. A date must accompany any number.

The **subadar** or **sipahsalar** headed provincial executive and military authority. The **provincial diwan** handled finance and reported through the central financial chain. A provincial bakhshi, sadr-qazi, **mir adl**, **kotwal**, **mir bahr** and news writer added personnel, judicial, urban-order, river-port and information functions. These officers were appointed from the centre rather than chosen by the governor.

```text
governor commands
       |
provincial diwan separately checks revenue
       |
news writer reports conditions
       |
centre compares accounts and conduct
```

The governor remained powerful, but he did not formally control every provincial information stream.

### 2. Sarkar

The **sarkar** stood below the suba and above the pargana. Important officials included:

- **faujdar**: military-police authority, route security and suppression of disorder;
- **amil** or **amalguzar**: revenue supervision and collection;
- associated clerks, treasurers and record personnel.

A faujdar was often a mobile officer responsible for a trouble-prone zone or route. His jurisdiction need not coincide neatly with every sarkar boundary. The chart is therefore an analytical guide, not a perfect map.

### 3. Pargana

The **pargana** grouped villages for revenue and administration. Its personnel could include:

| Officer | Broad function |
|---|---|
| shiqdar | order and executive support |
| amil/amin | assessment or collection functions, depending on context |
| qanungo | local revenue records and customary information |
| chaudhuri | mediation through locally influential rural groups |
| fotedar | treasury/cash custody |
| karkun | clerical record work |

Titles and exact division of labour varied over time and place. Memorise the functional family, not an imaginary empire-wide job description.

### 4. Village

The village was not simply the lowest government office. It was a community containing differentiated cultivators, artisans, servants and holders of customary rights.

- **muqaddam**: village headman or local leader;
- **patwari**: village-level record keeper;
- **panchayat**: forum for selected local affairs and disputes;
- cultivators: unequal in holding, caste, resources and bargaining power.

The state needed village knowledge to know fields, crops, cultivators and customary dues. That dependence gave local officers room to mediate, conceal or negotiate.

### 5. The direct 2021 PYQ

The official paper asks for ascending order by size. The institutional logic is:

```text
village -> pargana -> sarkar -> suba
```

However, no official answer key is stored locally. The sequence is taught as historical content; the paper's answer is not labelled as officially keyed.

### 6. Formal order versus lived government

**Objection:** Does the neat hierarchy prove complete central control?

**Reply:** No. It proves an imperial attempt to order territory and reporting. Zamindars, chiefs, customary claims, terrain and the availability of reliable records shaped actual authority. A suba could contain zones of dense assessment and zones of negotiated or thin control.

### UPSC application

- Remember ascending size: village, pargana, sarkar, suba.
- Date the twelve-suba figure to 1580.
- Explain provincial checks through the subadar-diwan relationship.
- Do not force the faujdar into a perfectly fixed modern district boundary.
- Use local record dependence to qualify centralisation.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain why Akbar's provincial organisation combined hierarchy with administrative counterweights and local dependence.

**Model answer:**
Akbar's territorial structure placed the suba above the sarkar, pargana and village. Twelve subas were organised in 1580, though later conquest changed the count.

At provincial level, the subadar exercised executive and military authority, while the provincial diwan controlled fiscal scrutiny through a separate central chain. Bakhshis, sadr-qazis and news writers added personnel, judicial and information channels. Below them, faujdars maintained order and routes, while amils or amalguzars handled revenue.

At pargana and village levels, the state depended on qanungos, chaudhuris, muqaddams and patwaris for records, custom, crop knowledge and collection. This made hierarchy operational, but it also gave local intermediaries bargaining power.

The structure should not be mistaken for a uniform modern district system. Faujdar jurisdictions could be mobile, office functions varied, and zamindars or chiefs could mediate authority. The administrative chart therefore expressed imperial ordering rather than complete penetration.

Akbar's provincial government was strongest where separate reporting channels and reliable local records worked together. Its centralising design remained dependent on negotiated local knowledge.

**Scoring note:** Require the complete territorial chain, subadar-diwan counterweight, named sarkar/pargana/village officers and a formal-chart-versus-practice qualification.

### Mansabdari: Ranking a Diverse Imperial Service

## Visual first: one rank connected several relationships

```text
                         MANSAB
                      numerical rank
                           |
          +----------------+----------------+
          |                |                |
       STATUS           SALARY           SERVICE
   court precedence   cash/jagir claim   civil + military
          |                |                |
          +---------- emperor's control ---+
```

A mansab was not a plot of land and not merely an army rank. It located a service-holder within an imperial hierarchy and connected status, remuneration and obligation.

### 1. What mansab means

**Mansab** means rank or position. A **mansabdar** was a holder of such rank. Numerical grades are generally associated with reforms from the later 1560s, but procedures evolved throughout the reign.

The hierarchy ranged broadly from low numerical ranks to very high commands. Canonical accounts commonly describe grades from 10 to 5000, with select nobles such as Man Singh and Aziz Koka reaching 7000 under Akbar. These numbers require contextual reading; they did not always equal the exact number of troops physically present.

### 2. A single imperial service

Mansabdars could receive:

- battlefield and garrison command;
- provincial governorship;
- revenue or administrative office;
- court responsibility;
- diplomatic or special assignment.

The system is sometimes called a single civil-military service because the same ranked elite could move among these tasks. "Single" does not mean every clerk, artisan or village officer held a mansab.

### 3. Recruitment and diversity

Akbar's service included Turanis, Iranis, Afghans, Indian Muslims, Rajputs and other groups. Incorporation widened the ruling coalition and reduced dependence on one ethnic faction.

Yet this was not open competitive recruitment:

```text
ability and military resources
          +
lineage and recommendation
          +
court patronage and political need
          =
entry and advancement
```

Merit mattered, but within a hierarchical patronage order.

### 4. Promotion, transfer and discipline

Because rank came from the emperor, promotion rewarded service and transfer disrupted local entrenchment. Demotion, confiscation or reassignment could punish failure.

This design had two effects:

| Intended effect | Possible cost |
|---|---|
| tie careers to emperor | intensify competition for favour |
| prevent hereditary provincial power | encourage short-term extraction |
| move talent across regions | weaken local continuity |
| widen elite coalition | preserve high social inequality |

### 5. Rank was not actual capacity

A paper rank could diverge from:

- troops actually maintained;
- quality of horses;
- revenue actually realised from a jagir;
- deductions and category rules;
- temporary exemptions or administrative change.

This gap explains why muster and branding became important. The next lesson turns from rank as status to rank as verified obligation.

### From Rank to Verified Service: Zat, Sawar, Dagh and Chehra

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: how a paper rank became a service claim

```text
MANSAB GRANTED
      |
      +-- ZAT: personal standing and pay, broadly
      |
      +-- SAWAR: cavalry obligation, broadly
                    |
                 muster
                    |
          +---------+---------+
          |                   |
       CHEHRA               DAGH
  descriptive roll      branding of horses
          |                   |
          +------ verification+
```

The state did not solve military organisation by writing a number beside a noble's name. It needed to test whether men and horses corresponding to the obligation actually existed.

### 1. Zat and sawar

The dual distinction is associated with Akbar's **fortieth regnal year, 1595-96**.

- **zat** broadly indicated the mansabdar's personal rank, standing and salary;
- **sawar** broadly indicated the cavalry obligation attached to the rank.

The word "broadly" matters. Scholars have debated detailed ratios, rates and changes. A late-Akbar formula should not be projected unchanged onto the beginning of the reign.

### 2. Why the distinction was needed

Earlier numerical rank could leave ambiguity:

```text
high personal status
        ?=
same number of cavalry actually maintained
```

Separating personal standing from mounted obligation gave the court a clearer way to reward a noble without treating every increase in dignity as an identical increase in cavalry.

### 3. Muster

A **muster** was an inspection of the contingent. It asked whether the service-holder presented the expected men, horses and equipment.

Potential problems included:

- substituting inferior horses;
- presenting borrowed animals;
- inflating troop numbers on paper;
- claiming salary against incomplete service;
- corruption or harassment by inspecting officials.

Verification therefore protected the treasury, but it could itself become a site of bargaining.

### 4. Dagh and chehra

**Dagh** was the branding of horses for identification. Under Akbar it was strengthened around **1573-74**, the eighteenth regnal year, amid resistance and administrative difficulty.

**Chehra** was the descriptive roll of troopers or personnel. Together:

| Control | Problem addressed | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| dagh | substitution or repeated presentation of horses | branding did not prove battlefield quality forever |
| chehra | fictitious or substituted personnel | records could become outdated or manipulated |
| muster | paper obligation versus presented contingent | inspection quality depended on officials |

These controls had Sultanate and Sur precedents. Akbar systematised them within the mansab order rather than inventing verification from nothing.

### 5. Numbers and caution

Canonical accounts commonly classify ordinary ranks from 10 to 5000, with exceptional elevations to 7000. They also describe equipment expectations for high mansabdars. Such figures belong to evolving regulations, not a single timeless table perfectly enforced in every year.

Do not infer:

- that a 5000 zat always meant 5000 horsemen;
- that sawar obligations were unchanged;
- that every recorded contingent was present;
- that the empire maintained one centrally salaried standing army.

### 6. The later boundary

**Du-aspa sih-aspa**, which allowed selected cavalry obligations to be counted at enhanced rates, is associated with Jahangir's reign. It is useful only as a boundary showing later development, not as Akbar's normal rule.

## Visual first: the assignment was a claim on revenue

```text
LAND AND CULTIVATION
        |
 assessed imperial demand = JAMA
        |
        +-- KHALISA -> reserved for central treasury
        |
        +-- JAGIR -> assigned toward an officer's salary
                         |
                  actual collection = HASIL
                         |
             jama may be greater than hasil
```

A jagirdar did not receive the land, its cultivators or unlimited ownership. The assignment directed a portion of state revenue demand toward remuneration.

### 1. Jagir

A **jagir** was a transferable assignment of revenue due to the state. A **tankhwah jagir** was assigned in lieu of salary. The jagirdar sought collection through officials and local intermediaries while remaining subject to imperial rules and transfer.

The key distinction is:

```text
revenue claim != proprietary ownership of soil
```

This does not mean jagirdars lacked coercive power. Control over collection could affect peasants profoundly even without ownership.

### 2. Khalisa

**Khalisa** land revenue was reserved for the imperial treasury and central expenditure rather than assigned as a jagir. The distinction concerned destination and management of revenue, not two different kinds of agricultural soil.

Other grants, including **madad-i maash** or revenue-free support, belonged to separate charitable or learned patronage arrangements.

### 3. Jama and hasil

- **jama**: assessed or expected revenue entered in accounts;
- **hasil**: revenue actually realised.

Example:

```text
paper assessment: 100 units = jama
collection after crop loss and resistance: 72 units = hasil
salary expectation still tied to the higher paper figure
```

The example shows why a high mansab and apparently valuable jagir did not guarantee equivalent usable income. It does not imply every shortfall was exactly 28 per cent.

### 4. Why transfer mattered

Transfer prevented a service-holder from turning an assignment easily into a hereditary territorial base. It also created a structural risk:

| Imperial objective | Jagirdar incentive |
|---|---|
| prevent local entrenchment | recover income before transfer |
| preserve assessed revenue | press for collection near jama |
| rotate service | rely on unfamiliar local agents |
| discipline nobles | seek favour or conceal shortfall |

The state could regulate and inspect, but it could not remove the tension between transferable service and long-term agrarian care.

### 5. Jagirdar, zamindar and peasant

```text
imperial demand
       |
 jagirdar or khalisa official
       |
 negotiation/coercion through local intermediaries
       |
 zamindar + chaudhuri + muqaddam + qanungo
       |
 differentiated cultivators
```

The jagirdar held an assignment; the zamindar possessed varied local, hereditary or customary claims and influence; the peasant cultivated under unequal conditions. These were not interchangeable labels.

### 6. Later jagirdari crisis: a bounded link

The gap between paper revenue and realisation, competition for assignments and short-term extraction became more severe in later Mughal history. Those later developments help identify a structural tension, but a fully formed seventeenth-century jagirdari crisis must not be projected backward onto Akbar.

## Lesson 7

### Building a Revenue Demand: Measurement, Classification and Written Knowledge

Progress: 7/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: revenue reform stages, rai, jarib, Ilahi gaz, land classes and contracts

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - Satish Chandra Part II PDF pp. 131-139 and *History of Medieval India* PDF pp. 244-247
CA search: "not run - cadastral digitisation would be only a limited modern analogy"
CA found: none required
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: assessment required a chain of information

```text
field boundary
     |
measurement by jarib using standard gaz
     |
crop and cultivation category recorded
     |
local yield and price information assembled
     |
rate schedule prepared
     |
demand stated to cultivator
     |
collection, remission, arrears or dispute
```

Revenue reform was an information problem before it was a taxation formula. The state needed to know how much land was cultivated, what it produced, what prices meant locally and who could pay.

### 1. Reform developed in stages

Akbar did not announce a complete system at accession. The sequence included:

1. early provincial experiments after restoration;
2. attempts to use central crop-price schedules;
3. problems caused by delay and differences between court and countryside prices;
4. karori and measurement experiments in the 1570s;
5. work by Muzaffar Khan Turbati, Todar Mal, Shah Mansur and other officials;
6. developed zabt-dahsala schedules around 1580;
7. continuing correction and regional alternatives.

The return from Gujarat in 1573 is associated with renewed personal attention to revenue administration.

### 2. Measurement

The **Ilahi gaz** was a standard yard. The **bigha** was an area unit built from the gaz. A bamboo measuring rope or **jarib**, joined with iron rings, was intended to reduce the stretching and shrinking possible with ordinary rope.

Measurement did not remove judgement:

- boundaries could be disputed;
- soil and irrigation differed;
- staff could make errors;
- local records remained necessary;
- cultivation changed between seasons.

### 3. Cultivation categories

| Category | Plain meaning | Assessment relevance |
|---|---|---|
| polaj | regularly cultivated | normal continuous cultivation |
| parauti/parati | temporarily fallow | expected return to cultivation |
| chachar | fallow for several years | gradual restoration could affect demand |
| banjar | long uncultivated | not equivalent to productive polaj |

These categories described cultivation history, not four fixed castes of cultivators and not universally identical soil quality.

### 4. Rai, patta and qabuliyat

**Rai** referred to crop-rate schedules or assessments used in the revenue tradition. **Patta** described the stated assessment or terms communicated to a cultivator, while **qabuliyat** represented acceptance of the obligation.

These terms are associated strongly with Sher Shah's programme and remained part of the institutional inheritance developed under the Mughals. They do not prove a modern direct contract between an equal state and an isolated peasant. Zamindars and local officers remained involved.

### 5. Todar Mal attribution

Todar Mal was a major organiser and skilled revenue administrator. But the system also depended on Muzaffar Khan Turbati, Shah Mansur, qanungos, amils, survey staff and accumulated Sultanate-Sur practice.

> Safe formulation: "Todar Mal was central to a team-based and evolving reform," not "Todar Mal invented every element alone."

### 6. Extraction and protection

Predictable measurement could limit arbitrary over-assessment and tell cultivators the demand. Yet measurement also expanded the state's capacity to identify cultivation and collect revenue.

```text
better records
   + reduce some arbitrary bargaining
   + improve predictability
   - increase extractive reach
   - expose cultivators to official error and coercion
```

The reform was neither pure welfare nor pure oppression. It was fiscal state-building with contested effects.

### UPSC application

- Present revenue reform as an evolving information system.
- Define gaz, bigha and jarib without inventing uniform modern equivalents.
- Distinguish cultivation history from soil quality.
- Qualify patta-qabuliyat against a ryotwari analogy.
- Attribute leadership to Todar Mal without erasing the wider team and precedents.

1. Revenue reform developed through experiments rather than one decree.
2. Central price schedules could misfit local prices.
3. Karori and measurement experiments preceded mature dahsala.
4. The Ilahi gaz standardised linear measurement.
5. The bigha expressed measured area.
6. Iron-linked bamboo jarib aimed to reduce manipulation.
7. Polaj was regularly cultivated land.
8. Parauti was temporarily fallow.

### Zabt and Dahsala: One Revenue Logic, Several Working Methods

Progress: 8/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: ten-year averages, dastur, one-third norm and regional alternatives

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - Satish Chandra Part II PDF pp. 133-140 and *History of Medieval India* PDF pp. 244-247
CA search: "not run - current tax systems would be a misleading direct comparison"
CA found: none required
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: do not collapse the vocabulary

```text
ZABT = measurement-based assessment framework
  |
  +-- DAHSALA = ten-year data used to frame annual cash rates
  |
  +-- DASTUR = regional crop-rate schedule/circle

ALTERNATIVES WHERE ZABT WAS UNSUITABLE
  |
  +-- BATAI = division of actual crop
  +-- KANKUT = measurement plus appraisal
  +-- NASAQ = estimate using previous/local assessment
```

The terms answer different questions. Zabt tells us the assessment framework; dahsala tells us how averages supported rates; dastur localised those rates; alternatives show the limits of measurement-based uniformity.

### 1. Dahsala

Around **1580**, ten-year information on produce and prices was used to construct annual cash schedules. This is **dahsala**.

It does **not** mean:

- tax was collected once in ten years;
- the settlement remained fixed for a permanent ten-year term;
- every district used the same price;
- every cultivator paid exactly one-third in practice.

### 2. The one-third norm

The state demand is commonly described as about one-third of average produce, converted into cash through scheduled prices. Actual burden varied because of:

- crop and productivity;
- local price conversion;
- cesses and collection costs;
- remission or arrears;
- intermediary claims;
- the difference between assessed and realised revenue.

The norm is historically important, but it is not a universal receipt from every field.

### 3. Dastur

A **dastur** was a regional schedule or circle of crop rates. Its purpose was to avoid using one court price for unlike localities.

Example:

```text
same crop
  |
district A average price != district B average price
  |
separate dastur circles reduce the distortion
```

The example establishes regional pricing logic. It does not prove perfect market data.

### 4. Alternative methods

| Method | Working principle | Where useful |
|---|---|---|
| batai/ghalla-bakhshi | actual crop divided in an agreed share | where produce-sharing was workable |
| kankut | measured area combined with inspection and estimate | where standing crop could be appraised |
| nasaq | demand estimated substantially from prior/local assessment | where fresh full measurement was impractical |
| zabt | measured land tied to crop-rate schedules | settled and comparatively recordable zones |

Batai itself had variants according to when and how the crop was divided.

### 5. Geography of implementation

Zabt was strongest in relatively settled and monetised regions commonly described from Lahore to Allahabad and in Malwa and Gujarat. It was less suitable in riverine Bengal, forest and hill zones, uncertain-crop areas and shifting frontiers.

Recent scholarship, reflected in the canonical source, cautions that zabt probably did not cover every field even within a nominal zabt province.

### 6. Cash demand and cultivator risk

Cash schedules helped treasury, jagir and army accounting. But a cultivator producing grain had to confront market prices:

```text
official cash demand
      |
crop must be sold or cash found
      |
market price may differ from schedule
      |
risk shifts toward cultivator
```

Normative remission and loans during flood, drought or crop failure reveal an official ideal. Arrears, flight and famine show that relief was not automatic or uniform.

- Keep zabt, dahsala and dastur distinct.
- Name and define batai, kankut and nasaq.
- Qualify the one-third norm and geographic reach.
- Connect cash assessment to both administrative predictability and market risk.

The state could issue a demand from above, but actual collection required people who knew fields, families, boundaries, crops and local power.

### 1. What "zamindar" could mean

**Zamindar** was not one uniform class. The word could include:

- village headmen with hereditary shares;
- lineage or clan chiefs;
- local landed right-holders;
- rulers with forts and armed followers;
- intermediaries claiming customary dues;
- persons incorporated into imperial service.

Some possessed wide territories and military strength; others operated close to village scale. Translating all of them as modern landlords erases this range.

### 2. Why the state needed zamindars

Zamindars could provide:

- local crop and boundary knowledge;
- collection and mediation;
- armed support and route security;
- settlement of cultivation;
- access to caste, clan and village networks.

They could also resist assessment, conceal resources or lead rebellion. The imperial strategy therefore alternated among negotiation, confirmation of rights, service incorporation and force.

### 3. Zamindar versus jagirdar

| Question | Jagirdar | Zamindar |
|---|---|---|
| basis of claim | imperial revenue assignment | varied local hereditary/customary rights and power |

| military role | contingent/service obligation may follow mansab | armed followers and forts varied |

A person could occupy more than one political role over time, but the concepts remain distinct.

### 4. Peasants were not one undifferentiated mass

Cultivators differed by:

- size and security of holding;
- resident or migrant status;
- caste and community position;
- access to irrigation, cattle, seed and credit;
- crop choice and market access;
- labour obligations and debt.

A one-third normative state demand did not equal the total burden. Intermediary dues, cesses, transport, credit and price conversion could add pressure.

### 5. Village institutions and evidence limits

Muqaddams, patwaris and panchayats handled selected records, collection, custom and disputes. But "village community" must not be romanticised as equal or autonomous. Powerful families could dominate local institutions, and surviving normative descriptions do not reveal every cultivator's experience.

### 6. Cultivation expansion and coercion

The state wanted more cultivated land because cultivation increased revenue. Loans, remission and protection could support recovery after failure. Coercion could also enforce demand.

```text

      |
can encourage settlement and relief
      |
can also intensify measurement and collection
```

The same fiscal objective could therefore produce assistance in one context and pressure in another.

### The Operational State: Army, Forts, Information, Justice and Mints

## Visual first: government required movement and verification

```text
REVENUE -> pay and supply
    |
    +-> cavalry, infantry, artillery, elephants
    |       |

    |
    +-> roads, relays, messengers, news reports
    |       |
    |     imperial response
    |
    +-> mints, stores and workshops
            |
        material credibility of rule
```

An office chart could not govern without horses, grain, records, roads, metal, craftsmen, forts and information.

### 1. A composite military establishment

The Mughal military included:

- contingents maintained by mansabdars;
- **ahadis**, cavalrymen enrolled more directly in imperial service;
- artillery and matchlock specialists;
- varied infantry, guards and labour;
- elephants for transport, battle and ceremony;
- fort garrisons;
- river and coastal craft where geography required them.

This was neither a purely feudal levy nor one uniform central standing army.

### 2. Artillery and logistics

Heavy guns supported sieges; lighter pieces could be carried by people, elephants or camels. Technology mattered only when linked to:

- trained personnel;
- powder and ammunition;
- transport animals and carts;
- roads and seasonal timing;
- grain and fodder;
- command and intelligence.

The state that could move and resupply weapons possessed an advantage over the state that merely owned them.

### 3. Forts

Forts served as:

- military strongpoints;
- stores and magazines;
- route and river controls;
- symbols of dynastic authority;
- refuges for resistance;
- anchors of garrisoned administration.

Holding a fort did not automatically create deep control over all surrounding countryside. It created strategic reach that still required revenue and local cooperation.

### 4. Information and post

**Waqia-navis** and other news writers reported events. Messengers and relay arrangements connected court, army and provinces.

Reports could:

- compare an official's claim with another account;
- warn of rebellion or shortage;
- transmit orders and appointments;
- support rapid concentration of force.

They remained vulnerable to delay, local patronage and selective reporting. This was an imperial communication network, not a universal public postal service.

### 5. Justice and police

Faujdars suppressed disorder and protected important routes. Qazis, mir adls, imperial officers and customary forums all participated in adjudication. The **kotwal** supervised important aspects of urban order and policing, while faujdars had wider military-police and route-security responsibilities. Neither office should be equated with a uniform modern police service.

Royal rhetoric presented the emperor as protector against oppression. Yet equality before an impersonal law cannot be assumed. Rank, access, local custom and enforcement shaped outcomes.

### 6. Mints and currency

Imperial mints issued gold, silver and copper coinage with controlled weight, legends, dates and mint names. Standardisation built on Sultanate and Sur precedents and assisted taxation, salary, trade and sovereignty.

Coins prove an issuing authority's claim and technical standards. They do not prove that every transaction across the empire was monetised or that all coins circulated uniformly.

The **Ilahi era/calendar**, introduced in the 1580s and back-reckoned to accession, assisted fiscal and court ordering. It must not be confused with Tauhid-i Ilahi religious terminology.

- Reject technological determinism by adding logistics and finance.
- Separate strategic fort possession from deep countryside administration.
- Treat news networks as controlled but imperfect information.
- Distinguish standard coinage from universal monetisation.
- Keep Ilahi calendar separate from religious terminology.

Artillery supplied siege and battlefield power only when supported by trained crews, powder, carts, animals, roads, fodder and command. Forts secured stores, routes and strategic prestige, though possession of a fort did not guarantee equal control over its countryside.

Waqia-navis, messengers and relay networks carried reports and orders between provinces, court and army. Multiple channels helped compare claims and respond to rebellion, but reports remained vulnerable to delay and patronage.

Faujdars maintained route order and coercive capacity, while qazis, imperial officers and customary forums produced legal pluralism rather than one uniform judicial code. Imperial mints strengthened currency standards and projected sovereignty, but did not create universal monetisation.

Karkhanas and household stores supplied court and campaigns. Akbar's operational state was therefore a fiscal-logistical-information system. Its capacity was considerable, yet always conditioned by distance, supplies, local access and the reliability of agents.

**Scoring note:** Require army composition, artillery-logistics link, forts, news network, justice or police, mints/workshops and at least two implementation limits.

### Integration and Negotiated Sovereignty: How a Centralising State Used Difference

## Visual first: central authority did not require identical local arrangements

```text

                          |

          |               |               |
     direct province   ranked noble    allied ruler/
     and khalisa       with jagir      watan authority
          |               |               |
      dense records    transferable    hereditary local
      and officers     service         base under empire
          +---------------+---------------+

             different depths of control
```

Akbar's state could be centralising in appointment and sovereignty while accepting different local arrangements. Uniform obedience was the claim; uniform administration was not the reality.

### 1. Administrative integration of nobles

Mansab, promotion, jagir, court honour and transfer turned diverse elites into competitors within one imperial hierarchy. A Rajput ruler, Turani commander or Indian Muslim officer could receive service outside a homeland and depend on imperial favour.

This broadened the ruling group, but did not erase hierarchy. High office remained concentrated among elites with military resources, lineage, patronage and court access.

### 2. Rajputs and watan

For incorporated Rajput houses, **watan jagir** referred to the ancestral or home territory retained within an imperial service relationship. Outside assignments and commands connected the ruler's household to the wider empire.

```text
ancestral base retained
        +
imperial rank and honours
        +
service outside homeland
        =
shared interest without social equality
```

Not every Rajput house entered by marriage, and Mewar's resistance proves that inclusion and coercion operated together.

### 3. Zamindars and graded sovereignty

Local chiefs might pay revenue, supply troops, keep forts, retain customary authority or enter mansabdari. Their relationship with the centre ranged from close incorporation to periodic submission.

This creates a crucial distinction:

- **imperial claim**: the ruler asserts supremacy;
- **effective administration**: officers, records and coercive capacity operate regularly;
- **negotiated authority**: local power performs obligations while retaining substantial autonomy.

### 4. Religion and legitimacy: bounded treatment

Removal of some discriminatory taxes, wider elite recruitment and curtailment of the sadr's uncontrolled patronage affected state legitimacy and service. Full treatment of the Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, sulh-i-kul and Tauhid-i Ilahi belongs to the religious-policy topic.

Here the relevant point is administrative: reducing one clerical patronage centre and recruiting across communities widened the ruler's coalition.

### 5. Centralisation versus negotiated sovereignty

**Centralising evidence**

- imperial appointment and transfer;
- common rank language;
- departmental accounts;
- provincial reporting;
- revenue schedules;
- mint and court symbols.

**Negotiated evidence**

- watan authority;
- zamindar rights;
- regional revenue methods;
- local customary forums;
- uneven frontier implementation;
- dependence on noble and chiefly cooperation.

The two are not mutually exclusive. The empire centralised the terms of service while negotiating how authority worked locally.

### 6. Costs and exclusions

The composite ruling class remained narrow compared with the population. Peasants, artisans and subordinated communities did not become equal political partners. Elite inclusion could strengthen extraction as well as moderation.

- Define watan jagir without treating it as sovereign independence.
- Separate imperial claim, effective administration and negotiated obligation.
- Keep religious policy bounded to legitimacy and recruitment.
- Reject both total centralisation and loose-confederacy extremes.

## Visual first: innovation was recombination at scale

```text
SULTANATE PRECEDENTS
iqta, khalisa, dagh, chehra, offices, measurement
                  \
                   +--> AKBAR'S LONG REIGN
                  /     experiment + records + expansion
SUR PRECEDENTS   /              |
sarkar, pargana, rai,            v
patta, roads, coinage       wider systematisation
                                  |
                         uneven regional practice
                                  |
                       later Mughal modification
```

The choice is not between "Akbar invented everything" and "Akbar copied Sher Shah." Administrative history is about inheritance, experiment, scale and adaptation.

### 1. What was inherited

From Sultanate and earlier Indo-Islamic government came traditions of:

- central departments and fiscal accounting;
- iqta or revenue assignment;
- khalisa;
- military branding and descriptive rolls;
- local officers and revenue records;
- royal justice and intelligence networks.

Sher Shah's regime strengthened:

- sarkar-pargana coordination;
- measurement and crop-rate traditions;
- patta-qabuliyat ideals;
- road, sarai, post and intelligence systems;
- currency standardisation;
- direct royal concern with order and revenue.

### 2. What Akbar systematised

Akbar's long reign and larger durable empire allowed:

- reduced concentration in the wakil;
- clearer departmental counterweights;
- suba organisation and provincial replication;
- graded mansabdari;
- renewed dagh and muster controls;
- late zat-sawar differentiation;
- broader elite incorporation;
- expanded records and regional schedules;
- developed zabt-dahsala alongside alternatives.

Systematisation means connecting components at larger scale, not inventing every component.

### 3. The *Ain-i Akbari*

Abu'l Fazl's *Ain-i Akbari* is indispensable for offices, ranks, revenue categories, prices, geography and the normative imperial order.

It must be read critically:

| What it offers | Why caution is needed |
|---|---|
| detailed classifications | categories may present an ideal order |
| numerical schedules | dates and compilation layers may differ |
| administrative geography | not every figure was simultaneously current |
| imperial rationale | court patronage shapes explanation |
| prices and resources | jama is not the same as hasil |

The *Ain* is better understood as a structured project of imperial knowledge than a modern synchronised census.

### 4. Other evidence

- *Akbarnama*: chronology and imperial causation, but teleological and celebratory;
- Badauni: valuable dissent, but morally and sectarianly hostile;
- Nizamuddin Ahmad: useful chronology with compilation limits;
- farmans and manuals: orders and transactions, but survival is selective and order is not implementation;
- coins and inscriptions: dates, mints and sovereignty claims, not full social practice;
- regional evidence: local variation, but archives are uneven;
- later Mughal texts: development over time, not proof of unchanged Akbar-period rules.

### 5. Structural limits

Akbar-period tensions included:

- jama-hasil gaps;
- short-horizon jagir incentives;
- paper cavalry and imperfect muster;
- manipulation of records;
- unequal cultivator burdens;
- regional revenue-method variation;
- local chiefly resistance;
- dependence on imperial attention;
- elite inclusion without mass political equality.

These were real limits. They should not be inflated into the mature later jagirdari crisis.

### 6. Final verdict

Akbar's government increased the state's capacity to classify, compare, mobilise and redistribute. It tied a diverse elite to the throne and connected revenue with military service.

It remained:

- personal at the apex;
- mediated in the countryside;
- plural in law and custom;
- unequal in social effect;
- uneven across ecology and frontier;
- dependent on records that could diverge from realisation.

- Use dates to show institutional evolution.
- Distinguish normative prescription from practice.
- Triangulate the *Ain* with documents, outcomes and alternate narratives.
- End with stronger capacity but uneven, negotiated implementation.

# REMEDIATION

## Misconception 1: Padshahship meant administration without rules

**Correction:** The emperor remained sovereign, but departments, records, schedules, precedents and reporting structured delegated action. Personal monarchy and institutional complexity coexisted.

## Misconception 2: Checks and balances meant separation of powers

**Correction:** The diwan, mir bakhshi, governor and news writers supplied overlapping scrutiny. None formed an independent constitutional branch above imperial authority.

## Misconception 3: Twelve subas describes the whole reign

**Correction:** Twelve applies to the 1580 organisation. Later conquest altered the count, commonly producing fifteen by the end of the reign.

## Misconception 4: Mansab was a land grant

**Correction:** Mansab was rank. Jagir was a revenue assignment. Zamindari described varied local claims and power.

## Misconception 5: Zat always equalled actual cavalry

**Correction:** Zat broadly expressed personal rank and pay; sawar broadly expressed cavalry obligation after the late-Akbar distinction. Muster tested actual presentation.

## Misconception 6: Dagh and chehra were invented without precedent

**Correction:** Branding and descriptive controls had Sultanate and Sur precedents. Akbar renewed and connected them to mansabdari.

## Misconception 7: Jagirdars owned assigned land

**Correction:** They held a transferable claim on state revenue demand. Collection power could be coercive without becoming proprietary ownership.

## Misconception 8: Jama and hasil were interchangeable

**Correction:** Jama was assessed or expected revenue; hasil was actual realisation. Their gap mattered for salary, service and agrarian pressure.

## Misconception 9: Dahsala was a ten-year settlement

**Correction:** Ten-year data helped calculate annual rates. It was neither a once-in-ten-years payment nor a permanent settlement.

## Misconception 10: Zabt covered every Mughal field

**Correction:** Crop-sharing, appraisal and prior-estimate methods remained necessary. Zabt itself could be incomplete within a nominal zabt province.

## Misconception 11: Todar Mal invented the revenue system alone

**Correction:** He was a major organiser within a team and a longer Sultanate-Sur-local institutional history.

## Misconception 12: Zamindar meant modern landlord

**Correction:** The category ranged from village intermediaries to armed chiefs and rulers with varied customary, hereditary and fiscal claims.

## Misconception 13: Village panchayats were egalitarian republics

**Correction:** Local institutions operated within caste, wealth, gender and holding inequalities and under wider fiscal authority.

## Misconception 14: Standard coinage proves a cash economy everywhere

**Correction:** Coins reveal mint standards and sovereignty; circulation and monetisation remained uneven.

## Misconception 15: Akbar either invented everything or copied Sher Shah

**Correction:** His contribution lay in experimentation, recombination, wider coordination and systematisation of inherited practices.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## Chronology

- 1556: Akbar accedes; Bairam Khan exercises powerful regental authority.
- 1565: Muzaffar Khan Turbati becomes diwan.
- 1567: numerical mansab hierarchy is generally associated with the eleventh regnal year.
- 1573-74: dagh system strengthened in the eighteenth regnal year.
- 1573: return from Gujarat followed by renewed attention to revenue administration.
- 1575: Todar Mal becomes mushrif-i-diwan; team-based reform continues.
- 1580: dahsala implemented and empire organised into twelve subas.
- 1581: Shah Mansur is executed in the context of the Mirza Hakim crisis.
- 1595-96: zat-sawar distinction introduced in the fortieth regnal year.
- 1605: Akbar dies; institutions continue with later modification.

## Sovereignty

- Padshahship centred appointments, transfers, command, audience and justice on the ruler.
- Personal monarchy could operate through departments and records.
- Jharoka darshan and audience made sovereignty visible.
- Farr-i izadi was court language of legitimate kingship, not proof of universal consent.
- Checks were operational and documentary, not constitutional separation.

## Central administration

- Wakil: broad authority and prestige; concentrated power reduced after Bairam.
- Diwan: income, expenditure, revenue, accounts, khalisa and jagir calculation.
- Mir bakhshi: mansab presentation, appointments, musters, pay verification and news links.
- Sadr-us-sudur: grants, learned appointments and charitable patronage.
- Qazi-ul-quzat/qazi: judicial and religious-law functions within legal pluralism.
- Mir saman/khan-i-saman: household, stores and karkhanas.
- Household accounts provided scrutiny of material expenditure.

## Province to village

- Twelve subas were organised in 1580; later expansion changed the count.
- Subadar or sipahsalar led provincial executive-military authority.
- Provincial diwan formed a separate fiscal reporting line.
- Bakhshi, sadr-qazi and news writer added personnel, legal and information channels.
- Mir adl, kotwal and mir bahr supported justice, urban order and river-port administration in the provincial establishment.
- Sarkar stood below suba; pargana stood below sarkar.
- Faujdar exercised mobile military-police and route-order authority.
- Amil or amalguzar handled revenue functions.
- Qanungo preserved local revenue and customary information.
- Chaudhuri mediated through influential rural groups.
- Fotedar handled treasury functions; karkuns performed clerical work.
- Muqaddam and patwari connected village authority with records and collection.
- Panchayat activity did not erase local inequality.

## Mansabdari

- Mansab was numerical rank connecting status, salary and service.
- Mansabdars could perform civil, military, provincial and court work.
- Numerical hierarchy developed during the reign.
- Recruitment included Turanis, Iranis, Afghans, Indian Muslims, Rajputs and others.
- Merit operated with lineage, resources, recommendation and patronage.
- Promotion and transfer tied careers to the emperor.
- Mansab did not equal hereditary land ownership.
- Not every state employee belonged to mansabdari.

## Zat, sawar and verification

- Zat broadly denoted personal rank, status and pay.
- Sawar broadly denoted cavalry obligation.
- The dual distinction belongs to 1595-96.
- Earlier ranks must not be read automatically through the later formula.
- Muster compared paper obligation with presented contingents.
- Dagh branded horses; chehra recorded descriptive details.
- Dagh was strengthened in 1573-74.
- Verification addressed substitution and fictitious troops but could be manipulated.
- Du-aspa sih-aspa belongs to the Jahangir-period boundary.

## Jagir and khalisa

- Jagir was transferable assignment of state revenue demand.
- Tankhwah jagir remunerated service.
- Jagir did not confer ownership of soil or peasants.
- Khalisa revenue was reserved for central expenditure.
- Madad-i maash and inam were separate grant categories.
- Jama was assessed or expected revenue.
- Hasil was actual realisation.
- A jama-hasil gap affected salary and service capacity.
- Transfer limited entrenchment but encouraged short-term extraction.
- Later jagirdari crisis should not be projected backward fully formed.

## Revenue measurement

- Reform developed through experiments and correction.
- Muzaffar Khan Turbati, Todar Mal, Shah Mansur and local staff contributed.
- Ilahi gaz standardised linear measurement.
- Bigha expressed measured area.
- Iron-linked bamboo jarib aimed to reduce stretching.
- Polaj: regular cultivation.
- Parauti/parati: temporary fallow.
- Chachar: several years fallow.
- Banjar: long uncultivated.
- Rai referred to crop-rate or assessment schedules.
- Patta-qabuliyat made demand and acceptance visible but did not create modern ryotwari.

## Revenue methods

- Zabt: measurement-based assessment.
- Dahsala: ten-year produce-price data used for annual rates.
- Dastur: regional rate schedule or circle.
- Common one-third norm requires implementation qualification.
- Batai/ghalla-bakhshi: division of actual produce.
- Kankut: measurement plus appraisal.
- Nasaq: assessment substantially based on earlier or local estimates.
- Zabt was strongest in settled, surveyed and monetised regions.
- Bengal, hills, forests, frontiers and uncertain crops required alternatives.
- Zabt may not have covered every field inside a nominal zabt province.
- Cash assessment supported accounting while shifting market risk toward cultivators.

## Zamindars and peasants

- Zamindar covered varied locally rooted right-holders, headmen, chiefs and rulers.
- Zamindars could possess forts, armed followers and customary shares.
- They provided information, collection and military support.
- They could also bargain, conceal or resist.
- Jagirdar and zamindar were distinct categories.
- Peasants differed by holding, caste, labour, irrigation, credit, crop and market access.
- State demand was not the cultivator's entire burden.
- Relief and loans were normative possibilities, not guaranteed outcomes.

## Operational state

- Mansab contingents, ahadis, artillery, infantry, elephants and garrisons formed a composite army.
- Technology required finance, crews, transport, fodder, roads and intelligence.
- Forts anchored strategic control but not uniform countryside administration.
- Waqia-navis and relays connected court and province.
- Information could be delayed, selective or patronage-bound.
- Faujdars combined coercive and route-order roles.
- Justice involved qazi, mir adl, imperial and customary forums.
- Kotwals handled important urban-order functions; faujdars exercised wider military-police and route-security authority.
- Mints standardised issues and projected sovereignty.
- Coinage did not imply universal monetisation.
- Ilahi era/calendar was distinct from Tauhid-i Ilahi.
- Karkhanas supplied household, court and military material.

## Final assessment

- Akbar inherited rather than invented every institution.
- His contribution lay in experimentation, coordination and scale.
- The state increased fiscal knowledge and military mobilisation.
- Diverse elite incorporation reduced dependence on one faction.
- Administrative power remained ruler-centred and hierarchical.
- Local intermediaries remained indispensable.
- Regional ecology prevented uniform revenue method.
- Records and prescriptions could diverge from practice.
- Stronger state capacity could provide predictability and intensify extraction.
- Best verdict: systematised, composite and durable, but mediated, unequal and uneven.

## Source cautions retained

- The twelve-suba count is dated to 1580; later expansion changed the number.
- Office titles and portfolios evolved and are not treated as permanently fixed modern ministries.
- "Checks and balances" means overlapping administrative counterweights under the ruler, not constitutional separation of powers.
- Mansab rank does not automatically equal troops physically present.
- Zat-sawar is treated as a late-Akbar distinction rather than projected over the whole reign.
- The canonical owners, detailed Part II and later mansabdari cross-topic source place the fortieth-regnal-year zat-sawar distinction in 1595-96; the concise OCR edition prints 1594-95, so the regnal-year formulation controls the chronology used here.
- Dagh and chehra are systematised controls with earlier precedents, not inventions without ancestry.
- Jagir is a claim on state revenue demand, not proprietary ownership of soil or peasants.
- Jama is distinguished from hasil.
- Todar Mal is treated as a major organiser within a team and inherited tradition.
- Dahsala means ten-year data used for annual schedules, not a permanent ten-year settlement.
- The one-third demand is treated as a norm whose incidence varied.
- Zabt is not treated as universal across provinces or fields.
- Zamindars are treated as a diverse category rather than modern landlords.
- Relief and peasant-protection claims are distinguished from actual implementation.
- Coin standards do not prove universal monetisation.
- The Ain-i Akbari is treated as a rich, court-sponsored and partly normative administrative account rather than a synchronised census.
- Later mansab refinements and jagirdari crisis are retained only as bounded forward links.
