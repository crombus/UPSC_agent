# Foreign Policy of the Mughals - Complete Learner-First Live Edition

## Learning roadmap

Mughal "foreign policy" was not conducted by a modern foreign ministry. It emerged from the emperor's court and joined several activities: guarding routes into Kabul, negotiating with neighbouring dynasties, holding or contesting frontier forts, protecting merchants and pilgrims, gathering intelligence and defending imperial rank. The learning sequence therefore begins with vocabulary and geography before moving through the Uzbek, Safavid, Ottoman and maritime fields.

| Lesson | Learning dependency | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What foreign policy means in a Mughal setting; claim, control and frontier vocabulary | Foundation |
| 2 | Timurid inheritance, Babur, Humayun, Kabul and the early Qandahar problem | Foundation |
| 3 | Courts, envoys, gifts, titles, intelligence and the Asian balance | Foundation |
| 4 | Akbar, Abdullah Khan Uzbek, Badakhshan, Kabul and strategic restraint | Core |
| 5 | Qandahar as fort, corridor, market and symbol; its acquisition in 1595 | Core |
| 6 | Jahangir, Shah Abbas I and the loss of Qandahar in 1622 | Core |
| 7 | Shah Jahan's diplomatic field and Qandahar's recovery in 1638 | Core |
| 8 | The Balkh-Badakhshan expedition of 1646-47 | Advanced |
| 9 | The loss of 1649 and the three failed Qandahar recovery campaigns | Advanced |
| 10 | Frontier peoples, routes, administration and the internal-external boundary | Advanced |
| 11 | Commerce, pilgrimage, the Portuguese and Ottoman maritime connections | Advanced |
| 12 | Aurangzeb's diplomatic coda, historiography, consequences and synthesis | Final synthesis |

The roadmap keeps four distinctions visible throughout: **dynastic memory is not an operational programme; diplomatic equality is not territorial control; a captured fort is not a durable frontier; and a ceremonial claim is not proof of another ruler's obedience**.

The map shows why no single word captures the whole subject. Some measures defended an imperial province, some dealt with another sovereign court, and some tried to shape a buffer beyond the empire.

### 1. Begin with an ordinary problem

Imagine a ruler whose richest provinces lie far from the north-western mountain passes. A hostile army may enter through those passes, but so may horses, merchants, letters and ambassadors. The ruler must therefore do more than conquer land. He must keep Kabul supplied, negotiate with Iran and the Uzbek khanates, monitor frontier groups, protect trade and decide whether a fort beyond the easiest defensive line is worth holding.

That is the problem called **Mughal foreign policy**. The phrase is useful because it groups relations with external powers. It must also be qualified because the Mughal empire had no modern ministry of external affairs, fixed international borders or doctrine of equal nation-states. Decisions were imperial and court-centred.

### 2. A vocabulary of political relationships

| Term | Precise use | What it does not automatically prove |
|---|---|---|
| diplomacy | managed communication through letters, envoys, gifts and negotiation | friendship or compliance |
| frontier policy | defence and management of routes, forts, tribes and provinces near an edge | action entirely outside the empire |
| conquest | military seizure of a place | lasting administration |
| occupation | physical possession by troops and officials | legitimacy or local cooperation |
| suzerainty | an asserted superior relationship allowing some subordinate autonomy | annexation or uniform taxation |
| annexation | formal incorporation into imperial territory | equally effective control everywhere |
| buffer | a friendly or dependent zone intended to separate the core from a rival | a fixed modern border state |
| claim | an expressed right to a territory or status | actual possession |

The essential exam move is to ask, "What kind of control is being claimed?" A court chronicle may celebrate submission, while local evidence shows bargaining and incomplete enforcement.

### 3. The strategic geography

**Kabul** was a Mughal province and a forward base. It guarded approaches from Central Asia and linked the empire with the Khyber route. **Qandahar** lay farther south-west. Its fort and watered oasis connected routes toward Herat and Iran, routes toward Kabul and routes through Baluchistan toward the Indus. It was therefore a military hinge and a commercial junction.

**Balkh** and **Badakhshan** lay beyond the Hindu Kush in the Central Asian political field. They mattered as possible buffers for Kabul and as lands associated with the Timurids, but occupation required movement across difficult mountains and the maintenance of long supply lines.

> **Map trap:** Qandahar, usually written Kandahar today, is the city and region in present-day Afghanistan. **Gandhara** was an ancient cultural region around the Peshawar-Taxila zone. They are not alternative spellings of the same place.

### 4. The four recurring objectives

```text
SECURITY
protect Kabul, passes and north-western routes
        |
        +--> BUFFER MANAGEMENT
        |    prevent a powerful hostile combination near the frontier
        |
        +--> COMMERCE AND MOVEMENT
        |    sustain caravan trade, horse supply, merchants and pilgrims
        |
        +--> STATUS AND LEGITIMACY
             defend Timurid rank and negotiate equality with major courts
```

These objectives could reinforce one another. Holding Qandahar could aid defence and commerce while enhancing prestige. They could also conflict. A spectacular campaign might increase temporary prestige but impose fiscal and logistical costs greater than its strategic gain.

### 5. Policy was made under uncertainty

Information travelled at the speed of messengers and caravans. Reports could be delayed, selective or shaped by an envoy's interests. The emperor therefore acted with incomplete knowledge about another court's intentions, a frontier chief's loyalty or an army's supply position. Diplomacy was partly a way of reducing that uncertainty.

This point prevents hindsight. The loss of Qandahar in 1622 looks predictable after the event. Jahangir's court, however, had to interpret cordial Safavid messages, reports of Persian preparation and a simultaneous princely crisis.

### 6. Interpretation: India-centred policy, but not India-only policy

A strong interpretation holds that Mughal policy increasingly protected an empire centred in India. Kabul, Qandahar and a friendly Balkh mattered chiefly because they shielded the imperial heartland and its communications. This corrects the claim that every Central Asian move was merely nostalgia for Samarqand.

The qualification is equally important. Timurid genealogy shaped prestige, court language and the range of imaginable action. Commercial communities and diplomatic rank also mattered independently of immediate invasion risk. The best explanation is therefore a hierarchy: **Indian security and imperial stability usually governed choices, while lineage, commerce and prestige shaped how those choices were framed**.

### 7. UPSC use

This lesson supplies the introduction to almost every answer on Mughal external relations. Define the analytical problem, locate the arenas, then classify the evidence under security, diplomacy, commerce and legitimacy. Do not begin with a ruler-by-ruler list.

**Verified PYQ linkage:** No direct CSE Prelims or GS-I question owned by this topic is verified in the available ledgers through 2026. The absence is not a reason to omit the topic; it means practice must test syllabus-relevant concepts without inventing a PYQ.

**Probable framing:** "Can the term foreign policy be applied to the Mughal Empire?" A balanced answer should say yes as analytical shorthand, but not as a modern bureaucratic category.

### Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain why "Mughal foreign policy" is a useful but qualified historical category.

**Model answer**

"Foreign policy" is useful because it brings together Mughal relations with the Safavids, Uzbeks, Ottomans and maritime powers. These relations involved embassies, letters, gifts, intelligence, commercial protection, frontier defence and occasional conquest. The category also clarifies why Kabul, Qandahar, Balkh and the sea routes formed connected strategic arenas.

Yet the term requires qualification. The Mughal state had neither a modern foreign ministry nor fixed nation-state borders. Frontier administration could involve imperial provinces, mobile communities and rulers beyond the frontier at the same time. Ceremonial claims of superiority did not necessarily produce obedience, while occupation did not guarantee durable administration.

Mughal policy is therefore best understood as court-centred management of security, routes, commerce and dynastic status. Its strongest India-centred logic was protection of Kabul and the imperial heartland, though Timurid memory and claims to parity with major Asian courts shaped its language and ambitions.

**Scoring note:** Reward the definition, the institutional qualification and a clear claim-control distinction; do not reward a mere list of neighbouring states.

## Visual first: from lost homeland to Indian base

```text
1494-1512: contests for Samarqand
          |
1504: Babur captures Kabul
          |
1507: brief possession of Qandahar
          |
1511: brief recovery of Samarqand with Safavid aid
          |
1522: Babur secures Qandahar
          |
1526: conquest of north India changes the dynasty's centre of gravity
          |
1545: Humayun recovers Qandahar and Kabul after Safavid-assisted return
```

The timeline shows continuity in Timurid memory but a decisive political shift: Kabul became a base, while conquest in India supplied the dynasty's durable imperial centre.

### 1. Why Timurid origin mattered

Babur descended from Timur through his father and from Chinggis Khan's line through his mother. This genealogy gave him prestige in a Central Asian political world where lineage affected claims to rule. His early career revolved around Farghana and repeated attempts to possess Samarqand.

The ordinary-language point is simple: a lost homeland can remain emotionally and politically meaningful without determining every later policy. Babur's memoirs preserve longing and contest, but his capture of Kabul in 1504 created a more defensible base. His Indian conquest in 1526 then changed where resources, succession and imperial survival were concentrated.

### 2. Babur and the Safavid connection

In 1511 Babur briefly recovered Samarqand with the support of Shah Ismail I. Safavid assistance had political and ceremonial costs, and Babur could not sustain himself against Uzbek power. The episode demonstrates that Sunni-Shia identity did not prevent tactical cooperation. It also shows that external aid could help obtain a city without solving local legitimacy and military sustainability.

Qandahar was contested among Timurid, Arghun and Persian-linked interests. Babur briefly possessed it in 1507 and secured it more firmly in 1522. The city's value arose from its location between Kabul, Iran and routes toward India.

### 3. Humayun: refuge, assistance and contested obligation

Sher Shah's victories displaced Humayun from India. After a difficult exile, he obtained support from Shah Tahmasp I of Iran. The precise ceremonial demands and promises surrounding that aid are filtered through partisan sources, but the political mechanism is clear: Safavid assistance helped Humayun rebuild military capacity and recover Qandahar and Kabul in 1545.

This created a recurring diplomatic problem. The Safavid court could present assistance as the basis of a Persian claim or obligation; the Mughal court could treat possession as part of its own Timurid and strategic sphere. A past favour did not settle future sovereignty because each court narrated the transaction differently.

### 4. Kabul and Qandahar performed different functions

| Dimension | Kabul | Qandahar |
|---|---|---|
| political position | durable Mughal provincial base after recovery | frequently contested Mughal-Safavid hinge |
| route role | Khyber and Central Asian approaches to India | routes to Herat, Iran, Kabul and the Indus |
| strategic problem | defend an incorporated province | decide whether and how to hold an exposed forward fort |
| symbolic role | Babur's base and burial landscape | test of parity and competing territorial narratives |

Conflating the two produces a weak answer. Kabul's defence was usually non-negotiable. Qandahar's value was high, but the cost of recovery and retention could be debated.

### 5. From memory to strategy

The **nostalgia thesis** says that Timurid rulers were drawn toward Central Asia by ancestral attachment. It explains court language and some ambition. It cannot alone explain why Babur consolidated in India, why Akbar often preferred balance to invasion or why Shah Jahan eventually withdrew from Balkh.

The **India-centred thesis** says that the dynasty used Kabul, Qandahar and possible buffers to defend an empire whose resources lay in India. It better explains sustained priorities. Yet if treated too mechanically, it underestimates genealogy, reputation and the political value of acting as a leading Timurid sovereign.

The best assessment treats memory as a **legitimating horizon** and security as an **operational filter**. Plans became policy only when resources, succession politics, local allies and rival weakness made them feasible.

### 6. Evidence limits

Memoirs and court histories reveal how rulers represented action. Persian chronicles may stress Safavid generosity; Mughal narratives may stress rightful recovery. Neither should be read as a neutral treaty transcript. Chronology, competing narratives and subsequent conduct must be compared.

### 7. UPSC use

Use Babur and Humayun as a foundation, not as a long biographical preface. In an answer on Shah Jahan's Balkh campaign, two sentences are enough: Timurid memory enlarged the imaginative field, but the immediate problem was the security of Kabul through a friendly trans-Hindu Kush power.

### Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish Timurid dynastic memory from the practical north-western strategy of the early Mughals.

**Model answer**

Timurid memory gave Babur and his successors a language of legitimacy and an attachment to the Central Asian world. Babur repeatedly contested Samarqand, captured Kabul in 1504 and briefly recovered Samarqand with Safavid aid in 1511. Such experiences kept Central Asia within Mughal imagination.

Practical strategy, however, changed with the conquest of India. Kabul became a durable base protecting the approaches to the new imperial centre. Qandahar, secured by Babur in 1522 and recovered by Humayun in 1545, mattered as a fortified junction linking Kabul, Iran and routes toward India. Humayun's Safavid-assisted restoration also created rival Mughal and Persian narratives of obligation.

Thus memory did not function as a compulsory programme to reconquer ancestral lands. It shaped prestige and possible goals, while resources, frontier security, rival power and local cooperation determined action. The early Mughal experience established the lasting tension between symbolic Timurid horizons and an increasingly India-centred imperial strategy.

**Scoring note:** Credit a chronological foundation and an explicit distinction between legitimating memory and operational policy.

## Visual first: an embassy as a moving institution

```text
RULER'S PURPOSE
     |
letter + oral instructions + gifts + titles
     |
ELCHI / SAFIR and accompanying mission
     |
journey: routes, customs, escorts, delays, observation
     |
foreign court: reception, rank, negotiation, counter-gifts
     |
return report: intentions, resources, factions and political climate
```

An embassy was not simply a polite visit. It carried a message, staged rank, gathered information and tested whether words would be followed by action.

### 1. Elchi, safir and the absence of permanent residence

The terms **elchi** and **safir** were used for envoys or ambassadors. Missions were normally temporary. The early modern Mughal world did not operate through a continuous network of permanent resident embassies like a later diplomatic service.

An envoy carried written and oral instructions. Because travel was slow, he needed discretion when circumstances changed. His entourage, route, reception and gifts all communicated status. On return, he could report military preparations, court factions, trade conditions and the reliability of earlier information.

### 2. Four major courts and a changing balance

| Court or political field | Mughal concern | Why relations changed |
|---|---|---|
| Safavid Iran | Qandahar, Khurasan, trade, pilgrimage and parity | succession, military opportunity and competing territorial narratives |
| Uzbek khanates | Balkh, Badakhshan, Kabul and trans-Oxus pressure | fragmentation or concentration of Uzbek power |
| Ottoman Empire | prestige, Iran's western flank, Red Sea and pilgrimage context | Ottoman-Safavid war, maritime politics and ceremonial claims |
| Mughal court | Indian security, routes, imperial rank and internal stability | succession struggles, Deccan commitments and frontier resources |

This was not a fixed alliance system. The Mughals could welcome Ottoman pressure on Iran without accepting Ottoman superiority. They could oppose Uzbek attacks while remaining unwilling to join a sectarian war against the Safavids.

### 3. Gifts, titles and the politics of equality

Gifts were material and symbolic. Horses, textiles, jewels or rare objects displayed resources and honour. Titles and forms of address could imply parity or hierarchy. A ruler might accept gifts while rejecting the political interpretation attached to them.

Therefore:

```text
GIFT RECEIVED
   does not equal
SUBMISSION ACCEPTED

COURTEOUS LETTER
   does not equal
TERRITORIAL CLAIM ABANDONED

GRAND RECEPTION
   does not equal
PERMANENT ALLIANCE
```

Diplomatic theatre matters precisely because sovereignty was contested. Its meaning must be checked against negotiations, troop movements and later conduct.

### 4. Information and its distortions

Embassies gathered intelligence, but not perfect intelligence. Four filters affected reports:

1. **delay** - news could arrive after conditions changed;
2. **performance** - a host court could stage abundance or unity;
3. **interest** - an envoy might defend his own decisions or patrons;
4. **translation** - titles and political concepts did not always carry identical implications across courts.

Merchant networks, pilgrims, frontier officials and travellers supplied additional information. Their accounts also had limits. A merchant knew routes and prices better than secret military plans; a court chronicler knew official claims better than village-level effects.

### 5. Religion mattered without dictating policy

Safavid Iran officially upheld Twelver Shiism. The Uzbeks and Ottomans were Sunni powers, as were the Mughal emperors in dynastic affiliation. Yet no permanent Sunni bloc mechanically followed. Akbar rejected conquest of Iran merely on sectarian grounds. Mughal-Safavid cooperation, conflict and ceremonial exchange continued under changing circumstances.

Religion influenced legitimacy, pilgrimage, rhetoric and court suspicion. **Realpolitik** - action shaped by practical power and interest - interacted with it. Replacing religious determinism with pure secularism would be equally misleading.

### 6. Diplomatic equality and imperial ideology

The Mughals wanted recognition as one of the great Asian sovereign houses. Safavid rulers grounded special prestige partly in sacred genealogy; Ottoman rulers claimed wider Islamic leadership; Mughal rulers combined Timurid descent, conquest and universal imperial language.

Disputes over precedence were therefore not decorative. They affected how a letter was phrased, how an envoy was seated and how a territorial concession could be represented. Nevertheless, protocol cannot be used as a direct map of power. A court could speak as an equal while accepting a strategic setback.

### 7. Strongest objection and balanced reply

**Objection:** If embassies lacked permanent residence and could not enforce agreements, diplomacy was merely episodic ceremony.

**Reply:** Precisely because communication was slow and coercion expensive, episodic missions performed vital functions: they transmitted proposals, reduced uncertainty, delayed conflict, recruited allies and allowed rulers to retreat without openly surrendering status. Their effect must be measured by specific outcomes, not modern institutional standards.

### 8. UPSC use

In a Mains answer, use diplomatic machinery as a mechanism paragraph between chronology and evaluation. It converts "the Mughals maintained relations" into an explanation of how relations worked.

**Question:** Analyse how diplomatic machinery converted information, ceremony and negotiation into instruments of Mughal external policy.

**Model answer**

Mughal diplomacy worked through temporary missions led by an **elchi** or **safir**, not through a modern network of permanent ambassadors. An envoy carried written and oral instructions, gifts and forms of address. He negotiated at the receiving court and reported on his return.

This machinery served three connected purposes. First, it communicated proposals on Qandahar, frontier security, alliance and trade when direct coercion was costly. Second, reception, seating, titles and counter-gifts staged the relative rank claimed by the Mughal, Safavid, Uzbek or Ottoman ruler. Third, envoys gathered intelligence about military preparation, court factions, routes and commercial conditions. Merchants, pilgrims and frontier officials supplemented these reports.

Diplomacy nevertheless had limits. Slow travel made information stale; host courts could stage unity; and envoys interpreted events through their own interests. Costly presents did not prove submission, while courteous correspondence could coexist with preparations for war.

Thus diplomacy was neither empty ceremony nor a modern professional service. It was a flexible court-centred institution that reduced uncertainty, preserved status, enabled bargaining and sometimes postponed conflict, but its claims must be checked against action and control.

The sequence is not a story of passive peace. Akbar combined ideological refusal, diplomatic engagement and concrete frontier consolidation.

### 1. Who was Abdullah Khan Uzbek?

Abdullah Khan II of Bukhara built a powerful Uzbek state. His expansion toward Balkh and Badakhshan brought concentrated Uzbek power closer to Kabul. The danger was not that every Uzbek army was already marching on India. It was that the political buffer north of the Hindu Kush was narrowing.

The chronology must be kept in two stages. Abdullah first seized Balkh in 1572-73. Shahrukh Mirza later recovered or held it, before Abdullah reconquered it in 1583. In 1585 Abdullah took Badakhshan. Compressing these events into one conquest erases the changing frontier context in which Akbar acted.

### 2. The 1577 proposal and Akbar's response

Abdullah's embassy proposed cooperation against Safavid Iran. Shared Sunni identity could make such a proposal rhetorically attractive. Akbar declined to treat sectarian difference as sufficient cause for attacking Iran.

This decision had several layers:

1. a joint attack could over-strengthen the Uzbeks;
2. destruction of Safavid power could remove a counterweight in western Asia;
3. war would unsettle routes and expose the Mughal frontier;
4. Akbar's developing imperial ethic resisted a purely confessional justification.

The decision was therefore principled and strategic. Calling it only religious tolerance misses balance-of-power logic; calling it only realpolitik misses the normative language through which Akbar rejected the plan.

### 3. From Mirza Hakim to the annexation of Kabul

Akbar's half-brother Mirza Hakim governed Kabul and sometimes became a focus for opposition to Akbar. His death in 1585 removed an unstable dynastic arrangement. Akbar incorporated Kabul more directly into the empire.

This was a major external-policy resource. A frontier cannot be managed effectively if its principal base remains politically uncertain. Annexation allowed more direct appointment, provisioning and intelligence, though geography and local power still limited control beyond the province.

### 4. The renewed embassy and Atak

An Uzbek embassy in 1586 renewed contact after Abdullah's advance into Badakhshan. Akbar moved north-west and remained for a period near **Atak/Attock**, where the Indus crossing and the road toward Peshawar and Kabul could be supervised.

The response reveals a layered strategy:

```text
No preventive invasion of the Uzbek realm
               +
No sectarian alliance against Iran
               +
Direct strengthening of Kabul and the Indus approach
               +
Continued diplomatic contact with Abdullah Khan
               =
BALANCE THROUGH RESTRAINT AND PREPAREDNESS
```

### 5. The "Hindu Kush understanding"

Historians sometimes describe an implicit understanding under which the Hindu Kush separated the main Mughal and Uzbek spheres. This is useful shorthand, not a surveyed treaty boundary. Mountain ranges contain passes, valleys and mobile communities; political influence did not stop at a modern line.

The safest formulation is that both powers increasingly recognized the costs of pushing deeply into the other's effective sphere. Akbar sought a stable north-western balance, while Abdullah's main interests remained in Central Asia.

### 6. Hakim Humam and diplomatic contact

Akbar sent Hakim Humam as an envoy to Abdullah Khan. Such missions communicated the Mughal position and collected intelligence. They also allowed each ruler to acknowledge the other's stature without conceding sovereignty.

No single embassy "created" the balance. The balance rested on military capability, the annexation of Kabul, Safavid survival and mutual calculation; diplomacy gave that calculation a usable language.

### 7. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Akbar's refusal to join Abdullah proves that his policy was vague idealism.

**Reply:** Refusal did not mean inactivity. Akbar annexed Kabul, supervised the frontier, maintained contacts and later acquired Qandahar. Avoiding a war that could strengthen the Uzbeks while weakening Iran was a strategic choice.

**Further qualification:** It is also too neat to present Akbar as designing a stable modern balance from the start. Decisions responded to changing information: the two-stage Balkh struggle, Badakhshan's fall, Mirza Hakim's death and the condition of Safavid Iran.

## Visual first: why one place carried four kinds of value

```text
                       HERAT / IRAN
                            |
                 [QANDAHAR: FORT + OASIS]
                    /          |          \
              KABUL        BALUCH ROUTES    INDUS / MULTAN
                |                              |
          Central Asia                    Mughal India

VALUE = defence + communications + caravan commerce + imperial prestige
```

Qandahar mattered not because it performed one magical function, but because several routes and political meanings converged there.

### 1. Fort and water

An army moving through an arid frontier needed water, fodder, food and defensible halts. Qandahar's oasis and fort made it a base from which roads could be watched and forces sustained. A strong garrison could deny easy passage and compel an attacker to transport heavy siege equipment over a long route.

This does not mean that holding Qandahar sealed every approach to India. Armies and communities could move along different corridors. The fort increased strategic depth; it did not create an impermeable wall.

### 2. Corridor and communication

Qandahar connected the Mughal zone around Kabul and the Indus with Herat and Iranian markets. It also linked routes through Baluchistan. Control could facilitate official communication, escort arrangements and the movement of high-value goods.

The word **gateway** is useful only if treated as plural. Kabul and Qandahar were twin gateways in different directions. Neither was the sole route through which all Central Asian or Iranian commerce passed.

### 3. Market and merchants

Indian merchants were active in Iranian and Central Asian cities. Caravan trade carried textiles, horses, precious metals and other high-value commodities. Qandahar's political stability affected transaction costs: insecurity could raise escort expenses, delay caravans or expose merchants to competing dues.

Yet trade volumes are difficult to reconstruct. Traveller estimates may be impressionistic; court chronicles may mention valuable exchanges rather than routine commerce. The safe argument is qualitative: commercial connectivity added to Qandahar's importance, but no unsupported numerical claim should be made.

### 4. Symbol and diplomatic rank

Both the Mughal and Safavid courts could present Qandahar as rightful territory. For the Mughals it helped complete the Kabul-Ghazni-Qandahar defensive conception and expressed Timurid standing. For the Safavids it lay within an eastern Iranian sphere and could be linked to earlier possession and assistance to Humayun.

Repeated transfer made the city a test of prestige. Once an emperor publicly committed to recovery, failure had reputational costs even if the fort's immediate economic return was modest.

### 5. Akbar's "golden key"

In 1591 Akbar instructed Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan to prefer the **"golden key"** in dealing with Qandahar - negotiation and inducement rather than an unnecessarily destructive assault. The expression captures a practical principle: where a strategically valuable fort could be obtained through political accommodation, force was not the only instrument.

In 1595 the local Safavid-linked command under Muzaffar Husain Mirza transferred or surrendered Qandahar to the Mughals amid weakness and disorder in the Persian field. It was acquired without a major siege. This was a diplomatic-political success backed by Mughal strength, not proof that the Safavid claim had disappeared.

### 6. A multi-causal test

| Weak explanation | Why incomplete | Stronger formulation |
|---|---|---|
| "Qandahar was only a trade centre" | ignores fortification and rival power | commerce raised the value of a strategic fort |
| "Qandahar was only a prestige issue" | ignores routes and Kabul's defence | prestige magnified a real communications and security asset |
| "Qandahar alone defended India" | treats one fort as an impermeable border | it added depth to a wider Kabul-Ghazni-pass system |
| "Its capture proved permanent control" | ignores recurrent transfer and supply | possession depended on garrison, diplomacy and rival capacity |

### 7. Objection and reply

**Objection:** If Qandahar changed hands repeatedly, it cannot have been strategically important.

**Reply:** Repeated contest can itself indicate value. The better question is whether the benefits of possession justified the costs in a particular decade. Its importance was dynamic: the strength of Iran and the Uzbeks, the condition of Kabul, trade routes and imperial prestige changed the calculation.

The sequence explains a central paradox: rich diplomacy did not remove the territorial dispute; it managed the relationship while each side watched for opportunity.

### 1. Shah Abbas's recovery and eastern confidence

Shah Abbas I strengthened Safavid Iran and recovered Khurasan after Abdullah Khan's death in 1598. The regional balance that had favoured Akbar's acquisition of Qandahar therefore changed. A stronger Safavid state could revive its eastern claim.

Jahangir and Shah Abbas exchanged warm letters and embassies. In 1611 Shah Abbas sent a lavish mission. Khan Alam later led a Mughal return embassy and spent an extended period at the Persian court. Muhammad Hussain Chalabi was associated with this diplomatic cycle.

Jahangir appointed **Muhammad Hussain Chalabi as a royal trade commissioner**. His role shows that embassy exchange and commercial oversight could intersect. It should not be expanded into a permanent diplomatic residence or a modern trade department.

The warmth was politically useful, but it should not be mistaken for abandonment of Qandahar. Diplomacy allowed both courts to preserve honour while the underlying claim remained unresolved.

### 2. Claim becomes warning

In 1620 the Persian envoy **Zambil Beg** raised the issue of Qandahar. This was not yet the fall of the fort, but it was a warning that ceremonial friendship coexisted with a concrete territorial demand.

The Mughal court had to decide whether the threat required immediate reinforcement. Slow communication, uncertainty over Safavid intentions and competing imperial commitments complicated the response.

### 3. The internal crisis

By 1622 Prince Khurram, the future Shah Jahan, was deeply involved in court and succession politics. Jahangir ordered him to move toward Qandahar, but the prince feared that leaving his power base and forces would weaken his position against the Nur Jahan grouping. His resistance reduced the court's capacity for a rapid, united response.

It is tempting to reduce the loss to "Nur Jahan's faction" or "Khurram's rebellion." That is incomplete. Internal conflict mattered because it interacted with:

- Safavid preparation and initiative;
- the exposed position of the fort;
- delayed Mughal mobilisation;
- Shah Abbas's ability to combine negotiation with force.

### 4. The capture of 1622

Shah Abbas besieged and captured Qandahar in 1622. The event ended Mughal possession established in 1595. It did not mean that Kabul fell or that the entire north-western frontier collapsed. Nor did it end diplomatic relations.

The strength of the Mughal garrison is not securely expressed by one uncontested number. The *Iqbalnama-i Jahangiri* gives **3,000 troops**, whereas Jahangir's memoir refers to only **300 or 400 servants**, probably the commandant's personal following. The figures may describe different categories. They must remain source-attributed rather than being fused into a false precise total.

The loss was strategically and symbolically serious. It removed the forward fort, weakened Mughal bargaining and exposed the gap between elaborate ceremonial friendship and hard territorial interest.

### 5. Why Jahangir could not simply recover it

```text
Strong Safavid initiative
        +
Fort difficult to relieve quickly
        +
Prince Khurram's succession calculation
        +
Court faction and divided command
        +
Delayed mobilisation
        =
LOSS WITHOUT IMMEDIATE RECOVERY
```

This causal bundle is stronger than a morality tale about one disloyal prince. Empires often fail at frontiers when external timing exploits internal coordination problems.

### 6. Sources and self-justification

Jahangir's memoir and court narratives must be read with attention to blame. A ruler has reason to explain why warning signs were discounted or why a subordinate failed to act. Safavid accounts have their own interest in presenting recovery as rightful and successful.

Evidence can firmly establish the sequence and result. It cannot always recover every private intention behind an envoy's words. Use "suggests," "presented as" or "appears to have" where motives remain interpretive.

### 7. Historiographical objection and reply

**Objection:** Diplomatic cordiality proves Jahangir was deceived by Shah Abbas.

**Reply:** Deception may have played a role, but diplomatic courtesy was a normal instrument, not a promise that disputes had vanished. Jahangir's problem was not merely credulity; it was failure to convert warning, resources and command into timely defence amid succession conflict.

**Objection:** Khurram alone lost Qandahar.

**Reply:** His refusal was important, yet it became decisive only within a wider system of Safavid strength, geographic exposure and court division.

The sequence shows how Shah Jahan used military readiness, inter-court diplomacy and elite defection together.

### 1. A renewed Uzbek warning

After 1622, Mughal and Uzbek discussions intermittently revived the possibility of common pressure on Iran. Uzbek leaders also communicated with the Safavid court, revealing why a confessional bloc remained unreliable. **Yalingtosh** raided toward Kabul and Ghazni and tried to draw Hazara and Afghan disaffection into his operations; Mughal defence and artillery checked the effort.

In 1628 Nazr Muhammad of Balkh entered Kabul city and invested the fort. He withdrew before the approaching Mughal relief force. Mughal forces then occupied Bamiyan temporarily and diplomacy helped isolate him. The episode did not transfer Kabul durably, but it showed that instability beyond the Hindu Kush could threaten the province.

The correct inference is limited: one raid did not prove an imminent permanent Uzbek conquest. It did reinforce the value of buffers, intelligence and secure communications.

### 2. Iran, the Ottomans and the 1636 proposal

Shah Jahan explored cooperation with Ottoman Sultan **Murad IV** in 1636 while the Ottomans were fighting the Safavids. Coordinated pressure could distract Iran and improve the Mughal position in the east.

This was not a durable Sunni alliance or Mughal acceptance of Ottoman supremacy. The courts had different theatres, priorities and claims. Their shared pressure on Iran could produce temporary strategic convergence without creating unified command.

Frequent missions between 1633 and 1639 accompanied discussion of a wider anti-Safavid arrangement. Their frequency proves sustained exploration, not operational unity: the Uzbeks were unreliable partners, Ottoman forces were distant, and claims to precedence complicated cooperation.

### 3. Ali Mardan Khan's defection

In 1638 **Ali Mardan Khan**, the Safavid governor of Qandahar, defected to Shah Jahan and transferred the fortress. His decision reflected difficulties with the Safavid court and the attractions of Mughal service. The Mughals rewarded him and later employed his administrative and engineering abilities.

The mechanism matters:

```text
SAFAVID GOVERNOR'S VULNERABILITY
            +
MUGHAL OFFER OF SECURITY, RANK AND REWARD
            +
NEARBY MUGHAL MILITARY CAPACITY
            =
TRANSFER OF QANDAHAR WITHOUT A LONG SIEGE
```

This was not simply "conquest." It was elite defection under geopolitical pressure. Nor did it prove that the population or all surrounding routes had chosen a permanent Mughal identity.

Shah Jahan continued conciliatory diplomacy and even offered a payment corresponding to the fort's revenue. Possession and negotiation therefore coexisted. Shah Safi prepared to recover Qandahar; Shah Jahan moved toward Kabul and entrusted Dara Shukoh with artillery for its defence. Shah Safi died during the movement in **1642**, postponing the confrontation.

### 4. Why 1638 differed from 1622

| 1622 | 1638 |
|---|---|
| a strengthened Safavid ruler chose the moment | Safavid authority at Qandahar was weakened by a governor's rupture |
| Mughal court coordination was damaged by succession conflict | Shah Jahan's court could offer rank and backing |
| Persia used siege and military occupation | Mughal acquisition centred on negotiated defection |
| Mughal possession ended | Mughal possession resumed |

Both transfers reveal that frontier fortresses depended on command loyalty as well as walls. A powerful fort is only as secure as its garrison, supply and relationship with the sovereign court.

### 5. Diplomatic contradiction or strategic flexibility?

Shah Jahan could maintain formal relations with Iran, discuss pressure with the Ottomans and welcome a Safavid governor's defection. This looks contradictory only if diplomacy is assumed to require permanent friendship or hostility.

Early modern courts managed several tracks at once:

- ceremonial correspondence preserved status;
- negotiations tested concessions;
- military preparation created leverage;
- offers of service targeted rival elites;
- third-power pressure altered timing.

### 6. Evidence caution: the governor's name

The accepted identification of the governor who transferred Qandahar in 1638 is **Ali Mardan Khan**. One OCR rendering in a secondary text gives a different name in this passage, an apparent textual or recognition error. When a name conflicts with the canonical chronology and wider identification, the historian must corroborate rather than reproduce the anomaly.

This is a practical source lesson: OCR is a search aid, not an authority independent of the page and corroborating scholarship.

### 7. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Because the fort changed hands by defection, the Mughal recovery was not a foreign-policy success.

**Reply:** Diplomacy and inducement are instruments of policy. Obtaining a strong fort without a costly siege was a significant success. The qualification is that control acquired through one commander's defection required continued investment and remained vulnerable to renewed Safavid pressure.

The campaign's central paradox is visible: an army won important operations but failed to create the political arrangement for which it had been sent.

### 1. The immediate political crisis

Imam Quli had divided authority with his brother Nazr Muhammad. Nazr Muhammad later brought Balkh and Bukhara under his control, but his son **Abdul Aziz** rebelled. Nazr Muhammad appealed to Shah Jahan in 1645. For the Mughals, a friendly ruler in Balkh and Badakhshan could buffer Kabul against a unified or hostile Uzbek power.

The campaign was not simply an unprovoked march to recover Samarqand. Shah Jahan's instructions envisaged helping and restoring Nazr Muhammad if he behaved with submission and cooperation. If conditions allowed, assistance farther toward Samarqand and Bukhara could be imagined, but that contingent possibility was not the same as a settled annexation plan.

### 2. Force and attribution

The 1646 army under Prince Murad is described in the main narrative as comprising **50,000 horse and 10,000 foot**, including musketeers, rocketeers, gunners and a Rajput contingent. These figures are source-attributed and should not be treated as a modern audited muster.

The composition nevertheless reveals the scale of commitment. Cavalry provided mobility; infantry and firearms supported occupation and siege; Rajput contingents reflected the composite imperial service elite.

### 3. Murad's political error

Shah Jahan instructed Murad to treat Nazr Muhammad with consideration. Murad advanced into Balkh and ordered entry into the fort before a trusted arrangement had been secured. Unsure of Mughal intentions, Nazr Muhammad fled.

This turned a difficult intervention into a deeper occupation problem:

```text
BUFFER PLAN required a cooperative local ruler
                 |
local ruler fled because trust collapsed
                 |
Mughals inherited direct occupation
                 |
population and Uzbek forces treated them as outsiders
```

Military access had outrun political preparation. The episode is a textbook example of the gap between entering a capital and constructing legitimate authority.

### 4. Aurangzeb and the battle outside Balkh

Prince Murad wanted to return and was recalled. Sadullah Khan was sent to organise administration; Aurangzeb, accompanied in the military crisis by Ali Mardan Khan, replaced Murad. In 1647 Abdul Aziz crossed the Oxus with Uzbek forces. Because the river was fordable, Aurangzeb did not trust it as a self-defending line: he used forward pickets while retaining a mobile force with artillery. Mughal troops defeated the Uzbeks outside Balkh. The victory demonstrated field discipline and command.

It did not solve the political problem. Abdul Aziz's support weakened enough to make negotiation possible, while Nazr Muhammad, then linked with Iran, also sought restoration. Shah Jahan favoured restoring him, but demanded apology and a submission to Aurangzeb that Nazr Muhammad found humiliating and unnecessary.

### 5. Logistics converted victory into withdrawal

| Constraint | Operational effect |
|---|---|
| Hindu Kush distance | slow reinforcement and difficult transport |
| limited local supplies | army depended on an extended provisioning system |
| hostile or uncertain population | occupation demanded more troops and bargaining |
| Uzbek mobile pressure | threatened detachments and retreat routes |
| approaching winter | narrowed the safe campaigning season |
| failed political settlement | left no accepted client to take over |
| reluctant imperial nobles | reduced willingness to sustain a distant and costly command |
| Persian opposition | discouraged durable Mughal influence across the Hindu Kush |

The Mughals withdrew in October 1647. The retreat suffered severe losses and harassment, though Aurangzeb's firmness helped prevent complete disaster.

### 6. Was the campaign a failure?

Three verdicts must be separated.

**Military verdict:** not a simple defeat. Mughal forces occupied Balkh and defeated Uzbek forces in the field.

**Political verdict:** limited. They could not install a workable friendly regime on acceptable terms.

**Strategic verdict:** mixed. The expedition displayed power and may have discouraged a unified Uzbek threat, but yielded little durable influence at high cost.

### 7. Historiographical debate

One interpretation treats the expedition as Timurid adventurism: Shah Jahan pursued ancestral glamour beyond realistic limits. Another treats it as forward defence: a friendly Balkh would protect Kabul. The strongest conclusion combines motive and feasibility. Buffer security was a defensible objective; the means became overextended once Nazr Muhammad fled and direct occupation replaced client restoration.

The phrase **scientific frontier**, used in some older historical writing for a defensible line based on physical geography, also needs care. The Hindu Kush was not a modern surveyed frontier. Extending to the Oxus did not automatically make defence rational if the supply and political base lay south of the mountains.

## Visual first: why repeated effort did not reverse possession

```text
1649  Shah Abbas II captures Qandahar
       |
       +--> 1649 Aurangzeb's first recovery attempt fails
       |
       +--> 1652 Aurangzeb's second recovery attempt fails
       |
       +--> 1653 Dara Shukoh's larger effort fails

LONG ROAD FROM LAHORE + STRONG FORT + PERSIAN DEFENCE
SEASONAL SUPPLY + HEAVY-GUN TRANSPORT + SCORCHED ENVIRONS
                    =
FIELD ARMY PRESENT, BREACH OR STARVATION NOT ACHIEVED
```

The important distinction is between bringing a large army to a fortress and creating the technical, logistical and temporal conditions needed to take it.

### 1. Why the Safavids could strike in 1649

The Mughal withdrawal from Balkh revealed the limits of sustained operations beyond the Hindu Kush. Afghan unrest and renewed Uzbek pressure also complicated the north-western field. Shah Abbas II exploited the moment and recovered Qandahar in 1649.

The loss was a strategic and reputational blow, but it did not cause a general collapse. The Mughal state retained Kabul and possessed the resources to attempt recovery repeatedly.

### 2. The three attempts

| Year | Mughal commander | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1649 | Prince Aurangzeb | first recovery attempt failed |
| 1652 | Prince Aurangzeb | second recovery attempt failed |
| 1653 | Prince Dara Shukoh | third and especially elaborate siege failed |

The sequence must not be turned into a rivalry cartoon. Aurangzeb and Dara commanded different campaigns under changing conditions. Dara's effort included very heavy guns transported toward Qandahar, but size and display could not guarantee an effective breach.

Aurangzeb's 1649 army achieved some field success outside the fort, yet the garrison remained secure. This distinction again prevents a simple victory-defeat label: defeating forces in the open did not capture the fortified objective.

### 3. The logistics of siege

The Mughal supply line extended from the Punjab, especially Lahore, through difficult terrain. An attacking army needed grain, fodder, ammunition and replacement animals. Heavy cannon moved slowly. The defenders could exploit prepared fortifications and local stores, while denying supplies around the fort.

```text
ATTACKER'S CLOCK
supplies consumed -> animals weaken -> weather worsens -> pressure to withdraw

DEFENDER'S CLOCK
walls hold -> stores rationed -> relief remains possible -> attacker pays daily cost
```

If bombardment did not create a usable breach and blockade did not starve the garrison, time favoured the defender.

### 4. Artillery: a precise diagnosis

A common claim says the failures proved that Mughal artillery was generally inferior. That is too broad. Mughal guns were effective in many field and siege contexts. At Qandahar the problem was relational:

- the fortress was strong and well commanded;
- Persian defensive artillery and repairs were effective;
- transporting the largest Mughal guns was difficult;
- available guns did not create a decisive breach within the supply season;
- a long blockade imposed greater costs on the attacker.

The conclusion is not "artillery did not matter." It is that artillery effectiveness depended on fortification, transport, ammunition, engineering and time.

### 5. Was Qandahar still worth the cost?

By the mid-seventeenth century both Safavid and Uzbek power faced limits. Some historians therefore argue that Shah Jahan's attachment became disproportionately sentimental or prestige-driven. The repeated failures themselves magnified the reputational issue.

The counterargument is that Qandahar still offered strategic depth and commercial connections, and its loss exposed Kabul's south-western approach. A rational interest existed even if the third campaign's marginal cost exceeded the likely gain.

The balanced verdict distinguishes the value of the place from the wisdom of every attempt to recover it.

### 6. Outcome after 1653

After Dara's failed siege, the Mughals did not recover Qandahar. That outcome did not end all relations with Iran, destroy Mughal trade or make Kabul indefensible. The empire adapted to a frontier line without the fort.

This is an important counterfactual discipline. If Mughal power continued without Qandahar, the city was not indispensable. If emperors repeatedly contested it, it was not irrelevant. Strategic importance lies between those extremes.

The frontier was an inhabited political zone. External rivals, imperial officers and local communities interacted along routes whose control varied by season and negotiation.

### 1. Why "internal" and "external" overlap

Kabul was inside the Mughal imperial structure, but a campaign to defend it could involve rulers beyond the Hindu Kush and communities whose authority crossed formal jurisdictions. The Khyber and other routes were not empty channels. They passed through territories where local chiefs controlled movement, supplied guides and could obstruct armies.

A frontier tribe might negotiate revenue or service with Mughal officials, resist a particular demand, and maintain contacts beyond the empire. Calling every such action foreign invasion is wrong; calling the same groups ordinary settled subjects under uniform administration is also wrong.

### 2. The Roshani and Yusufzai contexts

The **Roshani** movement associated with Bayazid Ansari combined religious, social and political dimensions and challenged Mughal authority in parts of the Afghan frontier. Yusufzai resistance likewise drew on local structures, terrain and disputes with imperial power.

For this topic, these histories matter in a bounded way. They show that defence against an Uzbek or Safavid state depended on the security of routes through locally contested territory. Full treatment of their doctrines and campaigns belongs to frontier and Akbar-era political history.

### 3. A layered control model

| Layer | Mughal instrument | Typical limitation |
|---|---|---|
| fortified node | garrison at Kabul, Peshawar or Attock | expensive supply and limited reach between posts |
| road and pass | escorts, patrols, negotiated passage | seasonal closure and local interception |
| local intermediary | allowance, rank, service or recognition | shifting loyalty and factional rivalry |
| provincial administration | revenue and appointment structure | uneven information and terrain |
| external diplomacy | agreements with neighbouring rulers | no direct command over their subjects |

Effective frontier power depended on links among the layers. A well-held fort could not by itself secure every convoy. A negotiated chief could not substitute for a strategic reserve if a major army advanced.

### 4. Intelligence as route governance

Information travelled through:

- provincial reports from Kabul and the Punjab;
- envoys returning from foreign courts;
- merchants and caravan leaders;
- local informants and chiefs;
- military scouts and messengers.

Each channel saw a different part of the field. A merchant could report unsafe roads; a governor might exaggerate danger to seek reinforcements; a local chief might present a rival as rebellious. Triangulation was therefore essential even when the term was not used.

### 5. Coercion and accommodation

Mughal frontier policy alternated and combined:

```text
NEGOTIATE allowances, service and passage
        |
FORTIFY strategic nodes
        |
PUNISH attacks or rebellion
        |
REOPEN routes and restore bargaining
```

This was not evidence of an empire incapable of choosing. It reflected a landscape in which permanent occupation of every valley was more costly than selective coercion joined to accommodation.

The danger lay in excessive generalisation. Punitive expeditions could deepen hostility and disrupt trade; subsidies could become expected payments without ensuring compliance. Policy required repeated adjustment.

### 6. Northwest frontier versus Qandahar policy

The two were connected but not identical. Qandahar concerned a major Mughal-Safavid fort and regional corridor. The Peshawar-Khyber-Attock field involved imperial administration and negotiated relations with local communities. A crisis in one could strain the other by diverting troops and supplies.

This distinction also explains why possession of Qandahar did not solve the whole north-western problem. Internal route security remained necessary.

### 7. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** The word "tribal" sufficiently explains recurrent disorder.

**Reply:** It labels social organisation without explaining the conflict. Analysis must identify revenue demand, route control, succession within local leadership, religious mobilisation, terrain and imperial coercion.

**Criticism:** Because sovereignty was uneven, Mughal control was fictitious.

**Reply:** Control can be real without being uniform. Garrisons, appointments, tribute, negotiated passage and military response created layered authority, though its intensity varied.

The Mughal court faced two different mobility problems: maintaining land corridors toward Iran and Central Asia, and negotiating maritime passage where it did not command the sea.

### 1. Trade as an interest, not a complete explanation

Indian merchants maintained communities in Iranian and Central Asian cities. Their activity linked Mughal production to wider markets and supported the movement of horses, textiles and bullion. States benefited from customs, supply and urban activity.

Sources often describe these traders as **Multanis** and also identify Hindu and Jain mercantile communities. Their networks could extend beyond Iran and Central Asia toward Russian markets. These labels and routes demonstrate reach, but traveller estimates of community size are not census returns.

Trade did not automatically make every foreign-policy decision commercial. A ruler might accept losses to commerce for security or prestige. Conversely, a formally hostile relationship did not necessarily stop private exchange.

The analytical rule is:

```text
COMMERCIAL CONNECTION
        influences
route security + customs + diplomatic protection
        but does not alone determine
war + alliance + territorial possession
```

### 2. Merchant communities and diplomacy

Merchants could provide route information, credit and informal communication. Their presence abroad created issues of protection and treatment. Diplomatic missions could discuss safe passage or respond to seizures.

Evidence must remain proportional. A traveller's count of merchants in a city may indicate a substantial community but should not be converted into an exact empire-wide trade total. Merchant presence proves connection, not direct state control of the market.

### 3. Horses and strategic exchange

Quality horses from Central and western Asia were important to elite military culture. This gave overland and maritime imports strategic value. Yet the Mughal army was not wholly dependent on a single route or one supplier. Multiple markets and breeding sources reduced but did not remove vulnerability.

### 4. The Portuguese maritime problem

The Portuguese Estado da India possessed naval strength and attempted to regulate shipping through the **cartaz**, a pass system. Mughal emperors controlled major ports and coastal revenues but lacked a comparable blue-water navy.

Pilgrimage ships travelling toward the Red Sea were especially sensitive. Portuguese interference could provoke pressure on Portuguese settlements, restrictions on trade or negotiation. This was external policy through coastal officials and commercial leverage rather than a sustained Mughal naval campaign.

Full institutional treatment of Portuguese companies, factories and Indian Ocean commerce belongs to the European-trade topic. Here the limited point is that a land empire still required maritime diplomacy when merchants and pilgrims travelled beyond its direct naval protection.

### 5. Ottoman relations

The Ottoman sultan's prestige, control of major western Islamic lands and rivalry with Iran made the Ottoman court relevant. The Mughals did not accept a simple Ottoman claim to universal political superiority. They sought relations compatible with imperial parity.

Ottoman-Safavid conflict could help the Mughals by dividing Persian attention. Shah Jahan's 1636 approach to Murad IV illustrates this. But distance and distinct objectives limited coordination. The Ottomans did not become the operational commanders of Mughal campaigns in Qandahar.

### 6. Pilgrimage, religion and state responsibility

The Hajj gave maritime movement religious significance. Protection of pilgrims affected imperial reputation. Yet piety did not eliminate bargaining over passes, shipping, customs or port power. A religious journey operated through material infrastructures.

When responding to Abdullah Khan's pilgrimage argument, Akbar could point to access opened through Gujarat and maritime routes. This did not remove risks created by Portuguese power; it showed that a ruler could answer a confessional-geopolitical proposal by identifying alternative routes rather than accepting war against Iran.

This is another example of religion and realpolitik interacting rather than cancelling each other:

| Religious concern | Material mechanism |
|---|---|
| safe Hajj passage | ships, ports, convoy timing and negotiation |
| ruler's Islamic legitimacy | protection claims and diplomatic representation |
| relations with Ottoman lands | route access and ceremonial parity |
| conflict with Portuguese coercion | pressure on settlements and commercial bargaining |

### 7. Criticism and reply

**Criticism:** Because the Mughals lacked a strong oceanic navy, they had no maritime foreign policy.

**Reply:** Naval weakness limited coercive options but increased reliance on diplomacy, port regulation and economic pressure. Policy is not identical with naval conquest.

**Criticism:** Trade proves that interstate hostility was superficial.

**Reply:** Commerce often survived rivalry because merchants and states had incentives to continue it. Persistence of trade does not make territorial conflict unreal.

The visual directs the final verdict away from "success or failure" as a single label. Different instruments worked at different scales and times.

### 1. A bounded Aurangzeb coda

Aurangzeb had commanded at Balkh in 1647 and at Qandahar in 1649 and 1652 before becoming emperor. As emperor, he did not reopen Shah Jahan's repeated siege cycle.

In **1664**, Shah Abbas II treated a Mughal envoy harshly and threatened invasion, generating preparation in Punjab and Kabul. The shah died before the threat developed, and the weaker Safavid succession reduced immediate danger. A secondary OCR rendering that places this episode in 1668 is chronologically defective because Shah Abbas II had already died; the corroborated 1664 date is retained.

In **1680**, an Ottoman embassy sought Aurangzeb's support. The event illustrates continuing Mughal recognition in the Asian diplomatic order; it does not prove Ottoman subordination or a new joint war.

These events belong only as a coda. The core narrative of Qandahar recovery ends in 1653, and Aurangzeb's later reign was increasingly dominated by other priorities, especially the Deccan.

### 2. What changed across reigns?

| Reign | Dominant pattern | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Babur-Humayun | creation and recovery of a Kabul-Qandahar base through war and Safavid interaction | Indian conquest changed the centre of gravity |
| Akbar | balance, Kabul consolidation, diplomacy and negotiated Qandahar acquisition | policy responded to changing Uzbek and Safavid power |
| Jahangir | ceremonial cordiality with Iran amid internal court strain | territorial rivalry survived diplomacy |
| Shah Jahan | active recovery, forward defence and repeated siege effort | military reach exceeded sustainable political control at Balkh |
| Aurangzeb | acceptance of a frontier without Qandahar and continued diplomacy | bounded later threats did not restore Shah Jahan's campaign cycle |

This is not a simple movement from strength to decline. Akbar and Shah Jahan each succeeded and failed in different instruments; Aurangzeb's restraint over Qandahar can be interpreted as reprioritisation rather than incapacity alone.

### 3. Historiographical debate I: Timurid obsession

Riazul Islam and other historians have drawn attention to dynastic ambition and the risks of Central Asian adventurism. The Balkh expedition offers evidence: courtly memories could make a trans-Hindu Kush opportunity attractive and elevate prestige costs.

The strongest objection is the campaign's concrete security setting. Nazr Muhammad's appeal, Uzbek concentration and Kabul's exposure made a friendly Balkh strategically intelligible. The failure arose less from having no rational objective than from political mistrust and unsustainable occupation.

**Verdict:** Timurid memory enlarged ambition; it did not by itself cause the expedition.

### 4. Historiographical debate II: the "scientific frontier"

Older accounts describe a north-western "scientific frontier" based on the Hindu Kush with Qandahar as an outer bastion. This highlights geography and strategic depth. Yet "scientific" can falsely suggest one objectively correct, permanently surveyed line.

A frontier is viable only relative to supply, political support and rival capability. The Kabul-Ghazni-Qandahar line could be defensible when the fort was held and supplied. An Oxus line beyond the mountains proved far harder to sustain. After 1653 the empire remained powerful without Qandahar.

### 5. Historiographical debate III: success or failure

```text
SUCCESS EVIDENCE
- Kabul consolidated
- major courts treated the Mughals as a leading power
- Qandahar gained in 1595 and 1638
- Uzbek field forces defeated near Balkh
- trade and diplomacy continued

FAILURE OR LIMIT EVIDENCE
- Qandahar lost in 1622 and finally in 1649
- three recovery campaigns failed
- Balkh occupation proved unsustainable
- logistics imposed high fiscal and human costs
- ceremonial claims often exceeded practical leverage
```

The balanced conclusion is **selective effectiveness**. Mughal policy successfully protected the Indian core and sustained imperial parity, but could not permanently impose an outer frontier against strong regional states and severe geography.

### 6. Structural constraints

Five constraints recur across rulers:

1. **distance:** the resource base lay deep in India;
2. **terrain and season:** mountains, aridity and winter restricted movement;
3. **fortification:** the attacker needed engineering and time, not numbers alone;
4. **political mediation:** buffers required trusted local rulers and chiefs;
5. **multiple commitments:** succession conflict, the Deccan or internal revolt diverted command and supplies.

These constraints did not make policy predetermined. Leadership and timing mattered. Akbar's negotiated acquisition and Ali Mardan Khan's defection show how diplomacy could lower costs; Murad's conduct at Balkh shows how political error could multiply them.

### 7. Evidence hierarchy and language discipline

Use contemporary court chronicles and memoirs for sequence and self-representation, but compare Mughal and rival perspectives. Use modern historians for reconstruction and debate. Use attributed troop figures cautiously. Avoid quotation marks around a phrase unless its wording and source are secure.

The following formulations are safer:

- "The court presented the transfer as..." rather than "all inhabitants accepted..."
- "The source attributes a force of..." rather than "exactly this many served..."
- "The campaign may be read as..." rather than "the sole motive was..."
- "Diplomatic parity was asserted..." rather than "equality was universally acknowledged..."

### 8. Final UPSC argument

A high-scoring answer should move through **geography -> objective -> instrument -> constraint -> differentiated outcome**. Chronology supplies evidence but should not control the whole structure.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question

**Question:** Distinguish diplomacy from subordination in Mughal relations with the Safavid court. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer**

Mughal-Safavid diplomacy used envoys, letters, gifts, titles and receptions to communicate claims and preserve relations. These practices displayed honour, sought concessions, gathered intelligence and could avert immediate confrontation.

They did not automatically establish subordination. A Mughal emperor could receive a lavish Persian embassy while rejecting the political meaning attached to it. Similarly, the Safavid court could exchange courteous letters while maintaining its claim to Qandahar. Khan Alam's mission during Jahangir's reign exemplified sustained communication, yet Shah Abbas captured the fort in 1622. Ali Mardan Khan's transfer of Qandahar in 1638 resulted from defection and Mughal inducement, not from ceremonial recognition alone.

Subordination requires evidence such as tribute, accepted command, constrained appointment or enforceable territorial authority. Diplomatic etiquette instead records a negotiated representation of rank. It must be tested against possession, military action and subsequent conduct. Thus ceremony was important but cannot be read as a map of sovereignty.

**Scoring note:** Reward an operational test for subordination and at least one event showing that cordial diplomacy coexisted with rivalry.

## Original 15-mark question

**Question:** Analyse the interaction of ideology and realpolitik in Akbar's and Shah Jahan's north-western policies. Answer in 200 words.

**Model answer**

Akbar's policy toward Abdullah Khan Uzbek joined ethical language with strategic calculation. When Abdullah proposed cooperation against Safavid Iran in 1577, Akbar refused to make sectarian difference a sufficient ground for conquest. This position suited his widening imperial ethic. It also prevented the Uzbeks from becoming overwhelmingly powerful by destroying Iran as a counterweight. Akbar meanwhile annexed Kabul in 1585, supervised the frontier and acquired Qandahar through political negotiation in 1595.

Shah Jahan employed a more active Timurid and imperial idiom. He explored coordinated pressure on Iran with Ottoman Murad IV in 1636 and accepted Ali Mardan Khan's transfer of Qandahar in 1638. His Balkh intervention sought a friendly ruler beyond the Hindu Kush, a defensible buffer objective shaped by Timurid prestige. Yet the demand for Nazr Muhammad's humiliating submission and the willingness to occupy Balkh enlarged political costs.

In both reigns ideology defined honour, legitimacy and acceptable presentation, while realpolitik addressed rival power, buffers and routes. Neither category operated alone. Akbar's moral refusal was strategically useful; Shah Jahan's security objective was intensified by dynastic status. Outcomes depended on whether symbolism remained aligned with logistical and political feasibility.

**Scoring note:** Reward a paired comparison in which each ruler contains both ideological and strategic elements; avoid an Akbar-idealism versus Shah Jahan-realism caricature.

## Original 20-mark question

**Question:** Assess the importance of geography, logistics and political mediation in shaping Mughal relations with Central Asia and Iran. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer**

Geography gave Mughal external policy its basic structure. Kabul guarded approaches to India; Qandahar was a fortified oasis connecting Kabul, Herat, Baluchistan and the Indus; Balkh and Badakhshan lay beyond the Hindu Kush. These locations offered strategic depth and commercial access but imposed sharply different costs.

Logistics determined whether political aims could endure. Qandahar required garrisoning and, during recovery campaigns, the transport of food, fodder, ammunition and heavy guns from the Punjab. In 1649-53 strong Safavid defence, limited campaigning time and the difficulty of achieving a breach or starvation defeated large Mughal armies. Balkh was still harder. Although Mughal forces occupied it and defeated Uzbeks in 1647, scarce supplies, approaching winter and harassment made withdrawal necessary.

Political mediation was equally decisive. Akbar obtained Qandahar in 1595 through negotiation; Shah Jahan recovered it in 1638 through Ali Mardan Khan's defection. By contrast, Murad's premature entry into Balkh destroyed Nazr Muhammad's trust and turned planned client restoration into hostile occupation. On the imperial frontier, passage also depended on bargaining with local chiefs.

Thus military resources alone did not determine outcomes. Geography set transport and seasonal constraints; logistics converted distance into cost; and intermediaries translated force into cooperation. Mughal policy succeeded most where it aligned all three, and failed where territorial ambition outran supply and political consent.

**Scoring note:** Reward causal interaction among the three terms, using both Balkh and Qandahar rather than separate descriptive paragraphs.

**Faulty thought:** Because Jahangir and Shah Abbas exchanged lavish embassies, Qandahar was secure.

**Faulty thought:** Qandahar was only a trade centre, only a fort or only a prestige symbol.

## 1. Definition and scope

- Mughal foreign policy is analytical shorthand for court-directed relations with external powers.

- His second attempt failed in 1652.
- Dara Shukoh's elaborate 1653 siege also failed.
- Strong fortification and determined defence mattered.
- Heavy guns were difficult to transport and did not create a decisive breach.
- Long supply lines, fodder and weather favoured the defender.
- These failures do not prove universal Mughal artillery inferiority.
- Qandahar retained value, but repeated effort became increasingly costly.

## 12. Frontier society and administration

- The frontier was an inhabited corridor.
- Fortified nodes did not create uniform control of every valley.
- Local chiefs could supply passage, intelligence and resistance.
- Roshani and Yusufzai contexts had specific causes and should not be flattened.
- Mughal policy combined negotiation, rank, payment, garrisoning and punishment.
- Local conflict and external rivalry interacted without being identical.
- Intelligence required comparison across governors, envoys, merchants and scouts.

## 13. Commerce, maritime power and Ottomans

- Indian merchants operated in Iranian and Central Asian markets.
- Sources identify Multani, Hindu and Jain merchant communities and links extending toward Russia.
- Caravan commerce added value to Kabul and Qandahar routes.
- Trade volumes remain difficult to quantify exactly.
- Horse imports had strategic importance but used multiple routes.
- Portuguese naval coercion and the cartaz affected shipping.
- Mughal naval limits encouraged negotiation and coastal economic pressure.
- Hajj protection connected piety, legitimacy and material shipping.
- Akbar cited Gujarat and maritime access when answering Abdullah Khan's pilgrimage argument.
- Ottoman-Safavid rivalry affected Mughal calculations.
- Mughal rulers sought parity rather than Ottoman political subordination.

## 14. Aurangzeb coda

- Aurangzeb did not restart the Qandahar sieges after becoming emperor.
- Shah Abbas II insulted a Mughal envoy and threatened invasion in 1664, but died before action.
- An Ottoman embassy sought Aurangzeb's support in 1680.
- These events demonstrate continuing diplomacy, not recovery of Qandahar.

## 15. Historiographical verdicts

- **Nostalgia thesis:** Timurid memory enlarged ambition and prestige.
- **Security reply:** buffers beyond Kabul addressed real Uzbek pressure.
- **Balanced view:** security shaped the objective; memory influenced scale and representation.
- **Scientific-frontier thesis:** Hindu Kush and Qandahar offered strategic depth.
- **Qualification:** no frontier was scientifically inevitable or legally fixed.
- **Artillery-decline thesis:** Qandahar failures reveal weakness.
- **Qualification:** performance depended on transport, fort, engineering, supplies and time.
- **Overall verdict:** selective effectiveness in protecting the Indian core, limited durability in outer zones.

## 16. Twenty-second answer spine

> **Geography -> objective -> instrument -> constraint -> differentiated outcome**

Example: Qandahar's route and fort value -> protect Kabul and status -> diplomacy or siege -> long supply and strong defence -> temporary acquisition but no recovery after 1649.

## 17. Final traps

1. Qandahar is not Gandhara.
2. Kabul's incorporation in 1585 is not Qandahar's acquisition in 1595.
3. Abdullah's Balkh expansion occurred in stages.
4. Diplomatic courtesy does not prove abandonment of claims.
5. Ali Mardan Khan, not a misread OCR name, transferred Qandahar in 1638.
6. Balkh field victory did not create durable occupation.
7. Aurangzeb led the 1649 and 1652 attempts; Dara led 1653.
8. Failed sieges do not prove universal artillery weakness.
9. Trade is one cause, not the whole explanation.
10. The 2020 Persian-sources question is adjacent, not directly owned.
