*The visual separates a military opening from the slower work of making authority survive rulers and crises.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Winning forts is like acquiring scattered workshops; building a state is making them send resources, obey appointments and continue operating after the commander dies. In 1206 the conquests existed, but the command system that tied them to Muizz al-Din had broken.

### 2. Terminology before narrative

- **FACT:** *Mamluk* means an owned person, especially here a purchased and trained military slave. Training, patronal incorporation, promotion and manumission could produce an elite commander.
- **FACT:** “Slave dynasty” is a later historical shorthand, not the rulers’ official self-name.
- **FACT:** The ruling sequence was not one uninterrupted biological family. Slavery, marriage, household ties, military reputation, noble choice and force all shaped succession.
- **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE:** The institution joined coercion with mobility. Calling it either ordinary domestic servitude or a pure meritocracy removes half the mechanism.

### 3. The governing mechanism

1. A commander inherits forts and armies but loses imperial backing.
2. He must secure revenue, followers and recognition inside India.
3. His successor must defeat rivals and make Delhi a durable centre.
4. Noble households then test whether the Sultan is master or merely first among peers.
5. Mongol pressure, distant provinces and local chiefs set limits on central authority.

### 4. Three boundaries that prevent bad answers

| Boundary | Correct treatment | Common collapse |
|---|---|---|
| Topic 02 → Topic 03 | Ghurid conquest ends at 1206; independent consolidation begins | Giving Aibak’s post-1206 rule to the invasion topic |
| Aibak → Iltutmish | Aibak begins; Iltutmish consolidates | Erasing Aibak or calling his brief reign complete consolidation |
| Topic 03 → Topic 04 | End with Qaiqabad and 1290 transition | Importing Alauddin’s markets, doab programme or Deccan expansion |

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

- **Strongest objection:** If the Turks already possessed Delhi, Ajmer and Ganga-plain positions, the Sultanate was complete in 1206.
- **Reply:** Possession did not settle succession, recurring revenue, rival commanders, Bengal, Punjab or Mongol defence. Those survival problems occupied the century.
- **Balanced verdict:** The Mamluk achievement was a reproducible Delhi-centred military-fiscal monarchy, not a modern uniformly administered territorial state.

### 6. UPSC integration

Prelims can test *mamluk*, manumission and the ruler sequence. A Mains answer should frame the century as a three-way tension among the Sultan, elite military households and external/provincial pressure.

### 7. Revision notes

1. Mamluk status combined ownership, training and patronal service.
2. Manumission changed legal status and enabled sovereign claims.
3. “Slave dynasty” is retrospective shorthand.
4. Aibak began independent rule after 1206.
5. Iltutmish was the principal consolidator.
6. Raziya exposed the gendered politics of noble consent.
7. Chihalgani was a fluid Shamsi elite, not a parliament.
8. Balban exalted monarchy and defended the core.
9. Authority was strongest around Delhi and the upper doab.
10. The dynasty ended in 1290; the Delhi Sultanate did not.

### Lesson-local Mains question and model

**Question:** Why should 1206 be treated as the opening of state formation rather than the completion of the Delhi Sultanate?

**Demand:** 10 marks · 150 words

**Model answer:** Muizz al-Din’s death in 1206 ended the command link connecting Indian conquests to the Ghurid centre. Aibak preserved an autonomous Lahore-Delhi base, but rival commanders, disputed succession and uneven revenue meant that conquest had not yet become a self-reproducing state. Iltutmish defeated Aram Shah, Yildiz and Qubacha, managed Bengal and Rajput frontiers, avoided a disastrous Mongol confrontation, adapted iqta assignments, issued authoritative coinage and made Delhi the principal centre. Yet the post-1236 crisis showed that noble coalitions could still create and depose rulers. Balban later strengthened kingship, intelligence, army discipline, routes and frontier defence. Thus 1206 marks independence, not completion. The thirteenth century converted delegated conquest into a Delhi-centred military-fiscal monarchy whose reach remained strongest in a core and conditional at distant frontiers.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers the chronological distinction directly, uses named rulers and institutions as evidence, and closes with a qualified state-formation verdict.

### Visual first — Triangulation

```text
court chronicle -> events, ideals, prejudice
coin             -> name, title, mint, monetary claim
inscription      -> dated act, patronage, formula
monument         -> fabric, phase, reuse, technique
regional record  -> reach, resistance, silence
          \________ compare before verdict ________/
```

*Each source answers a different question; no single witness supplies the entire state.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

A court chronicler is an informed insider, not a hidden camera. A coin is a public claim, not a census. A monument records material action, but stone cannot state every motive. Historical method begins by asking what a source can and cannot prove.

### 2. Named source positions

- **FACT:** Hasan Nizami’s *Taj al-Ma’asir* is useful for Aibak-era conquest and legitimacy, but its ornate panegyric selects and magnifies victory.
- **FACT:** Minhaj-i Siraj Juzjani’s *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* is central for Iltutmish, Raziya, nobles and succession. His Delhi-court and clerical position shapes the narrative.
- **FACT:** Barani wrote later. His account helps recover aristocratic memory of the Chihalgani and Balban, but chronological distance and high-born bias require checking.
- **FACT:** Coins can establish names, titles, mints and circulation. An isolated find does not map a uniformly administered province.
- **FACT:** Inscriptions and architecture preserve dated patronage, reuse and craft, while formula, damage and repair complicate interpretation.

### 3. Method step by step

1. Identify genre: panegyric, dynastic history, political advice, inscription or object.
2. Locate the author or patron in a relationship of power.
3. Extract the narrow proposition the source directly supports.
4. Compare it with independent textual or material evidence.
5. State the silence: peasants, women, local languages or actual implementation may be missing.

### 4. Comparison

| Evidence | Strongest safe use | Unsafe leap |
|---|---|---|
| Chronicle | Campaign sequence, elite attitudes and office politics | Literal acceptance of praise, speeches or numbers |
| Coin | Titulature, mint and monetary claim | Exact empire boundary or universal cash use |
| Monument | Construction phase, reuse and technique | One exhaustive political or religious motive |

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

- **Strongest objection:** Bias makes Persian chronicles unreliable.
- **Reply:** Bias is itself evidence of court ideals, hierarchy and prejudice. The error is neither use nor rejection, but uncritical use.
- **Balanced verdict:** Positioned testimony becomes strong history through genre awareness and corroboration.

### 6. UPSC integration

Every Mains source paragraph should use **claim → named source → value → limit**. The 2020 Persian literary-sources PYQ belongs to Topic 24, but Topic 03 supplies early Sultanate examples.

### 7. Revision notes

1. Hasan Nizami wrote *Taj al-Ma’asir*.
2. Minhaj-i Siraj wrote *Tabaqat-i Nasiri*.
3. Barani is later than the Mamluk events he narrates.
4. Panegyric preserves evidence but magnifies patrons.
5. Normative advice is not a neutral manual of actual practice.
6. Coinage proves a public monetary and sovereignty claim.
7. A coin find is not an empire map.
8. Inscriptions are dated but formulaic and unevenly preserved.
9. Architecture needs phased attribution and contextual interpretation.
10. Triangulation is the governing source method.

### Lesson-local Mains question and model

**Question:** How should Persian chronicles be used to reconstruct Mamluk politics without reproducing their silences?

**Demand:** 15 marks · 250 words

**Model answer:** Persian chronicles are indispensable because they preserve names, offices, campaigns, succession conflicts and the political vocabulary of the Mamluk court. Hasan Nizami’s *Taj al-Ma’asir* illuminates Aibak-era conquest and legitimation, but its panegyric genre can magnify victory and moral purpose. Minhaj-i Siraj’s *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* supplies a near-contemporary narrative of Iltutmish and Raziya. His praise of Raziya’s capacity followed by a gendered qualification records both political evaluation and patriarchal clerical assumptions. Barani’s later account preserves aristocratic memory of the Chihalgani and Balban, including idealised stories of royal justice, but chronological distance and his high-born programme shape selection.

A source-critical reconstruction therefore separates event, reported speech and normative judgement. Claims about titles and sovereignty should be checked against coins and inscriptions; claims about patronage and conquest against monuments, construction phases and reuse; claims about territorial control against repeated rebellions and regional evidence. Silence is also evidence: cultivators, artisans, enslaved people, women outside court and vernacular communities rarely speak directly.

Chronicles thus reveal the “spirit of the age” most clearly at the Persianate political apex. They become evidence for a wider society only through genre analysis, corroboration and explicit attention to what the courtly archive excludes.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies three named chroniclers/texts, distinguishes report from ideology, supplies a triangulation method, identifies social silences and gives a calibrated conclusion.

### Visual first — The mamluk career and its political afterlife

```text
purchase/capture -> training -> household incorporation -> command/iqta
       |                                                   |
       +--------------- coercive dependence ---------------+
                                                           v
                                           manumission + elite office
                                                           |
                                          followers and household of his own
```

*The same institution that created loyal officers could create powerful rivals after a patron died.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

A ruler wanted trained commanders who did not begin with an independent hereditary base. A purchased youth could be educated with the ruler’s household and rewarded for service. But success gave him troops, revenue and clients. Loyalty to one patron did not automatically become loyalty to an impersonal state.

### 2. Doctrine and mechanism

- **FACT:** Elite mamluks were trained for military and administrative service, not confined to domestic labour.
- **FACT:** Aibak received manumission because an unfreed slave could not legally be sovereign.
- **FACT:** Iltutmish had been Aibak’s slave and son-in-law; he later developed his own Shamsi corps.
- **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE:** Military slavery helped rulers bypass older aristocracies, but personalised authority in household bonds.

1. Patron invests training, office and revenue in a dependent outsider.
2. The officer gains reputation, troops and administrative experience.
3. Manumission changes legal status without erasing patronal identity.
4. The officer reproduces the system by acquiring clients and mamluks.
5. After the patron’s death, former dependants compete as armed peers.

### 3. Comparison

| Model | Basis of advancement | Political implication |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic-slave stereotype | Permanent menial dependence | Cannot explain Aibak, Iltutmish or Balban |
| Military-mamluk career | Patronage, training, service and manumission | Produces capability and household loyalty |
| Hereditary aristocrat | Lineage and inherited local base | Possesses a different source of autonomous power |

### 4. Strongest objection and reply

- **Strongest objection:** Upward mobility makes the institution meritocratic and therefore benign.
- **Reply:** Purchase and ownership remained coercive. Mobility explains political function; it does not cancel unfreedom.
- **Balanced verdict:** Military slavery was a coercive recruitment system with exceptional elite mobility and a built-in succession risk.

### 5. UPSC integration

Use the institution to explain both rapid state-building and factionalism. Do not write that “slaves ruled while still legally enslaved,” or that mamluks were simply domestic servants.

### 6. Revision notes

1. Mamluk service was specialised military-political training.
2. Patronal households could substitute for inherited aristocratic bases.
3. Manumission was legally important for sovereignty.
4. Aibak’s legal freedom supported his independent claim.
5. Iltutmish linked slavery, marriage and kingship.
6. Shamsi identity referred to Iltutmish’s household.
7. Officers could acquire iqtas and armed followings.
8. Personal loyalty was strongest while the patron lived.
9. Former dependants could become king-makers or rulers.
10. Coercion and mobility must remain in the same analysis.

### Lesson-local Mains question and model

**Question:** Analyse military slavery as both a resource and a structural risk for the early Delhi Sultanate.

**Demand:** 10 marks · 150 words

**Model answer:** Military slavery supplied rulers with trained officers whose careers initially depended on the patronal household rather than on an independent hereditary base. Education, conversion or socialisation, military command and iqta assignments produced skilled generals and administrators. Aibak and Iltutmish themselves demonstrate extraordinary mobility, while manumission allowed a successful mamluk to claim sovereignty. The same mechanism personalised authority. Officers accumulated troops, revenue and clients, and after a patron’s death they competed over offices and succession. Iltutmish’s Shamsi officers therefore strengthened the state during his reign but became decisive actors in later instability. Military slavery was neither ordinary domestic bondage nor a pure meritocracy. It was a coercive institution that created a capable new elite while making the political order dependent on a ruler’s ability to discipline the households he had empowered.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the institution, uses Aibak, Iltutmish and the Shamsi officers as named evidence, and balances administrative capacity against coercion and factional risk.

### Visual first — No referee after Muizz al-Din

```text
Muizz al-Din dies, 1206
          |
Ghurid command fragments
          |
 +--------+----------------+----------------+
 |                         |                |
Yildiz                  Qubacha           Aibak
Ghazni                  Multan-Uch        Lahore-Delhi
superior claim          Punjab-Sindh      Indian conquests
```

*Delegated jurisdictions became competing sovereignty claims when the common master disappeared.*

### 1. Plain-language intuition

Three senior commanders could each say: “I preserved part of the empire, so why should I obey the others?” Marriage connected households, but geography and armed resources made rivalry unavoidable.

### 2. What changed in 1206

- **FACT:** Yildiz succeeded at Ghazni and claimed a wider inheritance of Muizz al-Din’s conquests.
- **FACT:** Qubacha controlled Multan and Uch and competed for Lahore and Punjab.
- **FACT:** Aibak relied on Lahore-Delhi, Indian notables and military reputation.
- **FACT:** Marriage alliances existed among these houses but did not settle hierarchy.
- **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE:** The break with Ghazni removed reinforcement and legal certainty, yet reduced Delhi’s entanglement in Central Asian struggles.

### 3. Causal mechanism

1. Imperial death ends a recognised superior command.
2. Territorial commanders convert office into independent political claims.
3. Strategic nodes—Ghazni, Lahore, Multan-Uch and Delhi—anchor rival coalitions.
4. Each claimant seeks insignia, freedom, marriage and military recognition.
5. Indian resources become more important because Ghurid backing is unavailable.

### 4. Comparison

| Path | Advantage | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Continued subordination to Ghazni | Prestige and possible reinforcement | External control and Central Asian entanglement |
| Indian-centred autonomy | Independent institutional development | Loss of backing and immediate rivalry |

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

- **Strongest objection:** Fragmentation was only decline.
- **Reply:** It endangered every conquest, but also compelled rulers to finance armies and govern from Indian bases.
- **Balanced verdict:** The rupture was destructive and generative: it removed an empire and made a separate Sultanate possible.

### 6. UPSC integration

Prelims often match commanders with bases. Mains should explain why external disconnection forced institutional adaptation rather than narrating 1206 as a date alone.

### 7. Revision notes

1. Muizz al-Din died in 1206.
2. Yildiz’s base was Ghazni.
3. Qubacha’s core was Multan-Uch.
4. Aibak’s early royal centre was Lahore.
5. Delhi remained strategically indispensable.
6. Successor commanders were not a stable triumvirate.
7. Marriage did not remove territorial competition.
8. The Ghurid military safety net disappeared.
9. Indian fiscal resources became central.
10. Independent state formation was compelled by fragmentation.

### Lesson-local Mains question and model

**Question:** Why did the fragmentation of Ghurid authority create both crisis and opportunity for Delhi?

**Demand:** 10 marks · 150 words

**Model answer:** Muizz al-Din’s death in 1206 removed the command hierarchy linking Indian conquests to Ghur and Ghazni. Yildiz at Ghazni, Qubacha at Multan-Uch and Aibak in the Lahore-Delhi sphere became rival claimants. The crisis was immediate: reinforcement disappeared, Punjab was contested and marriage alliances could not decide sovereignty. Yet fragmentation also freed the Indian territories from routine subordination to Ghazni. Aibak could preserve an autonomous base, while Iltutmish later defeated rival successor claims rather than serving them. The rulers increasingly had to finance troops, assign revenue and negotiate with towns, chiefs and officials from resources inside India. The Ghurid rupture therefore endangered the conquests but also compelled the institutional adaptation through which a Delhi-centred state emerged. It was both imperial disintegration and the precondition of independent Sultanate formation.

**Why this earns marks:** It develops both sides of the question through named successor-commanders, explains the fiscal-institutional consequence, and ends with a balanced causal judgement.

**Question:** Critically evaluate Balban’s theory and performance of kingship.

**Demand:** 15 marks · 250 words

Prince Muhammad’s death damaged succession; Qaiqabad’s incapacity reopened faction, and Jalaluddin Khalji took power in 1290.

The Mamluk house failed to institutionalise heredity, but Delhi, fiscal-military offices, coinage, Persianate political language and territorial ambition survived. Its state-building achievement was durable, coercive and uneven.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** Distinguish military slavery from ordinary hereditary slavery in the context of the Mamluk rulers. (Answer in 150 words) 10

**Model answer**

**Thesis:** “Mamluk” means an owned person, but elite military slavery was a specialised system of recruitment, training and patronage, not a synonym for hereditary servitude.

- **Claim → evidence → analysis → qualification:** Aibak entered Muizz al-Din’s household as a purchased military slave and rose through command. Training created skilled officers without an inherited local aristocratic base, but the relationship began coercively.
- **Claim → evidence → analysis → qualification:** Manumission was normally required before sovereign rule; Aibak’s freedom and insignia therefore mattered. Legal freedom did not erase patronal networks.
- **Claim → evidence → analysis → qualification:** Iltutmish and Balban likewise rose from mamluk service to office and kingship. Their exceptional mobility explains the institution’s political value, not the experience of ordinary domestic, labouring or female slaves.

**Verdict:** “Slave dynasty” is qualified shorthand for rulers of servile origin who governed as manumitted sovereigns through military households and wider institutions.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the term, distinguishes legal status from social origin, uses three named rulers and preserves both mobility and coercion.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** Iltutmish was not merely Aibak’s successor; he was the principal consolidator of an independent Delhi Sultanate. Analyse. (Answer in 250 words) 15

**Model answer**

**Thesis:** Aibak preserved an autonomous Indian centre after 1206; Iltutmish converted that vulnerable inheritance into a defensible, Delhi-centred political order.

- **Sovereign survival:** He defeated Aram Shah’s claim and Taj al-Din Yildiz at Tarain in 1216. These victories secured the capital and rejected Ghazni’s asserted seniority, although elite opposition did not disappear.
- **North-western consolidation:** He waited until Qubacha had weakened and captured Uch in 1228, extending authority over Multan-Sindh. The achievement removed a rival successor state, but later Punjab control remained exposed.
- **Strategic restraint:** In 1221 he avoided an alliance with Jalal al-Din Mangburnu while Chinggis Khan stood near the Indus. This preserved a fragile state; it was prudent non-entanglement, not a military victory.
- **Graded territorial rule:** Repeated campaigns reasserted suzerainty in Bengal, and pressure recovered Bayana and Gwalior. Bengal’s recurrent autonomy and failures toward Gujarat-Malwa show that campaign reach exceeded routine administration.
- **Institutional integration:** Iqta assignments linked revenue to service; silver *tanka* and copper *jital* strengthened monetary and titulary authority; Delhi’s court, Hauz Shamsi and other patronage made the capital visible. The 1229 Abbasid investiture ratified these gains rather than creating them.
- **Qualification:** His nomination of Raziya did not settle succession, and distant provinces remained negotiated.

**Verdict:** Iltutmish was the principal consolidator because he joined military security, fiscal practice, currency, capital formation and legitimacy, while never producing a finished unitary state.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “analyse” through six dimensions, uses named dates and places, and qualifies every success with its territorial or institutional limit.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** The political history of the Mamluk Sultanate was a struggle to make delegated power serve the crown without becoming an alternative to it. Discuss. (Answer in 250 words) 20

**Model answer**

**Thesis:** Delegation was the operating principle and structural danger of the Mamluk Sultanate. The crown needed armed and fiscal agents, yet each successful agent could acquire the resources of an alternative centre.

- **Military households:** Aibak rose from Muizz al-Din’s household; Iltutmish later defeated successor-commanders Yildiz and Qubacha. Training produced capable service, but command, marriage and followers could convert dependants into rivals.
- **Iqta and provinces:** The iqta linked revenue to military-administrative service without granting private ownership. It financed expansion, yet Bengal’s governors used troops, revenue and distance to assert autonomy.
- **Shamsi elite:** Leading households helped enthrone or depose Ruknuddin, Raziya, Bahram and Masud. Barani’s “Chihalgani” denotes a fluid network; common privilege did not prevent rivalry.
- **Deputy power:** Balban accumulated office, clients and military reputation under Nasiruddin. His removal in 1253 and restoration in 1255 prove that a powerful *naib* remained coalition-dependent.
- **Centralising answer:** As Sultan, Balban used *zill-i ilahi*, barids and the *diwan-i-arz* to direct honour, information and military readiness. The old-soldier assignment inquiry nevertheless forced partial retreat.
- **Persistent limit:** Tughril’s rebellion, Bughra Khan’s autonomy and Prince Muhammad’s death show that reconquest and frontier defence did not institutionalise provincial obedience or succession.

**Verdict:** Mamluk state formation made delegation conditional through surveillance, transfer, punishment and bargaining. The Sultanate became durable and centralising, but remained layered, personal and uneven.

**Why this earns marks:** It follows the argument across military, fiscal, succession, deputy, administrative and provincial evidence, then gives a qualified judgement rather than a ruler-list.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Governing thesis

The Mamluk phase, 1206-90, was the period in which a Ghurid military zone became a Delhi-centred Sultanate. Its rulers converted conquest into a durable political framework through military households, revenue assignments, currency, cities, court ideology, intelligence and coercion. The result was centralising but not uniformly centralised: succession, provincial distance, noble coalitions and frontier pressure repeatedly exposed its limits.

## 2. Terminology: Mamluk and military slavery

- *Mamluk* literally refers to an owned person; *ghulam* could have a wider semantic range.
- Elite military slaves were purchased or captured young, trained in warfare, administration and court culture, and inserted into a patron’s household.
- Manumission was often the bridge from servile origin to lawful command and sovereignty.
- Patron-client and fellow-client solidarity could survive legal freedom.
- Aibak, Iltutmish and Balban demonstrate narrow elite mobility, not a general emancipation system.
- The label “Slave dynasty” is usable only with qualification: major rulers were former slaves, not enslaved sovereigns.
- Military slavery remained coercive and coexisted with domestic, labouring and gendered forms of unfreedom.

## 3. Aibak and the 1206 opening

- Topic 02 ends with the Ghurid conquest opening and Muhammad Ghuri’s death in 1206.
- Topic 03 begins with independent Sultanate formation.
- Aibak used his command network and north Indian base to establish an autonomous centre.
- Lahore and Delhi both mattered; Delhi’s later primacy should not be projected backwards too simply.
- His reign was short, 1206-10, and therefore foundational but not fully consolidating.
- Patronage at the Quwwat-ul-Islam complex, Qutb Minar and Ajmer made authority spatially visible.
- His death reopened the difference between military possession and accepted succession.

## 4. Aram Shah and uncertainty

- Aram Shah’s relation to Aibak and the exact mechanism of his elevation remain uncertain.
- Avoid categorical claims that he was certainly Aibak’s son or a securely chosen heir.
- Delhi elites invited Iltutmish, then governor at Badaun and Aibak’s former slave and son-in-law.
- Iltutmish defeated Aram Shah’s claim around 1211.
- The episode shows that succession depended on military capacity, elite recognition and control of strategic centres rather than fixed primogeniture.

## 5. Iltutmish and rival successor-commanders

- Iltutmish did not inherit uncontested sovereignty; he constructed it.
- Taj al-Din Yildiz at Ghazni claimed seniority within the Ghurid successor world.
- Iltutmish defeated Yildiz at Tarain in 1216, weakening Ghazni’s claim over Delhi.
- Nasiruddin Qubacha controlled Uch, Multan and Sind; his eventual defeat removed another rival centre.
- These victories gave Delhi external independence within the former Ghurid political field.
- Consolidation was military, political and institutional, not a single coronation event.

## 6. Bengal and Rajput frontiers under Iltutmish

- Bengal repeatedly moved between nominal submission and practical autonomy.
- Campaigns and appointments could restore recognition without eliminating distance, local military resources or recurrent rebellion.
- Rajput forts and chiefs were pressured, defeated or made tributary in particular zones; control was not uniform.
- A victory list is less useful than the mechanism: campaign -> submission/appointment -> withdrawal -> renewed local leverage.
- Frontier authority was layered, episodic and negotiated.

## 7. The 1221 Mongol-Mangburnu crisis

- Chengiz Khan pursued Jalal al-Din Mangburnu to the Indus in 1221 during Iltutmish’s reign.
- Iltutmish avoided binding Delhi to the fugitive Khwarazm prince.
- Restraint reduced the risk of direct entanglement with a vastly powerful Mongol force.
- This was strategic non-entanglement, not proof that Mongol pressure ended.
- The crisis introduced a geopolitical frontier problem that persisted through later reigns.
- In PYQ chronology, it refutes the statement that the first Mongol pressure began only under Jalaluddin Khalji.

## 8. Delhi, iqta, tanka-jital and investiture

- Delhi became the durable political centre through court, army, mint, communication and patronage.
- An *iqta* was principally an assignment of revenue from a territory in return for service; it was not identical to private ownership.
- A *muqti* administered the assignment, maintained forces and owed obligations to the centre.
- Transferability and accounting aimed to prevent complete local appropriation, though actual control varied.
- Silver *tanka* and copper *jital* expressed and facilitated sovereignty; caution is required against overprecise universal ratios.
- Abbasid investiture in 1229 enlarged Iltutmish’s symbolic legitimacy.
- Investiture ratified achievement; it did not cause Tarain, conquer Bengal or substitute for revenue and force.

## 9. Succession after Iltutmish

- Iltutmish’s judgement that Raziya was capable did not create a settled succession rule.
- Ruknuddin Firuz and Shah Turkan initially controlled the throne and household access.
- Their rapid fall arose from court conflict, provincial resentment and mobilisation in Delhi.
- The episode confirms that palace, army, city and provincial elites all mattered.
- Succession was not simply hereditary or elective; it was coalition selection backed by force.

## 10. Raziya: chronology, capability, gender and source criticism

- Raziya ruled from 1236.
- Use the standard chronology 1236-40.
- She adopted visible sovereign conduct and appointed officials on perceived service or loyalty.
- Jamal al-Din Yaqut, an Abyssinian/Habshi *amir-i akhur*, became politically important.
- His rise disturbed access and rank among entrenched Turkish households.
- Later romantic narratives about Raziya and Yaqut are not secure causal evidence.
- Gender mattered because it shaped acceptable royal behaviour and chroniclers’ judgements.
- Gender did not operate alone: appointments, patronage, provincial revolt, faction and military defeat were also causal.
- Best verdict: a capable sovereign constrained by gendered legitimacy within a competitive military-household order.

## 11. Chihalgani and the post-Raziya rulers

- *Chihalgani* or *Turkan-i Chihalgani* should not be imagined as a statutory council of exactly forty permanent members.
- It is safer to describe a fluid network of leading Turkish slave-origin nobles and households associated with Iltutmish.
- Muizuddin Bahram Shah and Alauddin Masud Shah depended on shifting coalitions and were removed when alignments changed.
- Nobles did not act as one timeless bloc; rivalries and interests changed.
- The crown remained valuable because factions competed to control recognised kingship rather than abolish it.

## 12. Nasiruddin Mahmud and Balban as naib

- Nasiruddin Mahmud reigned from 1246 to 1266.
- Balban gained the naibate, royal marriage connection, clients, offices and military prestige.
- His opponents among Turkish and Tajik nobles removed him in 1253.
- Imaduddin Raihan, a Hindustani eunuch, and wazir Junaidi participated in the replacement alignment.
- Balban rebuilt his coalition and returned in 1255.
- His removal is decisive evidence: dominant executive power remained reversible and dependent on formal kingship and elite support.
- Avoid the simplified picture of Nasiruddin as an irrelevant Quran-copying puppet.

## 13. Balban’s kingship

- Balban became Sultan in 1266.
- He claimed descent from Afrasiyab, the legendary Iranian hero-king; treat this as political genealogy used to legitimate rule, not verified ancestry.
- He invoked *zill-i ilahi*, the “shadow of God,” as an exalted royal metaphor, not literal divinity.
- *Sijda* was prostration; *paibos* was kissing the sovereign’s feet.
- Solemn audience, restricted access, magnificent dress and armed procession enacted hierarchy.
- Ritual had Persianate antecedents and attracted theological objection.
- Ceremony was political technology: it taught that the Sultan stood above noble households.
- It remained ineffective without information, revenue, military force and punishment.
- Balban’s preference for high-born privilege narrowed recruitment and sat uneasily beside the actual diversity of capable servants.

## 14. Barids, justice and army

- *Barids* reported provincial and noble conduct to the centre.
- Intelligence reduced the ruler’s information disadvantage but could fail, distort or be ignored.
- Balban advertised severe and impartial justice. Chronicles report punishment of Malik Bakbak of Badaun and Malik Haibat of Awadh after killings of servants.
- These exemplary cases demonstrate royal intention and political theatre, not uniform justice everywhere.
- Balban reorganised the *diwan-i-arz*, the military department; its head Imad-ul-Mulk supervised army organisation and horse inspection.
- An inquiry found old Turkish soldiers retaining fertile doab villages assigned in lieu of salary after their service capacity declined.
- Balban’s proposed pensioning and reassignment met resistance and intercession, forcing partial retreat and exposing the limits of reform.
- Cash payment existed for part of the army; do not universalise it.
- The governing circuit was information -> judgement -> force -> continuing surveillance.

## 15. Mewat, doab and Katehar

- Armed communities and chiefs challenged roads, revenue and communications near the core.
- Balban’s campaigns used forest clearance, forts, garrisons, strategic Afghan settlement and exemplary punishment.
- “Law and order” describes the state’s objective but hides costs to resisting populations.
- Routes may have become safer for merchants, troops and revenue while communities experienced dispossession, death or enslavement.
- Avoid ethnic essentialism: the political issue was armed resistance in strategic agrarian and communication zones.

## 16. Mongol frontier and Prince Muhammad

- Lahore, Dipalpur and Multan formed a defensive belt rather than one wall.
- Sher Khan had earlier frontier importance but later attracted Balban’s suspicion.
- Fort repair, commanders, forces and royal sons sustained layered defence.
- Sources report diplomatic feelers to Hulegu/Halaku and an honoured Mongol embassy alongside military preparation.
- A tacit accommodation may have reduced escalation while leaving western Punjab under Mongol influence; the frontier remained undefined.
- Prince Muhammad, Balban’s preferred heir, died near Multan in 1285-86.
- Delhi remained unconquered, but raids and severe losses continued.
- Best judgement: costly or qualified containment, neither perfect exclusion nor total failure.
- The heir’s death transformed frontier warfare into a succession crisis.

## 17. Tughril and Bengal

- Tughril governed from Lakhnauti and rebelled around 1279.
- Provincial command gave him troops, revenue and local prestige.
- Initial Delhi expeditions failed before Balban marched personally.
- Tughril was defeated and punished; Bughra Khan was installed.
- After Balban, Bughra Khan remained effectively autonomous.
- The episode exposes the delegation paradox: distant government requires resources that can sustain rebellion.
- Personal reconquest restored authority without eliminating structural distance.

## 18. Cities, religion, subjects and intermediaries

- Delhi and provincial towns joined court, garrison, mint, market, workshops and scholarship.
- Royal *karkhanas* were workshops and stores serving courtly and military consumption.
- Persian was the main language of high administration and historical writing.
- Arabic retained religious and scholarly prestige.
- Ulema and qazis advised, taught and adjudicated, but Sultans exercised political discretion.
- Sufi networks associated with figures such as Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and Baba Farid possessed authority distinct from the court.
- Non-Muslim subjects formed the majority; revenue demands, local chiefs, customary institutions and accommodation varied by reign and region.
- *Kharaj* and *jizya* are legal-fiscal categories that must not be projected as a uniform collection practice across the whole century.
- Temples, mosques and reused material require case-specific analysis: coercion, religious legitimation, fiscal need, patronage and local negotiation could coexist.
- Khuts, muqaddams and other local headmen mediated countryside extraction.
- Artisans, merchants, soldiers, scholars, migrants and enslaved persons formed diverse urban populations.
- Persianate elite culture did not erase local languages, customary practices or Indian intermediaries.
- The state combined coercion with accommodation.

## 19. Material and architectural evidence

- Aibak began major works at the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque and Qutb Minar complex; Iltutmish extended the complex.
- Arhai Din ka Jhonpra at Ajmer was adapted into a mosque under early Sultanate patronage.
- Sultan Ghari and Iltutmish’s tomb show experimentation in commemorative architecture.
- Hauz Shamsi belongs to Iltutmish’s material patronage and Delhi-centred urban-religious landscape.
- Balban’s tomb preserves an important surviving true-arch phase based on radiating voussoirs; it is not evidence that India first learned or used arches under Balban.
- Spolia and temple-derived material establish reuse within a conquest context.
- Do not infer one motive for every stone: victory messaging, material availability, local craft and building phases require distinction.
- Chronicles reveal actions and elite justifications but carry court bias.
- Coins reveal titles, minting and portable claims, with survival and circulation limits.
- Inscriptions identify patrons and formulas; monuments reveal spatial presence, labour and later alteration.
- Triangulation is stronger than dependence on one archive.
- Detailed architectural evolution belongs to Topic 11; here monuments illuminate state formation.

## 20. State character and 1290 transition

- The early Sultanate was military-fiscal: force and revenue assignments were mutually sustaining.
- It was urban-centred: capitals, garrisons, markets and workshops concentrated capacity.
- It was Persianate in elite political language but locally mediated in operation.
- It was layered: crown, noble households, muqtis, governors and village intermediaries possessed unequal but real agency.
- It was centralising: rulers created coins, offices, intelligence and royal ideology.
- It was not uniformly centralised: succession, Bengal, frontier raids and route resistance persisted.
- After Prince Muhammad’s death, succession remained uncertain.
- Balban died in 1287; Qaiqabad, son of Bughra Khan, was enthroned.
- Qaiqabad’s incapacity intensified faction; the infant Kayumars was briefly projected.
- Jalaluddin Khalji took power in 1290.
- The event ended Mamluk dynastic predominance, not the Delhi Sultanate.
- Khalji policy and its detailed innovations belong to Topic 04.
- Final verdict: Mamluk rulers failed to stabilise hereditary succession but created institutions and a political centre durable enough to survive their house.

## 22. Answer-writing spine

For almost any Topic 03 analytical answer:

1. **Define the process:** state formation, delegation, succession or centralisation.
2. **Name the mechanism:** military household, iqta, coinage, ritual, barid, army or intermediary.
3. **Give a precise episode:** Tarain 1216, Indus 1221, Raziya 1236-40, Balban 1253-55, Tughril 1279, Prince Muhammad 1285-86 or transition 1290.
4. **State the political effect:** legitimacy, extraction, surveillance, route security, frontier containment or coalition change.
5. **Add the strongest limit:** distance, social cost, gendered source bias, faction, non-uniform enforcement or succession.
6. **Conclude with the calibrated verdict:** durable foundations, layered and uneven power.
