The topic is best understood as an interlocking state-capacity problem. Sher Shah did not become important merely by defeating Humayun. He first assembled resources, then converted military victory into revenue, communication and coercive arrangements. Those arrangements were effective enough to influence later practice, but too dependent on rulers and political coalitions to guarantee dynastic survival.

## Lesson 1

### From Farid to Sher Khan: Learning Administration Before Winning an Empire

Progress: 1/12 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Bihar-Bengal resource building

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: rise as accumulation, not miracle

```text
Farid in a Sur Afghan service family
        |
management of the Sahsaram jagir
        |
knowledge of cultivation, revenue and local chiefs
        |
service and rivalry in Bihar
        |
Afghan followers + forts + finance
        |
Surajgarh victory, 1534
        |
Bengal tribute, territory and Gaur
        |
Sher Khan becomes an imperial contender
```

The key is accumulation. Farid's rise was not caused by one heroic exploit. It came from learning how land revenue, military households, forts, family claims and regional alliances worked.

### 1. Family position and early experience

Farid belonged to a Sur Afghan family whose fortunes grew through military service and territorial assignments. Around 1510, his father Hasan Sur received Sahsaram and Khawaspur-Tanda in the Bihar-Jaunpur political zone. The family was important locally but did not begin as an imperial dynasty.

Farid's management of his father's lands gave him practical experience:

- assessing cultivation and collecting revenue;
- negotiating with village headmen and local chiefs;
- disciplining officials and armed followers;
- understanding how a jagir could support cavalry and household power;
- seeing why written demand and local information mattered.

Later narratives attach the title **Sher Khan** to his killing of a tiger. A modern reconstruction cited by Satish Chandra instead treats the title as possibly awarded for service. The safe conclusion is that the title marked rising status; the dramatic tiger story cannot explain his political career.

### 2. Why Bihar offered opportunity

After Ibrahim Lodi's defeat in 1526, Afghan political power did not disappear. Bihar contained competing Nuhani, Sur and other Afghan leaders, local chiefs, forts and revenue resources. Their rivalries created danger, but also room for a skilled organiser.

Sher Khan moved between service, alliance and independent ambition. He recovered family holdings, attracted followers and gradually became the effective power behind the Bihar regime. "Afghan unity" was not automatic. It had to be assembled through success, patronage and control of resources.

### 3. Bengal changed the scale

The decisive transition came through conflict with Bengal:

| Stage | Political effect |
|---|---|
| struggle over Bihar | established Sher Khan among regional Afghan leaders |
| Surajgarh, 1534 | defeated a Bengal-Nuhani force and increased prestige |
| pressure on Bengal | secured territory and indemnity |
| capture of Gaur by his forces, 1537 | opened access to Bengal's treasury and political resources |
| protection of family and treasure | made forts and communications central to strategy |

Bengal was not simply conquered in one movement. Repeated campaigns, alliances and withdrawals allowed Sher Khan to build a fiscal-military base while Humayun was occupied elsewhere.

### 4. Source problem

Much narrative detail comes from Abbas Khan Sarwani's *Tarikh-i Sher Shahi*, written decades later under Mughal rule. It is invaluable for personalities, episodes and administrative ideals, but it often praises Sher Shah and turns political success into moral biography.

Use it through a four-part test:

```text
chronicle claim
   -> compare chronology
   -> identify political purpose
   -> seek material or institutional corroboration
   -> retain only a qualified conclusion
```

Thus the secure explanation of Sher Khan's rise is regional experience plus resources and coalition-building. The colourful anecdotes remain evidence of remembered kingship, not transparent fact.

### UPSC application

- Do not begin Sher Shah's career at Chausa.
- Treat Bihar and Bengal as the resource base behind later victory.
- Distinguish an Afghan political network from a permanently united ethnic bloc.
- Use the tiger-title story only with source caution.

### Revision notes

1. Sher Shah's original name was Farid.
2. His family held service-linked lands around Sahsaram.
3. Estate management gave him revenue and local-governance experience.
4. Afghan politics in Bihar remained competitive after 1526.
5. Sher Khan built unity through success and patronage rather than inheriting it.
6. Surajgarh in 1534 increased his prestige and resources.
7. Bengal campaigns expanded his fiscal-military base.
8. Gaur was captured by his forces in 1537.
9. The tiger-killing explanation of his title is a later heroic tradition.
10. Sarwani must be used as a retrospective and often idealising source.

#### Answers and explanations

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** It links administrative experience, political coalition and material resources in the sequence actually required for his rise.
- **B — Incorrect:** The title tradition did not itself create obedience, territory or the resources acquired through later campaigns.
- **C — Incorrect:** Mughal recognition neither supplied Bengal's treasury nor established dependable authority over its governors.
- **D — Incorrect:** Sahsaram was a limited service base, not an inherited sovereign kingdom equal to Humayun's realm.

- **A — Incorrect:** Surajgarh materially increased Sher Khan's resources and prestige rather than producing recognition without major gains.
- **B — Correct:** The 1534 victory increased Sher Khan's prestige and helped turn Bihar-Bengal resources into an imperial challenge.
- **C — Incorrect:** The victory weakened both Bengal and Nuhani opposition and opened access to greater eastern resources.
- **D — Incorrect:** Surajgarh increased Sher Khan's leverage and resources rather than leaving Bengal's fiscal position untouched.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Bravery narratives cannot replace the institutional explanation based on revenue, followers, forts and alliances.
- **B — Incorrect:** The story is not an administrative document and does not establish sovereignty or a secure accession date.
- **C — Correct:** It preserves the remembered title while separating narrative symbolism from political causation.
- **D — Incorrect:** Later retelling does not make his independently evidenced service career or Bihar background fictional.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain how Sher Khan's Bihar-Bengal career prepared the foundations of the Sur Empire.

**Model answer:**
Sher Khan's imperial success rested on a regional apprenticeship rather than a sudden battlefield rise. As Farid, he gained practical knowledge of cultivation, revenue collection, village intermediaries and armed households while managing family lands around Sahsaram. This experience later informed his emphasis on assessment, supervision and local intelligence.

Post-Lodi Bihar contained competing Afghan chiefs and regional powers. Sher Khan recovered holdings, entered shifting alliances and attracted followers through patronage and success. He did not inherit a unified Afghan nation; he constructed a working coalition.

Bengal transformed his scale. Victory at Surajgarh in 1534 weakened the Bengal-Nuhani challenge. Subsequent pressure yielded territory and resources, while the capture of Gaur by his forces in 1537 enlarged his treasury and strategic reach. Forts and communications protected these gains while Humayun's campaigns created further openings.

Therefore Bihar supplied political organisation and administrative experience, while Bengal supplied the resources for sustained warfare. Later heroic narratives illuminate Sher Shah's reputation but cannot substitute for this fiscal-military explanation.

**Scoring note:** Reward the Sahsaram apprenticeship, contested Afghan politics, Surajgarh, Bengal resources and a source-qualified rejection of the one-hero explanation.

### Verified PYQ linkage

No direct UPSC CSE PYQ through 2026 asks about Farid's rise. This lesson prepares an original causation answer without converting the tiger story into a verified fact.

## Lesson 2

### Chausa and Kannauj: From Regional Challenger to Sultan

Progress: 2/12 | Stage: Foundation | Subtopic: Mughal defeat and Sur sovereignty

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: two victories, different political work

| Battle | Date | Immediate mechanism | Political consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chausa | 26 June 1539 | surprise, river position, monsoon and broken communications | shattered Mughal confidence; Humayun escaped |
| Kannauj/Bilgram | 17 May 1540 | set battle amid weak Mughal cohesion and stronger Afghan organisation | expelled Humayun from north India |

Chausa exposed Mughal vulnerability. Kannauj converted that vulnerability into regime change.

### 1. The communications trap

Humayun moved into Bengal after a long siege of Chunar. Sher Khan avoided a premature showdown, removed resources from Gaur and targeted the Mughal connection with Agra. Distance, rivers, rain, food and political uncertainty turned occupation of a capital into strategic exposure.

At Chausa, Humayun's camp had the river Karmnasa at its back. Sher Khan seized the initiative and surprised the Mughal army. The battle should not be reduced to one bad campsite: Afghan intelligence, timing, Bengal-Bihar resources and Mughal isolation worked together.

### 2. Why Kannauj was decisive

After Chausa, Humayun still possessed dynastic legitimacy and forces. But trust among the brothers and nobles had deteriorated. Kamran did not provide his hardened Punjab army, while Humayun's newly assembled force lacked comparable cohesion.

Kannauj was bitterly contested, not a ceremonial transfer. Its result forced Humayun from north India and allowed Sher Khan, now Sher Shah, to establish Sur sovereignty.

### 3. Victory did not equal finished government

```text
defeat Mughal army
        !=
collect revenue everywhere
        !=
control every fort and chief
        !=
secure succession
```

Sher Shah still had to:

- discipline Afghan nobles;
- secure Punjab and the north-west;
- stabilise Bengal;
- campaign in Malwa, Rajasthan and Bundelkhand;
- create administrative routines;
- protect routes and revenue;
- construct a legitimate sovereign image.

His military victories opened an imperial opportunity. The next question is how he tried to govern it.

### UPSC application

- Pair Chausa with operational isolation and Kannauj with regime displacement.
- Avoid explaining Humayun's defeat by personality alone.
- Do not treat invitations, betrayal or gunpowder as sufficient single causes.
- Use 1540 as the beginning of Sher Shah's north Indian reign.

### Revision notes

1. Chausa was fought on 26 June 1539.
2. Rivers, monsoon and communications shaped the battle.
3. Humayun survived Chausa but lost heavily.
4. Fraternal and noble cohesion worsened after Chausa.
5. Kannauj/Bilgram was fought on 17 May 1540.
6. Kannauj decisively displaced Humayun from north India.
7. Sher Khan assumed sovereign status as Sher Shah.
8. Battlefield success did not settle revenue, forts or succession.

### Local practice

B. Chausa strengthened Humayun's Bengal position, whereas Kannauj transferred Bihar to Sher Khan through a negotiated territorial division.
C. Chausa removed Kamran's influence from Punjab, whereas Kannauj incorporated his experienced troops into the emerging Sur army.
D. Chausa exposed Mughal operational weakness, whereas Kannauj converted Afghan military advantage into north Indian regime change in 1540.

A. Sher Khan combined Bihar-Bengal resources, intelligence and command with Mughal failures of communication, cohesion and strategic follow-through.
B. Humayun's inherited legitimacy mattered less than his personal weaknesses, while Afghan organisation mainly occupied the political vacuum those weaknesses created.
C. Sher Khan's disciplined artillery decided both battles against a Mughal force that had lost its earlier experience with firearms.
D. Humayun's western and eastern campaigns mattered chiefly because they left Delhi undefended rather than because they exposed logistical weakness.

#### Answers and explanations

**MCQ 4: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Chausa gravely weakened Humayun, but Kannauj produced the decisive regime displacement rather than merely ratifying it.
- **B — Incorrect:** Humayun's Bengal advance became a liability, and no such negotiated territorial division followed Kannauj.
- **C — Incorrect:** Kamran's non-cooperation weakened Humayun, but his army was not transferred to Sher Shah.
- **D — Correct:** It distinguishes the operational shock of 1539 from the decisive political displacement of 1540.

**MCQ 5: A**

- **A — Correct:** It weighs Sher Khan's state-building and Mughal structural weaknesses instead of turning defeat into a character anecdote.
- **B — Incorrect:** Personal failings mattered, but treating Afghan organisation as a passive response erases Sher Khan's positive strategy and resources.
- **C — Incorrect:** Mughal forces retained gunpowder experience; weapons alone do not explain the Afghan victories.
- **D — Incorrect:** Humayun campaigned extensively in both Gujarat and Bengal, but failed to consolidate those gains.

### Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why was Kannauj more decisive than Chausa in the transfer of power to Sher Shah?

**Model answer:**
Chausa and Kannauj formed a sequence rather than interchangeable victories. At Chausa in June 1539, Sher Khan exploited Mughal isolation, river position, monsoon conditions and disrupted communications. Humayun narrowly escaped, but the Mughal regime still retained forces and dynastic legitimacy.

The defeat intensified distrust among Humayun, his brothers and nobles. Kamran withheld his experienced Punjab troops, while Humayun assembled a less cohesive army. At Kannauj in May 1540, Sher Khan defeated this force in a larger set battle. Humayun then lost north India and entered exile.

Kannauj was therefore decisive because it converted operational weakness into regime displacement. Yet it did not automatically create stable Sur administration: Sher Shah still had to secure forts, nobles, revenue and distant regions.

**Scoring note:** Require the two dates, operational-versus-political distinction, Mughal cohesion problem and a qualification about post-victory consolidation.

No direct UPSC CSE PYQ through 2026 asks for this battle comparison. Use the sequence to support future questions on conquest, communications and state capacity.

### Empire, Frontier and Afghan Kingship

## Visual first: an empire of differently controlled zones

```text
                  SUR IMPERIAL CLAIM
                         |
       +-----------------+-----------------+
       |                 |                 |
Delhi-Agra-Ganga    Punjab/frontier    Malwa-Rajasthan
court, roads,       forts, routes,      campaigns, forts,
revenue core        commanders          tribute/subordination
       |
Bihar-Bengal base and eastern settlement

Kashmir outside effective conquest; Gujarat not incorporated.
```

An empire on a map can look uniformly coloured. Actual power was graded: direct administration in some areas, garrison and route control in others, and negotiated submission elsewhere.

### 1. Extent without false uniformity

Sher Shah's authority stretched broadly from Bengal toward the Indus. It included the Delhi-Agra-Ganga core, Bihar, Bengal settlements, Punjab, Malwa and much of Rajasthan. Kashmir remained outside, and Gujarat was not incorporated.

This did not mean identical rule across the territory:

| Zone | Dominant form of control |
|---|---|
| Delhi-Agra and settled Ganga regions | court, revenue officials, roads and garrisons |
| Bihar-Bengal | formative resource base, river routes and politically managed chiefs |
| Punjab and north-west | commanders, forts and protection against Mughal return |
| Malwa and Rajasthan | conquest, fortified positions and subordinate rulers |
| Bundelkhand | active frontier campaigning at Kalinjar |

### 2. Afghan kingship was neither democracy nor simple despotism

Afghan nobles valued honour, consultation, military followings and territorial assignments. Sher Shah nevertheless claimed superior monarchical authority and disciplined nobles through appointment, surveillance and force.

The relationship was reciprocal but unequal:

```text
ruler gives rank, honour, cash or assignment
        |
noble supplies service, troops and regional influence
        |
ruler supervises and may punish or transfer
        |
noble cooperation remains politically necessary
```

Calling this "tribal democracy" understates kingship. Calling it absolute despotism ignores the bargaining power of chiefs and the ruler's dependence on their followings.

### 3. Personal centralisation

Sher Shah reportedly examined affairs closely, toured and restrained ministerial autonomy. This could speed decisions and prevent rival power centres. It also created a succession problem: routines tied closely to one ruler were harder to sustain after his death.

### 4. Empire-building as historical process

Sher Shah did not unite north India from an untouched political landscape. Sikandar Lodi, Babur and Humayun had already altered older balances. Sher Shah's success belonged to this longer process and remained incomplete; Akbar later expanded and reorganised a different empire.

### UPSC application

- Describe extent with graded control, not a flat modern map.
- Avoid equating Afghan political culture with either equality or disorder.
- Link personal supervision to both effectiveness and succession fragility.
- Treat Sher Shah as part of a longer north Indian state-building process.

### Revision notes

1. The Sur claim extended broadly from Bengal toward the Indus.
2. Kashmir remained outside effective Sur conquest.
3. Gujarat was not incorporated.
4. Core, frontier and subordinate zones were governed differently.
5. Afghan nobles expected honour, consultation and assignments.
6. Sher Shah asserted superior monarchical authority.
7. Noble service and ruler discipline existed together.
8. Personal supervision could produce speed and fragility.
9. The Sur Empire was part of a longer unification process.

#### Answers and explanations

- **A — Incorrect:** Gujarat and Kashmir were not incorporated, and territorial control was not institutionally uniform.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Assess the relationship between territorial extent, Afghan nobility and personal monarchy under Sher Shah.

**Model answer:**
Sher Shah ruled a wide but uneven empire. The Delhi-Agra-Ganga belt and Bihar formed its strongest fiscal-political core. Punjab and the north-west depended heavily on routes, commanders and forts, while authority in Malwa and Rajasthan often followed campaigning, garrisoning and the submission of local rulers. Kashmir remained outside and Gujarat was not incorporated.

This geography shaped kingship. Afghan nobles brought troops, clan connections and regional influence, and expected honour and territorial assignments. Yet Sher Shah did not treat them as equal partners. He asserted superior monarchy, used spies, supervised appointments and punished resistance. Noble bargaining and royal discipline therefore coexisted.

His personal attention produced rapid coordination across revenue, roads and the army. It also limited institutional resilience. A system dependent on an exceptional ruler and a negotiated Afghan coalition became vulnerable when succession was disputed.

Thus Sur power was more than a loose confederacy but less than uniform bureaucratic control. Its reach was substantial, while its durability remained tied to political geography and personal authority.

**Scoring note:** Reward graded territorial control, Afghan noble expectations, strong monarchy, personal supervision and the effectiveness-versus-durability tension.

### From Court to Village: How Sur Administration Actually Connected

Progress: 4/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Central, sarkar, pargana and village institutions

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried - Satish Chandra Part II PDF pp. 77-80 and *History of Medieval India* PDF pp. 233-235
CA search: "not run - modern district administration is not treated as an equivalent"
CA found: none required
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## Visual first: the administrative chain

```text
SULTAN AND CENTRAL OFFICES
finance | army | chancery | grants/judicial functions
                    |
             SHIQ / SARKAR
        faujdar or muqta + munsif
                    |
                PARGANA
 shiqdar + munsif/amil + clerks + khazanadar/poddar
                    |
                VILLAGE
 muqaddam + patwari + panchayat + cultivators
                    |
        zamindars and local chiefs mediate
```

The hierarchy did not operate like a modern bureaucracy. Orders moved downward, but revenue, records and coercion depended on people with local knowledge and social authority.

### 1. What is securely known about the centre?

Evidence for Sher Shah's central government is thinner than the detailed descriptions of revenue and local administration. Sultanate-derived offices probably continued:

- a finance or revenue department;
- an *ariz* establishment concerned with the army;
- a chancery for orders and correspondence;
- a *sadr* connected with grants and religious beneficiaries;
- qazis performing judicial functions.

Sher Shah reportedly kept leading officers under close personal control. Exact portfolios should not be projected backward from Akbar's more fully documented departments.

### 2. Pargana: the operational local unit

A **pargana** comprised a number of villages. Standard descriptions associate it with:

| Official | Main function |
|---|---|
| *shiqdar* | order, military-police authority and general administration |
| *munsif* or *amil* | land measurement, assessment and collection |
| *qanungo* | customary revenue records and local fiscal knowledge |
| Persian and Hindavi writers | parallel account keeping |
| *khazanadar*, *poddar* or *fotedar* | custody of collected cash |

Different texts and periods use titles with some variation. Function matters more than memorising a rigid chart.

### 3. Sarkar or shiq: the debated middle level

Above the pargana stood the **shiq**, increasingly called a **sarkar** from Lodi times. Later descriptions sometimes name a *shiqdar-i-shiqdaran*, but Satish Chandra notes that identifiable holders of that title are difficult to find. A *faujdar* or *muqta*, assisted by a revenue official or *munsif*, is the safer functional description.

Several sarkars could be grouped under a commander in frontier or turbulent areas. This was not Akbar's later, standardised **suba** system.

### 4. Village and intermediary power

The *muqaddam* or headman helped maintain order and mediate collection. The *patwari* kept local accounts. Panchayats and caste bodies continued to handle many disputes. Zamindars could possess customary rights, armed power and information needed by the state.

These actors were not salaried modern officials in a single chain. Their own interests could support, negotiate with or resist imperial demand.

### UPSC application

- Do not insert Akbar's suba into Sher Shah's regular hierarchy.
- Distinguish administrative title from practical function.
- Explain why local intermediaries survived measured assessment.

## Visual first: from field to assessed demand

```text
measure sown area with jarib/bigha conventions
                 |
classify land or expected yield:
good + middling + bad
                 |
calculate an average produce
                 |
apply crop-rate schedule (rai)
                 |
state share commonly framed as one-third
                 |
convert through local prices when paid in cash
                 |
record demand in patta
                 |
cultivator's acceptance: qabuliyat in standard accounts
```

This chain tried to make revenue predictable. It did not mean that every field was measured accurately or that collection matched the written ideal.

### 1. Measurement and classification

Sher Shah favoured **zabt**, measurement-based assessment, over reliance only on crop-sharing or rough estimation. Measurement itself was older. Alauddin Khalji and earlier regimes had used related methods. Sher Shah's importance lies in systematisation and the attempt to connect measured area with crop schedules.

The source tradition associates measurement with the ***jarib***, ***bigha*** and **Sikandari gaz**. These were historical measuring conventions, not timeless units with one safely transferable modern conversion.

The ideal description divides land or expected yield into good, middling and bad categories. Their average yield was calculated. A crop-rate schedule called the ***rai*** then helped determine the state's demand after the sown field was measured.

### 2. The one-third norm

The commonly stated state share was **one-third of average produce**. This is a normative formula, not proof that one-third was collected from each holding across the empire.

Actual incidence could vary because of:

- crop and local productivity;
- price used for cash conversion;
- measuring and collection charges;
- cesses;
- bargaining or coercion by intermediaries;
- frontier and ecological conditions.

Multan appears in the assigned account as a special exception where older practice continued and a one-fourth share is reported. Treat this as evidence against uniformity, not as a universal frontier rule.

### 3. Patta and qabuliyat

The ***patta*** recorded the cultivated area, crop and assessed amount due. The ***qabuliyat*** represented the cultivator's acceptance or acknowledgement of the demand in standard descriptions.

Their administrative purpose was clarity:

```text
known area + known crop rate + written demand
        -> less room for arbitrary additions in principle
```

But a written document did not create equal bargaining power. The state's coercive capacity and the role of local intermediaries remained.

### 4. Cash or kind

Cultivators were reportedly allowed to pay in cash or kind, though cash was preferred. Cash helped the treasury and army, but exposed cultivators to local price conditions. Kind payment reduced some price risk while creating storage and transport burdens.

### UPSC application

- Define *zabt*, *rai*, *patta* and *qabuliyat* separately.
- Do not call the system British-style ryotwari.
- Present one-third as a common normative share, not uniform realised burden.
- Compare with Akbar only after explaining Sher Shah's own mechanism.

## Visual first: one reform, several lived outcomes

| Administrative aim | Possible benefit | Possible burden |
|---|---|---|

| cash payment | easier state and army finance | dependence on market access |
| intermediary control | limit extra exactions | collective punishment and violent enforcement |

The same institution could reduce one form of uncertainty while intensifying another.

### 1. Why the peasant-protection image is incomplete

Sarwani presents Sher Shah as attentive to the *raiyat*. Regulations sought to prevent soldiers from trampling crops and provided compensation when damage was unavoidable. Officials were forbidden to demand unauthorised additions.

These statements reveal an ideal of productive protection: cultivation generated revenue, so protecting cultivation served the state. They do not prove that every cultivator received compensation or that officials obeyed consistently.

### 2. Intermediaries could not simply be removed

Muqaddams and zamindars knew boundaries, crops, households and local power relations. The state lacked the personnel to bypass them. It could regulate their claims, threaten punishment and appoint officials, but still needed their cooperation.

This produces a triangular relationship:

```text
imperial demand
      |
official measurement and records
      |
muqaddam / zamindar mediation
      |
cultivator's crop, cash and labour
```

### 3. Coercion was part of the system

Sher Shah's reputation for discipline should not be converted into benign administration. Recalcitrant zamindars and villages could face military action, seizure, enslavement and resettlement in the punitive descriptions preserved by the sources.

The state protected obedient cultivation and commerce because they sustained revenue. That protection existed alongside severe coercion against resistance or default.

### 4. Prescription versus implementation

Repeated orders against extra charges suggest that extractions persisted. The Part II account reports that profitable *amil* posts were rotated after two years so that other favoured servants might benefit. This exposes patronage incentives: a neat administrative manual tells us what the ruler wanted, not how consistently officials complied.

- Analyse protection and extraction together.
- Do not assume a written patta guaranteed fair collection.
- Separate assessed demand from realised burden.
- Use repeated prohibitions as indirect evidence that abuse continued.

## Visual first: how standardisation can widen exchange

```text
more reliable coin standard
          +
fewer authorised customs points
          +
safer routes and storage
          |
lower verification and transit costs
          |
easier tax, salary and market transactions
          |
greater circulation - but not universal monetisation
```

Money works through trust. A ruler who stabilises weight and fineness makes it easier for taxpayers, soldiers and merchants to accept a coin without repeatedly discounting it.

### 1. The rupee claim

Sher Shah issued fine gold, silver and copper coins. His silver ***rupiya*** became an influential long-term standard, while the copper ***dam*** served lower-value transactions.

He did not invent silver currency, the word "rupee" or money in India. Earlier Indian and Sultanate coin traditions existed. His importance lies in standardising denominations, weight and fineness within a large political zone.

Coins provide unusually direct evidence:

- metal and weight can be measured;
- legends show titles and sovereignty;
- mint and date may identify production;
- finds reveal circulation, though not complete economic coverage.

Issues attributed to multiple mints help trace the reach of sovereign claims, but mint variety and coin finds do not prove equal circulation in every region.

### 2. Customs simplification

The assigned account associates Sher Shah with customs collected at two main stages: entry into the kingdom or controlled frontier point, and sale. This aimed to reduce repeated road, ferry, town and local demands.

The same ruler-ideal tradition instructs officials to buy merchants' goods at market price and not seize the property of a merchant who died without heirs. These are significant protections, but their prescriptive form also shows the abuses that government was trying to restrain.

The mechanism was commercial and political:

| Reform | Economic effect | State effect |
|---|---|---|
| standard coin | easier valuation and payment | clearer revenue and salary accounting |
| fewer customs points | lower cumulative transit burden | concentrates authorised collection |
| safer routes | lower risk of loss | strengthens surveillance and authority |
| sarai markets | exchange and storage | creates taxable, monitored nodes |

### 3. Limits

Uniform coins did not eliminate barter, older issues or local shortages. A rule allowing two duties does not prove that unauthorised levies vanished. Repetition of similar regulations under later rulers suggests continuing abuse.

Trade gains were uneven. Merchants near secure corridors benefited more readily than cultivators or artisans distant from markets. State-facilitated trade also financed armies and extraction.

## Visual first: one corridor, several state functions

```text
ROAD
 |
 +-> army movement and frontier supply
 +-> merchant travel and market access
 +-> sarai lodging, water and animal care
 +-> horse/running relays for news
 +-> surveillance of officials and countryside
 +-> concentration of customs and revenue
```

A road is not merely a strip of transport infrastructure. It changes the time needed to move orders, troops, information and goods.

### 1. The Grand Trunk Road qualification

The label **Grand Trunk Road** became common later. Sher Shah restored, improved, connected and policed older imperial, commercial and pilgrimage corridors. He did not create an entirely new road across an empty landscape.

The source tradition associates him with:

| Corridor | Strategic-commercial significance |
|---|---|
| Sonargaon through Agra, Delhi and Lahore toward the Indus | east-west imperial spine |
| Agra-Jodhpur-Chittor | Rajasthan and routes toward Gujarat |
| Lahore-Multan | Punjab and caravans toward West and Central Asia |
| Agra-Burhanpur | Malwa-Deccan direction and western trade routes |

These are historical corridors, not exact modern highway alignments.

### 2. Sarais as multifunctional nodes

Sarais were fortified inns providing lodging, storage, food, water and care for animals. Accounts also associate the road programme with trees, wells and bridge or ferry arrangements, though survival and implementation varied. Some sarais developed markets and contributed to the growth of *qasbas*, or small towns.

Sources report separate arrangements for Hindu and Muslim travellers and a spacing of two *karohs*, rendered in the assigned Part II account as about four miles. Treat both as programme descriptions, not proof that each site had identical facilities or spacing.

### 3. Dak and intelligence

Horses and messengers at sarais formed relay stages for news. Speed claims such as hundreds of *kos* in a day come from celebratory accounts and should not be treated as measured averages.

The system helped:

- transmit orders;
- report rebellion or official misconduct;
- move military intelligence;
- coordinate revenue collection;
- watch nobles and local chiefs.

This was an imperial communication and surveillance network, not a modern universal public postal service.

### 4. Security and its coercive side

Village headmen and zamindars could be made responsible for theft or murder on roads. This may have encouraged local policing, but collective liability could punish people who had not committed the offence.

### UPSC application

- Say restored and extended older corridors, not invented the GT Road.
- Keep Sonargaon-Indus distinct from modern road numbering.
- Explain sarai as inn, market, relay and surveillance node.

***Dagh*** was horse branding; ***chehra*** was a descriptive roll of personnel. These controls aimed to prevent substitution of inferior horses or fictitious soldiers.

"Tolerant" and "intolerant" are too blunt when used alone. Policy combined Islamic legitimacy, practical accommodation, social hierarchy and episodes of severe coercion.

### 1. Religious position and governance

Sher Shah was a Sunni Muslim ruler who respected religious scholars and used Islamic political language. Yet his government was not run by the *ulama* alone. Revenue and local administration employed Hindus as scribes, officials and intermediaries where their knowledge was useful.

Jizya continued. Later evidence also refers to rent-free grants to Brahmans, temples and maths. Neither fact settles the whole character of the state.

Sarwani's ruler-ideal narrative also reports maintenance grants for disabled people and a *langar khana*, or free kitchen, for the poor. These claims broaden the evidence for social provision, but they are not audited proof of uniform access across the empire.

### 2. Raisen and Puran Mal

At Raisen, Puran Mal and his followers came out after a promise or binding safe-conduct in the chronicled account and were then attacked and killed with their families. Religious accusations were used to justify the action.

Three analytical levels are necessary:

1. **Event:** surrender was followed by violence.
2. **Source and rhetoric:** Sarwani and later accounts frame promises, accusations and religious justification.
3. **Political meaning:** breaching safe-conduct damaged legitimacy and cannot be excused as routine realpolitik.

The episode does not prove an empire-wide programme of persecution. Nor can administrative efficiency or employment of Hindu officials erase it.

### 3. Social reach and source silence

Cultivators, merchants, artisans, soldiers and local elites experienced the state differently. Women appear in dynastic households, labour, markets, agriculture and the displacement caused by war, but sources do not permit a uniform gender assessment.

Afghan predominance among nobles mattered. Administrative inclusion below the highest elite did not create equal political access.

### 1. Islam Shah was not an empty postscript

Sher Shah was succeeded by his son Jalal Khan, who took the title **Islam Shah** and ruled from 1545 to 1553. He continued administrative and military discipline, issued detailed regulations and attempted closer financial control over noble assignments.

He also tried to strengthen monarchy against Afghan chiefs. Symbolic court practices and strict treatment of nobles asserted that they were servants of the ruler, not partners in sovereignty. These measures increased coordination but generated resentment.

Islam Shah's regulation of administration and religious matters also limited dependence on jurists for every ruling. Cash-payment efforts and tighter controls should be treated as policy direction, not proof that assignments disappeared.

### 2. Why the dynasty fragmented

After Islam Shah's death:

- the young Firuz was killed;
- Muhammad Adil Shah faced rival claimants;
- Sikandar Sur and Ibrahim Sur established competing centres;
- nobles and armies divided;
- Hemu became Adil Shah's leading military-administrative figure;
- Humayun exploited the fractured field and recovered Delhi in 1555.

The collapse did not prove an inherent Afghan incapacity for government. It reflected weak succession settlement, personalised authority, elite resentment, rival armies and insufficient time for institutions to become independent of rulers.

### 3. Influence without a blueprint

| Earlier precedent | Sur adaptation | Later Mughal development |
|---|---|---|
| Sultanate measurement and crop schedules | stronger measurement-*rai*-patta ideal | Akbar's regionally differentiated *zabt-dahsala* |
| Alauddin's dagh and chehra | revived soldier and horse verification | incorporated into mansab-era inspection |
| shiq, pargana and local personnel | stabilised sarkar-pargana supervision | suba framework and paired provincial checks |
| older roads, sarais and messengers | repaired and connected imperial network | expanded Mughal routes and reporting |
| tanka and regional coin traditions | standard rupiya-dam-gold issues | wider imperial mint standardisation |

Akbar did not copy a complete Sher Shahi manual. Later officials selected, modified and expanded practices under a broader ruling class, different territorial scale and richer documentary system.

### 4. Final assessment

Sher Shah was an effective short-reign state-builder. His contribution lay less in inventing every institution than in connecting existing practices:

```text
measured demand
 + reliable money
 + safer circulation
 + military verification
 + information
 + ruler-centred enforcement
 = increased state capacity
```

The same formula reveals the limit. Personal supervision and coercion produced results quickly but did not solve succession or secure durable elite cooperation.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Why is it misleading to say that Sher Shah invented the Grand Trunk Road and the rupee?

**Model answer:**
Both claims confuse systematisation with invention. Sher Shah restored, improved and policed older imperial, commercial and pilgrimage corridors. The later label "Grand Trunk Road" should not be projected as a single newly constructed modern alignment. His Sonargaon-Indus spine and connecting routes mattered because sarais, markets, relays and security integrated movement with state control.

Likewise, silver currency and rupee-related terminology pre-dated Sher Shah. His contribution was to issue a reliable silver *rupiya* within a standardised gold-silver-copper system. Weight, fineness and broad acceptance aided trade, taxation and salaries.

The stronger verdict is therefore that Sher Shah coordinated and stabilised inherited practices. Their later influence reflects effective adaptation, not creation from nothing.

**Scoring note:** Require both prior-route and prior-coin traditions, the nature of Sur standardisation and a clear systematisation-not-invention verdict.

## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** "Sher Shah's revenue system increased predictability without eliminating agrarian coercion." Discuss.

**Model answer:**
Sher Shah's revenue system sought predictable assessment through measurement. Sown area was measured, land or expected yield classified as good, middling or bad, and an average yield computed. The *rai* supplied crop rates, while the state's share was commonly framed as one-third. The *patta* recorded area, crop and liability, and *qabuliyat* acknowledged the demand. Cash or kind payment was possible, though cash was preferred.

These measures could reduce uncertainty and restrain unauthorised additions. Rules protected standing crops during military movement and promised compensation for unavoidable damage.

Yet predictability did not mean equal power. Muqaddams, zamindars, patwaris and officials remained indispensable to information and collection. Price conversion, cesses and measuring charges could increase actual burden. Multan's exception and later evidence of limited measurement show regional variation. Repeated prohibitions suggest continuing exactions.

Enforcement could be severe: resistant zamindars and communities faced military action, seizure and collective punishment. Peasant protection was linked to the fiscal value of productive cultivation.

Thus Sher Shah made extraction more legible and potentially less arbitrary, but also strengthened the state's ability to demand and enforce revenue.

**Scoring note:** Reward mechanism, written records, potential predictability, intermediaries, market and charge risks, regional variation and coercion.

## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate Sher Shah as a state-builder and assess the nature of his influence on later Mughal administration.

**Model answer:**
Sher Shah converted a Bihar-Bengal resource base and victories at Chausa and Kannauj into a wide north Indian empire. His state-building joined institutions that are often studied separately.

Measurement, crop classification, *rai* and written demand sought predictable revenue. Standard rupiya and dam issues, simplified customs, safer roads and sarais lowered some transaction costs while improving fiscal and military circulation. Dagh-chehra controls, forts, garrisons and intelligence strengthened coercive reach. Sarkar-pargana administration connected central direction to local officials, muqaddams and zamindars.

The achievement must be qualified. Many practices had Sultanate precedents. Sarwani's praise describes ideals more clearly than uniform implementation. One-third assessment, sarai spacing and justice claims varied in practice. Raisen and collective punishment reveal coercion, while Afghan elite dominance and personal supervision limited institutional breadth.

Islam Shah continued regulation but deepened noble resentment. Succession conflict after 1553 fragmented the dynasty, enabling Humayun's recovery.

Later Mughals adapted Sur measurement, currency, roads and verification within a different suba, mansab and revenue framework. Sher Shah was therefore an effective systematiser and bridge, not the inventor of a complete Mughal blueprint.

**Scoring note:** Require an integrated state-capacity argument, at least four named reforms, source and coercion limits, Islam Shah, and continuity without direct-copy claims.

- Farid's estate management supplied practical fiscal experience.
- Bihar contained competing Afghan chiefs rather than automatic unity.
- Surajgarh and Bengal resources transformed Sher Khan's scale.
- Chausa exposed Mughal operational weakness.
- Kannauj produced regime displacement.
- Conquest still required administration, routes, forts and elite management.
- The empire combined a strong core with varied frontier and subordinate control.
- Gujarat and Kashmir remained outside Sher Shah's completed conquests.

## Administration

- Central portfolios retained Sultanate-derived finance, army, chancery and grant functions.
- Exact ministerial powers are less securely documented than under Akbar.
- Pargana comprised villages.
- Shiqdar handled order and general administration.
- Munsif or amil handled measurement, assessment and collection.
- Qanungo preserved customary fiscal knowledge.
- Persian and Hindavi writers maintained records.
- Khazanadar, poddar or fotedar held cash.
- Shiq or sarkar formed the middle territorial level.
- Larger frontier groupings varied; standard Mughal subas came later.
- Muqaddam, patwari, panchayat and zamindar remained indispensable.

## Revenue

```text
measurement
 -> good/middling/bad classification
 -> average yield
 -> rai crop schedule
 -> commonly one-third state share
 -> local-price cash conversion or kind
 -> patta and qabuliyat
 -> collection through officials and intermediaries
```

- Jarib, bigha and Sikandari-gaz conventions should not be assigned one timeless modern conversion.

- Protection of cultivation served fiscal reproduction.

## Money, customs and trade

- Sher Shah standardised gold, silver and copper issues.
- Coins from multiple mints projected sovereignty, although finds do not prove uniform circulation.
- Silver rupiya became a durable reference coin.
- Copper dam served lower-value transactions.
- He did not invent Indian silver currency or the rupee concept.
- Customs were ideally concentrated at entry and sale.
- Officials were instructed to respect merchant property and market prices, though prescription does not prove compliance.
- Standard money supported trade, revenue and military pay.
- Barter, local coins and uneven monetisation continued.

## Roads, sarais and information

- Grand Trunk Road is a later label.
- Sher Shah restored and linked older corridors.
- Main traditional routes: Sonargaon-Indus; Agra-Jodhpur-Chittor; Lahore-Multan; Agra-Burhanpur.
- Sarais offered lodging, storage, food, water and animal care; road accounts also mention trees, wells and crossings.
- Some became markets and qasba nuclei.
- Two-karoh spacing and identical facilities are idealised programme claims.
- Dak relays carried orders and intelligence.
- Roads served commerce, army movement, revenue and surveillance.
- Collective responsibility for crime could be coercive.

- Dagh = horse branding.
- Chehra = descriptive roll.
- Sher Shah revived earlier Alauddin-era practices.
- Direct recruitment coexisted with noble contingents and assignments.
- Reported army totals are chronicle figures.
- Chunar controlled eastern routes.
- Rohtas guarded the north-west; Rohtasgarh belonged to Bihar.
- Qazis coexisted with panchayats and local authorities.
- Justice rhetoric must be tested against collective punishment and implementation.

- Sher Shah used Sunni legitimacy and respected scholars.
- Jizya continued.
- Hindu officials and local intermediaries served the state.
- Grants to Brahmans, temples and maths appear in the evidence.
