*Neither state arose in an empty region: both recombined older territories, personnel and political claims after Delhi's southern reach weakened.*

### Intuition: new states usually inherit more than they invent

When a large political centre retreats, the resulting space is not blank. Existing chiefs, governors, warriors, religious institutions, merchants and cultivators already control resources. A successful new kingdom must bargain with, defeat or incorporate them.

The analogy is a change of management rather than construction on vacant land. Its limit is important: medieval state formation involved coercion, conquest and claims to sacred legitimacy that a modern administrative analogy cannot capture.

### 1. The prerequisite: Delhi's unstable southern expansion

Muhammad bin Tughlaq's southern empire reached widely but proved difficult to hold. Rebellions, long communication lines, regional military resources and the survival of earlier political formations weakened Delhi's authority. The Madurai Sultanate, Hoysala power, Warangal's successors, Reddi chiefs and Karnataka nayakas all formed part of the setting.

This matters because “Vijayanagara versus Bahmani” is the outcome of a broader regional reordering. It is not the whole beginning.

### 2. Vijayanagara: a useful date and a disputed biography

The conventional foundation or coronation anchor is **c. 1336**, associated with the brothers **Harihara and Bukka**. A well-known tradition links them to Warangal or Kampili, capture by the Tughlaqs, conversion to Islam, return to the south and reconversion under the influence of **Vidyaranya**.

The evidentiary problem is that the brothers' precise earlier affiliations, conversion story and Vidyaranya's exact founding role remain debated. Therefore:

- use c. 1336 as an exam-safe chronological anchor;
- identify Harihara and Bukka as the conventional founders;
- describe the detailed origin narrative as a tradition, not established biography;
- prefer inscriptions, expansion, institutions and the growth of the capital when demonstrating actual state formation.

The popular phrase “Hindu bulwark” is also inadequate as a complete explanation. It projects a later civilizational frame onto a polity that fought Hindu rivals, recruited Muslims and pursued territorial and commercial interests.

### 3. Bahmani formation: title, founder and later legend

In **1347**, **Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah** established independent rule in the Deccan. “Hasan Gangu” is common in historical memory, while the story that “Bahman” honoured a Brahman patron named Gangu is strongly associated with the later chronicler Firishta. Another tradition claimed Iranian heroic descent.

The correct distinction is:

| Secure anchor | Qualified tradition |
|---|---|
| independent Bahmani rule from 1347 | precise social origin of Hasan |
| Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah | service under a Brahman named Gangu |
| early capital at Gulbarga | genealogical explanations of “Bahman” |

Later claims could legitimate a dynasty without preserving a literal family history.

### 4. How to use the sources

| Evidence type | What it can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Inscriptions | grants, titles, offices, dates, irrigation and patronage | a neutral account of all groups |
| Coins | titulature, metal, mints and claims to sovereignty | exact political borders or complete monetization |
| Archaeology and buildings | urban planning, water, defence, ritual and craft | the beliefs or welfare of every resident |
| Persian chronicles | campaigns, court politics and remembered genealogy | exact numbers or unbiased motives |
| Court literature | ideals of kingship, conquest memory and patronage | uniform administrative practice |
| Travellers | observed markets, court, city and social practices | a census of the empire |

Use the sequence: **claim → source and date → corroboration → limitation**.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** If the founders' stories are disputed, even the dates and identities should be rejected.

**Response:** Historical uncertainty is claim-specific. A disputed conversion narrative does not erase the convergence of inscriptions, dynastic memory and political development around Harihara, Bukka and the conventional c. 1336 anchor. Good history narrows the doubtful claim instead of discarding every adjacent fact.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** c. 1336 belongs to Vijayanagara; 1347 belongs to the Bahmani kingdom.
- **Mains:** introduce both as products of regional recombination after Tughlaq contraction.
- **Trap:** “origin tradition” and “secure institutional evidence” are not interchangeable.
- **Answer line:** *The two states emerged from a contested Deccan field; conventional founding dates are firmer than the complete biographies later attached to them.*

2. Existing southern polities and local military elites remained active.
3. c. 1336 is the conventional Vijayanagara foundation anchor.
4. Harihara and Bukka are the conventional founders.
5. Their Warangal/Kampili-conversion-Vidyaranya narrative is debated.
6. Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah founded independent Bahmani rule in 1347.
7. “Hasan Gangu” and the Brahman-patron explanation require provenance caution.
8. A foundation legend can legitimate power without serving as literal biography.
9. Inscriptions and archaeology have different evidentiary reach.
10. Travellers provide observations, not comprehensive statistics.
11. “Hindu bulwark” cannot explain Vijayanagara's full political behaviour.
12. State formation involved incorporation of older institutions and elites.

### Original Mains application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why the rise of Vijayanagara and the Bahmani kingdom should be understood as regional state formation rather than the creation of two states in a political vacuum.

**Model answer**

The weakening of Tughlaq authority in the Deccan did not leave an empty territory. It released a competitive field containing the Hoysalas, the Madurai Sultanate, Warangal's successors, Reddi chiefs, Karnataka nayakas and provincial military elites.

Vijayanagara's conventional c. 1336 foundation under Harihara and Bukka illustrates recombination. Their precise Warangal/Kampili affiliations and conversion-Vidyaranya story are debated, but the new centre incorporated existing territorial and warrior networks. Similarly, Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah's independence in 1347 converted a Deccan provincial-military base into a sovereign kingdom centred initially at Gulbarga.

Both states therefore depended on inherited routes, forts, agrarian resources and personnel while constructing new dynastic legitimacy. Later chronicles and foundation traditions help explain political memory, but cannot by themselves settle biography.

Thus, their rise was a regional redistribution and consolidation of authority after imperial contraction, not creation on vacant ground.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the prior political field, names both foundation anchors, separates evidence from legend and reaches a causal rather than communal conclusion.

### Intuition: a fort matters because of what lies behind it

A frontier fort is not valuable merely as stone. It protects roads, food supplies, taxable cultivation and troop movement. Likewise, a port is not only a marketplace: in the Deccan it connected inland rulers to merchants and imported horses.

The limit is that geography creates incentives, not predetermined outcomes. Leadership, alliances, technology, monsoon conditions and local support still shaped each campaign.

### 1. The three-zone framework

The **Tungabhadra or Raichur doab**, between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers, was a long-contested fertile frontier. The **Krishna-Godavari basin** combined agrarian wealth with routes and eastern ports. The **Konkan and Goa-Dabhol coast** connected the interior to Indian Ocean commerce and the horse trade.

These stakes explain recurrence. A temporary victory rarely ended rivalry because the underlying resource geography remained.

### 2. Why “Hindu versus Muslim” fails as a sufficient cause

Religion and temple or mosque politics could sharpen conflict and supply rhetoric. Yet the pattern of alliances and recruitment defeats a sealed-bloc account:

- Vijayanagara fought the Hindu Hoysalas and later the Gajapati rulers.
- Warangal could align with the Bahmanis against Vijayanagara.
- Deva Raya I's house entered a political marriage with Firuz Shah Bahmani.
- Deva Raya II recruited Muslim cavalrymen and archers.
- Bahmani rulers used Hindu revenue personnel and negotiated with non-Muslim chiefs.

The correct verdict is not “religion was irrelevant.” It is that political geography, dynastic survival and military resources explain the recurrent structure of conflict more powerfully.

### 3. The 1367 Mudkal/Tungabhadra war

Satish Chandra associates the conflict with:

- a Bahmani advance across the Tungabhadra;
- artillery used by both sides;
- Bahmani superiority in cavalry and artillery;
- prolonged violence against non-combatants;
- exhaustion and restoration of an earlier balance in the doab.

The unusual historical point is the reported agreement that helpless and unarmed inhabitants should not be slaughtered in future wars. This did not create modern humanitarian law, nor did it end violence. It shows rival courts recognizing that endless devastation damaged a region both needed to rule.

### 4. Diplomacy remained part of rivalry

Deva Raya I's defeat by Firuz Shah Bahmani was followed by indemnity and a political marriage. Such a marriage did not create lasting peace, but it demonstrates that medieval interstate relations combined war, tribute, negotiation and kinship.

The same logic appears later when Rama Raya played rival Deccan Sultanates against one another. Alliances were instruments of balance, not permanent religious camps.

### 5. Conflict produced adaptation

Repeated fighting encouraged:

```text
enemy advantage observed
        ↓
specialists recruited
        ↓
weapons and tactics adapted
        ↓
new demands on revenue and supply
        ↓
further competition for land and ports
```

This feedback connects military history to economy and administration. Cavalry needs horses; horses need ports and merchants; armies need land revenue; forts need provisioning.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Emphasizing resources sanitizes religious violence and desecration.

**Response:** A material explanation should not deny ideological mobilization or event-specific destruction. It asks why conflict repeatedly centred on particular zones and why alliances crossed religious boundaries. The most accurate account keeps both: material and political structures explain recurrence; religious rhetoric could intensify particular wars.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** Tungabhadra doab, Krishna-Godavari basin and Konkan/Goa are the core spatial triad.
- **Mains:** organize the answer by resource chain rather than ruler list.
- **Trap:** artillery in the 1367 account does not mean every later victory had one technological cause.
- **Answer line:** *The Deccan rivalry joined agrarian frontier, maritime supply and military learning, while diplomacy repeatedly interrupted but never removed structural competition.*

2. The Krishna-Godavari basin joined cultivation to routes and ports.
3. Goa and Dabhol connected trade to imported horses.
4. Imported horses mattered especially for competitive cavalry.
5. Geography created incentives but did not mechanically decide campaigns.

### Original Mains application

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Analyse the Vijayanagara-Bahmani conflict through political geography. Does this explanation leave any role for religion?

**Model answer**

The recurrent conflict is best organized around three strategic zones. The Tungabhadra or Raichur doab combined fertile revenue with forts and a forward frontier. The Krishna-Godavari basin connected agrarian surplus to inland routes and eastern ports. The Konkan-Goa-Dabhol coast yielded customs and access to imported horses required by competitive cavalry forces.

These resources formed connected chains. A port supplied horses and revenue; land supported troops; forts protected movement and cultivation. Hence territorial victory mattered when it changed the capacity to finance, mount and provision armies. The 1367 Mudkal-Tungabhadra war, with artillery and cavalry in the narrative, illustrates how technological competition interacted with the doab. Its reported agreement against slaughter of helpless inhabitants also shows that devastation could undermine the governable resource base.

Religion cannot be deleted. Court rhetoric, sacred patronage and episodes of desecration could intensify conflict. However, Vijayanagara fought Hindu rivals, recruited Muslim cavalrymen, and entered political marriage with Firuz Shah Bahmani; Warangal also aligned with the Bahmanis.

Therefore, religion shaped legitimation and particular acts, while geography, revenue, ports, military supply and changing alliances explain the conflict's recurrent structure.

**Why this earns marks:** It maps each zone to a capability, integrates the 1367 example, acknowledges religion without making it monocausal and uses cross-boundary evidence.

*Dynastic change repeatedly reassembled imperial authority; it neither guaranteed continuity nor created a wholly new political world each time.*

### Intuition: continuity can survive a change of ruling family

A state may change its ruling house while retaining officers, temples, claims, military networks and a prestigious capital. Vijayanagara's dynasty changes are best read as elite reconfiguration.

The analogy must not hide discontinuity. Each seizure altered who controlled appointments, revenue and provincial alliances.

### 1. Sangama consolidation

Harihara I and Bukka I established the early Tungabhadra core. Bukka's period is associated with expansion and the campaign against Madurai, remembered in Gangadevi's Sanskrit *Madhuravijayam*. The poem is valuable for conquest memory and royal legitimacy, but it is court literature, not neutral reportage.

Harihara II extended influence towards the eastern coast and contested western access. The dynasty's importance lies in turning a regional base into an imperial project.

### 2. Deva Raya I: water and diplomacy

**Deva Raya I (1404–22)** fought Firuz Shah Bahmani, accepted a political marriage settlement and undertook major water works. A dam across the Tungabhadra and canals addressed the capital's water needs and irrigated nearby fields.

UPSC Prelims 2023 Q48 asks the exact ruler associated with a large Tungabhadra dam and a canal-cum-aqueduct to the capital. The locally held paper's exact options name Devaraya I, Mallikarjuna, Vira Vijaya and Virupaksha. No official local key is available; the answer **Devaraya I** is a high-confidence inference from the canonical and book evidence, not an officially certified key.

### 3. Deva Raya II: adaptation and prosperity

**Deva Raya II (1425–46)** strengthened the army by employing Muslim cavalrymen and archers and adapting practices associated with Sultanate warfare. This was a response to the Bahmani advantage in mounted archery and horses.

Nicolo Conti and Abdur Razzaq described the scale, cultivation and wealth of Vijayanagara during the broader fifteenth-century high phase. Their spectacular estimates require corroboration, but their observations support the picture of a large and prosperous capital.

### 4. From Sangama crisis to Saluva and Tuluva rule

After Deva Raya II, succession disputes, powerful ministers and increasingly autonomous feudatories weakened central reach. **Saluva Narasimha** seized the throne and restored a measure of order. The Saluva phase was followed by the Tuluvas.

This sequence demonstrates a recurring mechanism:

```text
succession weakness
      ↓
military intermediary accumulates practical power
      ↓
dynastic seizure
      ↓
old imperial claims and institutions are reused
```

### 5. Krishnadevaraya's apex

**Krishnadevaraya (1509–29)** of the Tuluva dynasty:

- restored internal order;
- defeated the Gajapatis and Deccan rivals;
- won the major Raichur campaign against Bijapur in 1520;
- invested in tanks, towns, temples and court culture;
- authored or is associated with the Telugu *Amuktamalyada*;
- maintained broadly cooperative relations with Portuguese actors.

His reign was a high point of imperial assertion, not proof of uniform prosperity or the disappearance of nayaka power.

### 6. Achyuta, Sadashiva and Rama Raya

After Krishnadevaraya, **Achyuta Raya** ruled amid continuing factional pressures. Under nominal ruler **Sadashiva Raya**, effective power rested with **Aliya Rama Raya** and his circle. Rama Raya's shifting interventions among Deccan Sultanates temporarily preserved leverage but accumulated hostility and risk.

He was not the founder of the Aravidu dynasty. **Tirumala**, after the 1565 catastrophe, established Aravidu continuation from new centres.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Four dynasties mean there was no meaningful Vijayanagara continuity.

**Response:** A single unchanged bureaucracy did not survive for three centuries. Yet dynastic successors reused the capital's prestige, territorial claims, military networks, temple patronage and political vocabulary. “Continuity through reassembly” captures both persistence and change.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims sequence:** Sangama → Saluva → Tuluva → Aravidu.

- **PYQ link:** 2023 Q48, exact wording and options retained; answer explicitly labelled inferred.
- **Answer line:** *Vijayanagara's longevity rested less on uninterrupted dynastic succession than on repeated reassembly of an imperial coalition.*

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “Vijayanagara's political continuity was repeatedly produced through dynastic change.” Discuss.

**Model answer**

Vijayanagara was not governed by one uninterrupted family. Its sequence—Sangama, Saluva, Tuluva and Aravidu—shows repeated elite reassembly within a continuing imperial project.

The Sangamas created the Tungabhadra core and expanded south and east. Deva Raya I linked kingship to irrigation and diplomacy; Deva Raya II reorganized the army and presided over a prosperous phase. After his death, succession disputes and provincial autonomy weakened the line. Saluva Narasimha's seizure restored order by replacing the dynasty rather than abandoning the imperial framework.

The Tuluvas again concentrated power. Krishnadevaraya's campaigns, Raichur victory, water works, literary patronage and Portuguese relations represented an apex. Yet later factionalism placed effective authority under Rama Raya while Sadashiva remained nominal ruler. Talikota destroyed Hampi's great-power centre, but Tirumala and the Aravidus continued from Penukonda and later capitals.

Continuity should not imply an unchanged state: each transition altered the centre-nayaka balance and territorial reach. Nevertheless, capitals, titles, temples, military networks and claims were repeatedly inherited.

Thus, Vijayanagara endured through negotiated institutional and elite reconstruction, not through simple dynastic permanence.

### Visual: central ambition operating through layered intermediaries

```text
                         RAYA
           war · justice · appointments · patronage
                          |
              ministers and great nobles
                          |
                 rajya / mandala
            princes, governors, commanders
                          |
                  nadu → sthala
                          |
                    grama / village
                          |

AMARAM: territorial revenue ----------------> troops and service
               ^                                   |
```

*The same intermediary who extended royal power could acquire the resources to bargain with or resist the centre.*

### Intuition: delegation creates reach and dependence

A ruler who cannot directly administer every locality delegates revenue and military tasks. This extends reach quickly. But the delegate gains local information, followers and income, which can become bargaining power.

The analogy is not modern federalism. Nayakas did not possess constitutionally protected jurisdictions; their rights and duties rested on service, power and changing royal relationships.

### 1. Kingship and territorial levels

The **raya** embodied war leadership, justice, protection and patronage. Krishnadevaraya's *Amuktamalyada* presents normative counsel that a king should protect the good, punish wrongdoing and levy taxes moderately. Such advice reveals an ideal of kingship, not proof that every ruler followed it.

The kingdom used territorial terms such as:

- **rajya/mandala** for provincial-scale units;
- **nadu** for a district-like level;
- **sthala** for a smaller territorial division;
- **grama** for village.

Usage varied regionally and over time. Do not turn a flexible vocabulary into one perfectly uniform chart.

### 2. The amaram-nayaka mechanism

An **amaram** was a territorial revenue-service assignment associated with a **nayaka** or military intermediary. The core exchange was:

```text
right to draw assigned revenue
             ↓
maintain troops, administer locally and render service
             ↓
support campaigns and imperial claims
```

Many nayakas developed hereditary bases. Their households, forts, followers and local connections made them more than salaried officers.

### 3. Why amaram was useful

The system:

- converted dispersed agrarian resources into troops;
- allowed rapid mobilization through established local leaders;
- connected frontier administration with military command;
- reduced the need for direct central management of every village;
- helped integrate newly conquered zones.

Its strength was political flexibility.

### 4. Why the same system constrained the centre

Hereditary command created risks:

- revenue could be retained locally;
- loyalty became negotiable during succession conflict;
- powerful nayakas could intervene in dynastic politics;
- military defeat at the centre could accelerate regional autonomy.

The system did not make nayakas automatically independent. It made imperial power relational.

### 5. Amaram is not simply iqta

Both amaram and the Sultanate **iqta** linked revenue to service, so functional comparison is useful. Institutional identity is not.

| Test | Amaram-nayaka | Transferable iqta ideal |
|---|---|---|
| Political setting | Vijayanagara regional military service | Delhi Sultanate revenue assignment |
| Hereditary tendency | often strong local hereditary base | transfer intended to restrain local entrenchment |
| Local administration | nayaka could exercise broad territorial authority | muqti's rights bounded by central assignment |
| Analytical use | negotiated military-fiscal power | centrally rotated fiscal-military delegation |

Actual iqtas also varied, so neither column should be treated as a timeless pure type.

### 6. The historiographical debate

Four interpretations emphasize different evidence:

1. **Segmentary state:** ritual sovereignty at the centre with strong local units.
2. **Centralized state:** taxation, court, army, records, capital and royal intervention show substantial capacity.
3. **Military-fiscal state:** revenue assignments, cavalry, forts and service connected territory to war.
4. **Negotiated or integrative state:** central control was stronger in cores and depended on bargains in peripheral regions.

The safest synthesis is **variable negotiated centralization**. It recognizes a commanding court without pretending that commands were equally effective everywhere.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Hereditary nayakas prove Vijayanagara was merely feudal.

**Response:** “Feudal” imports a European model and can obscure royal taxation, urban institutions, court intervention and changing service relations. Heredity demonstrates local power, not the absence of an imperial centre. Explain the mechanism rather than applying an undefined label.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** amaram is associated with nayaka military service, not Mahmud Gawan's taraf.
- **Mains:** compare functions while retaining institutional difference from iqta.
- **Trap:** ayagar descriptions should not be projected uniformly across every dynasty and region.
- **Answer line:** *Vijayanagara was centralized in aspiration and significant capacity, but exercised much of that capacity through locally rooted military intermediaries.*

### Revision notes

1. The raya represented military, judicial and patronage authority.
2. Normative kingship texts describe ideals, not uniform practice.
3. Rajya/mandala, nadu, sthala and grama were territorial levels.
4. Terminology and practice varied across regions and dynasties.
5. Amaram connected territorial revenue with military service.
6. Nayakas maintained troops and exercised local authority.
7. Many nayaka bases became hereditary.
8. Heredity enabled mobilization and constrained central discipline.
9. Amaram and iqta can be compared functionally but not equated.
10. Segmentary and centralized models each capture partial evidence.
11. Military-fiscal analysis explains the land-service connection.
12. Variable negotiated centralization is the most balanced verdict.

Provincial governors held courts, appointed personnel and commanded armies. Such evidence limits any image of direct control radiating evenly from Hampi.

The court made appointments, led campaigns, mobilized large forces, claimed taxation, built hydraulic and urban infrastructure and intervened in provinces. The capital, treasury, royal patronage and repeated attempts to discipline intermediaries show material capacity. Nor was amaram static: its operation varied by dynasty and region.

A military-fiscal interpretation explains how revenue assignments supported war, while an integrative interpretation explains changing bargains between core and periphery. Neither European feudalism nor a Delhi-style transferable bureaucracy is an exact model.

Therefore, Vijayanagara combined substantial central ambition and capacity with layered, locally rooted authority. “Variable negotiated centralization” better captures the evidence than an unqualified segmentary label.

### Visual: how water could become state capacity

```text
river / tank / reservoir
          ↓
water storage and diversion
          ↓
more reliable cultivation + urban supply
          ↓
larger or steadier assessable produce
          ↓
revenue, food and labour for court and army
```

*Irrigation could enlarge political capacity, but it did not automatically distribute benefits equally.*

### Intuition: infrastructure is a relationship, not merely a structure

A canal matters only when labour builds and maintains it, fields can receive its water, officials assess output and producers retain enough to continue cultivation. The structure is visible; the social bargain around it is harder to see.

This is why a dam should not be praised only as royal engineering. Ask who supplied labour, who controlled water, who collected the additional revenue and who bore risk.

### 1. Deva Raya I's hydraulic works

Satish Chandra records that Deva Raya I constructed a dam across the Tungabhadra to bring canals towards the city and irrigate neighbouring fields. The text reports an increase in revenue, but the precise figure comes through a narrative source and should not be generalized across the empire.

The analytical chain is:

- water for the capital improved provisioning;
- irrigation could stabilize nearby cultivation;
- increased production widened the taxable base;
- revenue supported the court, construction and military expenditure.

The ruler also built a dam on the Haridra river for irrigation.

### 2. Tanks and the dry-zone environment

Peninsular rainfall is seasonal and spatially uneven. Tanks, reservoirs, canals and wells therefore helped store monsoon water and support settlement. Large works required political coordination, but temples, local communities, chiefs and later rulers also invested in water.

Do not attribute the whole hydraulic landscape to one emperor. Hampi's water system accumulated over time.

### 3. Revenue demand varied

Evidence records different shares for different crops and conditions. The central principle is more important than one memorable fraction:

| Variable | Why assessment could differ |
|---|---|
| Crop | rice, millets, pulses and oilseeds had different yields and values |
| Soil | fertility changed expected output |
| Irrigation | wet cultivation could support a different assessment from dry land |
| Local rights | temples, chiefs, nayakas and village officers held overlapping claims |
| Political condition | war, remissions or local bargaining could alter collection |

Therefore, “the Vijayanagara land tax was always one fixed share” is unsafe.

### 4. Village intermediaries

Village and local officers—often discussed through the **ayagar/ayyangar** framework—performed record, revenue and service functions. Mature descriptions of the system should not be projected unchanged across every territory and reign.

Local institutions connected cultivators to:

- royal or provincial demands;
- nayaka claims;
- temple endowments;
- occupational services;
- irrigation maintenance.

This layering could make administration workable while multiplying demands on producers.

### 5. Prosperity and its distribution

Foreign visitors celebrated capital wealth, markets and royal stores. That evidence does not directly establish peasant welfare. Agrarian expansion could simultaneously:

- enlarge cultivation;
- feed cities;
- support temples and crafts;
- enrich courts and intermediaries;
- impose labour and revenue burdens on cultivators.

Historical significance lies in this double movement: productive expansion and unequal appropriation.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Because exact empire-wide tax rates are unavailable, little can be said about agrarian relations.

**Response:** Uncertainty about one universal rate does not prevent analysis of mechanisms. Irrigation, differentiated assessment, intermediary claims and the conversion of produce into state capacity are all evidenced. The correct response is a qualified model, not silence or false precision.

- **Answer line:** *Vijayanagara's agrarian strength rested on managed water and layered extraction, making irrigation both productive infrastructure and political power.*

### Visual: the horse-port-fiscal chain

```text

              ↓

              ↓

```

*A cavalry horse was the end-product of a commercial, diplomatic and logistical chain.*

### Intuition: technology works only inside a system

A superior horse or cannon does not win by itself. It needs trained personnel, maintenance, money, ammunition, transport, intelligence and commanders. Technology changes possibilities; institutions decide whether those possibilities become battlefield advantage.

### 1. The composite army

Vijayanagara and Bahmani forces combined:

- cavalry;
- mounted archers;
- infantry;
- elephants;
- forts and garrisons;
- artillery and firearms in changing proportions;
- commanders with locally recruited followings.

Numbers in chronicles are often inflated. Compare capabilities instead of repeating spectacular totals.

### 2. Deva Raya II's adaptation

Firishta's account attributes Bahmani advantages to stronger horses and mounted archers. Deva Raya II responded by recruiting Muslim cavalrymen, granting them assignments and training a larger body of archers.

This is an example of **selective institutional borrowing**. It establishes practical recruitment across religious boundaries; it does not prove that all political tension disappeared.

### 3. Forts and supply

Raichur, Mudgal and other forts controlled more than defensible walls. They anchored:

- roads and river crossings;
- grain stores and garrisons;
- frontier taxation;
- communication between capital and army;
- claims over surrounding cultivation.

A battle for a fort was therefore also a battle for the network it protected.

### 4. Artillery: important, never sufficient alone

The 1367 conflict contains an early report of artillery on both sides. By the sixteenth century guns and firearms mattered more in Deccan warfare. Yet outcomes still depended on cavalry, battlefield command, coalition cohesion, terrain and logistics.

Avoid “Talikota was won only by artillery.” This replaces a multi-causal event with a technological slogan.

### 5. Portuguese entry into the west-coast system

After Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498 and the Portuguese captured Goa in 1510, they tried to control maritime routes and the horse trade. Vijayanagara did not simply submit or remain isolated. It negotiated pragmatically because Portuguese shipping could affect access to horses and coastal commerce.

UPSC Prelims 2024 Q56 asks:

> Who of the following rulers of medieval India gave permission to the Portuguese to build a fort at Bhatkal?

Options: Krishnadevaraya; Narasimha Saluva; Muhammad Shah III; Yusuf Adil Shah.

The locally held official Set-A key identifies **Krishnadevaraya**. Historical caution remains necessary: the precise fort-permission wording rests mainly on Portuguese testimony, so do not invent a grant text, date or detailed treaty.

### 6. Cooperation had limits

Krishnadevaraya's relationship with Portuguese actors was generally cooperative, especially in the setting of horse supply and rivalry with Bijapur. But:

- Vijayanagara did not become a Portuguese dependency;
- Portuguese commercial goals differed from Vijayanagara's territorial goals;
- control of sea lanes could pressure all coastal states;
- Krishnadevaraya appears not to have built a navy equal to the new maritime challenge.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** If horses came from overseas, Portuguese control alone explains Vijayanagara's military fortunes.

**Response:** Imported horses created leverage, but Indian, Arab and Persian merchants predated Portuguese dominance; ports changed hands; local cavalry organization, revenue, commanders, forts and alliances remained indispensable. Maritime power altered the military economy without wholly determining it.

### Original Mains application

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine how maritime commerce shaped the military politics of Vijayanagara and the Bahmani-Deccan states.

**Model answer**

Maritime commerce affected Deccan warfare because cavalry power depended partly on imported horses from Arabia and Iran. West-coast ports such as Goa and Dabhol therefore joined customs revenue, merchant access and military supply.

Bahmani and Vijayanagara competition over coastal zones was not separate from inland contests. Horses arriving by sea required purchase, transport, trained riders and revenue. Mahmud Gawan's western expansion over Goa and Dabhol strengthened Bahmani overseas trade and injured Vijayanagara's access. Deva Raya II's recruitment of Muslim cavalrymen and archers shows the institutional adaptation demanded by this competitive environment.

Portuguese capture of Goa in 1510 introduced an armed maritime actor able to influence horse distribution and sea lanes. Krishnadevaraya maintained generally cooperative relations; the officially keyed 2024 Bhatkal PYQ identifies him as the ruler who permitted a Portuguese fort, although the exact formulation derives mainly from Portuguese testimony.

Maritime leverage was not absolute. Arab, Persian and Indian merchants, inland forts, agrarian revenue, commanders and artillery remained important.

Thus, ports converted oceanic commerce into inland military capacity, while Deccan states and Portuguese actors negotiated an unequal but not one-sided relationship.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the complete supply chain, uses Gawan, Deva Raya II and Krishnadevaraya, and qualifies both Portuguese power and the Bhatkal provenance.

*Hampi worked as a city-region because these systems reinforced one another.*

### Intuition: a capital is an organized flow

A city is not simply a dense group of buildings. It depends on flows of water, food, people, goods, information and armed protection. Hampi's landscape made those flows visible.

The limit is that surviving stone favours elite, sacred and durable structures. Perishable homes, ordinary labour and unequal access are less visible.

### 1. An urban region of enclosures

Abdur Razzaq described multiple enclosures, markets, canals and extraordinary wealth. Nicolo Conti also emphasized scale. UNESCO's official Hampi property page describes more than 1,600 surviving remains, including forts, riverside features, royal and sacred complexes, pillared halls, gateways, stables, water structures, bazaars and suburban settlements.

The evidence supports integrated planning, not the literal accuracy of every traveller's numerical estimate.

### 2. Royal and sacred centres

Hampi contained distinct but connected zones:

- sacred landscapes associated with Virupaksha and the Tungabhadra;
- monumental temple complexes such as Vitthala;
- a royal centre with ceremonial and courtly structures;
- market streets linked to temples and festivals;
- suburban settlements and agrarian spaces;
- fortifications using terrain as well as masonry.

Religious processions could structure commercial streets; royal ceremony could display military order; water could serve ritual, household and productive uses.

### 3. Markets and long-distance trade

Travellers described bazaars selling horses, textiles, precious stones, food and luxury goods. Inland roads connected the capital to agrarian zones and to ports such as Goa, Dabhol and Bhatkal. Arab, Persian, Indian and Portuguese merchants participated in overlapping circuits.

Markets therefore matter politically:

```text
market exchange
     ↓
urban provisioning + customs and transaction revenue
     ↓
merchant access to distant goods and horses
     ↓
court consumption and military supply
```

### 4. Temples as institutions

Temples were not modern banks, but they could:

- receive land and donations;
- employ priests, artisans, performers and service groups;
- organize festivals and processions;
- support markets around sacred centres;
- redistribute resources;
- make royal legitimacy visible.

Calling them “only religious” misses their social and economic roles; calling them modern commercial corporations is equally misleading.

### 5. Coinage

Vijayanagara gold **varaha**, often called the **pagoda** in later usage, expressed sovereignty and supported high-value exchange. Bahmani coinage likewise carried dynastic and religious-political claims.

Coins establish:

- rulers and titles;
- metal and iconography;
- minting and exchange;
- political messaging.

Coin finds do not prove that every village tax or transaction was monetized.

### 6. Living heritage and conservation

UNESCO treats Hampi as an integrated cultural landscape. It identifies pressures from development, encroachment, land-use change, visitor infrastructure and modern additions around living sacred spaces. This creates a present-day governance problem: conservation must protect archaeological integrity while recognizing continuing worship, pilgrimage, settlement and livelihoods.

This current link is relevant because the historical city itself joined sacred, economic and residential functions. Conservation cannot safely isolate monuments from their landscape.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Monumental remains and travellers prove Hampi was universally prosperous.

**Response:** They prove extraordinary urban investment, exchange and courtly concentration. They do not measure household distribution, rural burdens or all residents' living standards. Prosperous capital and unequal society can coexist.

### Intuition: a visitor sees what access permits

A diplomat entering a palace may accurately describe ceremony while knowing little about a distant village. A merchant notices prices and goods. A court poet presents an idealized moral world. Their evidence is useful precisely when we ask what they were positioned to observe.

### 1. A plural but hierarchical social field

The region included:

- royal households and nobles;
- Brahmans and temple or matha personnel;
- nayakas, soldiers and fort communities;
- Muslim cavalrymen and officials;
- merchants from local and overseas networks;
- artisans, builders, performers and scribes;
- cultivators, pastoralists and forest communities;
- enslaved, captive and other dependent persons;
- women across all these groups.

Varna and jati shaped status, occupation and residence, but social groups were not immobile sealed compartments. Military recruitment, trade, migration and service created movement without abolishing hierarchy.

### 2. Dependence, captivity and labour

War generated captives and enslavement. Court, household, military and productive labour could include dependent people. This must not be mapped mechanically onto Atlantic racial chattel slavery, which had a different legal and commercial formation.

The evidence is uneven. Acknowledging dependence is necessary; inventing precise proportions or uniform conditions is not.

### 3. Women: agency and constraint

Named evidence reveals several kinds of visibility:

- **Gangadevi** authored the Sanskrit *Madhuravijayam*.
- royal women could patronize institutions and participate in dynastic politics;
- inscriptions and literary works preserve elite women's religious and charitable activity;
- Portuguese accounts describe women in specialized courtly or urban roles;
- women also laboured in agrarian, artisanal, service, ritual and market settings, though documentation is thinner.

Agency at court does not prove equality across caste, class or region.

### 4. The 2021 Nuniz PYQ and its source problem

UPSC Prelims 2021 Q35 asks:

> According to Portuguese writer Nuniz, the women in Vijayanagara Empire were expert in which of the following areas?

It lists wrestling, astrology, accounting and soothsaying, with option D containing all four. The locally held official paper preserves this wording and options, but no official local key is available. **D is therefore an inferred, high-confidence answer—not an officially verified key.**

There is an attribution discrepancy: one repository summary associated a similar list with Paes, while the official question names Nuniz. For exam linkage, preserve UPSC's wording. In Mains, use “Portuguese accounts” unless a specific attribution has been independently verified.

### 5. Religion as institution and political practice

Vijayanagara rulers patronized Shaiva, Vaishnava and other Hindu institutions while employing Muslims. Bahmani rulers patronized mosques, madrasas and Sufi establishments while using Hindu officials and negotiating with non-Muslim chiefs.

Temples, mosques and dargahs:

- received property and patronage;
- organized ritual and learning;
- employed labour;
- attracted markets and visitors;
- connected rulers to communities.

This was neither uncomplicated harmony nor permanent civilizational war. Patronage, accommodation and destruction were event-specific political acts.

### 6. Composite culture: use the phrase carefully

“Composite culture” is useful only when attached to evidence:

- multilingual courts;
- mixed military recruitment;
- shared or adapted craft techniques;
- Persianate and southern architectural interaction;
- Dakhni linguistic development;
- commercial and diplomatic contact.

It should not erase conflict, hierarchy or coercion.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Elite and traveller sources are too biased to support social history.

**Response:** Bias limits range; it does not eliminate information. A traveller's list can establish that certain roles were visible in the observed milieu. An inscription can establish a grant. A building can establish patronage and labour. Corroboration and narrow claims turn partial evidence into responsible history.

*Architectural “amalgamation” was not random mixture: older forms were selected and assigned new urban, ritual and political functions.*

### Intuition: borrowing becomes historical when function changes

Builders rarely begin with a blank grammar. They inherit plans, materials and techniques, then adapt them to new patrons, ceremonies and cities. Vijayanagara architecture is significant because it made inherited Dravida forms operate at metropolitan scale while selectively using Islamicate forms in courtly buildings.

The limit: a shared arch or dome does not prove identical belief or equal social relations.

### 1. Multilingual court culture

Vijayanagara patronage supported **Telugu, Kannada, Sanskrit and Tamil**. Language choice could connect rulers to different literary publics and regions.

- Krishnadevaraya's Telugu *Amuktamalyada* combines devotion with normative reflections on kingship.
- Gangadevi's Sanskrit *Madhuravijayam* represents the Madurai campaign through courtly conquest memory.
- The **Ashtadiggajas** belong to the Telugu court tradition associated with Krishnadevaraya, though later canonization should not be treated as a complete contemporary attendance list.

Bahmani and successor courts operated through Persian and Arabic while interacting with Kannada, Marathi, Telugu and developing **Dakhni** cultural worlds. Language did not map neatly onto one religion.

### 2. Music and performance across political boundaries

Music and performance belonged to the same competitive patronage world as literature and architecture. Courts, temples, festivals and elite households required singers, instrumentalists, dancers, ritual specialists and teachers. Patronage could therefore move techniques and performers across political boundaries even when rulers remained military rivals.

```text
court and temple patronage
          ↓
festivals · recitation · dance · instrumental performance
          ↓
mobility of performers and techniques
          ↓
shared Deccan cultural traffic across rival states
```

This does not establish one uniform “Deccan music” controlled equally by both courts. The surviving evidence is strongest for elite and devotional patronage; it is weaker for reconstructing village repertoires, performers' social conditions or the experience of ordinary audiences. Nor does cultural traffic erase political conflict. The careful conclusion is that courtly and devotional performance crossed boundaries through patronage networks while remaining socially unequal and institutionally diverse.

### 3. Temple architecture: inherited Dravida grammar

Major Vijayanagara temples used:

- a sanctum and axial mandapas;
- walled enclosures;
- increasingly prominent **raya gopuras**, or monumental gateways;
- pillared halls;
- chariot streets and bazaars;
- **kalyana mandapas**, or ceremonial wedding halls;
- sculpted yali pillars;
- tanks and water channels.

The **Virupaksha Temple** stands in an older sacred landscape expanded under Vijayanagara; it was not wholly created by one ruler. The **Vitthala complex** includes pillared halls, a bazaar street, water features, a stone chariot shrine and a kalyana mandapa. The stone chariot should not be called monolithic.

### 4. Royal and civic architecture

| Monument or form | Political-cultural use | Necessary caution |
|---|---|---|
| Mahanavami Dibba | royal festival, army review and ceremonial display | attribution to Krishnadevaraya's Odisha victory comes through Paes |
| Hazara Rama Temple | royal-centre temple with Ramayana reliefs | religious narrative also served courtly visibility |
| Lotus Mahal | conventional modern name for an arched courtly structure | original name and exact function remain uncertain |
| Elephant Stables | domed and arched complex conventionally linked to royal elephants | architecture alone cannot reconstruct all court practice |
| Queen's Bath | water, leisure and palace-zone design with arches | modern label and elite setting limit social inference |

The strongest synthesis claim is not “temples became Indo-Islamic.” Vijayanagara temples remained predominantly Dravidian, while secular and royal buildings display more visible arched and domed vocabulary.

### 5. Bahmani architecture in comparison

Gulbarga's fort and Jama Masjid belong to the early capital landscape. Bidar's fort, palaces and Mahmud Gawan madrasa express a later Persianate-Deccan court. Coloured tile work, arches, domes and transregional scholarship interacted with local materials and artisans.

Comparison should identify function:

- Hampi's raya gopura enlarged sacred-urban visibility.
- Bidar's madrasa made scholarly patronage and ministerial prestige monumental.
- Both courts used architecture to order space and legitimate authority.
- Their forms differed because ritual institutions, inherited grammars and courtly networks differed.

The directive **elucidate** requires explanation through examples, not a list of monuments. The answer must show:

Architecture records organized labour, material knowledge and surplus concentration. Narrative reliefs, festival platforms and gateways staged royal order. Yet monuments foreground patrons. They do not tell us directly how workers experienced construction or how benefits were distributed.

Moving a capital can place a ruler nearer strategic regions, court networks and cultural resources. It can also weaken older elites and elevate new ones. The move from Gulbarga to Bidar should be read in this wider sense.

### 1. Early Bahmani sovereignty

Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah's foundation in 1347 created an independent Deccan sultanate. Gulbarga served as the early capital and military-political centre.

The state governed a region containing:

- older landed and warrior groups;
- Deccani Muslim families;
- newly arriving West and Central Asian elites;
- Hindu revenue personnel;
- merchants linked to inland and maritime trade;
- Sufi, mosque and scholarly institutions.

Bahmani rule was Persianate in important court forms but necessarily Deccan in personnel, resources and negotiation.

### 2. Firuz Shah Bahmani

Firuz Shah Bahmani (1397–1422) is associated with:

- interest in religious and natural sciences;
- multilingual learning in the chronicled image;
- patronage of learned people;
- attention to Chaul and Dabhol;
- expansion towards Berar and Kherla;
- recurrent war and diplomacy with Vijayanagara;
- political marriages.

His court's cultural ambition and commercial interest show that military history did not exhaust Bahmani statecraft.

### 3. Ahmad Shah I and Bidar

Ahmad Shah I (1422–36) shifted the capital from Gulbarga to **Bidar**. He expanded against Warangal and became associated in later memory with the Sufi saint **Gesu Daraz**.

The saintly connection is relevant to royal legitimacy and Deccan religious culture. Full Sufi doctrine belongs to the following topic; here the point is that courts linked sovereignty to respected religious institutions and memories.

### 4. Administrative outline

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| Sultan and court | sovereignty, appointments, war and patronage |
| Wakil-i-sultanat | high executive coordination; Gawan is the major case |
| Taraf | province or major territorial division |
| Tarafdar | provincial governor with military-revenue resources |
| Khalisa | resources reserved more directly for the crown |
| Fort governor | strategic command over defended sites |
| Local revenue personnel | connected the court to agrarian society |

The provincial system enabled expansion but gave large resources to governors. Central authority therefore depended on appointment, rotation, fiscal information and factional balance.

### 5. Economy and society

Bahmani agrarian revenue supported forts, cavalry, cities and court culture. Ports such as Chaul, Dabhol and later contested Goa linked the state to Gulf and Red Sea commerce. Textiles, horses, luxury goods and other commodities moved through these networks.

Deccani Brahmans and other local personnel had important roles in revenue administration. This does not prove absence of discrimination; it shows that workable governance crossed simple religious divisions.

### 6. Cultural significance

The Bahmani realm helped connect northern Persianate traditions with southern languages, crafts and institutions. Persian and Arabic learning interacted with emerging Dakhni and regional settings. Gulbarga and Bidar became centres of architecture, scholarship and Sufi presence.

“Bridge” is a useful metaphor only if its traffic is named: people, texts, techniques, merchants, military specialists and administrative practices.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Bahmani culture was merely imported from Iran and therefore external to Deccan history.

**Response:** Afaqi migration and Persianate forms were significant, but institutions operated through local revenue, Deccani elites, regional languages, Indian merchants and indigenous craft. The historical product was interaction, not pure transplantation.

### Intuition: reform changes who loses discretion

A rule that clarifies salaries and audits obligations may appear technical. Politically, it reduces someone's freedom to retain money, troops or forts. Those who lose discretion can organize resistance.

The analogy has a limit: Bahmani factionalism involved court honour, settlement history, patronage, kinship and sometimes sectarian alignment, not merely modern bureaucratic resistance.

### 1. Gawan's position

**Mahmud Gawan**, of Iranian origin, rose to become **wakil-i-sultanat**, the leading minister or prime minister, under Muhammad Shah III. He dominated Bahmani affairs for roughly two decades, conventionally c. 1463–1481/82.

He was a minister, not a sultan.

### 2. Expansion and the coast

Gawan extended Bahmani influence eastward and made major gains on the western coast, including **Goa and Dabhol**. These conquests:

- damaged Vijayanagara's maritime access;
- strengthened overseas trade with Iran and Iraq;
- improved access to strategic horse and luxury-goods circuits;
- joined commercial and military policy.

Expansion, however, increased the administrative burden he then tried to address.

### 3. Eight tarafs

The kingdom's four large provinces were reorganized into **eight tarafs**. Smaller units could reduce the concentration of territorial resources under a few governors.

Gawan also sought to:

- fix noble salaries;
- define the troop contingent attached to remuneration;
- distinguish cash salary from revenue assignment;
- reserve **khalisa** land or resources for the sultan;
- measure land and clarify assessment;
- place some fort commands more directly under royal control.

These were attempts, not proof of a fully standardized bureaucracy.

### 4. The institutional logic

| Reform | Problem addressed | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Eight tarafs | over-mighty provincial governors | new boundaries still required enforcement |
| Fixed salary/troops | uncertain service and retained surplus | records could not guarantee compliance |
| Khalisa | weak crown fiscal base | local collection remained mediated |
| Land measurement | uncertain assessment | coverage and implementation were uneven |
| Fort control | provincial military autonomy | court appointments remained factional |

### 5. Madrasa and scholarly patronage

At Bidar, Gawan built a major three-storeyed madrasa known for coloured tiles, teaching and facilities for scholars and students. It demonstrates:

- Persianate educational patronage;
- transregional scholarly links;
- concentration of resources at the capital;
- skilled architectural work.

It does not prove universal education across Bahmani society.

### 6. Deccanis and Afaqis

**Deccani** broadly referred to older-established groups in the Deccan political world; **Afaqi** or **gharib** often referred to newer overseas entrants. The labels could interact with patronage networks, language, regional identity and sectarian affiliation.

They were not:

- biologically fixed races;
- perfectly united parties;
- categories with unchanged membership and interests;
- the sole cause of every political crisis.

Gawan, associated with the Afaqi side, became vulnerable to hostile intrigue. His execution around 1481/82 removed a powerful minister and exposed the instability of the elite coalition.

### 7. Disintegration

Gawan's death accelerated Bahmani fragmentation but did not single-handedly cause it. Provincial resources, succession, court faction, military command and regional ambition combined over time. The successor states were:

**Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Berar and Bidar.**

Their emergence was a process, not one simultaneous event.

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Since the reforms did not prevent breakup, Gawan failed.

**Response:** Outcome alone is an incomplete measure. The reforms correctly targeted concentrated provincial power, uncertain service and weak crown resources. Their fragility reveals the political precondition of administration: centralization requires an elite coalition capable of enforcing it. His failure is therefore diagnostically important.

- **Answer line:** *Gawan strengthened the instruments of centralization but could not secure the political coalition on which those instruments depended.*

*The battle broke the concentration of imperial power at Hampi; it did not erase every Vijayanagara institution or successor claim.*

### Intuition: destroying a capital is not the same as deleting a polity

A capital concentrates treasury, ceremony, archives, workshops, markets and command. Its destruction can cripple an empire even if rulers, troops and provincial networks escape. The state may continue, but with a changed scale and geography.

### 1. Rama Raya's strategy

Rama Raya used shifting alliances among Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda and other Deccan powers. This balance-of-power strategy won short-term leverage but repeatedly intervened in the rivals' disputes and generated shared resentment.

The coalition of 1565 should not be presented as timeless religious unity. The same sultanates had fought one another before and would do so again.

### 2. Names and participants

The battle is known as:

- **Talikota**;
- **Rakshasa-Tangadi/Rakshasi-Tangadi**;
- **Bannihatti/Banihatti**.

The principal coalition is commonly associated with **Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda and Bidar**. Berar should not be inserted automatically into every list; accounts differ regarding participation and coordination.

### 3. Battlefield causation

Several factors interacted:

- coalition coordination;
- cavalry;
- artillery and firearms;
- command decisions;
- local terrain and knowledge;
- cohesion within Vijayanagara forces;
- capture and execution of Rama Raya;
- battlefield contingency.

Later stories of wholesale betrayal or one decisive technology require careful source testing. No exact troop or casualty total should be given without named provenance.

### 4. Why Hampi's sack mattered

Hampi concentrated:

- royal authority and ceremony;
- sacred legitimacy;
- markets and craft activity;
- water and provisioning systems;
- fortified and administrative space.

Its sack therefore disrupted more than residence. It destroyed the physical and symbolic concentration that made Vijayanagara a great power. UNESCO's official synthesis describes massive destruction of the city's physical fabric.

### 5. What survived

Tirumala withdrew and the **Aravidu dynasty** continued from **Penukonda**, later using other centres including Chandragiri. Imperial territory contracted.

Regional nayaka powers in Madurai, Tanjavur, Gingee and elsewhere gained greater autonomy. Temples, commercial routes, agrarian relations, artistic traditions and political titles persisted in changed forms.

Continuity does not minimize catastrophe:

| What ended or sharply weakened | What continued or changed form |
|---|---|
| Hampi's metropolitan great-power role | Aravidu dynastic claim |
| concentration of imperial military capacity | regional nayaka states |
| courtly and market functions at the old scale | temples and living ritual |
| pre-1565 balance of power | a new field of Deccan and southern states |

### 6. Wider significance

Bahmani fragmentation and Vijayanagara transformation produced a plural Deccan political field. Bijapur, Ahmadnagar and Golconda remained major powers. Nayaka states developed in the south. Portuguese coastal influence persisted. Later Mughal expansion confronted this already complex world.

Talikota was therefore neither:

- the end of all Hindu power in south India;
- restoration of a unified Bahmani empire;
- immediate Mughal conquest of the Deccan;

### Strongest objection and response

**Objection:** Calling Talikota a transformation understates the scale of destruction.

**Response:** Transformation is not a euphemism. It specifies what the destruction changed: metropolitan concentration, imperial command and regional balances. The term becomes accurate only when the sack and great-power collapse are stated before continuity.

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** “Talikota was a transformation of the Vijayanagara political order, not a simple end-date.” Discuss.

The victorious Sultanates themselves remained rivals.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks / 150 words

**Question:** Explain why the Vijayanagara-Bahmani rivalry cannot be understood without the horse trade.

**Model answer**

The horse trade connected Indian Ocean commerce to inland military competition. Deccan conditions made access to high-quality imported horses from Arabia and Iran important for cavalry and mounted archery.

West-coast ports such as Goa and Dabhol therefore carried strategic value beyond customs revenue. Merchants, shipping, payment and protected inland routes formed a supply chain linking the coast to armies contesting the Raichur doab. Mahmud Gawan's capture of Goa and Dabhol injured Vijayanagara and expanded Bahmani overseas trade. Deva Raya II's recruitment of Muslim cavalrymen and archers shows an institutional response to perceived Bahmani advantages.

The Portuguese later used control of Goa and maritime coercion to gain leverage over this existing trade, while Krishnadevaraya engaged them pragmatically.

Horse supply was not the sole cause of war: infantry, artillery, forts, revenue and commanders mattered. It was nevertheless a strategic connector between coast, commerce and state power.

**Why this earns marks:** It traces a complete supply mechanism, uses three named examples and avoids both maritime and technological determinism.

## 15 marks / 250 words

**Question:** Compare the military-fiscal foundations of Vijayanagara and the Bahmani kingdom.

**Model answer**

Both states converted agrarian and commercial resources into military force, but their institutional routes differed.

Vijayanagara used the amaram-nayaka relationship. Nayakas drew revenue from assigned territories, maintained troops and exercised local authority. This enabled rapid mobilization through established regional elites. Its hereditary tendency also gave commanders durable bases, making central power dependent on negotiated loyalty. Irrigation under rulers such as Deva Raya I enlarged cultivation and provisioning, while ports and horse imports sustained cavalry.

The Bahmani kingdom governed through tarafs and powerful provincial governors. Mahmud Gawan's redivision into eight tarafs, fixed salaries and troop obligations, khalisa reserves, land-measurement effort and tighter fort appointments attempted to reduce noble autonomy. Coastal gains at Goa and Dabhol linked overseas trade to cavalry supply and revenue.

Neither system was a complete bureaucracy. Vijayanagara was not merely decentralized, while Gawan's reforms were not fully implemented. Nayaka heredity constrained the raya; Deccani-Afaqi factional politics undermined Bahmani centralization.

Thus, both were military-fiscal states operating through intermediaries, but Vijayanagara institutionalized locally rooted nayaka service whereas Gawan tried to make provincial nobility more accountable to the sultan.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares common function and distinct form, integrates agrarian and maritime resources, and evaluates the limits of each system.

## 20 marks / 250 words

**Question:** “Conflict and cultural interaction were simultaneous processes in the medieval Deccan.” Evaluate with reference to Vijayanagara and the Bahmani kingdom.

**Model answer**

Vijayanagara-Bahmani relations were marked by recurrent war, but conflict did not create sealed cultural worlds. Both processes arose from participation in a shared Deccan political economy.

Competition centred on the Tungabhadra doab, Krishna-Godavari basin and Goa-Dabhol coast. The 1367 war involved artillery and severe violence, while later campaigns contested forts, revenue and horses. Yet political marriage linked Deva Raya I's house with Firuz Shah Bahmani, Warangal changed alliances, and Rama Raya later intervened in Sultanate rivalries. Deva Raya II recruited Muslim cavalrymen and archers, demonstrating military borrowing across religious boundaries.

Cultural interaction also appeared in multilingual courts, merchants, crafts and performance. Vijayanagara patronized Telugu, Kannada, Sanskrit and Tamil; Bahmani Bidar connected Persianate scholarship with Deccan languages and artisans. Courtly and devotional music moved through patronage networks without becoming one uniform or socially equal tradition. Hampi's secular arches and domes and the Mahmud Gawan madrasa show different forms of architectural exchange and patronage. Hindu personnel served in Bahmani revenue administration, while Vijayanagara rulers supported varied sects and employed Muslims.

“Composite culture” must not romanticize hierarchy, faction or desecration. Shared forms do not prove equality, and religion could intensify conflict.

The Deccan was therefore integrated through rivalry, migration, trade and selective borrowing at the same time.

**Why this earns marks:** It balances war and exchange, names political, military, linguistic and architectural evidence, and qualifies the composite-culture claim.

```text
+ Rama Raya's political order

Did not erase:
Aravidu dynastic claim
+ regional nayaka forces
+ temples, routes and agrarian relations
+ Vijayanagara artistic influence

Verdict:

```

## Source-criticism ladder

```text
1. What exactly is the claim?
2. Which source states or supports it?
3. When and for whom was that source produced?
4. What could its author or material form observe?
5. What independent evidence corroborates it?
6. What is the narrowest defensible inference?
7. What does the evidence leave unresolved?
```

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Origins and chronology

- Vijayanagara emerged in the context of weakening Tughlaq control and surviving southern powers.
- c. 1336 is the conventional foundation anchor; Harihara and Bukka are conventional founders.
- Their Warangal/Kampili, conversion and Vidyaranya narrative is disputed.
- The Bahmani kingdom began independent rule in 1347 under Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah.
- “Hasan Gangu” and the Brahman-patron explanation depend significantly on later tradition.
- Vijayanagara dynasties: **Sangama → Saluva → Tuluva → Aravidu**.
- Bahmani capital sequence: **Gulbarga → Bidar**, with the shift under Ahmad Shah I.

## 2. Strategic geography

- **Tungabhadra/Raichur doab:** fertile revenue, forts and frontier position.
- **Krishna-Godavari basin:** cultivation, inland routes and eastern ports.
- **Konkan/Goa-Dabhol coast:** customs, maritime trade and imported horses.
- Recurrent war arose because these zones repeatedly affected military and fiscal capacity.
- Political marriage, cross-religious recruitment and changing alliances disprove a sealed communal-bloc model.
- Religion remained relevant to legitimacy and particular acts but was not the sole structural cause.

## 3. Key Vijayanagara rulers

- **Deva Raya I (1404–22):** Tungabhadra dam/canal, Haridra work, war and political marriage with Firuz Shah.
- **Deva Raya II (1425–46):** cavalry and archer adaptation, Muslim recruitment, prosperous imperial phase.
- **Saluva Narasimha:** dynastic seizure amid late-Sangama crisis.
- **Krishnadevaraya (1509–29):** Tuluva apex, Raichur 1520, water/town and literary patronage, Portuguese relations.
- **Achyuta Raya:** successor amid factional continuity.
- **Sadashiva Raya:** nominal ruler while Rama Raya held effective power.
- **Rama Raya:** shifting Deccan alliances; captured and executed in 1565.
- **Tirumala:** Aravidu continuation after the Hampi catastrophe.

## 4. Vijayanagara administration

- Raya: war, justice, appointment and patronage.
- Territorial vocabulary: rajya/mandala → nadu → sthala → grama, with regional variation.
- Amaram connected revenue assignment to nayaka military service.
- Nayakas could administer locally, maintain troops and develop hereditary bases.
- The same system extended imperial reach and constrained central control.
- Amaram is not an exact duplicate of transferable iqta.
- Ayagar/ayyangar village-office descriptions should not be projected uniformly.
- Best state verdict: **variable negotiated centralization**.

## 5. Agrarian economy and water

- Tanks, canals, reservoirs and wells supported cultivation and urban provisioning.
- Deva Raya I's Tungabhadra works are the direct PYQ anchor.
- Assessment varied by crop, soil, irrigation and locality.
- Temples, nayakas, chiefs and village officers held layered claims.
- Agrarian expansion could enlarge both production and extraction.
- Royal revenue increase does not demonstrate universal cultivator welfare.

## 6. Military economy

- Armies combined cavalry, infantry, elephants, forts and artillery.
- Imported horses required merchants, ports, payment and inland transport.
- Deva Raya II's reforms were a response to Bahmani mounted capability.
- Artillery appears in the 1367 narrative but was never a self-sufficient cause.
- Forts protected routes, grain, garrisons and surrounding revenue.
- Portuguese entry altered an existing maritime-horse system rather than creating it.
- Official 2024 Bhatkal key: **Krishnadevaraya**; exact claim retains Portuguese-source caution.

## 7. Hampi, trade and coinage

- Hampi was an integrated Tungabhadra city-region.
- Natural terrain, walls, water, sacred centres, royal spaces, markets and suburbs interacted.
- Travellers described scale and wealth; archaeological and heritage evidence corroborates system, not exact numbers.
- Temples received resources, employed labour and organized festivals and markets.
- Inland routes linked Hampi to ports and wider Indian Ocean commerce.
- Vijayanagara gold varaha/pagoda represented sovereign coinage.
- Coin evidence does not prove universal monetization.

## 8. Society and religion

- Society included rulers, Brahmans, nayakas, Muslim soldiers, merchants, artisans, cultivators, forest and pastoral groups, dependants and enslaved people.
- Caste shaped hierarchy but did not prevent all occupational or regional mobility.
- Gangadevi is a named elite woman author.
- Portuguese accounts describe specialized female roles.
- 2021 Nuniz answer remains inferred because the local official key is unavailable.
- Travellers reveal situated observations, not a census.
- Temples, mosques, madrasas and dargahs combined ritual, property, labour, learning and legitimacy.
- Composite culture must be attached to concrete interaction and must not erase conflict.

## 9. Culture and architecture

- Vijayanagara patronized Telugu, Kannada, Sanskrit and Tamil.
- *Amuktamalyada*: Krishnadevaraya; kingship and devotion.
- *Madhuravijayam*: Gangadevi; conquest memory and legitimacy.
- Courtly and devotional music and performance moved through patronage networks across political boundaries.
- Surviving performance evidence is weighted toward courts and religious institutions; it does not reconstruct village repertoires or equal access.
- Vijayanagara sacred buildings retained a Dravida grammar.
- Raya gopuras, mandapas, chariot streets, bazaars and water systems expanded the urban-sacred complex.
- Kalyana mandapa was a wedding/celebration hall; Vitthala is the principal named example.
- Virupaksha is an older sacred site expanded under Vijayanagara.
- Vitthala's stone chariot is not monolithic.
- Mahanavami Dibba was a royal ceremonial platform.
- Secular structures show stronger arched and domed Islamicate vocabulary.
- Lotus Mahal is a conventional label; original function is uncertain.
- Bahmani Gulbarga and Bidar joined fort, mosque, palace and madrasa landscapes.
- The official 2026 Mains question requires a demonstrated amalgamation with examples.

## 10. Bahmani politics and Gawan

- Firuz Shah Bahmani combined learning, campaigns, ports and diplomacy.
- Ahmad Shah shifted the capital to Bidar.
- Taraf = major province; tarafdar = provincial governor.
- Khalisa = resources reserved more directly for the crown.
- Mahmud Gawan was wakil-i-sultanat, not sultan.
- Gawan captured Goa and Dabhol and expanded overseas trade.
- Four large tarafs were reorganized into eight.
- Salary, troop obligation, measurement and fort control sought accountability.
- The Bidar madrasa represents scholarly and architectural patronage.
- Deccani and Afaqi/gharib were changing faction labels.
- Gawan's execution c. 1481/82 accelerated, but did not alone cause, breakup.
- Successors: **Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Berar and Bidar**.

## 11. Talikota and aftermath

- Talikota/Rakshasa-Tangadi/Bannihatti occurred in 1565.
- The principal coalition included Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda and Bidar.
- Berar should not be inserted automatically.
- Rama Raya was captured and executed.
- Cavalry, artillery, command, cohesion and contingency interacted.
- Hampi's sack destroyed metropolitan concentration and great-power status.
- Aravidu rulers continued from Penukonda and later centres.
- Nayaka states gained autonomy.
- Temples, routes, agrarian relations and cultural traditions survived in altered forms.
- Correct verdict: **catastrophic transformation, not instant extinction**.

## 12. Four one-line UPSC theses

1. **Conflict:** The rivalry was a struggle for agrarian revenue, strategic corridors and maritime-military supply, sharpened but not wholly caused by religion.
2. **State:** Vijayanagara combined central ambition with hereditary nayaka mediation; Bahmani reform sought to discipline similarly powerful provincial resources through different institutions.
3. **Culture:** Deccan interaction produced selective borrowing and multilingual patronage without eliminating hierarchy or war.
4. **1565:** Talikota ended Hampi's imperial concentration and reconfigured, rather than erased, the Vijayanagara political world.
