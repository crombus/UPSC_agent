# Aurangzeb: Religious Policy, North India and the Rajputs — Live Session Edition

Aurangzeb's reign cannot be understood through either of two easy stories: that private piety mechanically dictated every state action, or that religion was merely decorative language covering ordinary politics. This session instead asks how belief, law, fiscal pressure, war, local conflict, imperial institutions and negotiated alliances interacted—and where the evidence does not permit certainty.

### Visual: the four-layer test

```text
PERSONAL BELIEF
      |
      v
NORMATIVE ORDER ------> IMPERIAL SELECTION / EXCEPTION
      |                              |
      v                              v
PROVINCIAL AND LOCAL IMPLEMENTATION / NON-IMPLEMENTATION
      |
      v

```

The central problem is simple. A ruler may believe something, issue an order in its language, modify it for political need, and then see it unevenly applied. Those are four different historical facts.

### 1. Where this topic begins

The succession struggle of 1657–59 belongs mainly to the preceding topic. Here only its result matters. Shah Jahan's illness in September 1657 opened a military contest because Mughal succession had no binding primogeniture. Aurangzeb defeated Dara at Dharmat and Samugarh, confined Shah Jahan, overcame Shuja and Murad, and consolidated power by 1659.

Dara's execution followed a juristic opinion describing him as dangerous to faith and public peace. Yet the political necessity of removing a rival was obvious. This is an early warning: religious language may be sincere and politically useful at the same time.

The war was not a plebiscite between “liberal” and “orthodox” camps. Hindu and Muslim nobles, including Shias, divided according to service ties, calculation and personal interest. Chronology therefore does not itself prove ideological causation.

### 2. Personal piety is not yet state policy

Aurangzeb was a pious Hanafi Muslim, learned in the Quran, hadis and jurisprudence. He preferred personal simplicity and disliked ostentation. He was also a Timurid emperor ruling a huge, religiously diverse agrarian empire through nobles, zamindars, accountants, soldiers and hereditary chiefs.

His belief influenced the language and direction of policy. It did not erase:

- political discretion;
- fiscal and military need;
- inherited Mughal institutions;
- dependence on Hindu, Rajput and later Maratha service;
- provincial variation; or
- reversal and non-enforcement.

The correct analytical question is not “religion or politics?” It is “how did religious commitment alter political choices, targets, symbols and legitimacy in a particular context?”

### 3. A usable chronology

| Phase | Secure developments | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| 1658–66 | consolidation, early moral rules, continued Rajput partnership | no uniform rupture with Akbarian institutions |
| 1667–78 | Jai Singh's death, frontier pressures, Jat/Satnami/Afghan conflicts, 1669 temple cases, stronger orthodox idiom | developments did not move in one synchronized line |
| 1679–87 | jizyah restoration, Marwar-Mewar war, sharper public differentiation, Deccan rulers denounced in religious language | politics and war selected timing and targets |
| 1688–1707 | continued recruitment, southern accommodation, uneven enforcement, wartime remissions and 1704 jizyah suspension in the south | not a simple return to early policy |

These are analytical phases, not sealed compartments.

### 4. Four distinctions that prevent bad answers

1. **Normative order ≠ implementation.** A farman shows an imperial direction, not identical compliance across every locality.
2. **Symbolic destruction ≠ general policy.** A major temple's destruction could communicate sovereignty widely, but it does not prove destruction of every temple.
3. **Personal belief ≠ administrative causation.** Belief mattered, but an appointment or war may also follow competence, debt, succession or security.
4. **Correlation ≠ causation.** A rebellion occurring after a discriminatory measure is not thereby caused by that measure alone.

### UPSC application

Begin an answer with a graded thesis: “Aurangzeb's orthodoxy reshaped the legal-symbolic idiom and enabled coercive differentiation, while political selection, institutional need and uneven implementation prevented a single uniform religious programme.”

### Revision notes

1. Topic 22 begins with the accession result, not a retelling of the whole succession war.
2. Mughal succession depended on military-political success, not automatic primogeniture.
3. Dara's juristic condemnation joined legal language to political removal.

5. Aurangzeb's personal piety is securely evidenced.
6. State policy still required discretion, officials and local enforcement.
7. Policy changed across phases and regions.
8. Orders, implementation and social effect are separate evidentiary questions.
9. A dated sequence does not by itself prove causation.
10. The strongest thesis rejects both communal monocause and religion-is-irrelevant reduction.

### Original Mains application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish personal belief from administrative causation in assessing Aurangzeb's reign.

**Model answer**

Aurangzeb's Hanafi piety shaped the language and direction of rule. Early restrictions on court ritual, patronage of jurists, major temple actions and the 1679 restoration of jizyah cannot be detached from his understanding of Islamic kingship. Personal belief therefore mattered.

Yet belief did not mechanically produce every administrative result. The empire depended on Rajput, Hindu and later Maratha mansabdars, while the emperor retained ruler-made regulations and political discretion. Orders were transmitted through provincial officials and could be modified, evaded or suspended. The 1671 Muslim-only accountant order was withdrawn for want of competent personnel; crop failure produced jizyah remissions; southern collection was suspended in 1704.

Thus the causal chain ran from belief through imperial selection to negotiated implementation. A sound judgement identifies the religious meaning of a measure while testing its political context, regional reach and institutional limits.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates belief, decision and implementation and supports each level with a named example.

### Visual: from norm to local practice

```text
Hanafi legal learning / juristic opinion
                 |
                 v
      emperor selects, orders or excepts
          /                      \
   sharia-oriented rule       zawabit
                              ruler-made regulations
          \                      /

```

The plain-language point is that a legal book does not collect a tax, demolish a building or appoint an officer. Institutions and choices stand between norm and result.

### 1. Sharia and imperial sovereignty

**Sharia** here means the Islamic normative-legal tradition as interpreted by jurists. Aurangzeb sought greater conformity with it and elevated its public prestige. But he did not transfer sovereignty to a clerical corporation.

**Zawabit** were ruler-made regulations. They remained necessary because an empire had to manage rank, revenue, roads, markets, war, appointments and disputed successions. Aurangzeb could choose between opinions, commission a ruling, appoint or remove a qazi, and make exceptions for reasons of state.

The Mughal state was therefore neither an ulama-run theocracy nor a modern secular state. It was an imperial monarchy whose ruler increasingly valued Islamic legality while retaining political command.

### 2. What the Fatawa-i-Alamgiri was

The *Fatawa-i-Alamgiri* was an imperially sponsored collective compendium of Hanafi jurisprudence. It demonstrates:

- organised patronage of legal scholarship;
- the prestige Aurangzeb attached to juristic learning;
- an effort to collect and order authoritative opinions.

It was not:

- a modern constitution;
- a single automatically enforced criminal code;
- proof that every provincial officer consulted it; or
- a replacement for imperial regulations and political judgement.

Its importance is normative and institutional, not evidence of uniform application.

### 3. Ulama were beneficiaries, not one obedient bloc

Ulama, qazis, sadrs, imams and mosque attendants received patronage. Mosques and religious establishments were repaired; salaries and offices expanded. Jizyah created additional posts and a separate revenue channel benefiting needy Muslims and learned men.

Yet theologians differed. Some officials were accused of corruption. In 1688 Qazi Shaikh-ul-Islam refused to legitimise war against Muslim Deccani rulers and resigned. Aurangzeb also complained about grasping beneficiaries at Mecca. Patronage did not buy a homogeneous political constituency.

### 4. Muhtasib: public regulator, not private-life police

Muhtasibs were appointed in the provinces to check open violation of rules concerning intoxicants, gambling, houses of ill repute and related public conduct. They also checked weights and measures. Their remit therefore combined moral regulation with market supervision.

Aurangzeb explicitly instructed them not to intrude into private life. That boundary may have been imperfectly observed, but it matters for defining the order. Traveller evidence that prohibitions were openly flouted demonstrates weak enforcement, not the absence of policy.

### 5. The institutional contradiction

Aurangzeb's project depended on officials whose conduct could undermine its moral claim:

```text
greater legal prestige
       -> more offices and collectors
       -> new points of contact with subjects
       -> discretion, extortion or evasion
       -> reduced legitimacy
```

The strongest criticism is that a morality-centred state could multiply coercive encounters without securing moral compliance. The defence is that uneven enforcement does not make legal-symbolic change fictitious. Both propositions can be true.

### 6. Shias, Sufis and the limit of a persecution catalogue

Aurangzeb's Sunni orthodoxy did not make every Muslim affiliation a single political camp, nor does the evidence support a generic empire-wide catalogue of persecution directed at all Shias, Sufis or other heterodox currents.

| Evidence | What it supports | What it cannot support |
|---|---|---|
| The narrative describes Shias as almost equally divided; among nobles of 1000 **zat** and above up to Samugarh, the associated count records 27 Iranis for Aurangzeb and 23 for Dara | elite alignment crossed an easy orthodox-versus-heterodox divide | an empire-wide census, or an assumption that every Irani was Shia |
| Aurangzeb was uninterested in philosophical debate or mysticism, yet sometimes visited Sufi saints for blessings and did not prevent his sons from engaging with Sufism | personal Sunni orthodoxy coexisted with bounded Sufi contact inside the dynasty | a claim that the reign was broadly tolerant, or that coercion toward other groups disappeared |
| Kashmir governor Iftekhar Khan is described as anti-Shia | an individual provincial office-holder could display sectarian hostility | a generic empire-wide anti-Shia programme inferred from one provincial case |
| Kashmir histories cited for the episode, including Narayan Kaul's 1710 history, did not report the claimed persecution of Hindus | later persecution narratives require chronology and genre checks | proof that no coercion occurred elsewhere or that community memory has no historical value |

These controls come from `_med_part2_mughal.txt`, OCR lines 8680–8685 (printed p. 242), 8784–8790 (printed pp. 244–245) and 9501–9505 (printed pp. 296–297).

In plain language, the labels must not do the explaining. **Shia** identifies a branch of Islam; **Irani** is a service-origin category and is not a perfect synonym for Shia; **Sufi** refers to varied mystical paths and affiliations; **heterodox** is an observer's category whose content changes with the source. The 27-to-23 figure concerns a defined high-ranking noble sample before Samugarh. It shows non-confessional elite alignment, not the beliefs of the empire's population.

The source rule is therefore:

```text
personal orthodoxy
    does not automatically prove
total social or cultural closure
    and an isolated provincial hostility
does not automatically prove
an empire-wide programme
```

This limit does not erase documented temple coercion, jizyah, unequal status, the execution of Guru Tegh Bahadur or pressure on other groups. It prevents evidence from one category, person or province being stretched beyond its reach.

> **UPSC trap:** “Aurangzeb was orthodox” is evidence about belief and orientation; it is not by itself evidence that every Shia, Sufi or heterodox group faced one uniform imperial policy.

### Original Mains application

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** Examine the relationship between Islamic law and imperial discretion under Aurangzeb.

**Model answer**

Aurangzeb elevated Hanafi legal learning within Mughal sovereignty, but did not replace imperial monarchy with clerical government. His sponsorship of the *Fatawa-i-Alamgiri*, patronage of qazis and sadrs, and appointment of muhtasibs gave sharia a stronger public and institutional presence.

The compendium, however, was a collection of juristic opinions rather than a modern constitution or automatically enforced code. Translation into action required imperial selection, farmans, provincial officers and local enforcement. **Zawabit**, or ruler-made regulations, continued because revenue, rank, markets, policing and war required sovereign administration beyond a legal compendium.

The ulama also lacked corporate unity. Some benefited from jizyah offices and grants; some collectors became associated with corruption; Qazi Shaikh-ul-Islam refused in 1688 to sanction war against Muslim Deccani rulers. Muhtasibs were told to check open violations and weights and measures, but not to intrude into private life, while travellers reported widespread evasion.

Thus legal orthodoxy altered legitimacy and priorities without ending political discretion. Aurangzeb's state is best understood as an imperial monarchy using a stronger Islamic legal idiom through institutions whose implementation remained selective, negotiated and fallible.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both terms, explains the administrative chain, and tests the theocracy claim against institutional evidence.

### Visual: classify before judging

| Measure | Main field | Reach question |
|---|---|---|
| no sijda before emperor | court-religious symbolism | audience practice |
| no kalima on coins | sacred-text protection | imperial coinage |
| Nauroz discontinued | ceremonial identity | court calendar |
| jharoka curtailed | kingship symbolism | public audience |
| imperial weighing restricted | austerity and religious objection | exceptions for princes |
| court singing stopped in 1669 | court patronage | instruments and private patronage continued |
| rahdari, cesses and tobacco octroi remitted | fiscal-social policy | implementation uncertain |

Different measures acted on different spaces. Calling all of them a single “ban” hides more than it explains.

### 1. Early moral-symbolic regulations

Aurangzeb ended **sijda**, or prostration before the ruler, because prostration properly belonged to God. He removed the **kalima** from coins to prevent sacred words being trampled or defiled. He discontinued **Nauroz**, associated with Persian-Zoroastrian and Safavid court culture.

These actions changed the public idiom of sovereignty. The emperor presented himself less as a sacral focus receiving quasi-devotional gestures and more as a Muslim ruler subject to divine law. Their primary reach was court and imperial representation, not every village.

### 2. Jharoka and weighing: change with exceptions

Aurangzeb discontinued **jharoka darshan**, the balcony appearance through which subjects viewed the emperor, calling it superstitious. He also opposed weighing the ruler against valuables on birthdays. Yet social and dynastic pressure produced exceptions: weighing was permitted for sons recovering from illness.

Astrologers were told not to prepare almanacs, but even the royal family ignored the order. The historical lesson is not that orders were meaningless. It is that an order can alter official legitimacy while failing to erase established practice.

### 3. Music: the precise claim

In 1669 court singing was stopped and official singers were pensioned. But:

- instrumental music continued;
- the **naubat**, or royal band, continued;
- women of the harem and nobles patronised singing;
- many Persian works on Indian classical music were written during the reign;
- Aurangzeb was reported to play the veena.

The musicians' “burial of music” anecdote records an angry exchange, not proof that music vanished. The safe formulation is: **imperial vocal patronage contracted; musical production and elite patronage continued.**

### 4. Moral and fiscal policy overlapped

Some regulations reflected austerity or economic stress rather than doctrine alone. Court furnishing and expenditure were reduced. Rahdari, pandari and many cesses considered unlawful were prohibited; tobacco octroi was abolished in 1666. Crop failures after the succession war and a reported excess of expenditure over income formed part of the setting.

Implementation remained uncertain because jagirdars could continue assessing remitted dues. Religious legality, social regulation and economy therefore intersected.

### 5. The 1671 competence test

Aurangzeb ordered that karoris in crown lands be Muslims and that Hindu accountants and clerks be replaced. Elite opposition and the shortage of competent Muslim personnel forced withdrawal.

This episode is analytically valuable:

```text
discriminatory preference
       -> administrative capacity loss
       -> noble resistance
       -> withdrawal
```

It proves both prejudice and the institutional limits placed upon it.

### UPSC application

Do not write “Aurangzeb banned music.” Write “He withdrew imperial patronage from court singing in 1669, while instrumental performance, naubat, manuscript production and non-imperial patronage survived.”

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why is the statement “Aurangzeb abolished music” historically misleading?

**Model answer**

Aurangzeb's policy reduced imperial musical patronage but did not abolish music. In 1669 he stopped singing at the imperial court and pensioned official singers. This reflected his austere religious outlook and a broader recasting of court ceremony.

Continuity is equally important. Instrumental music and the royal **naubat** remained. Women of the harem and nobles continued to patronise performers. Numerous Persian works on Indian classical music were written during the reign, and Aurangzeb himself was associated with the veena. The famous “burial of music” exchange is therefore an anecdote about royal displeasure, not evidence of cultural extinction.

The accurate verdict is a shift in the location and form of patronage: vocal performance lost its regular imperial place, while music survived in instrumental, household, noble and intellectual settings. This distinction also shows why a court order cannot automatically describe society-wide practice.

**Why this earns marks:** It corrects the claim through four distinct continuities rather than merely denying it.

## Lesson 4 — Temples: rule, site, context, action

Progress: 4/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Temple orders, destruction, patronage, region and war

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Satish Chandra, printed pp. 247–250 and 272–273

### Visual: the six-box evidence method

```text
DATED SITE
   -> SOURCE AND GENRE
      -> FORMAL RULE
         -> LOCAL OR MILITARY CONTEXT
            -> ACTUAL ACTION
               -> COUNTER-EVIDENCE / LIMIT
```

This method avoids both blanket condemnation without context and apologetic denial of coercion.

### 1. Formal old/new distinction

At the reign's outset, Aurangzeb reiterated a juristic distinction: long-standing non-Muslim places of worship should not be demolished, while new construction was restricted; old buildings could be repaired because buildings decay. Farmans to Brahmans at Banaras and Vrindavan express this position.

But the category “old” required official interpretation. Local prejudice, political pressure and the emperor's own outlook affected decisions. A formal distinction was real without being consistently honoured.

### 2. Earlier Gujarat and 1665

As Gujarat governor, Aurangzeb had ordered action against some new temples, sometimes involving defacement and bricking up rather than identical demolition. After images and worship were restored, a 1665 order renewed destruction. Somnath appears within this setting.

The example teaches precision: “destroyed” can refer to different material actions, and an order's stated category must be checked against the site's chronology.

### 3. The 1669 turn

In 1669 reports reached the court that Brahmans at Thatta, Multan and especially Banaras attracted Hindu and Muslim learners. Orders were issued to suppress such teaching and destroy associated temples. The Vishvanath temple at Banaras and Keshava Rai temple at Mathura became major cases; mosques were erected at the sites.

The court chronicle praised the Mathura act as a display of faith that humbled rajas. Religious coercion and political theatre were therefore explicit. These were not merely neutral municipal actions.

Yet no surviving general farman proves a universal command to destroy all temples across the empire. A later chronicle's broad language must be tested against the absence of a universal order, temple survival and continuing grants.

### 4. War changed the threshold

During hostilities, temple destruction could operate as punishment and intimidation. In 1679–80 old temples were destroyed in Jodhpur, its parganas and Udaipur. Teams of officials and stone-cutters in Marwar made the policy appear like conquest of hostile territory.

Political rebellion does not erase religious meaning. Nor does religious meaning prove that every target was selected independently of war. Symbolic and strategic motives could combine.

### 5. Patronage and regional variation

Aurangzeb made or confirmed grants to some temples, mathas, Vaishnava establishments and jogis. A grant supported Panth Bharati because he fed travellers and was worthy of prayer. A grant also went to the Sikh establishment associated with Ram Rai at Dehra Dun.

After southern conquests, countless temples in Bijapur and Karnataka were largely left untouched because broad destruction risked further resistance. This was political accommodation, not proof of equal treatment everywhere.

The balanced finding is:

| False universal | Corrected position |
|---|---|
| only new temples were affected | old temples were also destroyed in named political and wartime settings |
| all temples were ordered destroyed | no evidence supports a universal empire-wide destruction command |
| grants cancel coercion | grants show selectivity, not equality |
| politics cancels religion | political punishment could deliberately use religious symbolism |

### 6. Consequence

Even selective destruction changed expectations. Officials could infer that action against temples would be welcomed. Communities could read named sacred-site destruction as a statement of subordinate status. The effect exceeded the physical number of sites.

## Lesson 5 — Jizyah was a fiscal instrument and a status statement

Progress: 5/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Pilgrimage taxes, jizyah chronology, administration, exemptions and effects

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Satish Chandra, printed pp. 250–255
CA search: "historical jizyah Mughal fiscal administration research 2026"
CA found: none required; no modern tax analogy is used as a substitute for historical definition
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: what jizyah did—and did not do

```text
CLASSICAL STATUS TAX ON LIABLE NON-MUSLIM MALES
        |
        +--> differentiated political-religious subjecthood
        +--> separate assessment and collection encounters
        +--> proceeds assigned to separate treasury / Muslim beneficiaries
        +--> exemptions and remissions
        +--> protest, corruption and evasion

NOT land revenue | NOT customs duty | NOT collected from every Hindu
```

### 1. Before 1679

Akbar abolished pilgrimage tax in 1563 and jizyah in 1564. Aurangzeb revived pilgrimage taxes at places such as Mathura and Kurukshetra before restoring jizyah. This reversed an important part of Akbar's equalising fiscal symbolism.

Orthodox opinion sought jizyah soon after Aurangzeb's accession, but the emperor postponed it for political reasons. Its restoration in 1679—about two decades into the reign—therefore requires a political chronology, not a timeless command suddenly discovered.

### 2. Why restore it?

Several motives interacted:

- Hanafi legal obligation and personal orthodoxy;
- public assertion of Islamic rulership;
- alliance with theologians and provision of offices;
- differentiation amid political and military pressure;
- an expected fiscal yield.

The official justification stressed faith and sharia. Satish Chandra links timing to a wider crisis and an attempt to rally Muslims before or during conflict with Muslim Deccani states and Maratha power. Motive is therefore plural, not reducible to treasury need.

### 3. Administration and exemptions

Jizyah was assessed by property categories. The book supplies three rate classes, but rates should be quoted only with unit and category because conversion into modern value misleads.

Exemptions included women, children, the insane, indigent persons and persons in government service; application could vary. Crop failure could produce remission, often at jagirdar request.

Collection required personal contact with officials. In cities, traders protested through shop closures. Collectors acquired opportunities for humiliation and extortion; some were attacked. The apparatus could undermine the legitimacy it was meant to strengthen.

### 4. Revenue claims and conversion claims

Proceeds went to a separate treasury for needy Muslims and ulama, so the tax did not simply repair the general imperial treasury. A reported figure of four crore rupees is an official claim and must not be treated as verified realised revenue.

The tax was regressive because the lowest liable category bore a heavier relative burden. Yet it did not produce large-scale conversion. Earlier centuries of collection had not done so either. Coercive differentiation was real without generating the claimed demographic transformation.

### 5. Regional and chronological limits

Aurangzeb resisted general remission but accepted crop-failure relief. In 1704 he suspended collection in the south for the duration of the war. Because the war had no quick end, this operated like regional abolition. Formal empire-wide abolition in 1712 under Jahandar Shah lies beyond Aurangzeb's reign and must be marked as a post-reign boundary.

### 6. Evaluation

Jizyah's strongest effect was political-symbolic:

```text
legal orthodoxy + public hierarchy
          -> Hindu resentment and protest
          -> clerical offices and corrupt contact
          -> no reliable Muslim political unity
          -> legitimacy cost larger than secure fiscal gain
```

It could intensify an existing conflict but cannot automatically be assigned as the sole cause of a rebellion.

### UPSC application

The empire could include a group in service while treating its wider community unequally. Recruitment is not a certificate of religious equality.

These are historian-constructed categories based on surviving records. “Hindu” includes different service groups; later growth includes many Marathas. The table does not measure equality, local officeholding or the experience of ordinary subjects.

### 2. What increased numbers prove

They prove that Aurangzeb did not expel Hindus from the state and that administrative-military dependence on non-Muslim elites increased. Ninety-six Marathas held ranks of 1000 zat and above during 1679–1707 in the cited series, with several in high bands.

They do not prove:

- equal access to sensitive commands;
- equal quality or duration of jagirs;
- social intimacy like the earlier Rajput partnership;
- absence of discriminatory fiscal or religious policy.

Maratha mansabdars often held short month-scale jagirs and were not treated as an integrated political partner on the Rajput model.

### 3. Rajputs after 1679

The breach was with Marwar and Mewar, not all Rajputs. Amber, Bikaner, Bundi and Kota rulers continued in Mughal service. Raja Bishan Singh fought Jats as faujdar of Mathura; Bikaner and Bundi houses served in the Deccan.

Yet ceiling and trust mattered. Few other Rajput rulers rose above 3500 zat, and Jai Singh's later advancement was limited. Inclusion continued inside a downward spiral of confidence.

### 4. Discrimination with reversal

Aurangzeb reduced customs advantages for Muslim traders, but abuse required modification. The 1671 attempt to replace Hindu accountants collapsed because competent substitutes were unavailable and nobles resisted.

The evidence supports neither “merit alone” nor “religious exclusion alone.” Imperial need repeatedly constrained ideological preference.

### 5. Continuity and change from earlier Mughals

Akbar transformed Rajput alliance into a central pillar of integration through honour, watan security, high rank and social-political confidence. Jahangir and Shah Jahan broadly continued it, though tensions and hierarchy remained. Aurangzeb initially treated leading Rajputs as partners, then reduced their policy influence and deployed them to distant frontiers. After 1679, service survived but trust and privileged partnership weakened.

### 1. Jat date tension and causes

The canonical summary often gives **c. 1669**, but the assigned OCR narrative says the first prolonged rising under Gokula began **early in 1667**. This tension must be disclosed rather than silently harmonised. A safe answer writes: “The OCR narrative dates the early rising to 1667; many summaries associate its culmination and the Mathura context with c. 1669.”

Jats on both sides of the Yamuna were cultivators, some zamindars, and clan communities with traditions of armed self-defence. The centralising state threatened local arrangements through revenue demand and officials. Faujdar Abdun Nabi was killed; his large escheated property supports suspicion of extortion even though a chronicle praised him.

Gokula was defeated and brutally killed. The Keshava Rai temple was destroyed after Jat defeat. Religion therefore entered punishment and symbolism, but the book says religion played little role in the rising's origin.

Later Rajaram and Churaman combined revenue withholding, highway plunder, military organisation and conflict over zamindari rights. The struggle increasingly aimed at Jat political control.

### 2. Satnamis, 1672

The Satnamis were a monotheistic community critical of ritual, caste distinction and authority, drawing peasants and artisans. A clash with a local official near Narnaul escalated. They seized Narnaul and Bairat before a large imperial force with artillery suppressed them.

Religious identity helped organisation, but the immediate mechanism was local coercion and armed escalation. They were not a modern egalitarian revolution, though their social composition and discipline mattered.

### 3. Bundelas

Champat Rai had supported Aurangzeb but later resisted and plundered; imperial forces hunted him down without annexing all Bundela lands. Chhatrasal later created a more durable challenge in Bundelkhand.

The Bundela setting combined chiefship, service, extraction, fort-country autonomy and rebellion. It cannot be explained by jizyah alone, especially because key conflict dynamics predated 1679.

### 4. Afghan boundary

Yusufzai and Afridi risings in 1667 and 1672 involved routes, subsidies, tribal autonomy, charismatic leadership and attempts at regional kingship. Raushani religious revival supplied an ethical-political background. Aurangzeb personally went toward Peshawar in 1674–75 and used force and diplomacy.

This conflict is vital as a control case: resistance to Mughal centralisation was not confined to Hindus. It also drew Rajput forces and kept Jaswant Singh at Jamrud.

### 5. General mechanism

```text
local extraction or status pressure
       + existing clan/sect/chief networks
       + coercive official encounter
       + opportunity and leadership
       -> armed resistance
       -> imperial punishment or negotiation
       -> possible regional state formation
```

Religion may shape solidarity or punishment, but its weight differs case by case.

### UPSC application

Compare social base, immediate trigger, leadership, state response and long-term outcome. Never group all four under “Hindu reaction.”

### 1. Earlier relationship

The Sikh community had developed institutional resources, masands, armed experience and a strong ethical identity before Aurangzeb. Guru Hargobind's earlier conflicts under Shah Jahan show that Sikh–Mughal tension did not begin in 1675.

After accession, complaints linked Guru Har Rai with Dara. Aurangzeb summoned him; Ram Rai came instead and was retained at court. Ram Rai later received land at Dehra Dun. This shows suspicion, engagement and patronage rather than immediate general persecution.

Guru Tegh Bahadur succeeded in 1664, travelled in eastern India, joined Raja Ram Singh's Assam setting and returned to Punjab in 1671.

### 2. What is missing

No contemporary Persian account gives a transparent, complete narrative of the November 1675 execution. Aurangzeb was away from Delhi from April 1674 to March 1676 supervising Afghan operations. His absence does not remove responsibility: an execution of this scale required his consent and approval.

Later Persian accounts allege disorder, plunder or political danger. Sikh traditions interpret the Guru as resisting oppression and dying for religious freedom. The Kashmir Pandit tradition connects martyrdom to forced conversion, but the chronology, governor identification and silence of near-contemporary Kashmir histories require care.

### 3. A defensible conclusion

The evidence allows three statements together:

1. Imperial authorities framed the Guru as a security or public-order problem.
2. Sikh memory rightly centres martyrdom and defence of cherished belief.
3. No single late narrative transparently resolves the immediate chain of events.

Satish Chandra judges the execution uncalled for and rooted in a narrow law-and-order approach. That is a reasoned interpretation, not a substitute for missing contemporary detail.

### 4. Consequence without mechanical causation

The execution deepened Sikh alienation and became a foundational martyr memory. Guru Gobind Singh later formed the Khalsa at Anandpur in 1699, sharpening social and military identity.

But the Khalsa did not arise from one event alone. Earlier militarisation, masand institutions, hill politics, community growth and subsequent conflict also mattered. Topic 22 stops at this boundary and does not retell all later Sikh military history.

### 5. Late conflict limit

Mughal and hill-raja conflict with Guru Gobind Singh involved local power, supplies, Anandpur and imperial security. Claims that Aurangzeb ordered all Sikh shrines destroyed lack secure corroboration; the undated masand order reported later appears ineffective if issued. Aurangzeb's late instruction to conciliate the Guru and invitation to meet further complicate a linear persecution narrative.

### UPSC application

State secure facts first. Then label later traditions by genre. Conclude with responsibility and consequence without inventing a transparent motive.

### Visual: the Rajput bargain

```text

                         ↕
           military service + imperial loyalty
                         |
                         v
             credible mutual guarantees
```

The alliance was unequal, but it worked only when both sides expected the bargain to be honoured.

### 1. Early partnership

From 1658 to Jai Singh's death in 1667, Aurangzeb's relations with leading Rajput rulers were cordial. Jai Singh had long links with Aurangzeb, helped defeat Shuja and pursue Dara, advised on Rajput affairs, and secured Jaswant Singh's pardon and restoration. His Deccan viceroyalty was an exceptional mark of trust.

Rana Raj Singh of Mewar also received restored parganas, recognition over subordinate territories and a rank of 6000/6000 after supporting Aurangzeb in the succession contest. Bikaner, Bundi and Kota successions were not punished despite rulers having opposed Aurangzeb.

Orthodox belief therefore did not produce an immediate anti-Rajput policy.

### 2. Friction inside partnership

Partnership did not mean that Aurangzeb accepted Rajput advice. Jai Singh's proposed settlement with Shivaji diverged from the emperor's preferred policy. Shivaji's escape from Agra damaged prestige; Ram Singh was punished, and Jai Singh was recalled. Jai Singh died in 1667.

Jaswant Singh's reputation also suffered from suspicion surrounding Deccan events, though he retained high service. In 1671 he was sent to Jamrud on the north-west frontier. Ram Singh remained in Assam without the full provincial resources earlier commanders had enjoyed.

### 3. From partners to sword-arms

The change was gradual:

| Early phase | Transitional phase |
|---|---|
| advice in policy | service in difficult theatres |
| high personal confidence | suspicion and restrained promotion |
| visible partnership | continued military utility |
| negotiated influence | reduced voice at court |

Financial pressure, multiple rebellions and an expanding Deccan agenda also altered imperial priorities. Maratha recruitment made exclusive dependence on Rajputs less urgent, though not unnecessary.

### 4. Jaswant Singh as the hinge

Jaswant Singh was both a premier Rathor ruler and a Mughal grandee. His posting at Jamrud linked Rajput service to the Afghan frontier. His death on **28 November 1678** removed a powerful intermediary and opened a succession dispute while two widows were pregnant.

The crisis that followed cannot be explained as a pre-written annexation plan. But it entered a relationship already weakened by reduced trust, religious differentiation and centralising suspicion.

### 5. Continuity and change

Akbar had produced an upward spiral: service brought rank, honour and stronger confidence. Jahangir and Shah Jahan sustained the alliance while asserting hierarchy. Under Aurangzeb the early partnership survived, then entered a downward spiral in which service continued but guarantees became less credible.

## Lesson 10 — Marwar: a precedent became a provocation

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: how an administratively defensible step became politically disastrous

```text
Jaswant dies without acknowledged adult male heir
        |
khalisa + inventory + debt/escheat + route-security measures
        |
precedent exists, but officials act as conquerors
        |

        |
temple teams + qazi/muhtasib + jizyah climate + property searches
        |
partition proposal / confinement order rejected
        |
Durgadas escapes with Ajit -> Rathor uprising
```

### 1. Why khalisa was not automatically annexation

When news of Jaswant Singh's death reached court, Aurangzeb ordered Marwar taken into **khalisa**, meaning direct crown administration, while succession was settled. There were precedents for imperial occupation during disputed or minority succession.

Jaswant also owed money to the state. Escheat—the inventory and temporary seizure of a deceased noble's property when debts remained—was an established Mughal practice. Revenue withholding, competing claims to parganas and unsafe roads gave the centre additional administrative concerns.

Therefore khalisa alone does not prove a plan of permanent annexation or forced conversion.

### 2. Why precedent did not make policy wise

Officials searched for treasure, dug at Siwana, posted qazis and muhtasibs, and behaved as if Marwar were conquered territory. Temple-demolition teams amplified fear. The wider 1679 jizyah atmosphere made administrative action look like religious-political dispossession.

Rani Hadi resisted evacuation of Jodhpur and sought delay because Jaswant's widows were pregnant. Two posthumous sons were born; Ajit Singh became the surviving claimant.

### 3. Rival claims and Inder Singh

**Inder Singh**—also printed “Indra Singh” in the OCR—was a descendant of Jaswant's elder brother Amar Singh. He argued that Amar's line had been unjustly displaced and offered a large peshkash. Aurangzeb granted him the tika on 26 May 1679.

The issue was not that Inder's claim was imaginary. It was that overriding Jaswant's posthumous son threatened a foundational Rajput expectation: succession within a watan house should not depend solely on imperial preference and payment.

### 4. Failed compromise

Aurangzeb offered Ajit a mansab and the parganas of Sojat and Jaitaran, while proposing a division that left Jodhpur and Nagaur elsewhere. Rathor sardars judged this destructive to the state's integrity. When they rejected it, Aurangzeb ordered the princes and their mothers confined.

Durgadas Rathor and companions escaped from Delhi with Ajit after fighting. At Jodhpur the Rathors performed Ajit's tika, expelled officials from several areas and began rebellion. Inder Singh proved unable to command local legitimacy and was removed.

### 5. Religion as accelerant, not sole trigger

The immediate dispute concerned:

- watan succession;
- debt and escheat;
- central authority over tika;
- division of territory;
- honour and treatment of the ranis;
- official coercion.

Temple destruction and jizyah changed how each act was interpreted. Religion sharpened the legitimacy crisis without being its only cause.

### 6. Source revision

Older interpretations saw a pre-planned scheme to annex Marwar for forced conversion. The *Waqa-i-Ajmer* and *Jodhpur Hukumat-ri-Bahi* reveal debt, precedents, rival claims and failed negotiations. They weaken the conspiracy thesis but do not excuse clumsy imperial choices.

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Why does Satish Chandra describe Aurangzeb's Marwar policy as clumsy and blundering rather than simply annexationist?

**Model answer**

Succession was unsettled, two widows were pregnant, roads and revenues were insecure, and Jaswant owed money to the state. Escheat and temporary direct administration therefore do not by themselves prove a permanent annexation plan.

The policy became clumsy through implementation and political judgement. Officials searched property and acted as conquerors; qazis, muhtasibs and temple-demolition teams intensified religious fear. After posthumous sons were born, Aurangzeb recognised Inder Singh from Amar Singh's line, threatening the expectation that the watan house's succession would be respected.

The proposed division gave Ajit Singh mansab and Sojat-Jaitaran but withheld the political core of Jodhpur-Nagaur. Rathor sardars rejected it. An order to confine the ranis and princes then drove Durgadas's escape with Ajit and armed resistance. Inder lacked local legitimacy and was later removed.

Thus debt, khalisa and rival claims explain why the centre intervened; they do not justify coercive searches, territorial fragmentation and disregard of Rajput honour. Religion was not the sole trigger, but temple action and jizyah made imperial intent appear more threatening. The result was a preventable legitimacy crisis with no durable advantage.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes defensible administrative grounds from the decisions that converted them into rebellion.

## Lesson 11 — Mewar turned a succession dispute into a coalition war

Progress: 11/12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Mewar, Prince Akbar, settlements and the 1698 limit

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: escalation and partial settlement

```text
Marwar resistance
      +
Rana Raj Singh's strategic and political concerns
      |
Mewar terrain + Rathor-Sisodia cooperation
      |
Mughal occupation of plains / Rajputs in hills
      |
Prince Akbar rebels, January 1681
      |
coalition split by intelligence and manoeuvre
      |
Mewar settlement; Rathor war continues
      |
Ajit recognised in 1698, Jodhpur not restored
```

### 1. Why Mewar intervened

Rana Raj Singh did not simply act because Ajit Singh was a close blood relation; the assigned analysis rejects that common claim. Mewar had older grievances: restrictions concerning Chittor, sequestered parganas and erosion of its position relative to Amber and Marwar.

The Marwar succession offered a chance to assert that a major Rajput succession should not be settled without leading Rajput opinion. Mughal occupation of Marwar also threatened Mewar's strategic environment.

### 2. War and terrain

Mewar fortified Chittor and closed approaches. Aurangzeb moved against it; Mughal forces occupied plains and reached Udaipur, while the Rana withdrew into hills. Terrain, local knowledge and mobile resistance frustrated Mughal efforts.

Temple destruction in Udaipur and war devastation reinforced religious-political alienation. Yet the Rana's motives cannot be reduced to jizyah or temple policy; there is no secure evidence that protest against those measures alone caused intervention.

### 3. Rana Raj Singh and Rana Jai Singh

Rana Raj Singh died in September 1680. His successor Rana Jai Singh was less committed to the Rathor cause and sought peace. Existing friction between Rathor and Sisodia leaders also weakened the coalition.

This shift shows that “Rajput resistance” was not one permanent bloc. Houses pursued their own territorial, dynastic and strategic interests.

### 4. Prince Akbar, January 1681

Prince Akbar rebelled with Rathor support and attempted to seize Ajmer. The episode revealed concern among Rajputs and some Mughal nobles about Aurangzeb's policy. Aurangzeb used letters, intelligence and political manoeuvre to create distrust within the coalition. Akbar's support collapsed, and he fled toward the Deccan.

The rebellion did not prove that the entire nobility rejected Aurangzeb. Few nobles were willing to overthrow a functioning emperor.

### 5. Settlements and non-settlements

Mewar made peace after Raj Singh's death. It ceded Mandal, Bidnur and Mandalgarh in lieu of jizyah and promised not to aid Rathors; later disputes over restoration and payment continued. Aurangzeb restored the parganas in 1684 but demanded one lakh rupees annually as jizyah, keeping friction alive.

Rathor warfare continued intermittently. Durgadas returned; Ajit emerged publicly; a 1696 Mewar marriage further destroyed the “imposter” claim. In **1698** Ajit Singh was recognised as ruler of Marwar with mansab, but **Jodhpur was not restored**. He rebelled again in 1701 and 1706.

### 6. Consequences and limits

The military drain after the 1681 Mewar settlement should not be exaggerated. Operations were limited compared with the later southern war, and overland trade was not demonstrably destroyed.

The deeper costs were:

- loss of prestige;
- increased lawlessness;
- distrust of imperial guarantees;
- reduced Rajput political contribution;
- openings for princely intrigue.

These costs coincided with Aurangzeb's move south but should not be inflated into a complete explanation of later imperial decline.

## Lesson 12 — Historiography: neither acquittal nor monocause

Progress: 12/12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Sources, interpretations, centralisation, legitimacy and final judgement

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Satish Chandra, printed pp. 244–280, with canonical and advanced Topic 22

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: four interpretations tested

| Interpretation | What it sees well | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| communal/orthodox | discrimination, temple coercion, jizyah, religious idiom | local causes, continued inclusion, regional variation |
| political-pragmatic | succession, war, debt, competence, strategic selection | independent meaning of religious hierarchy |
| regional | local archives, zamindari, watan, terrain, community memory | empire-wide legal-symbolic direction |
| institutional | mansab, jagir, offices, centralisation, implementation | lived sacred injury and identity |

The best explanation combines these lenses without dissolving their differences.

### 1. The source archive

**Persian chronicles**, especially the *Maasir-i-Alamgiri*, provide orders, campaigns and official language. They are selective, court-centred and often celebrate legal-religious triumph.

**Farmans** establish dated commands, grants, protection and property decisions. A farman is strong evidence for one formal action; it does not prove uniform provincial enforcement.

**Waqa-i-Ajmer** supplies official news reporting on the Rajput crisis. **Jodhpur Hukumat-ri-Bahi** preserves Rathor administrative and dynastic material. Their local specificity revises broad theories but carries official or dynastic perspective.

**Inscriptions, grants and material remains** show survival, patronage, damage or rebuilding. Survival cannot prove benevolence; absence cannot prove motive.

**European accounts** illuminate protest, court practice and enforcement, but carry outsider interest, hearsay and commercial perspective.

**Sikh and other community traditions** preserve memory and meaning, yet require attention to date and genre.

### 2. The communal or orthodox interpretation

This approach rightly insists that Aurangzeb's personal orthodoxy mattered. Jizyah, major temple destruction, public hierarchy and coercive religious symbolism were not inventions. It becomes weak when every rebellion, appointment and imperial failure is derived from one identity conflict.

### 3. The political-pragmatic interpretation

This approach explains why specific temples were targeted during war, why Marwar entered khalisa, why officials reversed impractical orders and why southern accommodation grew. It becomes apologetic if “political” is used to erase the religious form and injury of chosen measures.

### 4. Regional and institutional approaches

Regional records recover local actors, debt, succession custom, terrain and zamindari conflict. Institutional analysis shows how the emperor depended on mansabdars, jagirs, qazis, collectors and chiefs. Together they explain uneven implementation.

Their limit is fragmentation: the empire still generated common symbols and legal signals. A major act at Mathura or the restoration of jizyah could shape expectations far beyond its administrative site.

### 5. Centralisation and negotiated empire

Aurangzeb was a hard-working centraliser who scrutinised officials and sought moral-legal discipline. But Mughal power remained negotiated:

```text
imperial command
  + noble service
  + hereditary watan
  + zamindar mediation
  + regional resources
  = effective rule
```

Overriding a local bargain could display coercive reach while weakening legitimacy. Marwar is the clearest example.

### 6. Consequences without importing later owners

Aurangzeb's policy:

- sharpened religious hierarchy;
- alienated sections of Hindu society;
- increased distrust among important allies;
- multiplied coercive administrative encounters;
- failed to create reliable Muslim unity;
- retained substantial elite inclusion and imperial capacity.

It contributed to disaffection but did not alone cause Mughal decline. The full Deccan-Maratha war, acute jagirdari crisis and post-1707 fragmentation belong to later topics.

### 7. Final verdict

Aurangzeb was neither a prisoner of circumstances nor a ruler free from them. Fiscal and military pressure narrowed choices; he still chose methods that deepened hierarchy and mistrust. His state remained composite in personnel but became less inclusive in public legitimacy.

### UPSC application

Use a conclusion with three levels: **real coercion**, **selective implementation**, **multi-causal consequence**.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks — Court austerity and public sovereignty

**Question (150 words):** Explain how Aurangzeb's early ceremonial measures altered the public idiom of Mughal sovereignty.

**Model answer**

Aurangzeb's early measures recast imperial ceremony in a more explicitly Islamic and austere idiom. He ended **sijda** before the emperor because prostration belonged to God, removed the **kalima** from coins to prevent defilement and discontinued Nauroz. Jharoka darshan was rejected as superstitious, while imperial weighing and luxury at court were restricted.

These acts reduced practices that had represented the emperor as a sacral, visibly accessible centre above sectarian boundaries. They instead presented the ruler as subject to divine law and moral discipline. Muhtasibs extended this claim into public conduct and market regulation.

Implementation remained qualified. Weighing returned for princes after illness, almanac restrictions were ignored, and court-singing withdrawal did not end music. Thus the measures changed official symbolism more consistently than they transformed all social practice. Their importance lay in the public redefinition of legitimate kingship, not proof of uniform obedience.

**Scoring note:** Reward an answer that explains what changed in sovereignty, not merely one that lists bans.

## 15 marks — Jizyah and temple policy

**Question (250 words):** Compare the administrative reach and symbolic effects of Aurangzeb's jizyah and temple policies.

**Model answer**

Both policies marked religious differentiation, but their administrative forms differed. Jizyah, restored in 1679, created a recurring assessment and collection apparatus for liable non-Muslims. Categories and exemptions existed; crop-failure remissions and the 1704 southern suspension show variation. Personal payment exposed subjects to collectors, protest and corruption. Its symbolic message of inferior subjecthood was empire-wide even where collection was weak.

Temple policy worked through dated orders and selected sites. A formal old/new distinction protected long-standing worship and allowed repair, yet old temples were destroyed at Banaras, Mathura and during the 1679–80 Rajput war. These acts were episodic rather than a uniform recurring tax, but major sacred sites amplified their political message. Grants and southern non-interference demonstrate selectivity without cancelling coercion.

The two policies therefore differed in mechanism: jizyah institutionalised repeated fiscal contact, whereas temple destruction produced concentrated acts of sovereign punishment and symbolic dominance. Both exceeded their material yield by reshaping expectations and trust. Neither was uniformly implemented, and neither alone explains rebellion or decline.

**Scoring note:** Credit explicit comparison of mechanism, frequency, reach and symbolic effect; do not reward two disconnected mini-essays equally.

## 20 marks — Religious and Rajput policy together

**Question (250 words):** “Aurangzeb's empire remained composite in personnel but became narrower in legitimacy.” Critically examine.

**Model answer**

Aurangzeb continued to govern through a composite ruling class. Rajputs remained in service, and Athar Ali's record-based series shows the listed Hindu share rising to about 31.6 per cent in 1679–1701, partly through Maratha recruitment. Amber, Bikaner, Bundi and Kota continued serving even during the Marwar-Mewar war.

Public legitimacy nevertheless narrowed. Court symbolism moved away from Akbarian sacral inclusiveness; the 1669 Banaras and Mathura actions and wartime temple destruction communicated coercive hierarchy. Jizyah's restoration in 1679 marked differentiated non-Muslim subjecthood and generated corrupt encounters and protest.

Rajput relations reveal the political consequence. Early partnership with Jai Singh and Jaswant Singh gave way to suspicion. After Jaswant's death, khalisa had precedents, but recognition of Inder Singh, coercive searches, temple teams and attempted confinement of Ajit's family damaged confidence in watan and succession guarantees. Mewar intervention and Prince Akbar's rebellion followed; Ajit's 1698 recognition still withheld Jodhpur.

The thesis must be qualified. Religious policy was uneven, grants continued, and politics selected targets. Yet composite recruitment could not substitute for confidence in imperial fairness. Aurangzeb preserved service capacity while weakening the broader legitimacy that made negotiated empire durable.

**Scoring note:** Award highest marks only when the answer links personnel statistics to public hierarchy and then demonstrates the connection through the Rajput bargain.

## 1. Central thesis

- Aurangzeb's personal Hanafi piety was real and politically consequential.
- The state remained a Timurid monarchy using sharia-oriented norms, zawabit and political discretion.
- Policy was neither uniform theocracy nor religion-free pragmatism.
- Separate belief, order, selection, implementation and response.
- Final formula: **orthodoxy shaped direction; politics selected targets; institutions mediated reach; regions shaped outcomes.**

## 2. Accession and chronology

- 1657–59 succession is a boundary inherited from Topic 21.
- The conflict was military-political, not a clean liberal-versus-orthodox vote.
- Dara's juristic condemnation joined religious language to reasons of state.
- 1658–66: consolidation and early regulation.
- 1667–78: Jai Singh's death, frontier strain, local revolts, 1669 temple actions.
- 1679–87: jizyah and Rajput war sharpen hierarchy.
- 1688–1707: continued recruitment, accommodation and uneven enforcement.

## 3. Sharia, zawabit and legal institutions

- Sharia = juristic normative tradition.
- Zawabit = ruler-made administrative regulations.
- Emperor retained selection, appointment and exception.
- *Fatawa-i-Alamgiri* = imperially sponsored collective Hanafi compendium.
- Not a constitution; not proof of uniform enforcement.
- Ulama, qazis, sadrs, imams and attendants received patronage.
- They did not become one loyal political bloc.
- Qazi Shaikh-ul-Islam's 1688 refusal shows juristic disagreement.

## 3A. Shia, Sufi and heterodox limits

- The narrative describes Shias as almost equally divided; its bounded high-rank count records 27 Iranis for Aurangzeb and 23 for Dara up to Samugarh.
- The figures show non-confessional elite alignment within a bounded rank-and-origin record; they are not an empire-wide census, and **Irani** is not a perfect synonym for **Shia**.
- Aurangzeb's lack of interest in philosophical debate or mysticism is consistent with personal Sunni orthodoxy.
- His occasional visits to Sufi saints for blessings and his sons' permitted engagement with Sufism rule out a claim of total social or cultural closure.

- The evidence does not support a generic empire-wide heterodox-persecution catalogue.

## 4. Muhtasib and moral regulation

- Muhtasibs checked open violations involving intoxicants, gambling and houses of ill repute.
- They also checked weights and measures.
- They were instructed not to intrude into private life.
- Traveller reports of evasion show implementation weakness.
- No sijda before ruler.
- No kalima on coins.
- Nauroz discontinued.
- Public Holi and Muharram displays restricted.

## 5. Court symbolism and music

- Jharoka darshan discontinued as superstitious.
- Imperial weighing restricted; exceptions occurred for princes.
- Almanac ban was ignored, including by royals.
- Court singing stopped in 1669; official singers pensioned.
- Instrumental music and naubat continued.
- Harem and nobles continued patronage.
- Persian music writing flourished.
- Burial anecdote is not proof that music ended.

## 6. Fiscal and appointment measures

- Rahdari, pandari and many cesses were formally remitted.
- Tobacco octroi abolished in 1666.
- Jagir assessments could make remission ineffective.
- Muslim traders received customs preference; abuse caused modification.
- 1671 Muslim-only karori/accountant order was withdrawn.
- Competence and elite resistance constrained discrimination.

## 7. Temple policy

- Formal rule: protect long-standing sites, restrict new construction, allow repair.
- “Old” required official interpretation.
- Gujarat actions could involve defacement, bricking and renewed destruction.
- 1669 order responded to teaching at Thatta, Multan and Banaras.
- Vishvanath and Keshava Rai were destroyed; mosques erected.
- Court language made faith and political intimidation explicit.
- No evidence of a universal order destroying all temples.
- Old temples were destroyed in 1679–80 Marwar-Mewar war.
- Grants to temples, mathas, jogis and religious beneficiaries continued.
- Southern non-destruction reflected political accommodation.
- Selective coercion had wider symbolic consequences.

## 8. Jizyah and pilgrimage taxes

- Akbar: pilgrimage tax abolished 1563; jizyah abolished 1564.
- Aurangzeb revived pilgrimage taxes at named sites.
- Jizyah restored in 1679 after long postponement.
- Poll/status tax on liable non-Muslims; not land revenue.
- Categories by property and multiple exemptions.
- Women, children, indigent and other categories exempt.
- Crop-failure remission occurred.
- Proceeds entered a separate treasury for needy Muslims/ulama.
