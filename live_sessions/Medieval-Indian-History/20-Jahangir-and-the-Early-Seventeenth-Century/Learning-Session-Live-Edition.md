# Jahangir and the Early Seventeenth Century — Live Session Edition

Jahangir inherited Akbar's large empire, but inheritance did not remove politics. He had to secure his accession, contain a princely rebellion, complete difficult regional settlements, govern through a changing court, and face a second succession crisis before his death. Nur Jahan belongs at the centre of this story, but neither as a decorative consort nor as a ruler who simply replaced the emperor.

### Visual: the evidence ladder

```text
royal memoir ─────> stated decision + imperial self-image
court chronicle ──> sequence + office + retrospective explanation
coin / farman ────> formal public authority in a dated setting
painting / tomb ──> representation + patronage + material form
visitor account ──> encounter seen through diplomatic-commercial aims

                           |
                           v
             corroborated fact + bounded inference
```

The first lesson is methodological because this reign is unusually vulnerable to dramatic shortcuts. Jahangir wrote about himself. European envoys described a court they wanted something from. Later historians built a “junta” theory around visible kinship. Sikh memory preserved Guru Arjan's martyrdom. Each source matters, but each answers a different question.

### 1. The main source types

The *Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri*, also called the *Jahangirnama*, is indispensable for the emperor's chronology, observations, orders, religious language and chosen public image. Jahangir composed much of it, while later portions involve continuation and transmission. A statement in the memoir is secure evidence that the courtly text made that statement. It is not automatically proof that an order was uniformly obeyed or that an accused person's motive has been independently established.

Mutamad Khan's *Iqbalnama* and other Persian narratives provide court sequence and competing explanations. Their patronage, hindsight and political position must also be considered. Surviving farmans and coins can show named, formal authority. They rarely reveal every negotiation behind a decision. Paintings show hierarchy, taste and desired image; they are not photographs.

William Hawkins, Sir Thomas Roe and factory records illuminate protocol, commerce and European frustration. They do not speak for the entire Mughal administration. Sikh traditions provide an indispensable community perspective on Guru Arjan, but genre, dating and transmission remain relevant.

### 2. A claim must carry its limit

Use four steps:

```text
claim
  -> named evidence
  -> what that evidence establishes
  -> what remains uncertain
```

For example, a coin carrying Nur Jahan's title demonstrates unusually public female authority. It cannot by itself prove that she selected every governor. The chain of justice demonstrates a royal claim to accessibility. It cannot measure how many rural petitioners obtained relief.

### 3. The chronological spine

| Date | Event | Why it matters |
|---:|---|---|
| 1605 | Salim became Jahangir | institutional inheritance required political confirmation |
| 1606 | Khusrau rebelled; Guru Arjan died after imperial punishment | succession and religious authority became entangled |
| 1608 | Islam Khan went to Bengal | eastern consolidation accelerated |
| 1611 | Jahangir married Mihr-un-Nisa | household access acquired a new political centre |
| 1615 | Mewar settlement; Roe's embassy began | negotiated incorporation and commercial diplomacy |
| 1620 | Kangra fell; Deccan pressure produced gains | imperial capacity remained substantial |
| 1621–22 | Khusrau died in February 1621 under the reconciled chronology; Itimad-ud-Daulah died and Qandahar fell in 1622 | succession and frontier pressures converged |
| 1622–25/early 1626 | Khurram rebelled | a successful prince became a rival focus of power |
| 1626 | Mahabat Khan seized Jahangir near the Jhelum | custody of the ruler tested the depth of the state |
| 1627 | Jahangir died; Asaf Khan secured Khurram's path | transition to Shah Jahan |

### Objection and resolution

**Objection:** So many biased sources make firm knowledge impossible.

**Resolution:** Bias does not erase evidence. It changes the question. A royal memoir may be weak proof of universal implementation but strong proof of royal self-presentation. Agreement across different source types can strengthen an event; disagreement requires a narrower conclusion.

### UPSC application

The safest answer sentence is: “The source establishes X in a specified setting; Y remains an interpretation because implementation, motive or wider reach requires corroboration.”

### Revision notes

1. The *Jahangirnama* is both narrative evidence and imperial self-fashioning.
2. Later continuation means authorship must not be assumed uniformly.
3. Court chronicles preserve sequence but carry patronage and hindsight.
4. Coins and farmans prove formal acts, not total control.
5. Paintings communicate political representation.
6. European visitors saw the court through diplomatic-commercial aims.
7. Sikh traditions preserve community memory and require genre awareness.
8. Chronology must separate the early accession crisis from post-1622 politics.
9. A cause is not proved merely because it precedes an event.
10. Every strong answer states both evidentiary value and limit.

### Original Mains application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why source criticism is central to the study of Jahangir's reign.

**Model answer**

Jahangir's reign is documented through sources that reveal different layers of power. The *Jahangirnama* records chronology, orders, observations and the emperor's self-image. It is therefore strong evidence for royal representation, but an announced order cannot prove uniform local implementation. Mutamad Khan's court narrative adds sequence while carrying retrospective and patronage limits.

Material evidence changes the enquiry. Nur Jahan's coins and farmans demonstrate formal public authority in particular settings, but do not prove sole rule. Paintings and tombs show hierarchy and patronage rather than unmediated events. Hawkins and Roe illuminate commercial diplomacy through interested European eyes. Sikh traditions preserve the community meaning of Guru Arjan's death, which the imperial memoir cannot exhaust.

Thus historians must triangulate genre, date and purpose. The method yields bounded facts and qualified causation instead of choosing between credulous acceptance and total scepticism.

**Why this earns marks:** It names distinct sources, explains their uses and limits, and shows how method changes substantive conclusions.

## Lesson 2 — Accession was an achievement, not an automatic transfer

### Visual: how a Mughal accession became effective

```text
dynastic seniority
      +
noble recognition
      +
control of treasury, court and army
      +
rewards, offices and clemency
      +
public language of justice
      =
effective accession
```

Salim was Akbar's surviving eldest son because Murad and Daniyal had died. Yet the Mughals did not follow a binding rule of primogeniture under which the eldest son inherited automatically. A prince needed dynastic legitimacy, elite support and practical control.

### 1. The Allahabad background

Between 1599 and 1604 Salim maintained a separate political base at Allahabad. He appointed officials, controlled resources and issued coins in his own name. He was also connected to Bir Singh Deo Bundela's killing of Abu'l Fazl in 1602. These acts show a serious pre-accession conflict. They should not be turned into a permanent psychological diagnosis that explains every later decision.

Akbar and Salim also reconciled. Dynastic politics could move between rupture and accommodation because both sides understood the cost of open civil war.

### 2. The Khusrau alternative

Raja Man Singh was Khusrau's maternal uncle, while Mirza Aziz Koka was linked by marriage. They considered Khusrau as an alternative while Akbar was dying. The proposal failed to secure decisive noble backing and lacked Akbar's endorsement. Opposition invoked Timurid-Chagatai custom against replacing a living father with his son.

This was not a popular election. Nor was it a Hindu bloc against a Muslim claimant. Kinship, service, reputation and the fear of disorder shaped the calculation.

### 3. Jahangir's first political choices

Jahangir did not carry out a general purge of Khusrau, Man Singh or Aziz Koka. He nevertheless distrusted parts of the older Akbar-era elite and promoted persons whom he considered reliable. Shaikh Farid Bukhari received high office. Mirza Ghiyas Beg, later Itimad-ud-Daulah, rose through financial-administrative service as well as later kinship.

The accession bargain can be stated plainly:

| Need | Instrument |
|---|---|
| reassure established elites | retention, pardon and confirmation |
| reward dependable supporters | titles, mansabs and offices |
| present legitimate sovereignty | imperial title, audience and ceremony |
| promise corrective rule | early regulations and justice imagery |
| preserve state capacity | continued mansab, jagir, revenue and provincial structures |

### 4. Continuity and change from Akbar

Jahangir inherited the composite nobility, provincial framework and fiscal-military machinery. He did not redesign the state in 1605. Change occurred in access, trust and the distribution of favour. This difference matters: institutional continuity can coexist with a new court coalition.

### UPSC application

Avoid “smooth hereditary succession.” Write instead that dynastic seniority supplied a strong claim, while noble recognition, control of institutions and political reconciliation made that claim effective.

### Revision notes

1. Salim became Jahangir in 1605.
2. Murad and Daniyal had predeceased Akbar.
3. Salim's Allahabad base displayed autonomy.
4. Abu'l Fazl's killing belongs to the pre-accession conflict.
5. Khusrau had kinship connections to Man Singh and Aziz Koka.
6. The alternative-candidacy proposal lacked decisive support.
7. Mughal succession was not automatic primogeniture.
8. Jahangir avoided a general purge at accession.
9. Trusted associates gained office without replacing the whole Akbarian elite.
10. Continuity of institutions required renewal of political confidence.

### Original Mains application

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why should Jahangir's accession be treated as political consolidation rather than a mere dynastic date?

**Model answer**

Salim possessed the strongest dynastic claim in 1605 as Akbar's surviving eldest son, but Mughal succession was not governed by automatic primogeniture. His earlier Allahabad establishment, coinage and association with Abu'l Fazl's killing had damaged trust. Meanwhile, Khusrau's kinship links encouraged Raja Man Singh and Mirza Aziz Koka briefly to consider an alternative.

The proposal failed because it lacked Akbar's approval and sufficient noble support. Salim's accession then required more than genealogy. He retained much of the inherited elite, avoided a general purge, rewarded trusted servants and presented public regulations and justice as marks of legitimate rule. The mansab-jagir, provincial and revenue frameworks continued.

Therefore 1605 represents renewal of a governing coalition inside an inherited state. Institutional continuity reduced uncertainty, but only active bargaining, office distribution and command made Jahangir's claim effective.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains succession through both dynastic legitimacy and practical coalition-building.

## Lesson 3 — The emperor as judge

Progress: 3/14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Kingship, twelve orders and the chain of justice

### Visual: claim, channel and result

```text
imperial order or justice symbol
             |
             v
transmission through officials
             |
             v
local petition, enquiry or enforcement
             |
             v
documented outcome in a particular case

Each arrow needs evidence; the first does not prove the last.
```

Jahangir described sovereignty as a divine trust requiring justice and benevolence. This language continued an Akbarian idea of the divinely favoured just ruler while giving Jahangir a personal public identity.

### 1. The twelve orders

The early regulations recorded in the memoir addressed unofficial road and river cesses, merchants' goods, inheritance, public works, sarais, hospitals, forcible seizure of houses or cultivated land, and mutilation as punishment. Their range projected an emperor who restrained official predation and protected circulation, property and bodily integrity.

The evidence has three levels:

| Level | What can be said |
|---|---|
| proclamation | the memoir records a benevolent programme |
| administrative circulation | commands could be transmitted to officials |
| implementation | each locality or case needs separate evidence |

It is therefore equally wrong to dismiss the orders as empty words and to call them a uniformly enforced modern code.

### 2. The chain of justice

The **zanjir-i-adl**, or chain of justice, was described as a chain with bells at the Agra riverfront-fort setting. It made a visual claim: an injured subject could seek the emperor's direct attention when intermediaries failed.

The chain represented:

- personal accessibility of the ruler;
- correction of official abuse;
- justice as the moral foundation of sovereignty;
- the emperor's body as the final hearing point.

It did not represent:

- an independent judiciary;
- equality before an impersonal constitution;
- universal practical access;
- abolition of local hierarchy or distance.

### 3. An intervention and its limit

Satish Chandra records the case of a widow whose daughter was taken in a matter connected with Muqarrab Khan's establishment. An enquiry led to punishment of an attendant, reduction of the governor's mansab and an allowance for the widow. This shows that exemplary intervention could cross status boundaries. One case cannot prove routine access for all.

The same ruler could act with arbitrary cruelty. Benevolent kingship and despotism were not mutually exclusive in a personalised monarchy. Punishment, mercy and public justice all enhanced the sovereign's centrality.

### Comparison with modern rule of law

| Jahangirian justice | Modern constitutional ideal |
|---|---|
| ruler-centred and discretionary | institution-centred and rule-bound |
| access imagined through the sovereign | access structured through courts and rights |
| exemplary correction | regular procedure and review |
| justice legitimises personal monarchy | law limits office-holders, including rulers |

The comparison clarifies difference; it should not become a moral scorecard detached from the seventeenth century.

### Revision notes

1. Jahangir linked kingship with divine selection and justice.
2. The twelve orders covered fiscal, commercial, property and welfare concerns.
3. A memoir records proclamation more securely than implementation.
4. The chain of justice made royal accessibility visible.
5. Direct petition remained ruler-centred.
6. The chain was not a modern appellate court.
7. A documented enquiry can prove intervention in that case.
8. Exemplary justice cannot establish empire-wide regularity.
9. Benevolence and arbitrary punishment could coexist.
10. The political effect was to centralise moral authority in the emperor.

### Original Mains application

**Question (15 marks, 200 words):** Examine Jahangir's chain of justice as both a political symbol and a possible administrative mechanism.

**Model answer**

The *zanjir-i-adl* linked Jahangir's kingship to the claim that an aggrieved subject could reach the sovereign when officials failed. Its position at the Agra riverfront-fort setting and its bells made justice visible and audible. Politically, it represented the emperor as accessible, benevolent and capable of correcting abuse.

The symbol also corresponded to an administrative possibility. Petitions could prompt enquiry, punishment, rank reduction or compensation. The reported case involving Muqarrab Khan's establishment illustrates an intervention against wrongdoing despite the status of a governor.

However, representation must not be confused with routine implementation. The chain did not create an independent judiciary, a universal appellate right or equality before impersonal law. Distance, social rank and official mediation remained powerful. A single exemplary case establishes that intervention occurred, not its frequency across the empire.

Thus the chain was neither empty propaganda nor modern rule of law. It was a ruler-centred mechanism whose practical use and symbolic force both strengthened personal monarchy.

**Why this earns marks:** It treats symbol and mechanism together while preserving the crucial implementation limit.

```text

          |
          v

          |

          |
          +--> new Badakhshani, Afghan and Indian supporters

```

Khusrau rebelled about six months after Jahangir's accession. He began with a small following, but movement created opportunity. A mobile prince could attract soldiers, clients and dissatisfied persons before the new emperor's authority was fully settled.

### 1. Why Punjab mattered

Punjab was not merely a backdrop. Lahore was a major centre, while north-western routes offered movement toward Afghanistan and possible recruitment. Jahangir also feared the uncertain loyalty of some older nobles, Rajputs, Badakhshanis and tribal military groups. These suspicions came through the emperor's own hostile language and should not be treated as an objective description of whole communities.

### 2. Why the revolt failed

Dilawar Khan refused Khusrau entry into Lahore. Without a secure urban base, treasury and sustained high-noble coalition, the prince's rapidly growing following remained fragile. Jahangir moved quickly. Khusrau was defeated near Bhairowal and captured.

The mechanism was:

```text
dynastic prestige + movement + local recruitment
                    versus
imperial pursuit + closed city + superior resources
```

### 3. Punishment as a political message

Jahangir punished followers severely. The brutality had an exemplary function: it warned nobles and military clients that a competing prince could not safely become a tool against the emperor. Penalties also reached persons suspected of blessing or assisting Khusrau.

Khusrau was later blinded after another alleged conspiracy. He remained a focus of intrigue and died at Burhanpur in Khurram's custody. This session uses **February 1621**: the detailed later narrative in `_med_part2_mughal.txt` lines 7709–7712 dates the strangling to February 1621, consistent with the canonical chronology. The same assigned OCR contains an isolated “1620” rendering at lines 7415–7419. That internal conflict is retained rather than silently harmonised; the detailed dated passage and canonical chronology control the working date. The later death must not be collapsed into the 1606 battle sequence.

### 4. What the revolt reveals

The episode does not show institutional collapse. It shows that a dynastic state could remain administratively capable while facing rival princely legitimacy. A prince was dangerous because succession, service and armed followings were connected.

Guru Arjan's death belongs to the aftermath of Khusrau's rebellion, yet chronology alone does not settle causation. A careful answer must hold political suspicion, religious hostility, punitive extraction and community memory together.

### 1. The imperial representation

Jahangir's memoir describes Guru Arjan as a religious leader attracting Hindu and Muslim followers. Satish Chandra notes differing evidence. One reconstruction emphasises an order of execution; Jesuit, Sikh and other testimony has been used to argue that a heavy fine was imposed and that torture used to realise it caused death. - an imperial accusation linked Guru Arjan to Khusrau;
- property, fine or confiscatory punishment appears in the source field;
- coercion ended in the Guru's death;
- Sikh tradition remembers martyrdom;
- the exact sequence and balance of motives remain debated.

### 3. Why it was a turning point

Guru Arjan's death transformed Mughal authority in Sikh memory and affected the position of Guru Hargobind. It contributed to a harder relationship. It did not instantaneously create the mature military conflicts associated with later Gurus. A turning point begins a changed trajectory; it is not the completed outcome.

### 4. Comparative punishment

Jahangir also punished Shaikh Nizam Thanesari for association with Khusrau, but banished him rather than inflicting the same fatal outcome. The comparison supports political context while also showing unequal severity. It cannot erase the emperor's religious language.

Jahangir's reign did not abandon Akbar's administrative machine. Mansabs ranked service-holders; sawar obligations connected rank to cavalry; jagirs assigned revenue demand rather than ownership of land; provincial officers and local intermediaries converted paper orders into uneven practice.

### 1. Nobility as a balancing field

Jahangir retained a composite elite. Rajput rulers beyond the Kachhwahas received high rank. Afghans such as Khan-i-Jahan Lodi rose, and leading Maratha sardars increasingly entered imperial service. This broadened the recruitment field without creating modern equality. Rank still depended on sovereign patronage, service and hierarchy.

Court favour mattered because mansab, jagir, command and access were linked. Yet no one household could simply disregard the wider nobility. That institutional fact later weakens the fixed-junta thesis.

### 2. A Jahangir-era mansab refinement

**Du-aspa sih-aspa** allowed specified cavalry obligations to be increased without a corresponding rise in a noble's personal **zat** rank. It was a way to demand more mounted service selectively. It did not abolish dagh, chehra, muster or the distinction between paper rank and troops actually produced.

### 3. Deccan: success with restraint

Malik Ambar recovered ground after Akbar's death. Mughal operations in 1616–17 and 1621 produced victories and restoration of claimed territories. Jahangir did not seek unlimited annexation after the 1617 settlement. Calling Bijapur's ruler *farzand*, or son, expressed hierarchy with accommodation.

This restraint was not proof of effortless success. Deccan terrain, supply, shifting alliances and Malik Ambar's mobile warfare limited durable control. Khurram's later rebellion helped Deccani recovery. Detailed campaigns belong to the wider Deccan study; here the point is how frontier service built Khurram's political stature.

### 4. Qandahar: external event, internal trigger

Safavid Shah Abbas captured Qandahar in 1622. Cordial letters and gifts had not guaranteed preparedness. The loss was an external-policy setback, but within Jahangir's court it created a command question: who would lead relief, with which forces and at what political risk?

Khurram feared that absence on a difficult campaign could weaken his succession position. Thus an external frontier crisis became an internal political accelerant.

Mihr-un-Nisa came from a Persian migrant family. Her father Ghiyas Beg entered Mughal service and rose through administrative competence. She first married Ali Quli Istajlu, known as Sher Afghan. After his death in a clash in Bengal, she lived at court and married Jahangir in 1611.

### 1. Biography without murder romance

Later chroniclers told a story in which Jahangir desired Mihr-un-Nisa earlier and engineered Sher Afghan's death. Satish Chandra rejects this as insecure. The known clash involved an attempt by Governor Qutbuddin Khan to act against or transfer Sher Afghan amid accusations concerning Bengal. Both men died.

Historical explanation should not rely on a sensational story when political and documentary evidence is available.

### 2. Why the household was political

The imperial household controlled proximity, petitions, marriage, charity, patronage and ceremonial access. These were not “private” in a modern sense. A request reaching the emperor through a powerful consort could affect office or favour.

Nur Jahan's principal channels included:

- close companionship with Jahangir;
- management of the imperial household;
- reception of messengers and petitioners;
- recommendation and patronage;
- ties to experienced officials;
- marriage connections affecting succession;
- travel, hunting and visible court participation.

### 3. The central relationships

Itimad-ud-Daulah became an important wazir, but his service career preceded his daughter's marriage. Asaf Khan was a major noble and father of Arjumand Banu, who married Khurram. Nur Jahan's daughter Ladli Begum married Shahryar.

These ties created overlapping interests. Asaf Khan's later support for Khurram shows why “Nur Jahan family” cannot be treated as one permanent actor.

### 4. Agency within patriarchy

Nur Jahan was an exceptional woman exercising authority through institutions available at the top of a patriarchal monarchy. Her career proves that the imperial household could become a channel of public power. It does not prove general political equality for women or the disappearance of gender hierarchy.

Nur Jahan's importance is not based only on court gossip. Evidence associates her with farmans issued in her name, coins carrying her title, reception of messengers and active political intervention.

### 1. Farmans

A farman issued under Nur Jahan's authority demonstrates that she could act formally in an administrative or patronage setting. The exact document should be read for date, seal, recipient, subject and transmission. One farman cannot measure the proportion of government decisions she controlled.

### 2. Coin evidence

Coins were struck with formulas linking Jahangir's rule and Nur Jahan's authority, including the title **Badshah Begum**. Coinage was public and material; this makes it stronger than anecdote for demonstrating exceptional visibility.

It still requires caution. A legend represents authority and political image. It does not prove that Jahangir ceased making decisions, that every mint used the same formula continuously or that constitutional co-sovereignty existed in a modern sense.

### 3. Patronage

Nur Jahan is associated with gardens, charity, court fashion and architecture. The tomb of her father, Itimad-ud-Daulah at Agra, is the strongest material anchor. Built in the 1620s, it uses white marble and extensive inlay and stands as a bridge in Mughal funerary architecture.

The monument supports family commemoration, patronage and artistic transition. It cannot prove a political junta. Lists crediting Nur Jahan with every perfume, textile or design should be accepted only when supported by dated evidence.

### 4. Painting and natural history at Jahangir's court

Jahangir valued portraiture, individual attribution and close observation of animals and plants. Ustad Mansur is associated with precise natural-history studies. Such works supported connoisseurship and the ruler's image as a discriminating observer.

A painting remains a constructed representation. Its details can illuminate atelier practice and desired hierarchy, not automatically the literal scene of a political event.

### 5. The graded conclusion

The evidence strongly establishes unusually formal and public female authority. It plausibly shows that household access affected patronage and crisis politics. It does not establish sole rule or general female emancipation.

### Visual: static bloc versus moving alignment

```text
FIXED MODEL                         DYNAMIC MODEL
Nur Jahan                           marriage, office, campaign,
 + Itimad-ud-Daulah                grievance and succession
 + Asaf Khan                                  |
 + Khurram                                     v
 = one enduring bloc              ties strengthen, weaken or reverse

Easy story; weak chronology        Harder story; better evidence fit
```

Beni Prasad proposed that Nur Jahan, her father Itimad-ud-Daulah, her brother Asaf Khan and Prince Khurram formed a dominant “junta” after 1611. The thesis rightly draws attention to kinship and family advancement. Its strongest form claims more stability and monopoly than the evidence supports.

### 1. What supports the thesis

Nur Jahan's relatives held important offices and governorships. Itimad-ud-Daulah was wazir; Asaf Khan held major office; the extended family occupied influential posts. Khurram married Asaf Khan's daughter and rose rapidly. Mutamad Khan complained that the family received extensive mansabs and jagirs.

These facts establish concentration of influence. They do not yet establish one permanent purpose.

### 2. Nurul Hasan's critique

Nurul Hasan showed that promotions remained distributed among several families and nobles. Mahabat Khan retained favour outside the proposed bloc. No secure contemporary evidence proves a stable Nur Jahan-Khurram alliance from 1611 to 1620. Khurram's rise followed the failure of Parvez and his own Mewar and Deccan performance.

European rumours are especially weak when treated as proof of the inner structure of court factions. Roe did not know Persian well and depended partly on intermediaries.

### 3. Irfan Habib's refinement

Evidence of the family's many offices confirms a substantial concentration of influence. It still does not prove that the entire nobility divided into two camps or that Persian nobles acted as one ethnic bloc. Political factors crossed family and sectional lines.

### 4. Better periodisation

| Phase | Most defensible reading |
|---|---|
| 1611–21 | family advancement, household influence and fluid alliances; Jahangir remains active |
| after 1622 | Itimad-ud-Daulah's death, illness, Qandahar, Khurram's rebellion and Mahabat's challenge produce active crisis politics |

Asaf Khan's later support for Khurram against Nur Jahan's succession preference decisively demonstrates that family connection did not freeze interest.

### 5. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Coins, farmans and family offices prove that the junta controlled the empire.

**Reply:** They prove exceptional authority and influence. “Junta” additionally requires evidence of stable joint action, monopoly over promotion and durable common interest. That additional claim is not established.

1. Beni Prasad formulated the fixed-junta thesis.
2. Its proposed members were Nur Jahan, Itimad-ud-Daulah, Asaf Khan and Khurram.
3. Family office-holding was real.
4. Mutamad Khan criticised the family's advancement.
5. Nurul Hasan tested promotion and alliance evidence.
6. Promotions were not monopolised by one group.
7. Khurram's campaigns explain much of his rise.
8. No firm 1611–20 Nur Jahan-Khurram alliance is demonstrated.
9. Irfan Habib confirms concentration without proving two permanent camps.
10. Post-1622 crisis politics differed from the earlier phase.
11. Faction should be analysed as changing relationship, not a permanent label.

**Question (15 marks, 200 words):** Critically examine the “Nur Jahan junta” thesis.

**Model answer**

Its value lies in highlighting the political role of the imperial household, kinship, marriage and patronage. Nur Jahan's family undeniably occupied major offices and governorships.

The model becomes weak when concentration is treated as monopoly. Nurul Hasan showed that promotions remained distributed among several noble families and that Mahabat Khan retained favour outside the proposed bloc. No secure contemporary evidence demonstrates a stable Nur Jahan-Khurram alliance between 1611 and 1620. Khurram's rise also followed his own success in Mewar and the Deccan.

Irfan Habib's evidence confirms extensive family office-holding, but not a binary division of the entire nobility. Persian, Rajput, Afghan, service and household ties crossed one another.

A better periodisation distinguishes family advancement and fluid alliances in 1611–21 from active crisis politics after 1622. Asaf Khan's later support for Khurram further disproves permanent family unity.

Thus the fixed junta should be rejected without erasing Nur Jahan's exceptional influence.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the thesis fairly, tests its key claims and replaces it with a better chronological model.

Khurram had become Jahangir's most accomplished son. He received the title Shah Jahan, very high rank and visible court honour. None of these created a legal right to succeed.

### 1. The succession field

Khusrau, Parvez, Khurram and Shahryar each mattered at different moments. Khurram secured custody of Khusrau, who died at Burhanpur in February 1621 under the reconciled chronology explained in Lesson 4. Satish Chandra attributes the killing to Khurram, while the public explanation was illness. This removal sharpened rather than settled distrust.

Shahryar's marriage to Ladli Begum gave Nur Jahan a relationship with another prince. Khurram could interpret that connection as a threat, but the marriage alone did not cause rebellion.

### 2. Qandahar and command

When Shah Abbas besieged and captured Qandahar in 1622, Jahangir summoned Khurram to lead relief. Khurram feared a long absence and sought full military command, influence over Punjab and security for his family, including Ranthambhor. These demands connected military feasibility to succession insurance.

An exchange of hostile letters, requests for troops to return to court, reassignment of jagirs and a clash near Dholpur increased conflict. Resources were not a secondary detail: jagirs financed troops and political independence.

### 3. The course

Khurram moved toward Agra but was defeated at Bilochpur in March 1623. He then moved through eastern India, temporarily controlling parts of Orissa, Bengal and Bihar. Mahabat Khan defeated him near Jhusi. Khurram retreated to the Deccan and sought Malik Ambar's support, but failed against Burhanpur.

He eventually submitted. Dara and Aurangzeb were sent as hostages, and Balaghat was assigned to him. The rebellion ended by early 1626.

### 4. Consequences

- imperial resources were diverted for several years;
- Qandahar relief coordination suffered;
- Deccani powers recovered ground;
- noble loyalties shifted;
- a prince's regional army became a rival focus of power;
- succession conflict hardened.

### 5. Why one-cause explanations fail

“Nur Jahan caused the rebellion” erases the emperor's commands, frontier emergency, jagir bargaining and Khurram's ambition. “Only resources mattered” erases kinship and succession. Multi-causal explanation is not vagueness when each cause has a named mechanism.

Mahabat Khan had played a major role against Khurram. His separation from Prince Parvez, transfer to Bengal, demands for accounts and war elephants, and summons to court created fear that rivals intended his ruin.

### 1. The coup de main

In 1626 Mahabat Khan approached with a trusted Rajput force while the royal camp was crossing the Jhelum on the way to Kabul. Surprise and the vulnerability of a moving court allowed him to seize Jahangir. **Coup de main** means a swift tactical seizure.

The emperor's body mattered because orders, audience and legitimacy were personalised. Custody therefore produced immediate command leverage.

### 2. Nur Jahan's response

Her first armed effort failed. She then entered the captive setting and worked through negotiation, legitimacy and shifting allegiance. Accounts vary in detail, but the broad result is clear: she helped detach nobles and expose Mahabat's inability to build durable government.

Mahabat was an accomplished soldier but could not secure the treasury, administration, broad nobility or an alternative dynastic legitimacy. Within months he withdrew and later joined Khurram.

### 3. Religion: neither perfect tolerance nor simple bigotry

Jahangir broadly continued **sulh-i-kul**, meaning peace with all as an imperial governing orientation. He did not restore jizya, prohibited forcible conversion in his rules, allowed temples and churches, made grants to brahmans and religious establishments, celebrated several festivals and met Hindu and Muslim holy figures. His meetings with the Vedantin Jadrup and Sufi figures reveal genuine intellectual curiosity.

Coercive episodes remain:

- Guru Arjan's fatal punishment;
- jihad language and temple destruction in the Mewar campaign;
- Islamic sovereignty ritual at Kangra;
- destruction of a Varaha image at Pushkar, though other temples remained and grants followed;
- a 1617 anti-Jain order in Gujarat which inscriptional and Jain evidence suggests was not uniformly implemented;
- imprisonment of Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi for a period.

Policy must therefore be studied by declaration, context, locality, implementation and reversal.

### 4. Culture and memoir

Jahangir's close interest in flowers, animals and paintings shaped his self-image as an observing connoisseur. Ustad Mansur's natural studies, refined portraiture and the emperor's claims of attribution belong to this courtly setting. Nur Jahan shared elite interests in architecture, gardens and design.

The *Jahangirnama* is valuable precisely because observation and self-fashioning coexist. Alcohol, opium and declining health matter when they affected mobility and access. They cannot become a one-word explanation for the entire reign.

### 5. Death and transition

Jahangir died in 1627 near Lahore. Asaf Khan controlled the immediate succession setting, restricted Nur Jahan, summoned Khurram and used support from the diwan, chief nobles and army to secure the transition. Rival claimants were eliminated before Khurram's formal accession as Shah Jahan in 1628.

Nur Jahan received a settlement and retired from active politics. Her failure to secure Shahryar confirms the limit of her authority; her ability to survive with status also shows continued recognition.

### 6. Graded verdict

| Weak shortcut | Evidence-led verdict |
|---|---|
| “pleasure-loving weak ruler” | health mattered, but governance, campaigns and settlements continued |
| “Nur Jahan ruled everything” | she held exceptional formal authority without replacing Jahangir |
| “unchanged Akbarian peace” | continuity coexisted with coercion, frontier loss and princely rebellion |
| “decline began in 1605” | serious strain appeared, but institutions retained capacity |

Jahangir's reign was **consolidation under pressure**. Mewar, Bengal and Kangra show achievement. Qandahar, Khurram and Mahabat show limits. Nur Jahan's career reveals the political power of the imperial household, while the failure of sole-rule and fixed-junta explanations protects analytical balance.

- **A — Correct:** The formulation incorporates documented achievements and strains without reducing one to the other.
- **B — Incorrect:** Survival demonstrates capacity, but rebellion, resource diversion and Qandahar were genuine costs.
- **C — Incorrect:** The evidence supports serious pressure without showing the disappearance of imperial institutions.
- **D — Incorrect:** Household access mattered, but frontier distance, princely armies and provincial mechanisms also shaped outcomes.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks — Justice and legitimacy

**Question:** Assess the political meaning of Jahangir's twelve orders and chain of justice. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer**

Jahangir's early regulations and chain of justice helped transform a contested accession into a claim of legitimate rule. The twelve orders addressed unofficial cesses, merchant property, inheritance, public works, seizure of houses or cultivated land, hospitals and mutilation. They presented the emperor as restraining official abuse and protecting circulation.

The zanjir-i-adl at the Agra riverfront-fort setting made accessibility visible: a petitioner could symbolically bypass failed intermediaries and summon the sovereign. This centralised moral authority in the ruler.

However, the memoir securely proves proclamation more than uniform implementation. The chain did not establish an independent judiciary, universal access or equality before impersonal law. A reported enquiry against wrongdoing connected with Muqarrab Khan's establishment proves exemplary intervention, not normal practice everywhere.

Thus the orders and chain were meaningful instruments of ruler-centred legitimacy whose actual reach must be tested case by case.

**Unique scoring note:** Award most credit for explicitly separating proclamation, administrative transmission and local implementation.

## 15 marks — Nur Jahan and faction

**Question:** “The rejection of a fixed Nur Jahan junta does not require rejection of Nur Jahan's power.” Discuss. Answer in 200 words.

**Model answer**

Nur Jahan's power is supported by household access, patronage, farmans, coin legends and active crisis intervention. Her father Itimad-ud-Daulah and brother Asaf Khan held important offices, while marriages connected the family to Khurram and Shahryar. These facts make a merely ceremonial interpretation untenable.

Beni Prasad converted this concentration into a stable junta of Nur Jahan, Itimad-ud-Daulah, Asaf Khan and Khurram. Nurul Hasan's critique shows why the stronger claim fails. Promotions remained distributed among other nobles, Mahabat Khan retained favour and no secure contemporary evidence proves a durable Nur Jahan-Khurram bloc from 1611 to 1620. Khurram's own Mewar and Deccan record explains much of his rise.

Periodisation resolves the problem. Between 1611 and 1621, family influence operated amid fluid alignments and continued Jahangirian decision-making. After 1622, illness, Itimad-ud-Daulah's death, Qandahar and rebellion forced Nur Jahan into active crisis politics.

Therefore a changing-network model preserves her exceptional authority while rejecting monopoly, permanence and sovereign replacement.

**Unique scoring note:** The answer must distinguish evidence of concentration from evidence of stable common action.

## 20 marks — Reign assessment

**Question:** “Jahangir's reign was one of consolidation under pressure rather than the beginning of Mughal decline.” Critically examine. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer**

Jahangir inherited Akbar's mansab-jagir system, provincial structure and composite nobility. The 1606 Khusrau rebellion shows that continuity did not make succession automatic, but its suppression also demonstrates continuing state capacity.

The middle reign produced substantial consolidation. The 1615 Mewar settlement combined military pressure with protection of Sisodia honour: Amar Singh accepted suzerainty, Karan Singh entered service and the dynasty remained. Islam Khan's campaigns, Dacca headquarters and selective conciliation weakened Afghan and Barah-Bhuiyan resistance in Bengal. Kangra's 1620 capture added a strategic and symbolic success. In the Deccan, Jahangir accepted limited settlement rather than unlimited annexation.

Serious pressures nevertheless accumulated. The fatal punishment of Guru Arjan exposed coercive kingship. Qandahar fell to the Safavids in 1622. Khurram converted military reputation, troops and jagir resources into a prolonged princely rebellion. Mahabat Khan's 1626 seizure exposed the vulnerability of a travelling, personalised court.

These crises did not dissolve the state. Mahabat could not secure finance, administration or broad legitimacy; imperial forces defeated Khurram; institutions continued into the transition. Nur Jahan's exceptional authority shaped access and crisis management without replacing Jahangir.

The reign therefore revealed structural risks—princely armies, frontier distance and household mediation—while retaining enough capacity to consolidate major regions. “Uneven consolidation” fits the evidence better than either golden calm or decline from 1605.

**Unique scoring note:** A top answer must use at least two achievements and two strains, then explain why strain did not equal institutional collapse.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Central thesis

- Jahangir, Prince Salim, reigned from 1605 to 1627.
- Best formula: **institutional continuity + regional consolidation + household authority + succession pressure + evidence limits**.
- Reject three absolutes: immediate decline, total Nur Jahan rule and smooth Akbarian continuity.

## 2. Accession and kingship

- Salim's Allahabad base, appointments, treasury control and coinage showed pre-accession autonomy.
- Bir Singh Deo Bundela killed Abu'l Fazl in 1602 in an action connected to Salim.
- Man Singh and Aziz Koka considered Khusrau, but the proposal lacked decisive support.
- Mughal succession was dynastic and political, not automatic primogeniture or noble election.
- Jahangir avoided a general purge and renewed the governing coalition through office, reward and public justice.
- Kingship was represented as divinely favoured, benevolent and corrective.

## 3. Twelve orders and chain of justice

- Orders addressed cesses, merchant goods, inheritance, public works, sarais, hospitals, property seizure and mutilation.
- Evidence ladder: **proclamation → transmission → implementation**.
- Zanjir-i-adl: bells and chain at Agra's riverfront-fort setting.
- Meaning: accessible ruler and final correction.
- Limit: not an independent judiciary, universal right or proof of equal practical access.
- Muqarrab Khan episode: useful case of enquiry and rank reduction; not proof of routine universal justice.

## 4. Khusrau and Guru Arjan

- Khusrau rebelled in 1606 and moved toward Lahore.
- Dilawar Khan closed Lahore; defeat followed near Bhairowal.
- His following was varied; do not create a communal bloc.
- Severe punishment deterred use of a rival prince.
- Later blinding followed another alleged conspiracy; the working date for death in Khurram's custody is February 1621.
- **Date-source caution:** `_med_part2_mughal.txt` lines 7415–7419 contain an isolated 1620 rendering, but its detailed later account at lines 7709–7712 gives February 1621, matching the canonical chronology. Use February 1621 and disclose the OCR conflict.
- Guru Arjan: Jahangir alleged blessing or assistance to Khusrau.
- Memoir proves imperial accusation and hostile religious language, not every alleged act.
- Fine, confiscation, execution order and fatal torture are treated differently across sources.
- Secure conclusion: excessive imperial coercion ended in death.
- Turning point in Mughal-Sikh relations; not instant completion of later militarisation.

## 5. Mewar

- Jahangir directed renewed pressure from Ajmer; Khurram led the decisive phase.
- War damage and exhaustion made negotiation possible.
- 1615: Amar Singh accepted Mughal suzerainty.
- Rana excused personal attendance; son or brother would serve.
- Karan Singh received honour and mansab.
- Sisodia dynasty retained; no direct annexation.
- No compulsory matrimonial alliance.
- Chittor's fortifications were not to be rebuilt.
- Formula: **force + exhaustion + protected honour = durable unequal settlement**.

## 6. Bengal and Kangra

- Islam Khan became governor in 1608 and shifted the centre to Dacca.
- He divided zamindar opposition and campaigned against Musa Khan's Barah-Bhuiyans.
- Usman Khan's defeat broke major Afghan resistance.
- Some defeated chiefs were restored or incorporated; Afghans entered service.
- Ahom expedition failed: eastward control remained uneven.
- Kangra attempt failed in 1615; Raja Bikramajit Baghela secured the fort in 1620.
- Garrison and faujdar supported occupation.
- Jahangir's khutba and mosque order performed sovereignty.
- Fort possession did not equal complete Himalayan administration.

## 7. Deccan, Qandahar and administration

- Mansab, zat, sawar, jagir and provincial offices continued.
- Jagir was revenue assignment, not ownership of soil.
- Du-aspa sih-aspa raised selected mounted obligation without equivalent zat increase.
- Jahangir-era Deccan gains in 1616–17 and 1621 remained limited and reversible.
- Deliberate restraint did not mean either total victory or simple weakness.
- Qandahar fell to Shah Abbas in 1622.
- Diplomatic cordiality had not ensured frontier readiness.
- Relief command became a succession and resource issue for Khurram.

## 8. Hawkins and Roe

- Hawkins: English Company representative seeking Mughal commercial access.
- Personal court access did not produce secure unlimited privileges.
- Swally/Suvali, 1612: English naval success changed bargaining against Portuguese power.
- No transfer of sovereignty followed.
- Roe represented James I from 1615 to 1619.
- Journal: audiences, gifts, rank disputes, delays and commercial aims.
- 1618 permissions: limited facilities, especially Surat-related, on Mughal terms.
- No territory, exclusive empire-wide monopoly, universal immunity or constitutional start of British rule.
- Visitor and factory sources require comparison with farmans and local practice.

## 9. Nur Jahan

- Mihr-un-Nisa was daughter of Ghiyas Beg/Itimad-ud-Daulah.
- First husband: Ali Quli Istajlu, Sher Afghan.
- Later murder-romance is insecure.
- Married Jahangir in 1611.
- Asaf Khan was her brother; his daughter Arjumand Banu/Mumtaz married Khurram.
- Ladli Begum, Nur Jahan's daughter, married Shahryar.
- Power channels: access, household, petitions, patronage, kinship, documents, coins and crisis action.
- Farmans prove formal acts at document scale.
- Coins bearing Badshah Begum establish public titulature.
- Neither proves control of every decision.
- Gender verdict: exceptional agency within patriarchal monarchy, not general equality.

## 10. Patronage and culture

- Itimad-ud-Daulah's Agra tomb: family commemoration, white marble and inlay transition.
- Popular claims about inventions require dated provenance.
- Jahangir valued portraiture, attribution and natural history.
- Ustad Mansur is the leading named painter for flora and fauna.
- Paintings are constructed representations, not photographs.
- The memoir combines observation with imperial self-fashioning.

## 11. Junta debate

- Beni Prasad: Nur Jahan + Itimad-ud-Daulah + Asaf Khan + Khurram formed a dominant fixed junta.
- Useful insight: kinship and patronage mattered.
- Weak claim: one stable alliance monopolised promotions and divided the whole court.
- Nurul Hasan: promotions were wider; no secure 1611–20 Nur Jahan-Khurram alliance.
- Khurram rose through Mewar and Deccan success.
- Irfan Habib confirms extensive family office-holding without proving two fixed camps.
- Better periodisation: 1611–21 fluid influence; after 1622 active crisis politics.
- Asaf Khan's later support for Khurram demonstrates shifting interest.

## 12. Khurram's rebellion

- Khurram possessed high rank, title Shah Jahan and campaign prestige.
- Khusrau died in his custody in February 1621 under the reconciled chronology; the isolated 1620 OCR rendering remains an explicit source caveat.
- Shahryar-Ladli marriage created another succession channel.
- Qandahar relief command threatened prolonged absence.
- Punjab control, Ranthambhor, jagirs, troops and family security were disputed.
- Bilochpur defeat: March 1623.
- Temporary eastern gains followed; Mahabat defeated him near Jhusi.
- Retreat to Deccan and failed Burhanpur effort.
- Submission by early 1626; Dara and Aurangzeb sent as hostages; Balaghat assigned.
- Consequences: diversion, Deccan recovery, failed Qandahar coordination and hardened succession.

## 13. Mahabat Khan and final transition

- Grievance setting: separation from Parvez, Bengal transfer, accounts, elephants and summons.
- 1626 Jhelum crossing enabled surprise.
- Rajput contingent provided compact coercive force.
- Capturing Jahangir gave immediate legitimacy leverage.
- Mahabat lacked treasury, administration, broad nobility and alternate dynasty.
- Nur Jahan's initial force failed; political manoeuvre succeeded.
- Jahangir died near Lahore in 1627.
- Asaf Khan restricted Nur Jahan and secured Khurram's route.
- Formal accession of Shah Jahan followed in 1628.
- Nur Jahan retired with a settlement.

## 14. Religion and final judgement

- Broad continuity with sulh-i-kul; no jizya restoration.
- Forcible conversion prohibited in Jahangiri rules.
- Festivals, grants, temples, churches, Sufi meetings and Jadrup show plural practice.
- Guru Arjan, Mewar/Kangra language, Varaha destruction, anti-Jain order and Sirhindi imprisonment show coercion.
- Implementation varied; the Gujarat anti-Jain order was not uniformly enforced.
- Health and intoxicants affected access but do not prove total abdication.
- Final verdict: **a governing emperor and an exceptional consort in an empire consolidating unevenly under growing pressure**.
