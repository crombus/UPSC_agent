# Shah Jahan and the Evolution of the Mughal Ruling Class - Complete Learner-First Live Edition

## Learning roadmap

Shah Jahan's reign is often remembered through marble, symmetry and court splendour. That view is real but incomplete. The same court that built monuments also ranked nobles, assigned revenue, verified cavalry, managed competing service groups and financed campaigns from the Deccan to Balkh. The topic therefore asks one connected question: **how did an emperor-centred ruling class sustain visible imperial power while adapting to a growing mismatch between rank claims and realised resources?**

| 11 | The succession struggle of 1657-58 as an institutional stress test | Advanced |

Four distinctions control the entire sequence:

```text
formal rank       != effective military resources
jama              != hasil
ethnic label      != fixed political faction
imperial splendour != universal prosperity or unlimited fiscal capacity
```

## Visual first: one reign, two simultaneous stories

```text
                    SHAH JAHAN, 1628-1658
                              |
             +----------------+----------------+
             |                                 |
       VISIBLE CAPACITY                    MANAGED STRAIN
             |                                 |
  court ritual and hierarchy          expanding ranks and salaries
  Peacock Throne and capital          jagir quality and transfers
  monuments and workshops             jama-hasil mismatch
  armies and provincial command       remount and muster adjustments
             |                                 |
             +----------------+----------------+
                              |
                 neither effortless "golden age"
                 nor completed imperial collapse
```

The reign makes sense only when these two stories are held together. Splendour demonstrates the ability to mobilise. Fiscal adjustment demonstrates that mobilisation was neither costless nor automatic.

### 1. Why this topic is not a biography

The learner's first temptation is to tell Shah Jahan's life from accession to confinement. That chronology is necessary, but the topic's analytical centre is the **ruling class**: the princes, mansabdars, high officers and incorporated regional elites through whom the emperor governed, fought and collected revenue.

The reign is therefore used as a window into three connected systems:

1. **political authority** - accession, obedience, patronage and succession;
2. **service hierarchy** - mansab, zat, sawar, promotion and command; and
3. **fiscal support** - jagir assignment, assessed revenue and actual realisation.

Architecture enters only where it reveals court ideology, labour mobilisation and consumption. The complete history of Mughal artistic forms belongs elsewhere.

### 2. Chronology as a chain of institutional pressures

| Date | Event | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| 1627-28 | Jahangir died; Khurram's succession was secured; formal accession is conventionally dated 1628 | power passed through coalition and force, not automatic primogeniture |
| 1628-29 | Jujhar Singh Bundela submitted after imperial pressure | early test of regional power and service obedience |
| 1629-31 | Khan Jahan Lodi rebelled and was defeated | noble defiance, Deccan links and imperial consolidation |
| 1631 | standard date of Mumtaz Mahal's death | source-control test because one OCR narrative prints 1630 |
| 1633 | Daulatabad fell and the ruling Nizam Shahi line ended | territorial gain created administrative and fiscal obligations |
| 1635 | Shah Jahan first sat on the completed Peacock Throne | ceremony made imperial hierarchy material |
| 1636 | settlements with Bijapur and Golconda; Aurangzeb became Deccan viceroy | indirect hierarchy and provincial command |
| 1638 | Shahjahanabad was begun | capital-making joined sovereignty, urban demand and court presence |
| 1646-47 | Balkh campaign | mobilisation succeeded farther than sustainable political control |
| 1649-53 | Qandahar was lost and three recovery efforts failed | siege, supply and prestige tested imperial capacity |
| 1654 | Chittor's repaired walls were pulled down and some Mewar parganas sequestered | late tension within an enduring Rajput relationship |
| 1656-57 | renewed Deccan interventions and high assessed-revenue claims | expansion and fiscal pressure converged |
| 1657-58 | illness and war of succession | the service elite faced an uncertain future dispenser of rank and jagir |

Chronology prevents two errors. A tendency visible under Shah Jahan must not be called the fully developed later jagirdari crisis. Equally, later crisis cannot erase the earlier signs of mismatch.

### 3. What can "golden age" safely mean?

The label can describe:

- concentrated court wealth and ceremonial magnificence;
- the ability to organise skilled labour and costly materials;
- territorial reach and large-scale military mobilisation;
- a sophisticated hierarchy of offices, ranks and records; and
- the prestige of the Mughal court among Indian and Asian elites.

It cannot automatically describe:

- actual cash collection in every assignment;
- the welfare of all cultivators and artisans;
- equal access to power;
- success in every campaign; or
- freedom from structural pressure.

> **Misconception check:** A magnificent building is evidence of resource concentration. It is not, by itself, either proof of universal prosperity or proof that building expenditure ruined the empire.

### 4. The governing thesis

The most defensible interpretation is **visible apex with managed strain**. The system remained capable. It recruited, promoted, campaigned, built and governed. Yet the number and expectations of service-holders increased faster than secure fiscal realisation. Salary reduction, contingent ratios and month-ratings were attempts to preserve the system by translating paper claims into workable obligations.

That verdict is stronger than both extremes:

| Extreme | Why it fails | Better formulation |
|---|---|---|
| frictionless golden age | court evidence becomes a social average | high capacity with unequal distribution |
| decline already complete | adaptation and continued mobilisation disappear | pressure was real but still managed |

### Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why is Shah Jahan's reign better described as an apex with managed strain than as either a frictionless golden age or an age of completed decline?

**Model answer**

Shah Jahan's reign was an apex of visible Mughal capacity. The Peacock Throne, Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Jama Masjid and Shahjahanabad demonstrate concentrated resources, skilled production and an emperor-centred political language. The empire also mobilised large forces in the Deccan, Balkh and Qandahar.

Yet capacity was uneven. Mansab and salary claims expanded faster than secure revenue realisation. A jagir's assessed jama could exceed its hasil, while contingent ratios and the month-scale adjusted nominal cavalry and pay claims to practical conditions. Frontier campaigns distinguished battlefield mobilisation from sustainable occupation.

These pressures did not amount to completed collapse. Administration continued, the nobility remained broadly serviceable and fiscal devices still reconciled claims. Nor did court splendour prove universal prosperity. The balanced verdict is therefore chronological: Shah Jahan presided over a powerful and adaptive imperial order in which structural strains were visible but still managed.

**Scoring note:** Reward a two-sided thesis supported by one court-capacity example, one fiscal mechanism and an explicit warning against retrojecting the later crisis.

## Visual first: succession was assembled, not inherited automatically

```text
Jahangir dies, 1627
        |
        +--> Asaf Khan, high officers, nobles and armed forces act
        |
        +--> rival claimants must be contained
        |
        +--> Khurram reaches the centre and is enthroned in 1628
        |
        v
appointments + rewards + punishment + campaigns
        |
        v
new reign converts dynastic claim into effective command
```

The crown did not pass like a private estate. A prince needed lineage, but lineage became sovereignty only through control of the court, army, treasury and elite recognition.

### 1. The boundary from Jahangir to Shah Jahan

Prince Khurram had already acquired military prestige in Mewar and the Deccan, rebelled against Jahangir and later submitted. That full story belongs to the preceding reign. Here the key point is that Jahangir's death in 1627 opened a contested field. **Asaf Khan**, Khurram's father-in-law, worked with high officials, leading nobles and military resources to secure his succession.

Temporary arrangements and rival claimants were part of this process. They should not be inflated into a modern constitutional transfer. Shah Jahan's accession in 1628 represented the successful consolidation of a coalition around the prince most capable of taking command.

### 2. Early rebellion as a test of the service relationship

Two cases reveal different forms of defiance:

| Case | Political issue | Imperial response | Analytical limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jujhar Singh Bundela | regional power, jagir, tribute and expansion against Gond territory | military pressure, indemnity, confiscation, restored rank at first, then renewed campaign | not a permanent Rajput rebellion |
| Khan Jahan Lodi | mistrust, Afghan noble power and Deccan connections | pursuit and defeat during 1629-31 | not proof that all Afghans formed one opposition bloc |

Jujhar Singh first submitted in 1628-29 after the fall of Irij, paid an indemnity and recovered his rank, though some jagirs were confiscated. He later attacked the Gond ruler Prem Narayan, seized wealth and renewed defiance. Imperial punishment then reorganised territory and succession. Religious symbolism accompanied victory, including the destruction of Bir Singh Deo's Orchha temple, but the causal mechanism included extraction, territorial power and dynastic control. It should not be reduced to a purely religious war.

Khan Jahan Lodi's rebellion similarly joined personal mistrust, command, Deccan politics and the emperor-noble relationship. His Afghan identity mattered within court perceptions, but it did not turn every Afghan officer into a rebel.

### 3. Zamindars and chiefs: submission was graduated

Shah Jahan also pressured rulers and zamindars in Gondwana, Palamau, Kumaon, Garhwal, the Punjab hills, Baltistan and the lower Indus field. Outcomes varied:

```text
formal respect
   -> tribute or indemnity
   -> hostages/service
   -> fort or thana
   -> restored mansab
   -> partial territorial seizure
```

This ladder matters. A campaign could secure tribute or route access without creating uniform direct administration. Jagat Singh of Mau Nurpur, for example, was eventually restored to mansab after resistance. The Baltistan campaigns of 1634 and 1637 forced submission and an indemnity while also serving route interests toward Yarkand and Khotan.

### 4. Hugli: commerce, fortification and coercive sovereignty

The imperial attack on the Portuguese settlement at Hugli in 1632 did not fit the same jagir-rebellion pattern, but it tested who could fortify, trade and exercise coercion within Mughal territory. Court accounts justified intervention through Portuguese abuses, including abduction and enslavement, while imperial concern also involved an armed enclave and contested jurisdiction.

The capture demonstrated Mughal land power rather than the disappearance of Portuguese maritime leverage. Treatment of prisoners was harsh, and coercive religious pressure in the aftermath should not be sanitised. The episode therefore warns against describing Shah Jahan's consolidation only as orderly incorporation: imperial sovereignty could be punitive toward groups the court treated as defiant.

### 5. Consolidation was both coercive and incorporative

The emperor punished defiance, but he also restored service when useful. This was not inconsistency. A service monarchy sought obedience, revenue and military utility. Destruction of every defeated local house would have removed intermediaries the state needed.

> **Key mechanism:** coercion established the cost of defiance; renewed rank or local authority converted submission into usable service.

### 6. UPSC use

A strong answer on accession should not say merely "Asaf Khan helped Shah Jahan." It should explain that succession required a coalition and that early campaigns renewed the relation between imperial appointment and locally rooted power.

### Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain how Shah Jahan's accession and early campaigns reveal the political logic of Mughal consolidation.

**Model answer**

Shah Jahan's accession shows that Mughal sovereignty was dynastic but not automatically hereditary. After Jahangir's death in 1627, Asaf Khan, high officers, nobles and military resources helped Khurram contain rivals and secure enthronement in 1628. Lineage supplied a claim; coalition and command made it effective.

Early campaigns renewed that authority. Jujhar Singh Bundela first submitted after military pressure, indemnity and partial jagir confiscation, and his rank was restored. His later Gondwana aggression and defiance produced harsher intervention. Khan Jahan Lodi's rebellion similarly linked noble mistrust, command and Deccan politics.

These episodes reveal a dual mechanism. Coercion demonstrated the cost of resistance, while restored rank or local position converted useful elites back into imperial service. Ethnic or religious labels alone do not explain alignment. Shah Jahan's early consolidation was therefore a negotiated service monarchy backed by force, not uniform annexation or impersonal bureaucracy.

## Visual first: the court converted hierarchy into government

```text
                         EMPEROR
                            |
          +-----------------+-----------------+
          |                 |                 |
      appointment        ceremony          review
          |                 |                 |
   wazir/diwan,         audience, rank,    reports, accounts,
   mir bakhshi,         throne, gifts,     musters, transfers
   mir saman, sadr      spatial access
          |                 |                 |
          +-----------------+-----------------+

```

Ceremony did not replace administration. It made administrative hierarchy visible and attached honour to obedience.

### 1. Shah Jahan's idea of kingship

Shah Jahan inherited an exalted Mughal conception of monarchy. Sovereignty was presented as divinely favoured, just, ordered and centred on the emperor. The ruler stood above the ranked service elite as the source of appointment, promotion, titles, robes, gifts and revenue assignments.

The imperial image became more formal and elevated. Access to the ruler, position in audience, the language of titles and the right to perform or receive forms of salutation expressed political distance. Shah Jahan exempted high theologians from forms such as **sijda** or prostration, while retaining ceremonies including **jharoka darshan** and **tuladan**. These choices show adaptation, not a complete rejection of Akbar-Jahangir practice.

### 2. The Peacock Throne as an institution of visibility

The Peacock Throne took seven years to complete and Shah Jahan first sat upon it in 1635. Lahori's description emphasises jewels, gold, columns and peacock forms. The exact valuation belongs to a courtly account and must be treated as such.

Its political work can be stated without accepting every number literally:

```text
rare material + skilled department + ordered audience
                 |
                 v
       emperor appears as centre of wealth,
       hierarchy, justice and distribution
```

The throne was not merely furniture. It staged the proposition that noble status flowed from proximity to an unequal sovereign.

### 3. Administrative continuity with selective emphasis

Shah Jahan did not create a new central bureaucracy. He inherited the differentiated structure developed under Akbar and continued under Jahangir:

| Office | Main function | Political significance |
|---|---|---|
| wazir/diwan | revenue, expenditure and financial coordination | linked imperial ambition to accounts and assignments |
| mir bakhshi | mansabs, presentation, muster and military personnel | connected court rank to service verification |
| mir saman | imperial household and workshops | organised consumption, stores and ceremonial material |
| sadr | grants and religious-charitable matters | joined patronage to normative authority |
| provincial subadar and diwan | command and finance in provinces | reproduced central checks while remaining court-appointed |

**Sadullah Khan**, drawn from the Indian Muslim or Shaikhzada element, became Shah Jahan's celebrated wazir. His career illustrates that central office could reward administrative competence within patronage structures.

### 4. Centralisation was relational, not absolute

The emperor's control was strong because high office, rank and jagir depended on his sanction. Yet information and implementation travelled through officers, princes, zamindars and local specialists. Centralisation therefore meant **the ability to appoint, audit, transfer and arbitrate among intermediaries**, not the disappearance of intermediaries.

This resolves an apparent contradiction:

- frequent consultation and bargaining do not prove a weak monarchy;
- concentrated royal appointment does not prove that nobles lacked leverage.

The Mughal court was a **negotiated hierarchy**. Negotiation occurred inside an unequal structure.

### 5. Architecture and the capital as bounded evidence

Shahjahanabad, begun in 1638, linked the Red Fort, Jama Masjid, markets, streets and courtly movement. The capital was not completed in one instant. Its historical relevance here is the organisation of political space: access moved from city to fort to audience, mirroring the social ranking of the court.

The Taj Mahal and other buildings show resource coordination and dynastic memory. Their styles belong primarily to cultural history. Their political use here is limited to sovereignty, patronage and mobilisation.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Highly elaborate ceremony made government ornamental and detached from practical administration.

**Reply:** Ceremony could become costly and exclusive, but it also communicated precedence, disciplined access and converted promotion into visible honour. It worked alongside records, appointments and musters. The stronger criticism is not that ceremony was empty, but that courtly visibility can hide uneven provincial realisation.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 200 words

**Question:** Examine the relationship between court ceremonial and administrative centralisation under Shah Jahan.

**Model answer**

Shah Jahan's ceremonial order was not separate from administration. It represented an emperor-centred hierarchy in which rank, office, honour and access flowed from the sovereign.

The Peacock Throne, first used in 1635, arranged the audience around a materially elevated ruler. Titles, seating, robes and forms of salutation made relative status visible. Shahjahanabad, begun in 1638, extended this language into urban space through the fort, mosque, markets and ceremonial movement.

Administrative power supported the display. The wazir or diwan coordinated finance, the mir bakhshi handled mansabs and musters, and the mir saman organised the imperial household and workshops. Provincial subadars and diwans remained centrally appointed. Sadullah Khan's rise as wazir also demonstrates recruitment of administrative competence.

Yet centralisation was not direct rule without intermediaries. Nobles, princes and zamindars retained information and bargaining power. Ceremony therefore strengthened a negotiated hierarchy: it advertised the emperor's supremacy while practical government still depended on dispersed agents. Its limitation as evidence is that visible order at court cannot establish uniform provincial compliance or fiscal realisation.

**Scoring note:** Reward one ceremonial example, two institutional mechanisms and the distinction between strong central appointment and continued intermediary bargaining.

### 1. What does "ruling class" mean?

In this topic, the term refers principally to the high service elite whose members:

- held mansabs and major offices;
- commanded troops or administered provinces;
- received cash or revenue assignments;
- participated in court politics;
- maintained households and client networks; and

No single channel was sufficient in every case. Patronage opened doors, but continued advancement usually required useful performance and political trust.

### 1. Recruitment was neither examination nor pure birth

The Mughal state had no competitive civil-service examination. Nor did every noble's son automatically inherit rank. Recruitment drew on:

- demonstrated military or administrative ability;
- possession of armed followers or local influence;
- family reputation and training;
- recommendation by a prince or grandee;
- household service;
- regional knowledge;
- political need; and
- the emperor's judgement and favour.

The correct model is **appointment-based but socially networked**.

### 2. Formal non-heredity and social reproduction

Mansabs and ordinary tankhwah jagirs were not automatically hereditary. On a holder's death, the emperor could review property and claims, settle obligations and decide what relatives received. Yet elite families often reproduced advantage.

They transmitted:

- literacy and administrative training;
- military retainers;
- court etiquette;
- marriage connections;
- patrons and clients;
- knowledge of offices; and
- a family reputation for service.

This is not a contradiction. **Legal inheritance** concerns an enforceable right to rank. **Social reproduction** concerns advantages that make a new appointment more likely.

### 3. Promotion and discipline

Promotion rewarded victory, loyalty, administrative success, useful information and princely service. Demotion, transfer, confiscation or delayed assignment could punish failure or suspected disloyalty. Because status, income and command were linked, a change in mansab could reshape both public honour and material capacity.

The emperor tried to balance groups so that no single network monopolised armed and administrative power. Yet balancing did not mean mathematical equality. It meant distributing opportunities while preserving the crown as arbiter.

### 4. Faction as a moving relationship

A faction was commonly a temporary coalition around a patron, prince, household or immediate interest. Its members might share origin, but origin did not determine the coalition.

```text
prince or grandee
      |
      +--> relatives
      +--> former subordinates
      +--> recipients of favour
      +--> regional allies
      +--> nobles calculating future reward
      |
      v
coalition changes when command, danger or patron changes
```

The debate over a fixed "Nur Jahan junta" in the previous reign is useful here. Family concentration was real, but promotions and alliances remained broader and fluid. The lesson transfers directly to 1657-58: do not predict noble allegiance from an Irani, Turani, Rajput or religious label alone.

### 5. Princes as alternative patrons

Princes held households, governorships and commands. Service under a prince could generate promotion and loyalty, but also create competing centres of expectation. Dara's privileged court position, Shuja's Bengal base, Aurangzeb's Deccan experience and Murad's Gujarat connection mattered because each prince could promise a future distribution of rank and revenue.

This is why princely patronage was both an imperial training system and a succession risk.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** If kinship and patronage mattered, competence was irrelevant.

**Reply:** The inference is too strong. Patronage influenced entry and opportunity, while repeated command, administration and survival required usable ability. Sadullah Khan's career, Rajput commands and the promotion of administrative-service families show a mixture of relationship and competence.

## Visual first: one rank system, several connected claims

```text
                         MANSAB
                 graded imperial position
                            |
             +--------------+--------------+
             |                             |
            ZAT                          SAWAR
 personal status and pay          cavalry obligation/pay claim
             |                             |
      court precedence              horses, troopers, remounts
             +--------------+--------------+
                            |
                    cash or jagir support
```

Mansab was not simply an army number. It joined a person's place in hierarchy to remuneration and expected service.

### 1. Institutional ancestry

The numerical mansab hierarchy developed under Akbar, with the dual **zat-sawar** distinction associated with his fortieth regnal year, 1595-96. Shah Jahan inherited this mature structure. Therefore:

- he did not invent mansabdari;
- he did not invent jagir assignment;
- he did not originate horse branding; and
- his importance lies in adapting an existing system to seventeenth-century scale.

### 2. Zat and sawar

**Zat** indicated personal rank, status and the related salary grade. It located a noble in court hierarchy.

**Sawar** indicated the cavalry obligation and the pay claim associated with maintaining horsemen. It was not identical to zat and not a guaranteed count of troops available on every campaign.

| Rank expression | Safe reading | Unsafe reading |
|---|---|---|
| 3000 zat | high personal status/pay grade | exactly 3,000 troops |
| 2000 sawar | stipulated cavalry field within the mansab | 2,000 campaign-ready men at every moment |
| 3000 zat / 2000 sawar | status rank exceeds cavalry obligation | the first number is civil and the second exclusively military employment |

Civil and military service were not separate cadres in a modern sense. A mansabdar could govern, command, attend court or perform specialised duties.

### 3. Why distinguish the two numbers?

Without separate fields, personal status and military demand could not be adjusted independently. The dual rank allowed the court to:

1. honour a noble without requiring an identical troop total;
2. specify cavalry responsibility;
3. vary military obligation according to need;
4. relate pay to service; and
5. preserve a common hierarchy for civil and military assignments.

The distinction increased flexibility, but it also created an information problem: did the nominal obligation correspond to actual horses and men?

### 4. Dagh and chehra

**Dagh** was branding or verification of horses. **Chehra** was the descriptive roll of troopers. Their purpose was to reduce substitution, false numbers and poor-quality presentation.

Their provenance is older than Shah Jahan. Alauddin Khalji used branding and descriptive controls; Sher Shah revived or adapted them; Akbar systematised them within the Mughal mansab order.

Badayuni's account of borrowed men and horses presented at muster shows the limit:

```text
paper obligation
      -> inspection
      -> temporary compliant display may be assembled
      -> actual force after inspection may differ
```

The existence of evasion does not make inspection useless. It shows that verification reduced information asymmetry without eliminating it.

### 5. Rank inflation

More and higher ranks created:

- more claims to salary;
- greater demand for jagirs;
- expectations of household and ceremonial consumption;
- more cavalry obligations; and
- political resistance to large nominal cuts.

The phrase **mansab inflation** should therefore mean growth in nominal rank and salary-service claims. It is not a direct troop census and not merely a rise in prices.

### 6. Comparison across reigns

| Reign | Principal analytical role |
|---|---|
| Akbar | numerical hierarchy, mature dual rank and systematic integration |
| Jahangir | generous rank increases and targeted du-aspa sih-aspa adjustment |
| Shah Jahan | salary, contingent and month-scale management under expanding claims |

This is evolution, not three unrelated inventions.

```text
       +--> posting/jagir ratio: 1/3, 1/4 or about 1/5
       |
       +--> muster quality, remounts and payment
       |
       v
effective force available in a particular setting
```

The system did not contain one secret "real troop number." Effective strength depended on the interaction of rank, special enhancement, location, finance, muster and campaign conditions.

### 1. Du-aspa sih-aspa

The expression means, literally, two-horse and three-horse. In institutional use it identified a selected portion of a mansabdar's sawar rank for enhanced cavalry obligation and pay. It allowed the state to demand more mounted resources without raising the noble's personal zat rank.

In the assigned evidence, the device is first associated with Jahangir. Shah Jahan inherited and used it. It must not be confused with:

- a third independent rank beside zat and sawar;
- a personal title;
- two or three total horses in a contingent; or
- proof that every selected trooper appeared with three horses.

### 2. The worked example

Part II gives a calculation for a mansabdar with:

- 2000 zat;
- 2000 sawar;
- 1000 of the sawar rank carrying du-aspa sih-aspa; and
- a jagir outside the province of posting, where the practical ratio is one-fourth.

The ordinary 1000 portion yields:

`1000 x 1/4 = 250`

The enhanced 1000 portion is doubled:

`1000 x 2 x 1/4 = 500`

Total practical obligation:

`250 + 500 = 750 effective sawars`

The lesson is not the arithmetic alone. A noble could carry a nominal 2000 sawar rank while the recognised practical contingent was smaller and partly enhanced.

### 3. Shah Jahan's contingent ratios

The source describes a rule connected to the location of a mansabdar's jagir:

| Assignment relationship | Practical contingent as share of nominal sawar |
|---|---:|
| jagir in province of posting | about one-third |
| jagir outside province of posting | about one-fourth |
| Balkh-Badakhshan, and probably difficult Deccan service | about one-fifth |

These ratios acknowledged a pre-existing practical gap. Shah Jahan systematised it; he did not suddenly cause nobles to keep fewer men.

The geographical logic is important. A jagir near a posting gave the holder better access to revenue and logistics. A distant assignment increased collection and transfer problems. Exceptional theatres imposed further costs.

### 4. Salary adjustments

Early seventeenth-century zat and sawar rates were reduced. A document of 1630 suggests broad reductions in zat salary, with larger percentage cuts at some lower ranks. The annual sawar contract rate in the source declines from approximately Rs 240 under Akbar to Rs 220 and then Rs 200.

This money was the contract support claimed by a mansabdar for maintaining cavalry. It was not necessarily a uniform wage paid directly by the state to every trooper. Actual compensation varied with:

- horse quality;
- number of horses;
- service arrangement;
- background conventions; and
- deductions or delays.

Do not turn an aggregate rate into a standardised modern payslip.

### 5. Why adjustment did not automatically mean weakness

Three interpretations must be separated:

| Interpretation | Evidence | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| fiscal economy | lower rates and ratios reduced paper burden | may lower available force if finance was already weak |
| administrative realism | rules acknowledged actual practice | formal recognition can also normalise shortfall |
| imperial decline | reduced obligations show military decay | too early and too general without campaign and provincial evidence |

The strongest verdict is that Shah Jahan's government tried to make claims **credible and payable**. That adaptation preserved capacity but reveals why paper rank cannot be treated as material force.

### 6. Muster and remount logic

Cavalry effectiveness depended on more than initial headcount. Horses tired, fell sick or died. The state therefore required remount capacity. Dagh, chehra and periodic presentation checked identity and quality, while du-aspa sih-aspa and payment rates financed additional mounted resources.

Field performance still depended on forage, roads, climate, command and enemy tactics. A verified horse at muster was not a guarantee of arrival at a distant battlefield.

## Visual first: a salary claim moved through land revenue without becoming land ownership

```text
imperial assessment (jama)
          |
          +--> KHALISA: collection retained for the crown
          |
          +--> JAGIR: revenue claim assigned toward a service-holder's pay
                          |
                          v
                    actual collection (hasil)
                          |

```

A jagirdar received a claim to revenue, not private ownership of the soil or automatic possession of the whole assessed amount.

### 1. The essential vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| jagir | transferable assignment of a state revenue claim toward remuneration | private landed estate |
| khalisa | revenue area whose receipts were retained for the crown | tax-free religious grant |
| jama | assessed or expected revenue | cash certainly collected |
| hasil | revenue actually realised | a second name for assessment |
| tankhwah jagir | assignment supporting salary/service | necessarily hereditary holding |
| watan jagir | locally rooted hereditary-associated grant in specific contexts | model for every jagir |

The system connected a cash-defined salary claim to revenue collected in the countryside. That bridge was politically useful because the state could not conveniently pay every high service-holder wholly in cash. It also shifted collection risks onto the assignee.

### 2. Jagirdar and zamindar are different

A **jagirdar** held an imperial assignment linked to service and transfer. A **zamindar** possessed a locally rooted hereditary or customary claim, often combining revenue rights, social leadership and coercive resources. Some zamindars were rulers or chiefs; others were smaller intermediaries. Their powers varied.

They interacted because the jagirdar needed local information and collection, while the zamindar dealt with cultivators and local society. They could cooperate, bargain or fight.

```text
imperial claim
     |
 jagirdar ---- bargaining/coercion ---- zamindar ---- cultivator
     |                                     |
 transfer and salary                local roots and knowledge
```

### 3. Why transfer mattered

Frequent transfer prevented a jagirdar from turning an assignment easily into an autonomous hereditary principality. It preserved imperial discretion and rotated access to resources.

But transfer could also create a short horizon. A holder uncertain of tenure might prioritise immediate collection rather than long-term improvement. Whether that produced severe extraction depended on local conditions, the zamindar, crop, security and administrative oversight.

### 4. Jama-hasil mismatch

If a jagir carried a jama of Rs 10 lakh but yielded only Rs 6 lakh after local conditions and costs, the holder's paper salary claim was not fully realised. The mismatch could arise from:

- inflated assessment;
- war damage;
- crop failure;
- collection costs;
- local resistance or bargaining;
- weak information;
- remissions; and
- assignment duration.

The mismatch affected troop maintenance and household expenditure. It did not prove that no revenue was collected.

### 5. The month-scale

Jagirs could be rated as twelve-, ten-, eight-, six-, four-month or lower assignments. This expressed the degree to which the assignment was expected to meet a full annual claim.

| Rating | Basic meaning |
|---|---|
| 12-month | expected to support the full annual claim |
| 8-month | expected to realise roughly an adjusted portion |
| 6-month | lower realisation and corresponding adjustment |
| 4-month | heavily discounted effective claim |

Only princes and a few highest grandees might receive ten-month jagirs. Many holders received eight-month assignments. North Indian ratings generally did not fall below four months in the described scheme, while Deccan assignments could be three or four months because jama was highly inflated.

The rating affected both zat and sawar claims. The source gives an illustrative monthly sawar contract amount:

| Jagir rating | Contract amount per month in illustration |
|---|---:|
| 12-month | Rs 40 |
| 8-month | Rs 30 |
| 6-month | Rs 25 |

The fall was not exactly proportional because lower ratings also reduced remount obligations.

### 6. A source tension about meaning

The detailed Part II narrative presents the month-scale as an adaptation to lower realised jagir income. The concise *History of Medieval India* cautions that it applied to cash and jagir pay and should not simply be equated with a fall in the assignment's income.

The two readings can be reconciled cautiously:

1. the scale adjusted salary and obligation to a rated fiscal capacity;
2. it therefore responded to realisation problems;
3. but a lower month label was an administrative rating, not a direct audited statement of every rupee actually collected.

### 7. Revenue growth versus elite growth

The assessed jama series in the source is:

| Year | Approximate jama | Index |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1595-96 | 516.251 crore dams | 100 |
| 1627 | 630 crore dams | 122 |
| 1647-48 | 880 crore dams; about 862 excluding Balkh-Badakhshan | about 162 |
| 1656-57 | 912 crore dams | 176 |

One OCR line prints 1947-48; context and table establish **1647-48**.

Over broadly comparable dates, mansabdars of 500 zat and above increased from 123 to 518, roughly 4.2 times, while the jama index rose from 100 to 176. The comparison signals a growing claims problem. It does not compare identical populations or convert jama into GDP.

The jagir system remained functional and enabled a large service monarchy, but its effectiveness depended on assignment quality, local cooperation and real collection. It was simultaneously an instrument of centralisation and a channel through which fiscal strain reached army and countryside.

## Visual first: expansion created claims and costs together

```text
campaign success or treaty
          |
          +--> new jama entered on paper
          +--> commands and promotions distributed
          +--> forts, garrisons and routes maintained
          +--> tribute collected unevenly
          +--> new elites incorporated or opposed

```

A larger map could strengthen the empire, but only if revenue and compliance exceeded the costs of holding it.

### 1. The 1636 Deccan settlement as a ruling-class arrangement

After the final extinction of the ruling Nizam Shahi line, Shah Jahan's 1636 settlements placed Bijapur and Golconda under a coercive Mughal superiority. They accepted tribute and territorial or political terms; the Mughal emperor also asserted an arbitrating role.

This was not full annexation. It created:

- tribute and territorial claims;
- a frontier of supervised autonomy;
- commands for Mughal princes and nobles;
- opportunities to recruit Deccani and Maratha actors; and
- continuing costs of enforcement.

Aurangzeb's first Deccan viceroyalty from 1636 made the region a princely training ground. His second term from 1653 and campaigns against Golconda and Bijapur in 1656-57 reopened the settlement's limits.

### 2. Maratha chiefs before a mature Maratha state

Shahji Bhonsale moved among Nizam Shahi, Bijapuri and Mughal political fields. He is evidence of a mobile military entrepreneur whose forts, followers and regional knowledge had value. He is not evidence that the later Maratha state already existed in full form.

The small Maratha presence in the high-rank mansab table by 1656 shows limited incorporation. A durable bargain was difficult because:

- Bijapur offered competing employment;
- local bases mattered;
- Mughal demands were changing;
- assignments could be distant or poorly realised; and
- later Shivaji-led mobilisation was only emerging.

### 3. Balkh: battlefield capacity without sustainable occupation

The 1646-47 Balkh campaign mobilised major commanders, including Prince Murad and later Aurangzeb. Mughal forces could win field engagements, but occupation faced:

- Hindu Kush communications;
- severe weather;
- fodder and supply limits;
- Uzbek mobile opposition;
- unreliable local support; and
- no durable client settlement.

Withdrawal does not erase tactical success. It demonstrates that conquest, garrisoning and political sustainability are separate tests.

### 4. Qandahar: a siege and prestige limit

Qandahar fell to the Safavids in 1649. Mughal recovery efforts in 1649, 1652 and 1653 failed despite substantial mobilisation. The problem combined:

- strong fortification;
- artillery and counter-battery performance;
- transport of heavy guns;
- engineering and siege time;
- long supply lines;
- fodder; and
- the defender's strategic advantage.

Failure did not prove that Mughal artillery was useless everywhere. It revealed a relational disadvantage in a demanding siege theatre.

### 5. Fiscal consequences without invented totals

The campaigns consumed cash, animals, equipment, provisions and noble attention. They also generated promotion and rank claims. Exact aggregate campaign costs are not securely available in the assigned evidence, so no total should be invented.

The safe causal statement is:

```text
campaign mobilisation
      -> temporary concentration of money and manpower
      -> higher reward and assignment expectations
      -> distant or damaged revenue fields
      -> uneven net fiscal return
```

### 6. 1656-57: expansion near the succession boundary

Mir Jumla's movement from Golconda service into Mughal service and Aurangzeb's pressure on Golconda show how elite defection, commerce, artillery and princely command interacted. Operations against Bijapur followed. Shah Jahan's intervention produced negotiated restraint before the succession crisis redirected priorities.

These episodes belong here only as stress on the ruling class. Their detailed diplomatic chronology belongs to Topic 18.

## Visual first: elite income circulated, but the chain was unequal

```text
cultivator production
        |

        |

        |
```

Court consumption could support skilled production and still rest on an unequal agrarian surplus.

### 1. Noble expenditure was political

A high mansabdar had to maintain:

- cavalry, horses and remounts;
- servants and armed retainers;
- a residence and hospitality;
- clothing, jewels and ceremonial display;
- gifts to patrons and dependants;
- transport; and
- charitable or cultural patronage.

Consumption signalled rank and sustained networks. It was not simply private luxury. Yet competitive display could deepen dependence on timely jagir income and credit.

### 2. Workshops, artisans and merchants

Imperial karkhanas and noble households demanded textiles, metalwork, jewellery, stone, weapons, paper and food. Monumental construction employed architects, masons, carvers, transporters and labourers. Urban centres benefited from this demand.

The distributional question remains:

- artisans could gain employment;
- merchants could supply elite markets;
- credit networks could expand;
- but cultivators and producers did not necessarily receive an equal share of the value concentrated at court.

### 3. Building expenditure and the arithmetic problem

Satish Chandra cites Shireen Moosvi's calculation that Shah Jahan's buildings and gardens cost slightly over **Rs 289 lakhs** over twenty-eight years. Dividing 289 lakhs by twenty-eight gives roughly **Rs 10.32 lakhs per year**.

The printed or OCR-extracted annual figure appears internally inconsistent because it reads approximately Rs 1,03,391. The arithmetic does not match the total. The safe procedure is:

1. report the attributed aggregate with caution;
2. show the arithmetic transparently if an annual average is used;
3. identify the textual inconsistency; and
4. avoid treating either number as a complete budget.

The source's broader conclusion is that building expenditure was large but not demonstrated to have crippled military and administrative finance.

### 4. Zamindars as connected but distinct elites

Zamindars were not mansabdars by definition. They possessed local claims rooted in custom, lineage and armed-social authority. Some entered mansabdari, while many remained outside its higher ranks.

Their position mattered because a jagirdar's collection often depended on them. They could:

- provide information;
- mobilise cultivators or armed retainers;
- negotiate assessment;
- resist collection;
- remit revenue; and
- mediate local disputes.

The emperor-jagirdar-zamindar-cultivator chain was therefore not a simple command line. Each link had bargaining power, though unequally.

### 5. Peasant pressure: mechanism before judgement

Peasant pressure could intensify when:

- assessment was high relative to output;
- a transferred assignee sought rapid collection;
- war damaged crops or security;
- zamindar and jagirdar competed over shares;
- remissions were inadequate;
- revenue farming raised extraction; or
- coercive collection accompanied arrears.

But month ratings alone do not prove how every cultivator was treated. A low-rated jagir might reflect inflated assessment, war, high costs or local resistance. Its effect on peasants depended on how the holder attempted to close the gap.

### 6. Bernier's criticism and its limits

François Bernier argued that the absence of secure private landed property and frequent transfers discouraged improvement and produced misery. His observation draws attention to incentive and extraction.

Historians qualify it because:

- zamindars possessed substantial hereditary claims;
- cultivators had customary rights and varied conditions;
- jagirs were revenue assignments, not the emperor's simple ownership of all soil;
- Bernier compared India with European property ideas; and
- regional outcomes differed.

Use Bernier as a sharp situated observer, not as a transparent statistical survey.

### 7. Architecture as sovereignty, not monocausal decline

The Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Jama Masjid and Shahjahanabad show:

- the emperor's power to concentrate resources;
- dynastic memory;
- coordination of workshops and labour;
- urban demand; and
- a visual language of order.

They do not alone explain later decline. A causal argument must compare construction with wars, administrative expenditure, revenue growth and jagir pressures.

The succession war was not a referendum on theology. It was armed competition to control the institution that allocated rank, office and jagir.

### 1. Why the crisis became military

The Mughal dynasty lacked a fixed rule of primogeniture. Every adult prince with resources and support could claim sovereignty. Shah Jahan's illness in September 1657 created uncertainty over whether the emperor could continue to dispense appointments and command obedience.

Dara Shukoh enjoyed exceptional favour and proximity. He held the title **Sultan Buland Iqbal**, sat on a golden chair beside the throne and eventually received a source-reported rank of **60,000 zat and 40,000 sawar**, including **30,000 du-aspa sih-aspa**. These extraordinary numbers must not be treated as an ordinary mansab example. They express princely elevation and require source attribution.

Shuja governed Bengal, Aurangzeb drew on Deccan and earlier frontier command, and Murad held Gujarat. Provincial service gave each access to officers, troops and revenue.

### 2. Chronology through the decisive battle

| Date | Event | Institutional meaning |
|---|---|---|
| September 1657 | Shah Jahan fell ill | uncertainty over the patronage centre |
| late 1657-early 1658 | princes asserted claims and mobilised | provincial commands became succession resources |
| 15 April 1658 | battle of Dharmat | imperial force failed to stop Aurangzeb-Murad advance |
| 29 May 1658 | battle of Samugarh | Dara's principal field position was defeated |
| 1658 | Aurangzeb took control and confined Shah Jahan | victorious coalition captured the centre |
| 1658-59 | Khajwa and Deorai extended consolidation | bounded aftermath, not part of Shah Jahan's active government |

The topic's main cutoff is Samugarh and the transfer of control. Khajwa and Deorai are mentioned only to show that victory did not end all rivalry instantly.

### 3. Why nobles chose differently

Noble allegiance could reflect:

- earlier service under a prince;
- household or kinship ties;
- current provincial command;
- anticipated promotion;
- access to troops and cash;
- judgement of military prospects;
- fear of punishment; and
- ideological or personal sympathy.

Religious position formed one variable, not a complete predictor. Dara's broad intellectual interests and Aurangzeb's orthodox image did not divide the elite into fixed liberal and orthodox camps.

Part II gives a bounded comparison among nobles of 1000 zat and above before Samugarh: **27 Iranis supported Aurangzeb and 23 supported Dara**. The figures refute a simple claim that Iranian origin determined one allegiance. They do not describe every noble or prove origin was irrelevant.

### 4. Rajputs and other elites

Rajput houses also made political calculations. Jaswant Singh commanded the imperial force at Dharmat; Rajput participation did not amount to one uniform community choice. Different relationships with Dara, Aurangzeb, the emperor and court grandees affected alignment.

The same applies to Afghans, Indian Muslims and Turanis. A category can shape networks without deciding action.

### 5. What the crisis proves about the system

It proves four things:

1. the emperor's personal control over patronage was a source of strength and vulnerability;
2. princely governorships trained commanders but supplied rival bases;
3. the nobility was integrated enough for contenders to fight through the same mansab order; and
4. the institutions survived the violent change of sovereign.

The war was severe, but it did not instantly abolish the revenue departments, mansab ranks or provincial administration. Aurangzeb captured and reoriented an existing apparatus.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** The war proves that Mughal administration was merely personal and had no institutional continuity.

**Reply:** Personal patronage was central, but contenders required existing provinces, mansabdars, forts, accounts and revenue. The administrative machinery continued through regime change. The more precise claim is that strong institutions were organised around a personal sovereign, making succession especially dangerous.

## Visual first: every source sees a different empire

```text
court chronicle ---> ceremony, campaigns, appointments, imperial intention
administrative document ---> rates, rank, assignment, a particular rule
prosopography ---> group patterns across many careers
traveller account ---> vivid observation filtered through comparison
building/material evidence ---> labour, design, patronage and political space
                         |
                         v
         corroborate before moving from claim to generalisation
```

No single source can answer every question about ruling-class composition, actual collection, peasant experience and court ideology.

### 1. Persian court chronicles

The **Padshahnama** traditions associated with Qazwini and Abd al-Hamid Lahori are indispensable for:

- campaigns;
- appointments and promotions;
- court audiences;
- imperial buildings;
- ceremonials; and
- official reasoning.

Their closeness to court gives detail and creates limitation. They write within patronage, celebrate sovereignty and may convert contested submission into imperial success. A chronicle statement about tribute or victory must be compared with subsequent control and collection.

### 2. Administrative and quantitative evidence

The **Ain-i Akbari** supplies a 1595 baseline and administrative vocabulary. It is often normative: a rule in the Ain does not prove identical implementation everywhere.

Farmans, mansab records, jagir documents and salary orders can give precise rules. Their survival is uneven. A 1630 salary document may demonstrate a policy or tendency without proving that every noble experienced the same deduction.

Athar Ali's prosopographical reconstruction permits comparison of hundreds of careers. Its strength is pattern. Its limits are:

- defined rank thresholds;
- uneven surviving records;
- historian-made origin categories;
- selected dates; and
- the difference between count and political behaviour.

### 3. European travellers

**Bernier** is valuable for the land-revenue debate, court politics and the outsider's view of elite concentration. His argument that the king owned all land and insecure assignment blocked improvement reflects comparison with European property ideas and understates zamindari and customary rights.

**Tavernier** is useful for trade, jewels and courtly material life. **Manucci** supplies vivid political and social narratives, but chronology, hearsay and his long retrospective composition require checking. A memorable description is not automatically a representative statistic.

### 4. Material evidence

The Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Jama Masjid, Shahjahanabad and the Peacock Throne demonstrate:

- design and labour coordination;
- material concentration;
- patronage;
- dynastic and imperial ideology; and
- the spatial ordering of court.

They cannot independently establish the share of imperial revenue spent, the condition of every artisan or the actual size of a cavalry force.

### 5. The major historiographical positions

| Position | Strong point | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| golden-age/apex thesis | visible capacity, wealth, court culture and administration | may confuse elite concentration with general welfare |
| despotism/property critique | highlights extraction, transfer and incentive problems | Bernier's categories understate varied rights and local institutions |
| composite-ruling-class thesis | explains broad recruitment and imperial integration | composition does not mean equal power or absence of faction |
| proto-crisis thesis | expanding ranks, jama-hasil gaps and adjustments show strain | must not project the acute post-1680 crisis backward |
| negotiated-empire thesis | emphasises princes, nobles, zamindars and local bargains | negotiation occurred within coercive imperial hierarchy |

### 6. Final verdict

Shah Jahan's reign was neither a static continuation nor a rupture. The empire inherited Akbar-Jahangir institutions and altered their operation:

- the composite high elite expanded;
- formal rank claims multiplied;
- contingent and salary rules acknowledged practical limits;
- month ratings managed fiscal realisation;
- campaigns created distinction between paper expansion and sustainable control;
- court consumption displayed and circulated concentrated wealth; and
- the succession war exposed the risks of personal patronage without destroying institutions.

The phrase **evolution of the ruling class** therefore means change in scale, composition, fiscal support and political behaviour within a durable imperial framework.

**Question:** "The evolution of the Mughal ruling class under Shah Jahan is best understood as institutional adaptation under expanding imperial claims." Critically examine.

**Model answer**

Shah Jahan inherited the Akbari-Jahangiri service monarchy rather than founding a new order. Mansab, zat-sawar rank, jagir assignment and specialised departments continued, but their scale and operation changed.

Athar Ali's series for mansabdars of 500 zat and above rises from 123 in 1595 to 518 in 1656. This composite elite increased imperial reach but also expanded claims to salary, office and productive assignments.

In 1657-58, patronage uncertainty made succession violent, yet the victor retained the existing apparatus.

The proposition is therefore persuasive if "adaptation" does not become a euphemism for effortless success. Strain was measurable, but the mature later jagirdari crisis should not be projected backward. Shah Jahan's order remained powerful because it could still adjust.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-mark question - 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish formal rank from effective resources in the Mughal mansabdari system under Shah Jahan.

**Model answer**

Formal rank expressed an authorised claim within the imperial hierarchy. Zat fixed personal status and salary grade, while sawar stated cavalry obligation and its support. Neither number was a direct census of battle-ready troops.

Effective resources depended on additional filters. Du-aspa sih-aspa enhanced only a selected portion of sawar. Shah Jahan's practical ratios could reduce ordinary obligations to about one-third for a same-province jagir, one-fourth for an outside assignment and about one-fifth in difficult theatres. Dagh and chehra verified horses and troopers but could not remove all evasion. Jagir quality, jama-hasil mismatch, remounts, distance, forage and payment further shaped actual strength.

Thus nominal rank remained politically meaningful: it organised precedence, remuneration and expectation. Effective capacity, however, arose only when the fiscal and logistical system converted that claim into maintained men and horses.

**Scoring note:** Reward exact zat-sawar definitions, one ratio or calculation and at least two implementation filters.

## Original 15-mark question - 200 words

**Question:** Did the widening social composition of the Mughal ruling class weaken factional politics under Shah Jahan?

**Model answer**

The widening composition of the ruling class expanded recruitment but did not eliminate faction. Athar Ali's series for mansabdars of 500 zat and above rises from 123 in 1595 to 518 in 1656. Iranis and Turanis remained numerous, while Afghan, Indian Muslim, Rajput and limited Maratha representation increased.

This diversity strengthened the empire by adding military traditions, administrative skill and regional connections. It also prevented a single ancestry from monopolising service. Yet the groups were not equal in access to central office or governorship.

Faction arose less from fixed ethnicity than from patronage. Kinship, household service, princely command, prior reward, marriage and expected promotion linked nobles to shifting coalitions. Origin could provide a network, but did not dictate allegiance. Before Samugarh, the bounded high-rank evidence of 27 Iranis for Aurangzeb and 23 for Dara demonstrates divided choice within one category.

Composition therefore broadened imperial integration without ending factional competition. It changed the resources from which coalitions could be built, while the emperor's control of mansab, jagir and office kept patronage at their centre.

**Scoring note:** Reward one quantitative trend, diversity as capacity and a clear patronage-not-determinism explanation.

## Original 20-mark question - 250 words

**Question:** Was fiscal strain under Shah Jahan principally a problem of insufficient revenue or of the relationship between assessment, assignment and service claims?

**Model answer**

Fiscal strain cannot be reduced to absolute revenue scarcity. Assessed imperial jama increased: the source index rises from 100 in 1595-96 to 176 in 1656-57. The problem lay more precisely in the relationship among expanding claims, uneven assignments and actual collection.

High mansabdars multiplied much faster. Those of 500 zat and above rose from 123 to 518 between 1595 and 1656. Each rank carried salary, household and cavalry expectations. Yet jama was an assessment, not guaranteed hasil. War damage, inflated estimates, collection costs, local bargaining and short tenure reduced realisation.

The state responded through adjustment. Sawar contract rates were reduced; practical contingent ratios linked obligation to posting and jagir location; du-aspa sih-aspa concentrated enhancement; and month ratings calibrated zat-sawar claims to rated fiscal capacity. These measures reveal pressure but also administrative flexibility.

Campaigns sharpened the issue. Deccan settlements added territorial and tribute claims alongside garrison costs. Balkh and Qandahar demanded transport, animals and rewards without durable corresponding revenue. Court and building expenditure concentrated resources but is not demonstrated to have crippled finance.

Thus revenue was substantial, but its assessed amount, location, collection and allocation did not automatically match service claims. Shah Jahan's fiscal problem was chiefly relational and distributive. It foreshadowed later crisis without being identical to the acute jagirdari breakdown of Aurangzeb's long Deccan war.

**Scoring note:** Reward the growth comparison, four fiscal mechanisms, campaign linkage and a clear distinction between proto-strain and later crisis.

# REMEDIATION

## Misconception 1: "Zat and sawar are the number of soldiers"

**Repair**

```text
zat = personal rank/status and salary grade
sawar = authorised cavalry obligation/pay field
effective troops = sawar filtered through enhancement, ratio, payment, muster and campaign
```

**Diagnostic:** A 3000-zat/2000-sawar noble is not proved to command 5,000 soldiers. The numbers describe different claims.

## Misconception 2: "Jagir means private land ownership"

**Repair**

A normal tankhwah jagir was a transferable claim to collect specified state revenue toward remuneration. It did not convey absolute ownership of the soil. Watan jagirs were context-specific locally rooted arrangements and cannot be used to make every jagir hereditary.

## Misconception 3: "Jama means the state received the money"

**Repair**

```text
jama = assessed/expected revenue
hasil = realised revenue
jama - collection conditions = variable hasil
```

Never use an empire-wide jama total as GDP, welfare or cash-in-treasury.

## Misconception 4: "Composite nobility means harmonious equality"

**Repair**

Composite means recruited from several origins and communities. Rank, office access and political influence remained unequal. Diversity widened state capacity and also widened competition.

## Misconception 5: "Ethnic group equals faction"

**Repair**

Use a five-part test:

1. Who was the immediate patron?
2. Under which prince or officer had the noble served?
3. What office or jagir was at stake?
4. What was the military risk?
5. Does bounded evidence show divided allegiance within the origin group?

## Misconception 6: "Lower contingent ratio proves total decay"

**Repair**

A lower ratio can indicate fiscal economy, administrative realism and normalisation of shortfall at the same time. Determine whether the empire still recruited, collected, mobilised and won in the relevant theatre.

## Misconception 7: "Architecture ruined the empire"

**Repair**

Construction had opportunity costs, but the attributed total, its arithmetic problem and the absence of a complete budget block a monocausal conclusion. Compare buildings with revenue, wars, households and administration.

## Misconception 8: "The later jagirdari crisis was complete under Shah Jahan"

**Repair**

Shah Jahan's reign shows claim growth, jama-hasil mismatch and adaptation. The later long Deccan war, annexations, Maratha resistance and be-jagiri intensified the crisis after this topic's boundary.

## Misconception 9: "Succession was only a religious conflict"

**Repair**

Religious reputation influenced rhetoric, but nobles also calculated previous service, patronage, provincial resources, military probability and future reward. The divided Irani evidence rejects a simple confessional-origin map.

## Misconception 10: "A court chronicle reports neutral outcomes"

**Repair**

Chronicles reveal imperial intention and detail. Test their claims through subsequent appointments, collection, fort possession, local action and independent descriptions.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core thesis

Shah Jahan's reign was an apex of visible imperial capacity and a period of managed institutional strain. A rapidly expanding, composite high nobility depended on mansab, jagir and emperor-centred patronage. Fiscal and contingent adjustments translated growing paper claims into practical obligations. The system was powerful and adaptive, but not frictionless.

## 3. Kingship and administration

- Accession required coalition, armed support and control of the centre.
- The emperor granted mansab, office, title and jagir.
- Ceremony represented political hierarchy.
- Peacock Throne first used in 1635 after seven years of work.
- Shahjahanabad began in 1638.
- Wazir/diwan coordinated finance.
- Mir bakhshi handled rank, presentation and muster.
- Mir saman organised household and workshops.
- Sadullah Khan exemplifies Indian Muslim administrative advancement.
- Centralisation meant appointment, transfer, audit and arbitration through intermediaries.

## 5. Recruitment and faction

- Mansab was an imperial appointment, not automatic inheritance.
- Family training, kinship, clients and reputation reproduced advantage.
- Ability and connection were complementary.
- Promotion joined rank, salary, honour and command.
- Transfer, demotion and confiscation disciplined service.
- Factions clustered around emperors, princes and grandees.
- Patronage, prior service, marriage, reward and risk shaped allegiance.
- Princely households trained commanders and created succession bases.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| mansab | graded imperial service rank |
| zat | personal status and salary grade |
| sawar | cavalry obligation and related pay field |
| dagh | horse branding/verification |
| chehra | descriptive roll of troopers |
| du-aspa sih-aspa | enhanced obligation/pay on selected sawar portion |

- Dual zat-sawar associated with Akbar's 1595-96 reform.
- Dagh and chehra had Sultanate-Sur precedents and Akbari systematisation.
- Jahangir is associated with du-aspa sih-aspa in the assigned evidence.
- Shah Jahan adapted rather than invented mansabdari.
- Formal rank was not effective field strength.

## 7. Contingent ratios and salary

| Condition | Practical share of nominal sawar |
|---|---:|
| jagir in province of posting | about 1/3 |
| jagir outside province | about 1/4 |
| Balkh-Badakhshan/probably difficult Deccan service | about 1/5 |

```text

1000 ordinary at 1/4 = 250
1000 du-aspa sih-aspa: 1000 x 2 x 1/4 = 500
total = 750
```

- Sawar contract rates decline approximately Rs 240 -> Rs 220 -> Rs 200 annually.
- Rates supported cavalry contracts; they were not uniform direct wages.
- Shah Jahan systematised practical shortfall.

| tankhwah jagir | salary/service assignment |

- Jagirdar and zamindar were distinct.
- Transfer reduced entrenchment but could shorten incentives.
- Jama-hasil gap could result from inflated assessment, war, costs, bargaining and crop conditions.
- Month ratings adjusted salary and obligation below a twelve-month standard.
- Deccan assignments could be three or four months.
- Month-scale was a calibration, not a bonus or direct audit.

## 10. Campaign and frontier effects

- 1636 Deccan settlement: suzerainty and tribute, not total annexation.
- Territorial claim could add garrison and command costs.
- Shahji was a mobile service actor, not the later state.
- Balkh: battlefield ability, unsustainable occupation.
- Qandahar: fortification, transport, artillery, engineering and supply constraints.
- Failed frontier recovery did not prove universal artillery inferiority.
- Exact aggregate campaign costs are not securely established in assigned evidence.

## 11. Political economy and society

- Nobles maintained armies, households, retainers and display.
- Court demand stimulated artisans, merchants and credit.
- Circulation did not equal distribution.
- Attributed building total: slightly over Rs 289 lakhs over twenty-eight years.
- Implied average: about Rs 10.32 lakhs annually.
- Printed annual figure is internally inconsistent and must be qualified.
- Architecture was not proven to cripple imperial finance.
- Zamindars had varied locally rooted rights and coercive power.
- Peasant pressure depended on assessment, collection, transfer, war and bargaining.
- Bernier's critique is useful but overgeneralised.

## 12. Succession stress

- No fixed primogeniture.
- Dara received exceptional favour and extraordinary attributed rank.
- Shuja: Bengal base.
- Aurangzeb: Deccan and frontier experience.
- Murad: Gujarat base.
- Noble alignment joined patronage, prior service, resources, risk and ideology.
- Defined high-rank Irani evidence: 27 for Aurangzeb and 23 for Dara before Samugarh.
- Dharmat: 15 April 1658.
- Samugarh: 29 May 1658.
- Institutional administration survived the change of sovereign.
