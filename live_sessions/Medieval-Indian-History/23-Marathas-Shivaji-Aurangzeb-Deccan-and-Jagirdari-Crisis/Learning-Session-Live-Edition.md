The hills did not automatically create a Maratha state. They offered possibilities. People, institutions and political choices turned those possibilities into power.

### 1. Start with the political landscape

The western Deccan linked three different zones:

| Zone | Operational value | Built-in limit |
|---|---|---|
| Sahyadri uplands and Maval valleys | defensible heights, infantry recruitment and knowledge of passes | forts could be isolated without food, water and relief |
| Konkan coast and creeks | ports, coastal supply, seafaring labour and access to commerce | strong Sidis and European companies limited freedom |
| Plateau and routes east/south | cavalry movement, agriculture and links to Bijapur, Golconda and Karnataka | open movement also exposed forces to larger armies |

The Deccan was not cut off from north India. Soldiers, merchants, administrators and claimants moved between Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda and the Mughal Empire. Long routes, monsoon conditions, fodder shortages and fort belts nevertheless made occupation easier than durable control.

### 2. Society before a unified “Maratha nation”

“Maratha” could refer to overlapping regional, linguistic, landed and service identities. Bhonsles, Mores, Nimbalkars, Ghatges and other families competed as well as cooperated. Deshmukhs held locally rooted revenue and social influence; patils headed villages; cultivators, artisans, Brahman record-keepers, cavalrymen and infantrymen occupied unequal positions.

⚠️ **Qualification:** These groups were not a primordial nation or a single caste bloc waiting to unite. Regional language and devotional traditions could aid communication and legitimacy, but service, kinship, locality, office and material interest often divided them.

### 3. How the fort system really worked

A fort was not simply a stone wall.

```text
fort
 ├─ garrison and commander
 ├─ grain, water, ammunition and repair
 ├─ messengers and intelligence
 ├─ nearby villages and revenue
 ├─ routes to neighbouring forts
 └─ mobile field force able to relieve or retake it
```

This network logic matters. A large army could capture a fort after a siege, yet the victor then had to provision and garrison it. If surrounding routes and countryside remained contested, possession could be temporary.

### 4. Sources: every narrative has a viewing angle

| Source type | What it can reveal | What it may distort or omit |
|---|---|---|
| Persian court chronicles | campaigns, commands, imperial rank and official reasoning | court-centred victory language and limited access to local motives |
| Marathi bakhars and later traditions | regional memory, heroic narrative and political vocabulary | retrospective celebration, chronology problems and hagiography |
| Letters and administrative papers | dated orders, bargaining, revenue claims and local problems | fragmentary survival and narrow purpose |
| European factory records | trade disruption, coastal politics and outside observation | commercial bias, hearsay and limited inland knowledge |
| Forts, routes and archaeology | material scale, siting and defensive design | cannot alone reveal motive, loyalty or actual control |

The famous encounter between Shivaji and Afzal Khan illustrates the rule: the secure outcome and political consequences are clearer than every dramatic detail of intention and sequence.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** If the Sahyadris gave mobile fighters such a major advantage, geography was the principal cause of Maratha success.

**Reply:** Similar terrain existed before Shivaji and did not continuously produce the same state. Geography lowered some military costs, but forts required fiscal extraction, commanders, intelligence, diplomacy and legitimacy. It enabled; it did not independently cause.

### UPSC use

- **Prelims trap:** a fort network is not equivalent to permanent territorial sovereignty.
- **Mains move:** connect ecology to institutions, then qualify environmental determinism.
- **Source-method link:** the adjacent 2020 GS-I Q12 on Persian literary sources belongs to Topic 24, but its method—reading genre, patronage and silence—helps evaluate Mughal accounts here.

### Revision notes

1. Western Deccan power joined upland forts, plateau routes and the Konkan coast.
2. Terrain created strategic opportunities, not an inevitable state.
3. Maratha groups were internally differentiated by locality, status and service.
4. Deshmukhs were locally rooted hereditary power-holders, not merely clerks.
5. Forts required stores, revenue, intelligence, garrisons and relief.
6. Mughal control of a fort did not guarantee control of its countryside.
7. Persian chronicles illuminate the imperial view but require genre criticism.
8. Bakhars preserve regional memory but may magnify heroic episodes.
9. Material remains prove capacity and design better than motive.
10. The safe causal formula is geography + organisation + finance + diplomacy.

3. Which statement best describes a functioning fort network?

   - B. It combined political symbolism with secure garrisons, making relief forces less important where masonry and water were adequate.

### Answers and option-wise explanations

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** The proposition makes terrain an opportunity structure activated by fiscal, informational, institutional and political capacity.
- **B — Incorrect:** A defensive advantage shaped warfare, but terrain did not predetermine expansion or reduce organisation to a question of tempo.
- **C — Incorrect:** Konkan access, passes and coastal resources formed part of the same strategic system as the upland forts.
- **D — Incorrect:** Service families remained divided by patronage and locality; geography did not first create a settled political alignment.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Regional location can illuminate memory, but it does not give a later text privileged access to the actors' motives.
- **B — Correct:** Comparison across genres permits confidence about the outcome without converting disputed intention or sequence into fact.
- **C — Incorrect:** Narrative fullness may reflect literary elaboration; compression in another source does not validate the more detailed version.
- **D — Incorrect:** Later accounts may share a narrative tradition, so recurrence without independent evidence is not separate corroboration.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** Field forces mattered, but forts themselves depended on linked supply, revenue and communication rather than functioning chiefly as refuges.
- **B — Incorrect:** Strong walls and water increased endurance, yet relief, replenishment and external information remained operationally necessary.
- **C — Correct:** The option identifies the material and mobile connections that converted separate strongholds into a resilient network.
- **D — Incorrect:** Securing principal routes did not by itself settle revenue collection or political allegiance in the surrounding countryside.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why geography enabled but did not cause Maratha state formation.

**Model:** The Sahyadri ranges, Maval valleys and Konkan creeks gave emerging Maratha forces defensible heights, local routes, concealment and access to coastal resources. Numerous forts could protect treasuries, control passes and provide bargaining assets. Yet terrain was only an opportunity structure. A fort needed water, stores, a garrison, intelligence, nearby revenue and a mobile force capable of relief or recapture. The same region also contained rival Maratha families, deshmukhs with hereditary interests, Bijapuri officers, Sidis and European maritime powers. Shivaji's achievement lay in combining these fragmented resources through leadership, fiscal supervision, diplomacy, recruitment and a political claim to kingship. Therefore, environmental conditions explain the form and cost of warfare, but not the timing or success of state formation. Geography became power only through institutions and strategic choice.

**Scoring note:** Award highest credit for a two-sided answer: named geographic advantages plus at least three institutional conversion mechanisms and an explicit rejection of determinism.

Before Shivaji built an independent kingdom, his family lived in a political world where military households served competing courts and changed patrons.

### 1. Shahji's career in plain language

Shahji Bhonsle rose through Deccan service politics. He worked within Ahmadnagar's struggles, entered Mughal service, received rank and assignments, defected when interests changed, supported a Nizam Shahi claimant with Bijapuri backing and later served Bijapur in the south. The 1636 settlement forced him to give up the Ahmadnagar territories he controlled and moved his principal career toward southern assignments.

This was neither random treachery nor a straight anti-Mughal liberation movement. Service was a negotiated relationship involving rank, jagir, protection, opportunity and survival.

### 2. What Shivaji inherited

| Inheritance | Use for Shivaji | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Poona-region jagir and household position | a territorial and fiscal starting point | jagir did not equal independent sovereignty |
| military retainers and service contacts | recruitment and knowledge | loyalties could shift |
| experience of Ahmadnagar-Bijapur-Mughal rivalry | diplomatic room for manoeuvre | each state could also combine against him |
| local management associated with Jijabai and Dadaji Kondadev | continuity in the Poona base | later stories vary in emphasis and detail |
| southern connections | later Karnataka claims and movement | patrimony did not guarantee uncontested possession |

Shahji's career also shows why “Maratha versus Mughal” is too simple. Maratha sardars served Mughal and Deccani rulers; Muslim and Hindu officers appeared on different sides; political coalitions crossed religious boundaries.

### 3. From king-maker to king

Satish Chandra frames the Bhonsle rise as movement from participation in dynastic politics to autonomous kingship. Shahji could sustain a claimant and bargain among states, but Shivaji gradually claimed forts, revenue and royal status in his own name.

```text
service under a court
        ↓
regional military household
        ↓
control of forts and local resources
        ↓
independent diplomatic bargaining
        ↓
coronated sovereignty
```

The movement was incremental. Early acquisitions could still be explained by contemporaries as jagir management, opportunism or rebellion against Bijapur. Sovereignty became clearer through sustained territorial institutions, diplomacy and the 1674 coronation.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Shahji's shifting allegiances prove that Shivaji's later state had no meaningful regional or ideological basis.

**Reply:** The conclusion is too strong. Mobile service explains the political tools and mixed personnel from which the state emerged. Shivaji later added a more independent territorial project and a Hindu kingship idiom. Continuity of resources can coexist with transformation of political purpose.

### UPSC use

- Use Shahji to explain the **prehistory** of Maratha power, not to project Shivaji's completed programme backward.
- Contrast **jagir-holder/service noble** with **sovereign ruler**.
- In a Mains answer, show continuity in personnel and practice alongside discontinuity in political claim.

### Revision notes

1. Shahji operated among Ahmadnagar, Mughal and Bijapur interests.
2. He supported a Nizam Shahi claimant rather than a modern national cause.
3. Mughal mansab and jagir were service instruments, not ownership of a kingdom.
4. The 1636 settlement displaced Shahji from his Ahmadnagar position.
5. Bijapur service and southern assignments widened Bhonsle experience.
6. The Poona base supplied Shivaji with an opening, not a ready-made sovereign state.
7. Maratha families could compete and change allegiance.
8. Political coalitions in the Deccan crossed religious identities.
9. Shivaji transformed inherited service resources into autonomous institutions.
10. Do not confuse genealogical continuity with an unchanged political programme.

**MCQ 4: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Shahji's Ahmadnagar involvement did not amount to a continuous, prefigured programme for an independent Bhonsle kingdom.
- **B — Incorrect:** Ambition mattered, but movements among courts were structured by mansab, jagir, claimant politics and changing military pressures.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** How does Shahji Bhonsle's career illuminate the political world from which Shivaji emerged?

**Model:** Shahji's career reveals a Deccan political order organised through mobile military service rather than fixed communal blocs. He moved among Ahmadnagar, Mughal and Bijapur interests, held mansab and jagir, supported a Nizam Shahi claimant and later served Bijapur in southern territories. Such mobility gave the Bhonsle household troops, contacts, administrative experience and a Poona-region base. It also exposed the instability of patronage: assignments could be transferred, allies could defect and larger states could redefine subordinate status. Shivaji inherited these resources but changed their political use. Instead of remaining only a service noble, he linked forts, local revenues and military followers to an increasingly independent territorial claim, eventually formalised by coronation. Shahji therefore represents both continuity and contrast: his world supplied the instruments of Maratha state formation, but it should not be misread as an already formed nationalist or sovereign programme.

**Scoring note:** Full marks require Shahji's multiple alignments, the service/jagir mechanism, the resources inherited by Shivaji and a clear continuity-versus-transformation verdict.

### 1. Early forts, 1645–47

Shivaji acquired Torna, Kondana and other forts around the Poona-Maval region in the mid-1640s. Murumbdev, later associated with Rajgad, became a major base. These acquisitions mattered because they linked defensible positions to local routes and revenue.

Do not read the map backward. At this stage, a mature kingdom did not already exist. The early process combined initiative, ambiguity about Bijapuri authority, local bargains and gradual consolidation.

### 2. Why Javli mattered in 1656

Javli lay in rugged country controlled by the More chiefs. Its conquest:

- strengthened Shivaji's hold over the Maval highlands;
- opened movement toward Satara and the Konkan;
- added men, treasure and strategic depth; and
- reduced a rival local power.

⚠️ **Limit:** Javli was important, but it was not proof that all Maratha chiefs naturally supported Shivaji. It was also a coercive conquest of another Maratha house.

### 3. Afzal Khan and Pratapgad, 1659

Bijapur sent Afzal Khan against Shivaji. The two met near Pratapgad under negotiated conditions; Afzal Khan was killed, and the Marathas rapidly exploited the result.

The analytical significance is firmer than the theatrical sequence:

```text
Bijapuri punitive expedition
          ↓
meeting under deep mutual distrust
          ↓
Afzal Khan killed
          ↓
prepared Maratha forces attack
          ↓
Bijapuri prestige falls; Shivaji's following expands
```

Different traditions assign prior treachery differently and embellish weapons, gestures and motives. A careful answer states the contested sequence, then focuses on the secure political outcome.

### 4. Panhala: correction to the “only guerrilla” image

Pressure around Panhala in 1660 exposed Shivaji to siege and retreat. The episode reminds us that Maratha warfare included:

- defence of fixed positions;
- attempted relief and escape;
- sacrifice by rearguards in remembered tradition;
- negotiation and temporary loss; and
- later recovery.

Mobile methods reduced the advantage of a larger opponent, but did not eliminate danger from siege, blockade or coordinated pursuit.

### Objection, qualification and verdict

**Heroic reading:** Pratapgad proves that personal courage alone created the state.

**Institutional reply:** The encounter mattered because troops, intelligence, terrain preparation and an expansion plan converted one death into strategic gains.

**Moral qualification:** Source criticism does not require pretending that coercion, deception and violence were absent. It requires distinguishing what is verified from what is narrated to legitimise a side.

### UPSC use

- Chronology: early forts → Javli (1656) → Pratapgad (1659) → Panhala crisis (1660).
- Use Javli to challenge the idea of automatic intra-Maratha unity.
- Use Panhala to qualify an over-romantic “invincible guerrilla” claim.
- In source questions, separate **event**, **contested sequence** and **later memory**.

### Revision notes

1. Early fort acquisitions date broadly to 1645–47.
2. Torna, Kondana and Rajgad anchored the initial Poona-Maval base.
3. Javli was conquered from Chandra Rao More in 1656.
4. Javli improved access toward Satara and the Konkan.
5. Its conquest also demonstrates conflict among Maratha elites.
6. Afzal Khan died at Pratapgad in 1659.
7. The exact meeting sequence is disputed across partisan traditions.
8. The political consequence was rapid expansion and increased prestige.
9. Panhala shows the continued importance of siege, retreat and negotiation.
10. Ganimi kava should not erase conventional or positional warfare.
11. UNESCO's 2025 listing is a modern heritage recognition, not a substitute for historical evidence.

### Answers and option-wise explanations

**MCQ 6: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Javli altered both local power and access toward the Konkan rather than preserving the previous route structure.
- **B — Correct:** The conquest joined territorial and route gains to the removal of Chandra Rao More, without implying Maratha political unity.
- **C — Incorrect:** The More house was displaced through conquest; the episode was not an incorporation that left its position substantially intact.
- **D — Incorrect:** Javli's strategic value included changing access between the highlands and the Konkan, not preserving that route pattern.

**MCQ 7: C**

- **A — Incorrect:** The broad territorial and prestige gains are better established than the encounter's detailed choreography.
- **B — Incorrect:** Preservation of omitted detail can reflect later elaboration and does not establish priority over contemporary evidence.
- **C — Correct:** This formulation separates the securely attested outcome from disputed intention and moment-by-moment reconstruction.
- **D — Incorrect:** Overlapping motifs may derive from related traditions, so agreement does not license a composite sequence without corroboration.

**MCQ 8: D**

- **A — Incorrect:** Contested routes and blockade exposed the limits of insulation even where Maratha mobility remained effective.
- **B — Incorrect:** Withdrawal from a fort could be strategically rational, but forts retained military and bargaining significance.
- **C — Incorrect:** A stronghold could support collection, yet blockade and uncertain routes prevented possession from ensuring stable revenue.
- **D — Correct:** Panhala reveals that a mobile system still faced positional pressure, hazardous withdrawal and negotiated concession.

**MCQ 9: A**

- **A — Correct:** The inscription documents selected surviving relationships among forts and landscapes; period evidence must still establish particular operations and chronology.
- **B — Incorrect:** Selection and material survival make the serial property an important sample, not a representative reconstruction of every fort and route in the wider network.
- **C — Incorrect:** Shared inscription supports a relational landscape interpretation, but it cannot flatten components with different construction, acquisition and operational chronologies into one continuous network.
- **D — Incorrect:** Material location can support strategic interpretation, but heritage assessment cannot resolve disputed campaign sequences or date each fort's role without period evidence.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):** “The rise of Shivaji cannot be reduced either to heroic encounters or to favourable terrain.” Discuss with reference to the period 1645–60.

**Model:** Shivaji's early rise unfolded through a cumulative regional process. The acquisition of Torna, Kondana and nearby forts in 1645–47 created a defensible Poona-Maval base. Geography offered concealment, passes and compact routes, but each fort required provisions, intelligence, garrisons and access to local revenue. The conquest of Javli in 1656 added strategic depth toward Satara and the Konkan and removed the rival More house; it therefore shows coercive intra-regional consolidation rather than automatic Maratha unity.

Afzal Khan's death at Pratapgad in 1659 became the most celebrated episode. Its importance lay not only in personal action but in preparation: terrain knowledge, intelligence and positioned forces allowed Shivaji to convert the encounter into rapid gains and recruitment. Yet conflicting accounts require caution about exact intentions and sequence. The Panhala crisis of 1660 supplies the counterweight. Maratha forces could be blockaded, compelled to retreat and forced into costly positional warfare.

Thus, neither environmental determinism nor heroic biography is adequate. Leadership mattered because it connected forts, followers, finance and political opportunity. Terrain shaped the method; organisation converted it into state-building; rival pressure continually exposed its limits.

**Scoring note:** A top answer must use all four anchors—1645–47 forts, Javli, Pratapgad and Panhala—while explicitly distinguishing secure chronology from disputed narrative detail.

### 1. Shaista Khan: occupation did not mean security

Aurangzeb sent Shaista Khan to contain Shivaji. Mughal forces occupied Poona in 1660, demonstrating imperial reach. Shivaji's night raid in 1663, which wounded the Khan and killed members of his household and following, exposed the difference between entering a city and securing the local political environment.

The raid was a prestige blow, not the destruction of Mughal power. Its success depended on intelligence, surprise and familiarity with the city.

### 2. Surat, 1664: extraction plus theatre

Surat was a wealthy Mughal port linked to overseas trade and pilgrimage. Shivaji's first sack yielded resources and advertised his ability to strike a prominent imperial-commercial centre.

⚠️ **Limit:** Raiding Surat did not establish permanent Maratha administration over the port. It was coercive extraction and political signalling, with real costs for inhabitants and merchants.

### 3. Purandar, 1665: an attempted incorporation

Jai Singh combined siege with diplomacy. Under the treaty:

- Shivaji ceded 23 of the 35 forts he held;
- he retained 12 forts and an associated territorial base;
- Sambhaji received a mansab of 5000; and
- Shivaji was to cooperate in a wider Deccan project against Bijapur.

```text
Mughal military pressure
          +
retained Maratha base
          +
imperial rank for Sambhaji
          +
planned joint action
          =
attempted subordinate alliance, not final extinction
```

Purandar was a severe concession, but not final surrender. Jai Singh sought to make Shivaji useful within a Mughal-led Deccan settlement.

### 4. Agra, 1666: rank became politics

Shivaji went to Aurangzeb's court at Agra. His placement and expected status produced offence; he withdrew angrily, was confined under guard and escaped.

The popular basket story is not securely verified and should not be stated as settled fact. What matters is that a possible relationship of service and alliance broke down over status, trust and imperial control.

Satish Chandra considers Aurangzeb's failure to appreciate Shivaji's political value a major mistake. This is a strong interpretation, not proof that durable accommodation was guaranteed. Shivaji later sought pardon and Sambhaji's mansab was restored for a time; relations again broke down, and conflict renewed by 1670.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Purandar proves that Shivaji accepted permanent Mughal sovereignty.

**Reply:** The treaty imposed subordination and major territorial concessions, but Shivaji retained a base, negotiated terms and entered a contingent political project. The later breakdown shows that incorporation was attempted, not completed.

### UPSC use

- Distinguish **raid**, **occupation**, **treaty subordination** and **durable incorporation**.
- Use Purandar as the strongest evidence that conflict and negotiation were alternatives within the same relationship.
- At Agra, make status and rank central; avoid the unverified basket detail.
- A balanced verdict says Aurangzeb missed an opportunity, not that one court incident alone doomed the empire.

### 1. Why a victorious commander still needed coronation

By 1674 Shivaji controlled substantial territory and armed forces. Yet military success did not automatically settle social rank or diplomatic standing. Coronation at Raigad, conducted with the learned Brahman Gaga Bhatta, presented Shivaji as a properly consecrated Kshatriya king.

The ceremony:

- raised him above rival Maratha chiefs;
- asserted a sovereign rather than merely jagirdari status;
- regularised titles, court ritual and a regnal era;
- strengthened dealings with Deccan rulers; and
- gave a wider idiom to swarajya and royal protection.

⚠️ **Qualification:** The need to establish a genealogy and answer objections shows that status was being made and contested. Coronation confirmed a claim; it did not simply unveil an uncontested ancient fact.

### 2. Hindu idiom, composite statecraft

Shivaji used Sanskritic titles, Brahmanical ritual and protection of Hindu institutions. Those features were politically meaningful. But the state also borrowed heavily from Deccani-Persianate administration and diplomacy, employed Muslims, fought Hindu as well as Muslim opponents and negotiated with all neighbouring powers.

The right formula is:

```text
Hindu kingship idiom
        +
regional Marathi mobilisation
        +
composite Deccani institutions and personnel
        =
historically specific sovereignty
```

It was neither a religion-free state nor an ethnically pure modern nation-state.

### 3. Karnataka expedition, 1676–79

Shivaji moved into Karnataka partly to claim his father Shahji's southern patrimony and partly to widen resources and strategic depth. Golconda cooperation helped the campaign, while local Nayaks and the politics of Bijapur shaped outcomes.

The expedition demonstrates:

- Maratha strategy was not confined to mountain defence;
- dynastic inheritance and fiscal opportunity worked together;
- interstate alliances could be temporary and interest-based; and
- distant conquests created communication and control problems.

### 4. Diplomacy was part of warfare

Shivaji alternately fought, aided or bargained with Bijapur, Golconda and the Mughals. This was not inconsistency in the simple sense. A middle power sought room among larger states whose own factions and priorities shifted.

**Limit:** Strategic flexibility should not be romanticised as perfect foresight. Ambitious expansion could alarm possible partners and overextend resources.

- Mention Gaga Bhatta, Raigad and 1674, followed immediately by the status-making qualification.
- Use Karnataka to show expansion, patrimonial claim and interstate diplomacy—not only “guerrilla defence.”
- Avoid describing the state as either purely Hindu or merely a copy of Deccani institutions.

### 1. Ashtapradhan: eight officers, not a cabinet

The **Ashtapradhan** was a group of eight principal offices. Its members advised and served Shivaji, but did not form a modern cabinet with collective responsibility to a legislature.

| Office | Main sphere | Necessary caution |
|---|---|---|
| Peshwa | general administration and coordination | later Peshwa dominance must not be projected backward |
| Amatya or Mazumdar | accounts and finance | titles and practical functions could overlap |
| Sachiv | correspondence and records | record control supported command |
| Mantri or Waqia-navis | court record and intelligence | not a modern home minister |
| Sumant or Dabir | diplomacy and external affairs | drew on wider Deccani practice |
| Senapati | military command | did not independently control the state |
| Nyayadhish | justice | jurisdiction coexisted with custom |
| Panditrao | religious duties and grants | ritual office did not make all administration sacerdotal |

Except for the explicitly judicial and religious offices, civil and military responsibilities could overlap. Officers answered to the ruler.

### 2. Territorial layers

Shivaji's kingdom used central supervision, provincial divisions and local officers. Terminology and boundaries varied, so an answer should explain functions rather than force one rigid chart.

- territorial officials transmitted orders and supervised revenue;
- village patils and accountants remained essential;
- deshmukhs and mirasdars possessed hereditary local influence;
- officials checked accounts and attempted to curb unauthorised exactions; and
- military commanders could hold administrative responsibilities.

Shivaji constrained over-mighty local intermediaries but did not abolish hereditary rights or every form of mokasa/jagir.

### 3. Fort administration

Forts were deliberately prevented from becoming effortless private bases. Divided responsibilities, record-keeping and direct royal oversight sought to reduce betrayal and local capture.

```text
commander: defence and readiness
stores officer: grain, arms and accounts
record/intelligence chain: reports upward
local villages: supply and labour
mobile force: relief or recovery
```

The exact nomenclature varied across sources; the institutional principle is checks, supplies and information.

### 4. Intelligence as infrastructure

Night raids, rapid concentration and avoidance of stronger forces depended on:

- scouts and local informants;
- knowledge of routes and guard routines;
- fast transmission of orders;
- political intelligence about rival courts; and
- monitoring of commanders and forts.

Intelligence did not make operations infallible. False information, defection and local hostility could reverse advantage.

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Strong royal supervision proves a fully centralised bureaucracy that displaced local society.

**Reply:** Central direction was real, but collection, policing and supply still passed through locally rooted actors. The state constrained, audited and bargained with them. “Centralised” describes aspiration and some mechanisms, not uniform penetration.

### 1. Assessment in the core

Shivaji's government used measurement, crop or land classification and village officials. Annaji Datto's assessment, completed around 1679, is an important anchor. The aim was to know the resource base, reduce uncontrolled intermediary exactions and improve the ruler's share.

Sources associate the demand with a substantial share, often described around two-fifths with additional cesses. This must not be repeated as one uniform realised rate. Crop, locality, customary rights, remissions, official conduct and bargaining affected incidence.

> **Key distinction:** assessment is the stated demand; realisation is what the state actually collects.

### 2. Local rights did not vanish

Deshmukhs, patils and mirasdars possessed inherited claims and local authority. Shivaji could discipline them, destroy fortified resistance, confirm rights or use them in collection. Reform therefore meant greater supervision, not a blank institutional slate.

Likewise, claims that all mokasa or jagir grants disappeared rest too heavily on isolated formulations. Cash payment to regular troops was preferred in important contexts, but assignments and service grants persisted.

### 3. Chauth

**Chauth** literally refers to a one-fourth claim. In neighbouring or contested territory it could operate as payment for protection or non-raiding and later as an organised political-fiscal claim.

It was not simply:

- land revenue routinely assessed inside every village of Shivaji's core;
- proof of full administrative sovereignty;
- a religious tax; or
- unstructured loot in every instance.

### 4. Sardeshmukhi

**Sardeshmukhi** was an additional ten-per-cent claim, justified by an asserted superior deshmukh right. It was conceptually distinct from chauth even when later agreements mentioned both.

| Test | Chauth | Sardeshmukhi |
|---|---|---|
| conventional magnitude | one-fourth | additional one-tenth |
| stated logic | protection/non-raiding and political claim | superior deshmukh right |
| relation to sovereignty | may signal influence without full government | may assert superior right without direct administration |
| collection reality | depended on agents, force and bargains | likewise depended on recognition and enforcement |

### 5. The fiscal frontier

During the later war, Maratha kamaishdars and commanders sought chauth in Mughal districts. Mughal jagirdars sometimes made private bargains to protect collection. This created overlapping claims:

```text
cultivator/local revenue
       ├─ Mughal jagirdar's demand
       ├─ zamindar/local dues
       └─ Maratha chauth claim
                    ↓
       bargaining, evasion or heavier pressure
```

The existence of a claim did not prove that every rupee was collected. But even partial parallel extraction could weaken Mughal authority and raise transaction costs.

### UPSC use

- Always distinguish chauth from sardeshmukhi.
- Distinguish **assessment**, **incidence**, **collection** and **sovereignty**.
- Do not claim that revenue reform abolished local intermediaries.
- Use parallel claims to connect Maratha expansion with the jagirdari crisis.

### 1. Ganimi kava in plain language

**Ganimi kava** is best understood as an adaptable system of operating against an enemy through speed, surprise, dispersed movement, local intelligence and attacks on vulnerable communications. “Guerrilla warfare” is a useful shorthand only if it does not suggest a timeless, uniquely ethnic doctrine.

Maratha forces did not always avoid battle. They fought sieges, defended forts, used infantry assaults, concentrated cavalry and negotiated surrenders. The method was about choosing a favourable relationship between aim, terrain and force.

### 2. Troops and discipline

- Mavali infantry was important in broken hill terrain and fort operations.
- Light cavalry enabled reconnaissance, raids and rapid concentration.
- Regular troops were often paid in cash, though plunder and assignments also existed.
- Command discipline sought to control unauthorised violence and preserve operational cohesion.
- Intelligence linked military movement to local society.

⚠️ **Qualification:** Norms of discipline do not prove that civilians never suffered. Raiding, forced contributions, sieges and competing armies imposed real burdens.

### 3. Logistics: the hidden contest

The Mughal army possessed numbers, artillery, engineers and the capacity for sustained sieges. Its problem was converting those strengths into affordable, repeatable control.

| Mughal requirement | Maratha counter-pressure |
|---|---|
| grain and fodder over long communications | raids on convoys and countryside |
| artillery moved to hill forts | delay, monsoon and route difficulty |
| garrison after capture | multiplication of posts and expense |
| reliable revenue near army | parallel claims and local bargains |
| concentrated opponent | dispersion and later reassembly |

This does not make Mughal defeat automatic. The empire captured major forts and annexed kingdoms. The issue was the declining return on each success.

### 4. Navy: regional and littoral

Shivaji recruited Kolis, Bhandaris, Muslims and other seafaring groups. Gunboats, grabs, gallivats, trading craft and coastal forts supported:

- defence of creeks and shoreline;
- supply and intelligence;
- protection or taxation of commerce;
- pressure against the Sidis of Janjira; and
- bargaining with Portuguese and English interests.

The navy was a significant regional littoral force. It did not command the Indian Ocean like a modern blue-water navy, and it did not remove European or Sidi power.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** A strategy based on avoiding decisive battle cannot build a state.

**Reply:** Avoidance was selective. Mobile operations protected the resource base, isolated strongpoints and imposed costs; forts, revenue officers, infantry and diplomacy converted movement into political endurance. The limit was that distant conquest and naval competition required capacities beyond raiding.

- Define ganimi kava operationally, not romantically.
- Compare force structures through logistics rather than “brave versus slow.”
- Mention Mavali infantry and light cavalry together.
- Qualify cash payment and discipline with the persistence of assignments and civilian costs.
- Call the navy **littoral/regional**, never blue-water.

### 1. What the state claimed

Coronation, Sanskritic titles, protection of religious institutions and the language of swarajya gave Shivaji's rule a recognisably Hindu kingship idiom. This helped distinguish his sovereignty from service under Bijapur or the Mughals and could mobilise support.

### 2. How the state worked

Its personnel and techniques were composite:

- Muslim officers and sailors served in military and maritime roles;
- administrative vocabulary and offices drew from Deccani-Persianate practice;
- alliances crossed religious lines;
- Hindu chiefs could be opponents; and
- control of forts, revenue and routes structured conflict.

Inclusion was not modern secular citizenship. It was practical service, protection and loyalty within a hierarchical monarchy.

### 3. Protection and extraction

Rulers present taxation as protection and order. Subjects may experience the same state through assessment, cesses, requisitions, war and discipline. Shivaji's efforts to restrain unauthorised exactions could improve predictability, yet the kingdom still required substantial revenue and military labour.

| Claim | Achievement | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| protection from enemy and arbitrary intermediary | stronger oversight and defended zones | warfare itself exposed communities to raids and requisitions |
| inclusion in service | mixed personnel and cross-cutting alliances | rank and access remained unequal |
| regional self-rule | independent institutions and kingship | local chiefs and cultivators did not all experience policy identically |
| religious legitimacy | coherent public idiom | cannot alone explain appointments, wars or taxes |

### 4. How to handle “Hindu state”

The phrase may identify an important legitimising language if immediately qualified. It fails when used to imply:

- ethnic purity;
- exclusion of all Muslims;
- warfare driven only by religion;
- modern nationalism or democracy; or
- absence of extraction and hierarchy.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Composite personnel make Hindu kingship merely decorative.

**Reply:** Symbols and rituals shaped rank, loyalty and sovereign communication; they were not decorative. But political significance does not make them the sole causal explanation of administration or war.

Progress: 11 / 14 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Rajaram, Gingee, Tarabai and decentralised resistance

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: a state can lose its centre and retain a network

```text
1689 Sambhaji executed
          ↓
Rajaram escapes to Gingee
          │
          ├─ distant royal legitimacy
          └─ Mughal siege absorbs resources
                    │
Maharashtra commanders operate with wider autonomy
                    │
1698 Gingee falls after Rajaram has escaped
                    ↓
1700 Rajaram dies → Tarabai leads for Shivaji II
                    ↓
resistance survives through distributed command
```

### 1. Why Gingee mattered

Rajaram's flight to Gingee shifted the royal centre far from Maharashtra. Gingee's formidable position and southern links forced the Mughals to conduct a prolonged siege while Maratha commanders continued operations elsewhere.

The arrangement divided labour:

- royal continuity helped legitimise resistance;
- a distant fort absorbed imperial attention;
- commanders exploited local intelligence in Maharashtra; and
- southern connections widened resources and routes.

### 2. Gingee's fall was not the end

The fort fell in 1698, but Rajaram had escaped. This is another example of the difference between a captured place and a captured political network. Mughal siege success was real; its strategic yield was incomplete.

### 3. Decentralisation: strength and future cost

Commanders such as Santaji Ghorpade and Dhanaji Jadhav operated with considerable initiative. Decentralisation:

| Immediate advantage | Longer-term risk |
|---|---|
| many operational centres | harder coordination |
| rapid local decisions | stronger personal followings |
| no single decisive target | disputes over revenue and rank |
| resilience after royal loss | later confederate tendencies |

Do not describe the system as chaos. Royal legitimacy, common enemies, fort networks and fiscal claims continued to connect commanders. But cohesion was negotiated rather than bureaucratically uniform.

### 4. Tarabai after 1700

Rajaram died in 1700. Tarabai led resistance in the name of her young son Shivaji II. Her leadership demonstrates that royal women could exercise political and military direction within dynastic legitimacy.

Tarabai coordinated commanders, maintained the claim of the royal house and resisted during Aurangzeb's most personal phase of fort campaigning. Her role should not be reduced to guardianship without agency.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Decentralisation proves that the Maratha state had collapsed into independent bandit groups.

**Reply:** Autonomous command and extraction increased, but dynastic authority, shared enemies, forts and recognised fiscal claims still created a political field. The accurate description is resilient but loosening coordination.

### UPSC use

- Chronology: Rajaram to Gingee (1689); Gingee falls 1698 after escape; Rajaram dies 1700; Tarabai leads for Shivaji II.
- Use Gingee to show imperial opportunity cost.
- Give both sides of decentralisation: resilience now, autonomy later.
- Treat Tarabai as a political leader, not only a regent label.

## Lesson 12 — Annexation and the Buffer Paradox

### Visual: conquest enlarged both assets and liabilities

```text
1636 arrangement:
Mughal provinces ─ buffer sultanates ─ southern political field

1686 Bijapur annexed + 1687 Golconda annexed
                         │
          ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
          ▼                             ▼
 more territory, forts, revenue       more garrisons, claimants,
 and imperial prestige                routes and unsettled elites
          └──────────────┬──────────────┘
                         ▼
             conquest without cheap pacification
```

### 1. Why Aurangzeb moved into the Deccan

Aurangzeb entered the Deccan in 1681 amid several concerns: Prince Akbar's rebellion and contact with Sambhaji, continuing Maratha attacks, uncertain cooperation from Bijapur and Golconda, contested tribute and the desire to impose firmer imperial authority.

No single motive is sufficient. Security, sovereignty, dynasty and fiscal-military calculation converged.

### 2. Bijapur, 1686

After prolonged pressure and an eighteen-month siege, Bijapur fell in 1686. The Mughal Empire removed an Adil Shahi court that had alternately resisted, bargained and acted as a buffer.

### 3. Golconda, 1687

Golconda fell in 1687. Its commercial resources and strategic position made conquest attractive. Yet annexation also displaced officials and soldiers, widened assignment claims and extended imperial responsibility into difficult southern spaces.

### 4. The buffer paradox

A buffer state can be troublesome, but it can also:

- absorb frontier conflict;
- negotiate with regional forces;
- maintain local administration at its own expense;
- separate imperial provinces from mobile opponents; and
- offer a partner short of direct annexation.

Destroying Bijapur and Golconda removed independent rivals, but also removed negotiating layers. The empire inherited their forts, debts, personnel, factions and frontiers.

### 5. Capacity and strain together

The annexations prove that Mughal capacity remained enormous. It could concentrate troops, artillery, money and imperial leadership. The same victories increased commitments. This is why “weak empire” is an inadequate explanation.

```text
high capacity → conquest
conquest → larger commitments
larger commitments + dispersed war → declining marginal return
```

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Annexing wealthy kingdoms must have solved the jagir shortage by adding land.

**Reply:** New paper jama came with new mansabdars, Deccani entrants, garrisons, damaged districts and hard-to-realise assignments. Quantity of territory was not identical to quality of available jagirs.

### UPSC use

- Give exact dates: Bijapur 1686; Golconda 1687.
- Explain the buffer function before discussing its removal.
- Use annexation to show capacity and overextension simultaneously.
- Never assume that increased jama meant equivalent hasil.

5. Annexation demonstrated major Mughal military capacity.
6. It removed independent rivals and buffers.
7. The empire inherited more forts and communication lines.
8. Deccani nobles and soldiers added new service claims.

### Local Mains practice

Their annexation transferred responsibility for distant forts, routes, factions and revenue collection directly to the Mughals. Displaced Deccani soldiers and nobles entered a service market already under pressure, while garrisons and campaigns consumed income. Paper jama did not guarantee hasil in districts disrupted by war, zamindar resistance and Maratha chauth.

The annexations also exposed longer communications and brought the empire into closer contact with decentralised Maratha forces. Yet it would be wrong to infer immediate collapse: Mughal capacity remained formidable and continued to take forts.

Aurangzeb therefore won territorial sovereignty at a rising administrative price. The sultanates' removal increased imperial prestige and potential resources, but destroyed buffers and multiplied obligations that direct rule could not cheaply stabilise.

## Lesson 13 — The Fort-Siege Cycle and Negotiated Sovereignty

Progress: 13 / 14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Long war, fort-siege cycle, negotiated sovereignty and strategic/fiscal consequences

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Part II PDF pp. 305–311 and concise volume PDF pp. 378–382
CA search: "Maratha forts conservation UNESCO management official 2025 2026"

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: diminishing return from repeated capture

```text
Mughal army besieges fort
          ↓
fort surrenders or falls
          ↓
payment + garrison + supplies + repairs
          ↓
army moves to next target
          ↓
Marathas raid route / bargain / recover old fort
          ↓
new siege requirement
          ↺
```

### 1. Aurangzeb's personal campaign cycle, c. 1700–05

After Rajaram's death, Aurangzeb personally moved from fort to fort. Imperial forces continued to display endurance, engineering and siege capacity. Yet each capture required time, money, transport and a permanent or rotating garrison.

Maratha defenders could:

- negotiate surrender and payment;
- preserve men by giving up a fort;
- attack supply lines after the main army moved;
- recover a fort when its garrison weakened; or
- shift the political centre elsewhere.

This was not effortless Maratha victory. Strongholds fell, regions suffered and both sides absorbed heavy costs.

### 2. Negotiated sovereignty

Sovereignty in the war zone was often layered:

| Formal claim | Local reality |
|---|---|
| Mughal emperor assigned a jagir | jagirdar might lack safe collection |
| Mughal garrison held a fort | neighbouring countryside might pay or aid Marathas |
| Maratha agent claimed chauth | collection could require support from a commander |
| zamindar acknowledged one side | private bargain might protect local interests with another |

Formal titles still mattered; they were not empty. But actual rule depended on revenue, force and cooperation.

### 3. Why settlement remained possible but elusive

Possible settlement moments existed at Purandar, after Agra, with Sambhaji and in later talks involving Shahu. Barriers included:

- incompatible claims to sovereign status;
- Mughal insistence on hierarchy;
- Maratha insistence on forts and fiscal claims;
- distrust after repeated reversals;
- gains from war for commanders and intermediaries;
- court and succession politics; and
- uncertainty about enforcing promises.

Later Persian evidence suggests discussion of restoring swarajya and granting sardeshmukhi, but willingness to grant chauth is not established. This distinction matters.

### 4. Strategic and fiscal consequence

The empire did not simply “run out of soldiers.” Rather:

```text
campaign expense rises
       +
hasil in disturbed jagirs falls
       +
new garrisons and claimants multiply
       +
northern oversight weakens
       =
strategic return falls despite continuing capacity
```

### Objection and reply

**Objection:** Since Aurangzeb kept taking forts, the campaign remained strategically successful.

**Reply:** Operational results must be measured against cost, durability and political objective. Repeated recapture and insecure collection could turn tactical success into strategic stalemate.

### UPSC use

- Use the capture–garrison–supply–recovery loop as the central diagram.
- Explain layered sovereignty with one concrete jagirdar-chauth example.
- Mention settlement possibilities without asserting that peace was inevitable.
- Do not confuse sardeshmukhi discussion with confirmed willingness to grant chauth.

### Local Mains practice

Once the principal army moved, Maratha forces could attack supply lines, bargain with local officers or recover weakened positions. They also preserved manpower through negotiated surrender and shifted operations among multiple centres. Thus, fort possession and countryside control frequently diverged.

The fiscal effect reinforced the military dilemma. Jagirdars held formal assignments whose jama could not be realised as hasil in disturbed districts. Maratha kamaishdars demanded chauth, zamindars bargained or resisted, and some jagirdars privately paid for protection. Poor realisation weakened contingents, encouraging harsher extraction or further negotiation.

Settlement was conceivable but obstructed by rank, sovereignty, fort and revenue claims, distrust and the interests of wartime commanders. The result was not immediate imperial incapacity: Mughal armies continued to win sieges. It was declining strategic return, as each victory expanded commitments without reliably ending resistance or financing the next operation.

## Lesson 14 — The Jagirdari Crisis and the Final Synthesis

Progress: 14 / 14 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Mansab-jagir strain, be-jagiri, jama-hasil, local resistance and historiographical synthesis

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: queried — Part II PDF pp. 302 and 312–315; concise volume PDF pp. 383–387; Topic 21 for pre-1681 strain
CA search: "Mughal jagirdari crisis historiography recent 2026"
CA found: none found; the lesson relies on historical and historiographical evidence
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: the crisis as a feedback system

```text
more mansab/service claims + uneven productive jagirs
                         ↓
be-jagiri or poor assignment quality
                         ↓
paper jama exceeds actual hasil
                         ↓
weak contingent OR harsher short-term collection
                         ↓
zamindar/cultivator resistance, flight or Maratha bargain
                         ↓
still lower hasil + noble debt and faction
                         ↓
frequent transfer, corruption and renewed coercion
                         ↺
```

### 1. First define the terms

| Term | Meaning | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| mansab | imperial rank carrying status, pay and service obligation | treating rank as an exact troop count |
| jagir | transferable assignment of a revenue claim | treating it as private ownership of all land |
| jama | assessed paper revenue | equating it with cash actually collected |
| hasil | realised collection | assuming it matched jama |
| paibaqi | revenue/land available or awaiting assignment | assuming all available entries were equally productive |
| be-jagiri | delay or shortage of suitable assignable jagirs | claiming that literally no land existed |

### 2. The crisis predated the final Deccan war

Under Shah Jahan, mansabdars of 500 zat and above grew much faster than assessed revenue. Month-scales and reduced effective contingents adapted pay and obligation to poor realisation; Deccan jagirs could be rated at only three or four months. These mechanisms show strain before 1681.

Therefore, Aurangzeb's war aggravated an existing claim-resource gap; it did not create the entire problem from nothing.

### 3. The acute Deccan form

The long war added interacting pressures:

1. annexation brought Deccani and Maratha service claimants;
2. the best or more manageable revenues could be reserved for khalisa and war costs;
3. jagirdars received zor-talab or difficult-to-realise areas;
4. forts and raids disrupted cultivation and transport;
5. chauth created parallel extraction;
6. zamindars controlled local force and cooperation;
7. frequent transfers encouraged short horizons;
8. low hasil weakened troop maintenance; and
9. weakened contingents made revenue collection still harder.

Contemporary Bhimsen described mansabdars unable to govern assignments because their forces were too small while zamindars strengthened and joined or bargained with Marathas. This is evidence of a social-administrative-military loop, not fiscal arithmetic alone.

### 4. Three explanations to combine, not caricature

| Emphasis | Contribution | Limitation when isolated |
|---|---|---|
| too many claimants, too few good jagirs | identifies be-jagiri and assignment competition | ignores why realisation and local control failed |
| Deccan overextension/Aurangzeb's choices | explains war cost, annexation and political rigidity | strain existed earlier and outside one ruler's personality |
| agrarian-zamindar resistance | explains hasil, coercion and local links | cannot alone explain court rank, garrisons and interstate chronology |

Satish Chandra's mature explanation connects them. Jagirdari crisis was part of a wider social, administrative and military crisis compounded by a prolonged, unprofitable war.

### 5. Capacity and crisis are not opposites

The Mughal Empire:

- annexed Bijapur and Golconda;
- sustained armies for decades;
- took major forts; and
- continued appointments and assignments.

Yet the quality, cost and durability of those operations deteriorated. Crisis means a self-reinforcing mismatch, not that the state stopped functioning before Aurangzeb died in 1707.

### 6. The balanced verdict

```text
inherited mansab-jagir strain
        +
annexation and new claimants
        +
long war and parallel chauth
        +
zamindar/cultivator bargaining and resistance
        +
political failure to settle
        =
acute but uneven fiscal-military crisis
```

Do not carry the account automatically into post-1707 collapse. Topic 25 owns succession crises, successor states, invasions and eighteenth-century Maratha expansion.

### UPSC use

- Define terms before using the phrase “jagirdari crisis.”
- Build a causal loop, not a shopping list.
- Mention pre-existing Shah Jahan-era adjustments.
- Include zamindar and cultivator links.
- End with “capacity and strain coexisted; no automatic collapse before 1707.”

### Revision notes

1. Mansab was rank and service claim; jagir was transferable revenue assignment.

### Local Mains practice

**Question (20 marks, 250 words):** Was the jagirdari crisis mainly a shortage of jagirs? Critically examine.

**Model:** Shortage of suitable jagirs was an important component of the crisis, but not a complete explanation. Mansab and salary claims expanded faster than dependable resources, creating be-jagiri and competition for productive assignments. Under Shah Jahan, month-scales and reduced muster expectations already adjusted formal rank to poor realisation, especially in the Deccan.

The later crisis centred equally on quality and control. Jama often exceeded hasil. Aurangzeb's annexation of Bijapur and Golconda added assessed territory, but also Deccani and Maratha claimants, garrisons and disturbed districts. Easily managed revenue could be reserved for khalisa, leaving jagirdars with difficult zor-talab areas. Maratha chauth, raids and private bargains competed with Mughal demands. Zamindars and cultivators could resist, flee or negotiate; frequent transfers encouraged harsh short-term extraction. Low hasil then prevented mansabdars from maintaining contingents, and weak contingents further reduced collection.

Thus, jagirdari crisis was a feedback loop joining fiscal assignment, military service, local society and political strategy. Aurangzeb aggravated rather than invented it. The empire continued to annex kingdoms and capture forts, so crisis did not mean automatic collapse before 1707. It meant that imperial achievements generated increasingly costly and insecure returns.

**Scoring note:** A top answer defines be-jagiri, jama and hasil, establishes pre-war roots, builds the local-power feedback loop and ends with capacity-with-strain rather than collapse.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Original 10-marker

**Question:** Distinguish the capture of territory from the consolidation of sovereignty in Shivaji's state-building. (10 marks, 150 words)

**Model answer:** Territorial capture gave Shivaji forts, routes and revenue opportunities, but sovereignty required more than possession. Early acquisitions around Torna, Kondana and Rajgad created a defensible base; Javli widened access toward Satara and the Konkan. Yet forts needed stores, garrisons, intelligence, local revenue and relief. Control therefore depended on institutions.

Purandar illustrates the distinction from the opposite direction. Shivaji ceded twenty-three forts but retained twelve and a political base, allowing recovery after attempted Mughal incorporation. The 1674 Raigad coronation then elevated practical power into publicly articulated kingship through titles, ritual and Kshatriya validation. Revenue assessment, central supervision and diplomacy made the claim repeatable.

Thus, capture was an event; consolidation was a continuing relationship among territory, resources, legitimacy and local cooperation. Shivaji's achievement lay in connecting them, although control remained uneven and negotiated.

**Unique scoring note:** A 6/10 answer lists forts and coronation. An 8+/10 answer explicitly contrasts event with process and uses both Purandar and the fort-support mechanism.

## Original 15-marker

**Question:** “Shivaji's state was simultaneously innovative, inherited and negotiated.” Examine. (15 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer:** Shivaji innovated by joining forts, mobile forces, intelligence, supervised revenue and sovereign ritual into a durable regional state. Ganimi kava converted speed and local knowledge into pressure on stronger opponents, while a littoral navy supported coastal defence, trade and bargaining. The 1674 coronation raised him above rival chiefs and gave practical autonomy a validated royal form.

Yet the state was not created from an institutional vacuum. Shahji's service career supplied a Poona base, military contacts and experience of Ahmadnagar-Bijapur-Mughal rivalry. The Ashtapradhan and administrative vocabulary adapted Deccani-Persianate precedents. Village patils, deshmukhs and mirasdars retained locally rooted roles; cash payment did not abolish every assignment.

Power was also negotiated. Purandar attempted to incorporate Shivaji through territorial concessions and Sambhaji's mansab. Chauth and sardeshmukhi extended influence without always creating direct rule. Alliances with Golconda, conflict with Hindu chiefs and Muslim participation in service qualify a homogeneous communal description.

The strongest verdict therefore avoids both heroic exceptionalism and institutional reduction. Shivaji rearranged inherited resources into a new sovereign project, but its implementation depended on bargains with local society and neighbouring states. Innovation lay in the combination and political direction of institutions, not in inventing every component anew.

**Unique scoring note:** High marks require one example each for innovation, inheritance and negotiation, followed by a synthesis explaining why these are compatible rather than competing labels.

## Original 20-marker

**Question:** “Aurangzeb's Deccan policy displayed imperial capacity while undermining the conditions that made that capacity sustainable.” Critically analyse. (20 marks, 250 words)

**Model answer:** Aurangzeb's Deccan policy cannot be explained by simple imperial weakness. The Mughal state annexed Bijapur in 1686 and Golconda in 1687, sustained armies for decades and repeatedly captured major forts. These achievements reveal exceptional mobilisation, siege engineering and command.

The victories nevertheless enlarged the burden of rule. Annexation removed buffer states and transferred their forts, factions, officials and frontiers to direct imperial responsibility. New Deccani and Maratha entrants increased claims on jagirs, while war-damaged districts often yielded less hasil than their paper jama. Long communications, garrisons and Aurangzeb's fort cycle around 1700–05 consumed resources without securing the countryside.

Maratha adaptation intensified the mismatch. Sambhaji's execution removed a recognised negotiating head but decentralised resistance. Rajaram's move to Gingee divided imperial attention; Tarabai and mobile commanders sustained warfare. Kamaishdars claimed chauth, zamindars bargained or resisted and some Mughal jagirdars privately paid for security. Poor revenue weakened contingents, which further impaired collection.

Yet crisis did not mean collapse before 1707. Administration, appointments and conquest continued. Aurangzeb aggravated an older mansab-resource strain through annexation and failed settlement. His policy's paradox was therefore not incapacity, but the conversion of repeated operational success into declining fiscal and strategic returns.

**Unique scoring note:** A superior answer proves capacity before analysing strain, includes the buffer paradox and jagirdari feedback, and explicitly rejects both monocausal blame and pre-1707 collapse.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## A. Core thesis

Shivaji converted western Deccan geography, inherited service resources, forts, local revenue and mobile forces into an independent regional monarchy. His state used a Hindu kingship idiom but retained composite Deccani-Persianate institutions and personnel. Aurangzeb's post-1681 campaign displayed immense Mughal capacity, yet annexation, decentralised resistance, fort logistics and parallel fiscal claims deepened an inherited mansab-jagir strain. The result by 1707 was severe but uneven crisis, not automatic imperial collapse.

## B. Regional setting and sources

- Sahyadri ridges and Maval valleys favoured defensible nodes, infantry and knowledge of passes.
- The Konkan supplied ports, creeks, commerce and maritime communities.
- Plateau routes connected Poona, Bijapur, Golconda and Karnataka.
- Geography enabled; organisation, revenue, intelligence and leadership caused usable power.
- Maratha families and local groups were internally differentiated.
- Persian chronicles, Marathi bakhars, letters, European records and material remains answer different questions.
- Secure outcomes must be separated from partisan detail, especially at Pratapgad.

## C. Shahji to early Shivaji

- Shahji served Ahmadnagar, Mughal and Bijapur interests and supported a Nizam Shahi claimant.
- His career represents mobile service politics, not a continuous national programme.
- The Poona jagir, troops, contacts and southern links gave Shivaji a starting platform.
- Early forts were acquired around 1645–47.
- Javli in 1656 opened strategic routes and defeated a rival Maratha house.
- Afzal Khan died at Pratapgad in 1659; the exact encounter sequence remains disputed.
- Panhala in 1660 shows the limits of mobility under siege pressure.

## D. Mughal confrontation and incomplete accommodation

- Shaista Khan occupied Poona in 1660; Shivaji's standard raid date is 1663.
- Surat was sacked in 1664 for wealth and political effect, not permanently governed.
- Purandar in 1665: 23 forts ceded, 12 retained, Sambhaji mansab 5000.
- Jai Singh attempted to fit Shivaji into a Mughal-led campaign against Bijapur.
- Purandar was subordinate accommodation, not final surrender.
- At Agra in 1666, rank, honour and control produced rupture.
- Shivaji escaped; the popular basket mechanism is unverified.
- Limited accommodation resumed before war and a second Surat sack in 1670.

## E. Sovereignty and expansion

- Raigad coronation occurred in 1674 with Gaga Bhatta.
- It elevated Shivaji above rival chiefs and expressed independent kingship.
- Kshatriya legitimacy had to be constructed and defended.
- Hindu symbolism was substantial but did not create an institutionally pure state.
- Karnataka expedition, 1676–79, joined Shahji's patrimony, resources and strategy.
- Diplomacy with Bijapur, Golconda and the Mughals shifted with interests.

## F. Administration

- Ashtapradhan was eight high offices, not a modern cabinet.
- Peshwa: general coordination; Amatya/Mazumdar: finance; Sachiv: records.
- Mantri/Waqia-navis: court record/intelligence; Sumant/Dabir: diplomacy.
- Senapati: military command; Nyayadhish: justice; Panditrao: religious grants/duties.
- Ministers answered to Shivaji; civil and military functions could overlap.
- Provincial and local administration relied on patils, accountants, deshmukhs and mirasdars.
- The state curbed some illegal exactions but did not abolish hereditary local rights.
- Forts required divided duties, accounts, stores, intelligence and mobile relief.

## G. Revenue and external claims

- Measurement and classification sought stronger fiscal information.
- Annaji Datto's assessment is anchored in 1679.
- A substantial stated demand, often described around two-fifths plus cesses, was not one uniform realised incidence.
- Assessment, incidence and actual collection must be distinguished.
- Cash salaries were important for regular troops, but assignments did not disappear.
- Chauth: one-fourth protection/non-raiding and political claim.
- Sardeshmukhi: additional ten-per-cent claim based on superior deshmukh right.
- Claims could indicate influence without full direct sovereignty.
- Kamaishdars organised later chauth collection in contested districts.

## H. Military system and coast

- Ganimi kava meant flexible irregular operations using mobility, surprise and intelligence.
- It was not a timeless romantic doctrine or the whole of warfare.
- Mavali infantry, light cavalry and fort garrisons performed complementary roles.
- Marathas fought sieges and battles and used negotiation.
- Mughal strength lay in scale, artillery, engineers and siege endurance.
- Mughal weakness lay in long communications, fodder, seasonal movement and garrison costs.
- Shivaji's navy recruited Koli, Bhandari, Muslim and other seafaring personnel.
- It defended creeks and forts, supported supply and trade, and checked rivals.
- It was a regional littoral force, not a blue-water navy.

## I. State character and social qualification

- Hindu kingship shaped public legitimacy and sovereign rank.
- Deccani-Persianate practices continued in administration and diplomacy.
- Muslims served; Hindu chiefs could oppose; alliances crossed religious lines.
- Inclusion was service within hierarchy, not modern citizenship.
- State protection coexisted with tax, requisition and wartime burdens.
- “Hindu state” is useful only as a qualified legitimacy description.
- Modern nationalism, democracy and ethnic purity are anachronistic.

## J. Successors and decentralisation

- Shivaji died in 1680; Sambhaji prevailed in a contested succession.
- Prince Akbar's rebellion and Deccan movement formed one reason for Aurangzeb's concern.
- Sambhaji faced Mughal, Sidi, Portuguese and internal pressures.
- Captured at Sangameshwar, he was executed in 1689.
- Execution removed a negotiating head but did not end resistance.
- Rajaram escaped to Gingee, creating a distant royal centre.
- Gingee fell in 1698 after Rajaram had escaped.
- Rajaram died in 1700.
- Tarabai actively led resistance for Shivaji II.
- Decentralisation raised resilience and local initiative but strengthened autonomous commanders.

## K. Aurangzeb and the long war

- Aurangzeb entered the Deccan in 1681.
- Bijapur fell in 1686; Golconda in 1687.
- Annexation removed rivals and buffers while adding forts, territory, personnel and claims.
- More paper revenue did not mean more dependable realised revenue.
- Around 1700–05 Aurangzeb personally moved through a fort-siege cycle.
- Marathas could surrender, preserve manpower, raid communications and recover forts.
- Fort possession, countryside control and revenue collection often diverged.
- Settlement was possible at several moments but never guaranteed.
- Rank, sovereignty, forts, fiscal claims, distrust and wartime interests obstructed peace.

## L. Jagirdari crisis

- Mansab = rank/service obligation; jagir = transferable revenue assignment.
- Jama = assessed revenue; hasil = realised collection.
- Paibaqi = available/awaiting assignment; quality varied.
- Be-jagiri = shortage or delay of suitable assignments, not literal absence of land.
- Pressure predated 1681; month-scales adjusted pay to weak realisation.
- Annexation added service claimants as well as territory.
- War disruption, chauth and local resistance widened the jama-hasil gap.
- Productive revenues could be retained in khalisa for war costs.
- Jagirdars might receive difficult zor-talab assignments.
- Frequent transfers encouraged short-term extraction and corruption.
- Poor hasil weakened contingents; weak contingents weakened collection.
- Zamindar and cultivator responses made the crisis social as well as fiscal.
- A pure shortage theory, pure ruler-blame theory or pure agrarian theory is incomplete.
- Capacity and strain coexisted; the Mughal state did not automatically collapse before 1707.
