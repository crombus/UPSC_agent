# Provincial and Regional Kingdoms: Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Kashmir and Ahom-Assam — Live Session Edition

> **Syllabus position:** Prelims GS-I, “History of India”; Mains GS-I support through the culture clause on literature and architecture.
>
> **Learner-first lock:** This session treats the fifteenth century as a connected regional-state system. It does not reduce Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Kashmir or Ahom-Assam to a dynasty list, and it does not assume that one state form, religious policy or cultural pattern applied everywhere.
>
> **Evidence convention:** **Fact** means directly supported by the permitted canonical, book, official-paper or official-heritage evidence listed in the source ledger. **Analytical inference** means a reasoned interpretation built from that evidence. Contested traditions are identified rather than silently converted into facts.

### Visual: a map by strategic zones, not modern state borders

| Zone | Political centres | Material advantage | Main pressure line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eastern delta and lower Ganga | Bengal: Pandua/Gaur, Satgaon, Sonargaon, Chittagong | Rivers, rice lands, textiles, sea access | Delhi/Lodis, Jaunpur, Odisha, Kamrup/Assam, Arakan |
| Upper and middle Ganga | Jaunpur | Revenue-rich plain, towns, eastern routes | Delhi/Lodis and Bengal |
| Western port-hinterland | Gujarat: Patan, Ahmedabad, Champaner, Khambhat | Fertile interior, crafts, merchant capital, Indian Ocean ports | Malwa, Mewar, Khandesh, Portuguese |
| Central plateau hinge | Malwa: Dhar and Mandu | Fortified plateau and north-south/east-west routes | Gujarat, Mewar, Delhi/Lodis, Bahmanis |
| Himalayan valley | Kashmir | Valley agriculture, crafts and mountain routes | Internal factions and trans-Himalayan connections |
| Brahmaputra valley | Ahom-Assam | Wet-rice landscape, river corridors and service organisation | Local polities, Bengal/Kamrup frontier, later imperial pressures |

The visual shows why “regional kingdom” is a geographical and institutional category, not simply a label for a dynasty outside Delhi.

### Intuition: when the capital weakens, power does not evaporate

Imagine a transport company whose head office loses control over distant depots. The depots already possess warehouses, local staff, customers and roads. Some can become independent firms. They do not all copy the head office, because each works with a different market and landscape.

That is the central problem of the fifteenth century. Delhi's contraction redistributed resources and political initiative. Bengal worked through a delta and maritime outlets; Gujarat through ports and merchant-rich towns; Malwa through a defensible plateau; Jaunpur through the Ganga plain; Kashmir through a valley; and Ahom power through the Brahmaputra world.

### 1. Delhi's decline was an opening, not a complete explanation

**Fact:** Bengal had asserted autonomy before Timur. Gujarat, Malwa and Jaunpur emerged from provincial commands as Tughlaq authority weakened. Kashmir's Shah Mir transition and Ahom state formation followed their own regional histories.

**Analytical inference:** “Delhi declined, therefore regional kingdoms arose” is incomplete. A durable state also needed:

1. a material base in land, ports, routes or crafts;
2. military and revenue networks;
3. a capital or fortified centre;
4. cooperation or contest with local chiefs, merchants and landed groups;
5. a language of sovereignty expressed through coinage, titles, buildings and patronage.

### 2. Balance of power did not mean peace

Satish Chandra uses a balance-of-power frame because conflicts often remained regionally bounded:

| Arena | Checks operating within it | Result |
|---|---|---|
| West | Gujarat, Malwa and Mewar | No power easily absorbed the whole western zone |
| East | Bengal, Odisha/Gajapatis and Jaunpur | Bengal was strong but not unopposed |
| North | Delhi/Lodis and Jaunpur; Mewar pressure farther west | The Ganga-Yamuna and Rajasthan-Malwa fields remained contested |
| Valley and frontier zones | Kashmir and Ahom-Assam | Distinct political ecologies limited Delhi-centred explanations |

Balance means reciprocal constraint, alliance and rivalry. It does not imply equal power, stable frontiers or permanent blocs.

### 3. Regionalisation was productive as well as conflictual

**Fact:** Regional courts supported capitals, mosques, tombs, forts, markets, scholarship, translation and vernacular literature. Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa and Jaunpur created recognisable architectural idioms. Kashmir under Zain-ul-Abidin fostered crafts and translations.

**Qualification:** Court culture represents elite patronage. It cannot by itself prove social harmony, equal participation or prosperity for every group.

### 4. Do not assume uniform state forms

| Wrong shortcut | Better formulation |
|---|---|
| Every kingdom was a miniature Delhi Sultanate | Institutions were selected, inherited and localised |
| Every ruler followed one stable religious policy | Policies varied by ruler, campaign, constituency and source |
| Ports made every state maritime | Ports were decisive in Gujarat and Bengal, much less so in Jaunpur or Kashmir |
| Regional culture was isolated from Persianate culture | Persianate and local traditions interacted |
| Political fragmentation equals economic decline | Some regions experienced urban, commercial and cultural growth |

### 5. Source discipline

Chronicles privilege courts and victories. Inscriptions advertise patronage. Coins prove claims and mints, not exact borders. Architecture shows resources and craft choices but over-represents elites. Later traditions can preserve memory while reshaping it.

The safe historical chain is:

| Step | Question |
|---:|---|
| 1 | What kind of source is this? |
| 2 | When and for whom was it produced? |
| 3 | What does it directly show? |
| 4 | What inference is reasonable? |
| 5 | What remains unproven? |

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims:** ruler-region, capital-monument, dynasty and chronology matching.
- **Mains support:** reinterpret “decline” as political reorganisation; relate architecture and language patronage to legitimacy.
- **Trap:** a strong regional culture did not necessarily produce lasting military supremacy.
- **Answer-use line:** “The fifteenth century was less an interregnum than a polycentric political order in which ecology, routes and local elite coalitions converted Delhi's contraction into durable regional states.”

### Revision notes

1. Regionalisation began before and accelerated after Timur's 1398 raid.
2. Delhi's weakness was a necessary opening, not a sufficient cause.
3. Bengal's delta, Gujarat's ports and Malwa's plateau generated different state capacities.
4. Jaunpur competed for the Ganga plain; Kashmir followed a valley trajectory.
5. Ahom-Assam was not a Delhi province that merely broke away.
6. Balance of power means reciprocal constraint, not peace or equality.
7. Regional architecture was adapted through local materials and craft traditions.
8. Court inclusion does not establish universal social equality.
9. Coins, chronicles, inscriptions and buildings answer different questions.
10. Mughal expansion later confronted an already organised regional field.

In the balance-of-power framework, which pairing is most accurate?

B. Gujarat–Malwa–Mewar formed a western field of reciprocal constraint.
C. Gujarat–Malwa–Mewar interacted mainly through stable territorial settlements.

#### Answers and option explanations

**MCQ 1: A**

- **A — Correct:** Regional autonomy survived where political opportunity joined a workable material base, local networks and legitimating institutions.
- **B — Incorrect:** Regional rulers frequently retained and adapted inherited offices, court practices and revenue arrangements rather than discarding them wholesale.
- **C — Incorrect:** Alliances shifted, and these states also fought one another; no permanent anti-Delhi league explains the period.
- **D — Incorrect:** Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Kashmir and Ahom-Assam developed under distinct ecological and political conditions.

**MCQ 2: B**

- **A — Incorrect:** Gujarat was powerful, but neither Malwa nor Mewar remained consistently subordinate to it.
- **B — Correct:** Gujarat, Malwa and Mewar repeatedly checked one another in western and central India.
- **C — Incorrect:** Their frontiers and alignments remained contested rather than settled.
- **D — Incorrect:** Their alliances shifted; no durable anti-Delhi bloc organised the western field.

### Lesson-local Mains model

**Question:** “The rise of regional kingdoms in fifteenth-century India represented political reorganisation rather than simple decline.” Discuss in **150 words**.

**Model answer — ceiling: 150 words**

The weakening of Delhi, intensified by factional conflict and Timur's raid, opened political space; it did not empty India of state capacity. Bengal used deltaic communications, agrarian surplus and Chittagong-linked commerce to sustain autonomy. Gujarat joined a fertile hinterland to Khambhat and other ports, while Malwa's plateau and route position made it the hinge of the Gujarat-Mewar-Delhi contest. Jaunpur organised the eastern Ganga plain and rivalled the Lodis; Kashmir and Ahom-Assam followed valley-based trajectories not reducible to Delhi's provincial history.

Regional courts also built capitals, issued claims of sovereignty, patronised Persian and vernacular learning, and developed local architectural idioms at Gaur-Pandua, Ahmedabad-Champaner, Mandu and Jaunpur. Yet regionalisation was not peaceful decentralisation: warfare, faction and unequal incorporation remained common. Thus the period is best understood as a polycentric order—politically fragmented at the imperial level but institutionally and culturally generative across regions.

**Unique scoring note:** It converts the decline thesis into a causal argument, uses four named regional examples and closes with a qualification rather than romanticising fragmentation.

### Visual: Bengal's autonomy machine

| Layer | Bengal condition | Political effect |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | Long separation from Delhi | Expensive repeated campaigns |
| Delta | Rivers, marshes and changing channels | Local mobility could defeat unfamiliar armies |
| Climate | Hot and humid conditions | Added logistical strain for north-western forces |
| Multiple urban zones | Lakhnauti/Gaur, Pandua, Sonargaon and Satgaon | Power required integration rather than occupation of one city |
| External outlets | Chittagong and Bay of Bengal routes | Trade and diplomacy reduced dependence on north India |

The matrix does not make geography destiny. It shows how geography altered the cost of conquest and the organisation of rule.

### Intuition: controlling a delta is not the same as holding a road

An army may seize a capital yet fail to command dispersed waterways, forts and local supply networks. Bengal's rulers repeatedly used this gap between battlefield success and durable control.

### 1. From renewed breakaway to Ilyas Shahi consolidation

**Fact:** Bengal again broke from Delhi in 1338 during Muhammad bin Tughlaq's troubled reign. In 1342 Ilyas Khan captured Lakhnauti and Sonargaon and assumed the title Sultan Shamsuddin Ilyas Shah.

The important point is not a memorised date alone. Ilyas connected major political zones of Bengal and then expanded westward toward Tirhut, Champaran, Gorakhpur and Banaras. This expansion provoked Firuz Shah Tughlaq.

### 2. Ekdala and the limit of Delhi's victory

Firuz occupied Bengal's capital and defeated Ilyas after drawing him out from the stronghold of Ekdala. Yet Ilyas withdrew again into the fort, and Firuz did not establish permanent subordination.

**Fact:** A settlement fixed the Kosi as a boundary. Ilyas exchanged gifts with Firuz but remained independent.

**Analytical inference:** The episode separates three ideas that Prelims options often merge:

| Event | What it proves | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Delhi reaches the capital | Delhi retained expeditionary power | Delhi governed Bengal afterward |
| Bengal loses a battle | Ilyas could be defeated tactically | His dynasty lost sovereignty |
| Gifts pass between courts | Diplomatic accommodation | Vassalage by itself |

### 3. Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah: justice, diplomacy and reputation

**Fact:** Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah ruled from 1389 to 1409. Satish Chandra records a justice tradition in which he appeared before a qazi, as well as contacts with Hafiz of Shiraz and Ming China.

The justice story is useful as an ideal of kingship, not a court transcript. Its value lies in what it claims a ruler should do: submit even sovereign conduct to adjudication.

China contacts were more concrete diplomatic-commercial signals. A Chinese mission reached Bengal; a request for Buddhist monks also indicates that Buddhist communities had not vanished completely.

### 4. External relations were multi-directional

| Direction | Relationship | Historical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| West | Delhi/Firuz Tughlaq | Boundary settlement and recognition of practical autonomy |
| North-east | Kamrup/Assam | Expansionary claims met local resistance and shifting alliances |
| South-west | Odisha | Raids and border conflict |
| North | Nepal/Kathmandu direction | Raiding and prestige projection |
| East and sea | China through Chittagong | Diplomacy, shipbuilding, textiles and re-export commerce |

Bengal was therefore not simply Delhi's “remote province.” Its political horizon included eastern India, the Himalaya and the Bay of Bengal.

### 5. Objection, reply and qualification

**Objection:** Geography alone made Bengal independent.

**Reply:** Geography raised Delhi's costs, but durable independence required Ilyas Shahi coalition-building, fortified refuges, revenue and urban-commercial networks.

**Residual qualification:** The surviving narrative is court-centred. It tells us more about rulers and campaigns than about how peasants or riverine communities experienced state expansion.

### UPSC integration

- Remember **1338 → 1342 → Ilyas Shah → Kosi settlement → Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah 1389–1409**.
- Never convert gift exchange into proof of subordination.
- Link diplomacy to commercial revival: China contacts and Chittagong belong in one causal paragraph, not as disconnected trivia.
- **Answer-use line:** “Bengal's ecology did not cause sovereignty automatically; it multiplied the value of local forts, waterways and supply networks in the hands of an integrating dynasty.”

### Revision notes

1. Bengal reasserted independence in 1338.
2. Ilyas Khan took Lakhnauti and Sonargaon in 1342.
3. His regnal title was Shamsuddin Ilyas Shah.
4. Firuz's campaign won engagements but not permanent control.
5. The Kosi became the practical boundary after settlement.
6. Gifts to Delhi did not make Ilyas subordinate.
7. Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah ruled 1389–1409.
8. His justice story expresses an ideal and needs genre caution.
9. Bengal renewed relations with Ming China.
10. Chittagong linked diplomacy, shipbuilding and overseas trade.
11. Bengal's external field included Odisha, Kamrup/Assam and Nepal.

### Lesson-local Mains model

**Question:** Explain how geography and political strategy combined to sustain Bengal's independence from Delhi. Answer in **150 words**.

**Model answer — ceiling: 150 words**

Bengal's distance, humid climate and riverine communications raised the cost of repeated northern campaigns, but geography became politically effective only through organised resistance. After the 1338 breakaway, Ilyas Khan united Lakhnauti and Sonargaon in 1342 and used the fortified refuge of Ekdala against Firuz Shah Tughlaq. Firuz could occupy the capital and win a battle, yet could not impose durable administration; the subsequent Kosi boundary and gift exchange left Ilyas independent.

The dynasty also widened its options beyond Delhi. Expansion toward Kamrup, Odisha and Nepal projected regional power, while Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah's contacts with Ming China supported diplomatic prestige and overseas commerce through Chittagong. Thus Bengal's autonomy rested on a combination of difficult terrain, fortified withdrawal, local supply and revenue networks, dynastic integration and external connections. Geography constrained Delhi, but strategy converted that constraint into sovereignty.

**Unique scoring note:** It avoids environmental determinism, names Ekdala, the Kosi settlement and China contacts, and distinguishes tactical defeat from political subordination.

### Visual: Bengal's state-capacity wheel

| Spoke | Named evidence | What it enabled |
|---|---|---|
| Court and officials | Hindu officers under Alauddin Husain Shah, including Rupa and Sanatan in high service | Wider elite incorporation |
| Agrarian hinterland | Deltaic rice lands and local revenue intermediaries | Court, army and towns |
| Ports and production | Chittagong, Satgaon, textiles, silk, shipbuilding and re-export | Overseas wealth and diplomatic reach |
| Capitals | Gaur and Pandua | Royal concentration, markets and monumental patronage |
| Language | Maladhar Basu and Bengali literary patronage | Regional legitimacy beyond Persian court culture |
| Architecture | Adina Mosque, Dakhil Darwaza, brick, terracotta and curving roof forms | Visible localised sovereignty |

No spoke alone explains Bengal's strength; the wheel works through their combination.

### Intuition: legitimacy is built in offices, markets and language

A ruler does not govern only by winning battles. He must persuade nobles to serve, intermediaries to collect, merchants to trade and communities to recognise the capital. Bengal's Husain Shahi phase makes this wider machinery visible.

### 1. From interruption to Husain Shahi consolidation

The Ilyas Shahi line experienced interruptions, including the rise of Raja Ganesh and a period of unsettled court politics. This did not end Bengal's regional political structure.

**Fact:** Alauddin Husain Shah established a new dynasty in 1494 and ruled to 1519. His reign is associated with restored order, territorial ambition and a notable phase of Bengali literary patronage.

### 2. Administration: inclusion without romanticisation

Satish Chandra records Hindus in high offices under Husain Shah: the wazir, chief physician, bodyguard head and mint master are presented as Hindu office-holders; Rupa and Sanatan held important positions.

What can this evidence support?

| Safe conclusion | Unsafe conclusion |
|---|---|
| The court recruited across religious communities | Every subject enjoyed equality |
| Political competence could outweigh a narrow communal barrier | Conflict and hierarchy disappeared |
| Regional legitimacy required alliances with local elites | The state ceased to be a sultanate |

### 3. Economy: delta surplus plus maritime networks

**Fact:** Bengal exported fine textiles and possessed shipbuilding capacity. Chinese goods could be re-exported through Bengal. Chittagong connected the sultanate toward Southeast Asia and China, while wider Indian Ocean routes reached westward.

**Analytical inference:** Ports strengthened the state in three ways:

1. customs and commercial revenue;
2. access to imported goods and diplomatic exchange;
3. urban demand for artisans, transporters and brokers.

Yet commerce depended on the agrarian hinterland. Rice, silk, cotton production and river transport linked countryside to port.

### 4. Language and religious-cultural interaction

The sultans patronised Bengali as well as Persianate culture. Maladhar Basu, compiler of *Sri-Krishna-Vijaya*, received the title Gunaraja Khan. Husain Shah's period supported Bengali writers; traditions also associate the courtly environment with Chaitanya's activity and with Rupa and Sanatan.

**Qualification:** Patronage is evidence of interaction, not proof that ruler, poets and devotional communities held identical beliefs.

### 5. Architecture: local material changes the imported form

| Feature | Regional logic |
|---|---|
| Brick and mortar | Alluvial Bengal lacked abundant building stone |
| Terracotta and carved surface | Local craft adapted ornament to brick construction |
| Curvilinear or sloping roof | Translation of rain-shedding local roof forms into monumental architecture |
| Broad arches and mosque vocabulary | Participation in wider Indo-Islamic forms |
| Lotus, swan and other local motifs | Workshop continuity and local visual idiom |

Adina Mosque at Pandua belongs to an earlier Ilyas Shahi monumental phase; Dakhil Darwaza at Gaur represents the mature regional idiom. Do not attribute every surviving monument to Husain Shah.

### 6. Expansion and its limits

Husain Shah sought influence toward Assam/Kamrup, Odisha, Tipperah, Chittagong and Arakan. His most durable success lay toward Chittagong and the south-east.

**Strong qualification:** Claims of conquest in court narratives may exceed lasting control. A campaign, tribute and annexation are different political outcomes.

### 7. Comparative takeaway

Bengal's regional state combined Persianate kingship with Bengali language, local building forms and plural elite recruitment. It was neither culturally sealed nor socially uniform.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims clusters:** Husain Shah–Bengal; Chittagong–overseas trade; Gaur/Pandua–brick and curvilinear roofs.
- **Mains support:** use office-holding, literature and architecture as three different mechanisms of legitimacy.
- **Trap:** Adina Mosque is not automatically a Husain Shahi monument merely because it is in Bengal.
- **Answer-use line:** “Bengal's synthesis was institutional as much as artistic: recruitment, vernacular patronage and localised building practice joined an agrarian-maritime economy.”

### Revision notes

1. Alauddin Husain Shah ruled 1494–1519.
2. His reign restored order after court instability.
3. High Hindu office-holders show cross-community elite recruitment.
4. Inclusion at court is not evidence of universal equality.
5. Chittagong linked Bengal to eastern Indian Ocean commerce.
6. Fine textiles and re-export trade supported urban prosperity.
7. River transport connected agrarian production with ports.
8. Bengali and Persian operated in overlapping cultural fields.
9. Maladhar Basu received the title Gunaraja Khan.
10. Gaur and Pandua were royal-urban centres.
11. Brick, terracotta and curving roofs mark Bengal's local architecture.
12. Expansion toward Assam, Odisha and Arakan must be assessed campaign by campaign.

Adina Mosque and Dakhil Darwaza show distinct phases of this evolving idiom.

Thus economy financed administration and culture, while recruitment and patronage converted resources into legitimacy. The outcome was not a fusion without tension, but a regionally rooted sultanate able to speak through several social and cultural registers.

## Lesson 4 — Gujarat I: Muzaffarids, Ahmad Shah and the making of Ahmedabad

Progress: 4/12 | Stage: Core | Subtopic: Independence, administration, capital formation and political complexity

CA search: "Historic City of Ahmedabad UNESCO heritage management 2025 2026"

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Visual: from provincial governorship to capital-centred state

| Political move | Actor | Administrative meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Assertion of autonomy | Zafar Khan, later Muzaffar Shah | Provincial power becomes dynastic sovereignty |
| Formal independence, c. 1407 | Muzaffar Shah | Separation from Delhi becomes explicit |
| Noble control and settlement | Ahmad Shah I, 1411–42 | Court authority becomes more regular |
| Capital shift | Patan to Ahmedabad, founded c. 1411–13 in the source tradition | New administrative, commercial and symbolic centre |
| Frontier campaigns | Saurashtra and Gujarat-Rajasthan border | Tribute, forts and political bargaining |
| Monumental patronage | Jama Masjid and Tin Darwaza | Local craft becomes a language of sovereignty |

### Intuition: a new capital is an administrative argument

Founding a capital says: power will be concentrated here, markets will gather here, roads will point here and the dynasty's memory will be built here. Ahmedabad was therefore more than a royal residence.

### 1. Muzaffarid beginnings

Zafar Khan, a governor under the late Tughlaqs, became Muzaffar Shah and formally asserted independence around 1407. The dynastic transition shows how provincial command, local resources and weak imperial supervision could combine.

**Qualification:** “Founder” varies by criterion. Muzaffar Shah founded the independent line; Satish Chandra calls Ahmad Shah I the real founder in the sense of consolidating administration and territorial power.

### 2. Ahmad Shah I: consolidation before expansion

Ahmad Shah I ruled from 1411 to 1442. His achievements included:

1. bringing nobles under tighter control;
2. settling administration;
3. expanding and consolidating the kingdom;
4. shifting the capital from Patan to Ahmedabad;
5. building palaces, bazaars, mosques and madrasas.

The sequence matters: expansion without a manageable nobility and administrative centre would not have produced a durable state.

### 3. Ahmedabad as a state-making instrument

| Function | How the capital served it |
|---|---|
| Political | Concentrated court and noble supervision |
| Fiscal | Connected fertile hinterland, crafts and markets |
| Strategic | Supported operations toward Saurashtra, Rajasthan and Malwa |
| Cultural | Displayed a distinct regional building style |
| Social | Brought merchants, artisans, scholars and officials into one urban field |

### 4. Religious policy must be described event by event

Ahmad Shah attacked temples at Sidhpur and imposed jizyah/peshkash in his political field. Yet he also employed Hindus in government, including commercial groups represented in the narrative by Manik Chand and Motichand.

The correct method is not to choose one label and suppress the other evidence.

| Evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Temple destruction during campaigns | Coercive and religious-political action |
| Jizyah and tribute | Hierarchical fiscal sovereignty |
| Hindu office-holding | Administrative pragmatism and elite incorporation |
| Warfare against Muslim Malwa, Khandesh and Deccan rulers | Conflict cannot be reduced to a Hindu-Muslim binary |

### 5. Architecture: local craft, not a copy of Delhi

Gujarat's builders drew on established Jain and regional stone-working traditions. Slender turrets, fine carving, ornate brackets and screens interacted with mosque plans and Islamic patronage.

**Analytical inference:** The decisive process was a shared building industry adapting patronal requirements. Calling the result either purely imported or purely indigenous obscures the workshop.

### UPSC integration

- Distinguish **Muzaffar Shah = independent dynasty** from **Ahmad Shah I = consolidator and Ahmedabad**.
- Use the date as **c. 1411–13** where source traditions differ on the exact foundation year.
- Avoid the “tolerant/intolerant” single-word ruler label.
- **Answer-use line:** “Ahmedabad converted Gujarat's commercial geography into a supervised political centre and its local craft economy into dynastic representation.”

2. Formal Gujarat independence is placed around 1407.
3. Ahmad Shah I ruled 1411–42.
4. Muzaffar founded the line; Ahmad consolidated the state.
5. Ahmedabad's foundation is safely expressed as c. 1411–13.
6. The capital linked administration, markets and strategy.
7. Ahmad Shah campaigned in Saurashtra and Rajasthan border zones.

**Question:** Examine the role of Ahmad Shah I in transforming Gujarat from an autonomous province into a consolidated regional kingdom. Answer in **150 words**.

**Model answer — ceiling: 150 words**

He disciplined the nobility, regularised administration and expanded toward Saurashtra and the Gujarat-Rajasthan frontier. The new capital concentrated the court, markets, craft production, mosques and madrasas, linking a fertile hinterland to Gujarat's commercial routes.

His rule also resists a single religious label: temple destruction and jizyah coexisted with Hindu participation in administration and wars against Muslim neighbours.

Thus Ahmad Shah's significance lay in joining political control, urbanisation and regional cultural expression. His achievement was consolidation, not merely conquest.

### Visual: Gujarat's port-hinterland-strategy triangle

The triangle explains why forts, roads, ports and diplomacy belonged to one political strategy.

### Intuition: a rich coastline creates both capacity and vulnerability

Ports give rulers revenue and connections, but they also attract competitors who can attack shipping without conquering the hinterland. Gujarat's encounter with the Portuguese therefore introduced a naval problem into a land-based regional balance.

### 1. Mahmud Begarha's long reign

Mahmud Begarha ruled from 1459 to 1511. His sobriquet is conventionally connected with his capture of two major forts: Girnar and Champaner, although an alternative moustache tradition exists.

**Exam-safe use:** connect Begarha to **Girnar/Junagadh + Champaner**, not to an unqualified folklore explanation of the name.

### 2. Girnar and the Saurashtra problem

Saurashtra was fertile and commercially valuable, but the source also describes robbery and piracy affecting traders. Girnar offered a strong administrative and military base. After conquest, Begarha established Mustafabad near the fort.

The campaign illustrates:

| Immediate aim | Larger function |
|---|---|
| Defeat a resistant fort | Integrate Saurashtra more closely |
| Address insecurity affecting trade | Protect commercial routes |
| Establish Mustafabad | Convert conquest into administration |
| Use a forward base | Watch Sindh and the coast |

### 3. Champaner: fortress, route and capital

Champaner stood on a strategic route toward Malwa and Khandesh. After a hard siege, Mahmud established Muhammadabad-Champaner and made it a principal residence.

**Official heritage evidence:** UNESCO describes Champaner-Pavagadh as a short-lived regional capital containing fortifications, palaces, religious buildings, residential areas and water-retaining installations. Its Jama Masjid embodies an important regional blend of local and Islamic building practice.

### 4. Commerce and internal order

Satish Chandra associates Begarha's long reign with prosperous trade, safe roads, caravanserais and inns. These are not decorative welfare details: they reduce transaction risk and support merchants, pilgrims, officials and armies.

### 5. Portuguese pressure: keep two rulers separate

| Ruler | Secure association |
|---|---|
| Mahmud Begarha | Resisted Portuguese interference; cooperated with the ruler of Egypt against Portuguese naval power, unsuccessfully |
| Bahadur Shah, 1526–37 | Under pressure from Humayun, made concessions at Bassein and in the Diu context; later died during a confrontation aboard a Portuguese vessel |

**Trap:** Do not attribute the surrender/concession at Diu to Begarha. The 2023 UPSC question directly tests this separation.

### 6. Gujarat in the wider regional system

Begarha's Gujarat was powerful enough to remain a major commercial state. Yet attempts under Bahadur Shah to dominate Malwa and Rajasthan drew Gujarat into conflict with the Mughals. Sea pressure and land pressure converged.

**Analytical inference:** Gujarat's prosperity increased the stakes of both regional conquest and Portuguese intervention.

### Visual: the Malwa hinge

| Route or neighbour | Why Malwa mattered |
|---|---|
| Gujarat to north India | Controlled transit between ports and inland markets |
| North India to Deccan | Plateau routes connected northern and southern arenas |
| Mewar to central India | Rival claimants and frontier forts made Malwa a contested buffer |
| Delhi/Lodis toward west-central India | Control of Malwa could widen imperial reach |
| Bahmani sphere toward north | Berar and Deccan connections drew Malwa into wider contests |

Mandu's height gave defence; Malwa's location gave connectivity. The combination made it a prize.

### Intuition: a hinge is valuable because several doors move through it

Malwa was not powerful merely because Mandu was beautiful or fortified. Whoever influenced Malwa could affect roads between Gujarat's ports, Rajasthan, the Ganga plain and the Deccan.

### 1. Dynastic sequence

| Phase | Marker |
|---|---|
| Dilawar Khan Ghuri | Late-Tughlaq governor who established autonomy |
| Hushang Shah Ghuri | Consolidated Malwa and developed Mandu |
| Khalji dynasty of Malwa | Replaced the Ghuri line |
| Mahmud Khalji, 1436–69 | Most expansionist ruler; fought Gujarat, Mewar, Gondwana, Odisha, Bahmanis and Delhi |

### 2. Mandu as a political landscape

The capital shifted from Dhar to Mandu. Its plateau position, cliffs and access routes made it defensible. Monumental construction concentrated the court and displayed power.

| Monument | Safe association | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Jama Masjid | Malwa's monumental congregational architecture | Do not confuse with Ahmedabad or Champaner Jama Masjids |
| Hushang Shah's tomb | Early Malwa mausoleum tradition | Later admiration does not prove direct copying of every feature |
| Hindola Mahal | Distinctive audience-hall form | Name does not mean the building literally moved |
| Jahaz Mahal | Later Mandu palace in the wider complex | It belongs to a different construction phase from Hushang Shah |

### 3. Architecture and environment

Mandu architecture is described as massive, often raised on lofty plinths, with coloured and glazed tiles. Large openings, water systems and the plateau setting served climate, defence and courtly display.

**Qualification:** “Malwa style” is a useful family resemblance, not proof that every building was made in one reign or by one unchanged workshop.

### 4. Internal faction and external intervention

Malwa's nobles and succession conflicts gave Gujarat and Mewar opportunities to intervene. Muzaffar Shah of Gujarat once imprisoned Hushang Shah but later restored him because direct control was difficult.

Mahmud Khalji fought Rana Kumbha repeatedly. Temple destruction occurred in warfare, but Satish Chandra cautions against automatically treating every act as evidence of a continuous general policy.

### 5. The Gujarat-Mewar-Malwa triangle

| Actor | Aim in Malwa | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Gujarat | Prevent hostile control of routes and install influence | Could not permanently absorb Malwa |
| Mewar | Expand eastward and protect Rajasthan interests | Faced Gujarat and Malwa simultaneously |
| Malwa | Preserve autonomy and exploit rivalries | Internal faction weakened bargaining power |

By the early sixteenth century, conflict involving Mahmud II, Medini Rai and Rana Sanga further fragmented Malwa. This drew Lodi attention and helped produce the competitive field into which Babur entered.

### 6. Objection and reply

**Objection:** Malwa's importance was purely geographical.

**Reply:** Geography created opportunity, but forts, nobles, revenue extraction, capital building and alliances determined whether that opportunity became state power.

### UPSC integration

- **Prelims cluster:** Dilawar Khan → Hushang Shah → Malwa Khaljis; Mandu → Jama Masjid/Hindola Mahal/Jahaz Mahal.
- **Mains support:** Malwa is the best example for geography operating through routes and political competition.
- **Trap:** do not place every Mandu building under Hushang Shah.
- **Answer-use line:** “Malwa's plateau was a strategic multiplier, not an autonomous cause: faction could turn the same hinge from a barrier into an invitation to intervention.”

The Ahom state mobilised households and adult male service through arrangements that differed from the iqta-jagir world. Similar functions—labour, military service, revenue—do not imply identical institutions.

### 1. Sukapha and the early polity

The traditional date for Sukapha's entry into the Brahmaputra valley is **1228**. Tai-Ahom groups entered through the Patkai region and established an early centre at Charaideo.

The polity expanded through:

- alliance and conflict with existing groups;
- incorporation rather than simple ethnic isolation;
- wet-rice cultivation and control of productive landscapes;
- adaptation to river-valley conditions;
- gradual construction of a wider Ahom identity.

**Qualification:** Later mature institutions should not be projected in fully developed form onto Sukapha's first generation.

### 2. The paik system

A **paik** was an adult male subject liable for rotational state service. Service could include military duty, public works and productive labour.

| Misreading | Correction |
|---|---|
| Paik means chattel slave | Paiks were service-bearing subjects organised through households |
| Every paik served continuously | Rotation allowed some members to serve while others sustained production |
| The system was unchanged from 1228 onward | It developed and was reorganised over time |
| Paik was identical to Delhi's iqta | One mobilised service obligations; the other centred on revenue assignments and military administration |

### 3. The khel system

**Khel** refers to functional or occupational-administrative groupings through which labour and specialised service could be organised in the mature Ahom state.

The safest relationship is:

| Term | Core function |
|---|---|
| Paik | Individual/household service obligation |
| Khel | Organisational grouping of service and occupation |

Avoid giving one rigid, timeless numerical formula unless the source and period are specified.

### 4. Buranjis as evidence

**Buranjis** are Ahom court chronicles. They are indispensable for ruler sequence, diplomacy, conflict and administration.

They are not neutral:

- surviving versions may be copied or compiled later;
- court priorities shape selection;
- elite political events receive greater attention;
- institutional terms can change across time.

### 5. Moidams and the strict boundary

UNESCO describes the Moidams at Charaideo as Tai-Ahom royal mound burials forming a sacred necropolis over roughly six centuries, from the thirteenth to nineteenth centuries. The 2026 Prelims question tests their function.

For this topic, remember only:

1. Charaideo was an early Ahom capital and royal necropolis.
2. Moidams were royal burial grounds, not forts, recreation centres or drill grounds.
3. Buranjis help explain funerary practice and labour.
4. The detailed architecture and UNESCO criteria belong to Art and Culture.

### 6. Bengal-Assam connection without subordination

Bengal rulers sought influence in Kamrup and Assam, but climate, geography, local resistance and Ahom involvement limited their reach. Temporary occupation or tribute did not mean the whole Brahmaputra valley became a stable Bengal province.

### Visual: four questions to identify a regional style

| Diagnostic question | Bengal | Gujarat | Malwa | Jaunpur |

### Intuition: architecture is a negotiation between patron, material and craft

A ruler may request a mosque or palace using a transregional vocabulary. The actual building is produced by local stone, brick, climate, engineering knowledge and workshops. Regional style is the result of that negotiation.

### 1. Bengal: translating roofs into masonry

Bengal's alluvial environment favoured brick. Builders used terracotta, broad arches, curved cornices and roof shapes related to local rain-shedding forms. Local motifs appeared within Islamic monuments.

**Limit:** Climate helps explain form, but patronage and craft transmission are still necessary.

### 2. Gujarat: stone carving and urban refinement

Gujarat drew heavily on sophisticated Jain and regional stone-working traditions. Slender supports and turrets, ornate brackets, screens and fine carving were integrated into mosque and tomb architecture.

At Champaner, the urban ensemble also includes fortifications, water-retaining structures, palaces and living sacred spaces. Architecture therefore records a capital system, not isolated monuments.

### 3. Malwa: mass, height and landscape

Mandu's buildings use massive forms and high plinths. Coloured and glazed tiles introduced surface variation. Water structures and openings responded to the plateau environment and courtly needs.

The style should not be reduced to “heavy”: monument, function and phase differ.

### 4. Jaunpur: the facade as a political statement

Jaunpur's Sharqi architecture is marked by lofty screen-like entrances and huge arches. The absence of prominent minarets distinguishes it from a generic mosque stereotype.

**Analytical inference:** In a kingdom contesting Delhi, an independent facade language could make cultural autonomy visible.

### 5. Shared Indo-Islamic process

| Shared element | Regional transformation |
|---|---|
| Arch and mosque plan | Adjusted to local material and engineering |
| Persianate inscription and court patronage | Combined with regional visual motifs |
| Congregational and royal functions | Embedded in different urban systems |
| Indigenous craftsmen | Carried trabeate habits, carving skills and local forms into new commissions |

### 6. Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Calling these “regional styles” exaggerates difference because all use Indo-Islamic forms.

**Reply:** Shared religious and structural vocabulary is real, but material, facade, roof, ornament, water design and urban context vary systematically. Regional does not mean isolated.

**Residual:** Surviving monuments are an elite sample. Ordinary houses, workshops and market structures survive unevenly.

### UPSC integration

- Use **feature + cause + monument**, never a bare monument list.
- Avoid assigning all Mandu or Bengal buildings to one ruler.
- Avoid calling local idioms either copied Delhi forms or fixed communal styles.
- **Answer-use line:** “Regional architecture was not the provincial dilution of Delhi; it was the local re-engineering of a shared building vocabulary.”

### Revision notes

1. Bengal's alluvium favoured brick and terracotta.
2. Curving roofs connected masonry to local rain-adapted forms.
3. Gujarat's style drew on accomplished Jain and regional stone craft.
4. Ahmedabad and Champaner must be distinguished by ruler and phase.
5. Champaner was a complete capital landscape, not only a mosque site.
6. Mandu used mass, high plinths and coloured or glazed surfaces.
7. Mandu monuments accumulated across multiple patronage phases.
8. Jaunpur emphasised monumental screen facades and huge arches.

### Visual: six different state-capacity profiles

| State | Agrarian or service base | Commercial/route base | Capital idiom | Principal constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bengal | Delta agriculture and local intermediaries | Rivers, textiles, Chittagong | Gaur-Pandua brick regionalism | Odisha, Jaunpur/Lodis and frontier resistance |
| Gujarat | Fertile hinterland and local chiefs | Ports, crafts, merchant capital | Ahmedabad and Champaner stone craft | Malwa-Mewar rivalry, Portuguese and Mughals |
| Malwa | Plateau hinterland, forts and tribute | North-south and port-interior routes | Mandu's massive landscape | Noble faction and intervention |
| Jaunpur | Ganga-valley revenue and towns | Inland eastern routes | Sharqi monumental facade | Lodi military recovery |
| Kashmir | Valley agriculture and crafts | Mountain and Central Asian links | Multilingual court and craft patronage | Internal religious-political fracture |
| Ahom-Assam | Wet-rice expansion and paik service | Brahmaputra corridors | Charaideo political-sacred centre | Frontier contest and evolving incorporation |

### Intuition: do not search for one “regional kingdom model”

The states resemble one another because all mobilised resources, built authority and negotiated elites. They differ in the resources, institutions and geographies through which they did so.

### 1. Agrarian change

Regional courts depended on countryside surplus. Expansion involved cultivation, revenue claims, irrigation or water management, landed intermediaries and the incorporation of local chiefs.

- Bengal's deltaic expansion connected rice lands to river towns.
- Gujarat's fertile interior supported ports and capitals.
- Malwa's plateau hinterland sustained forts and route control.
- Kashmir's agrarian revival formed part of Zain-ul-Abidin's repair.
- Ahom expansion interacted with wet-rice cultivation and household service.

**Qualification:** Court sources rarely provide an even social history of cultivators.

### 2. Commercial and urban change

| Commercial pattern | Best examples |
|---|---|
| Maritime export and re-export | Bengal and Gujarat |
| Merchant-rich capital and craft concentration | Ahmedabad, Gaur/Pandua |
| Transit-route taxation and security | Malwa and Jaunpur |
| Specialist craft transfer | Kashmir |
| Service mobilisation linked to production | Ahom-Assam |

Urbanisation did not require a modern industrial economy. Capitals, ports, garrison towns, pilgrimage centres and craft settlements could all create specialised demand.

### 3. Cultural change

Persian remained important in courts and administration, while Bengali, Assamese, Sanskrit, Kashmiri and regional literary forms developed. Translation, titles and patronage linked language to rule.

Architecture likewise combined:

1. transregional Islamic patronal forms;
2. local material;
3. inherited trabeate and carving practices;
4. climate adaptation;
5. political competition among capitals.

### 4. Religious policy was neither uniform nor irrelevant

Regional rulers used accommodation, coercion, patronage and fiscal distinction in different combinations. Ahmad Shah's record includes temple destruction and Hindu office-holding. Husain Shah's inclusion did not eliminate conflict. Sikandar's coercive memory is followed by Zain-ul-Abidin's restoration.

The historian's task is to specify ruler, act, context, source and limit.

### 5. From regional balance to Mughal expansion

The balance began to shift before Babur:

- Lodis annexed Jaunpur in 1484.
- Malwa fragmented under faction and Mewar intervention.
- Rana Sanga pressed toward the Agra region.
- Gujarat expanded into Malwa and later confronted Humayun.
- Bengal remained a powerful eastern state.

Babur did not invade a vacuum. The Mughals later expanded by defeating, allying with, absorbing or adapting these regional networks. Regional capitals, officials, routes and elites became part of the resources over which Mughal power was constructed.

### 6. Decline and absorption were multi-causal

| Cause family | Illustration |
|---|---|
| Succession and faction | Malwa |
| Stronger neighbour | Lodi annexation of Jaunpur |
| Compounded external pressure | Gujarat between Humayun and Portuguese power |
| New imperial military project | Mughal expansion |
| Route and resource competition | Gujarat-Malwa-Mewar |
| Internal legitimacy repair or failure | Kashmir's contrasting reigns |

### 7. Final qualification

Regional states were not democratic alternatives to empire. They taxed, fought, coerced and represented elite interests. Their significance lies in showing that political plurality could coexist with institutional innovation, commercial expansion and cultural production.

- For Mughal transition, avoid inevitability: regional fragmentation created opportunity, but conquest still required organisation and strategy.
- **Answer-use line:** “Mughal expansion did not replace disorder with order; it reorganised an already productive but competitive landscape of regional states.”

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks | 150 words

**Question:** How did capital cities contribute to regional state formation in fifteenth-century India?

**Directive decode:** “How” requires mechanism, not a list of capitals.

**Model answer — ceiling: 150 words**

Regional capitals concentrated the institutions that converted autonomy into durable rule. Ahmad Shah I's Ahmedabad joined court, markets, crafts, mosques and hinterland-port routes. Mahmud Begarha's Champaner added fortification, water systems and routes toward Malwa and Khandesh. Mandu's plateau gave Malwa defence and control of north-south transit, while monuments displayed dynastic authority. Gaur and Pandua connected Bengal's riverine agrarian base with administration and brick-built regional architecture. Jaunpur similarly turned an eastern command into a learned court and a distinct monumental style.

Capitals therefore did more than house rulers: they gathered revenue, officials, merchants, artisans and symbols of legitimacy. Yet a capital could not guarantee survival. Jaunpur fell to the Lodis and Malwa suffered factional intervention. Capital-building strengthened regional states only when supported by wider territorial and elite networks.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains five mechanisms with named capitals and uses political failure to qualify architectural evidence.

**Unique scoring note:** Award the top band only if the answer links urban form to administration and circulation; a monument catalogue should remain mid-band.

## 15 marks | 250 words

**Question:** “The political economy of Bengal and Gujarat rested on different geographies but comparable connections between hinterland and trade.” Analyse.

**Directive decode:** “Analyse” requires components, causal links, comparison and limits.

**Model answer — ceiling: 250 words**

Bengal and Gujarat were both commercially dynamic regional states, but they connected hinterland and trade through different geographies. Bengal's delta supplied rice, silk and textile production. Rivers linked dispersed agrarian zones to Gaur, Pandua, Satgaon and Chittagong. Overseas contacts with China and the re-export of Chinese goods widened the commercial field, while the difficult delta also raised the cost of Delhi's intervention.

Gujarat's strength lay in a fertile western hinterland, accomplished handicrafts, merchant capital and ports such as Khambhat. Ahmad Shah's Ahmedabad concentrated administration and markets; Mahmud Begarha's conquest of Girnar integrated Saurashtra, while Champaner controlled inland routes toward Malwa and Khandesh. Safe roads and caravanserais connected port wealth with territorial rule.

The comparison is therefore structural: both states turned agricultural and craft production into urban and military capacity through transport networks. Their vulnerabilities differed. Bengal faced riverine frontier contests with Odisha, Jaunpur and Assam; Gujarat faced the western land balance and, increasingly, Portuguese naval interference. Begarha's resistance and Bahadur Shah's later concessions show how maritime wealth invited an external sea power.

Thus hinterland-trade integration strengthened both kingdoms, but deltaic and Arabian Sea settings produced distinct strategic problems.

**Why this earns marks:** It sustains comparison through the same variables and ends by showing how geography changed vulnerability.

**Unique scoring note:** Full marks require both similarity and difference; answers discussing the two regions in isolated halves should lose comparison marks.

## 20 marks | 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate the proposition that fifteenth-century regional kingdoms were “laboratories of political and cultural adaptation.”

**Directive decode:** “Evaluate” requires a criterion, supporting evidence, counter-evidence and a graded verdict.

**Model answer — ceiling: 250 words**

The proposition is persuasive if “laboratory” means selective adaptation rather than peaceful experimentation. Politically, regional states combined inherited Sultanate practices with local resources. Bengal used delta forts, waterways and cross-community officials; Gujarat built a capital-port-hinterland system; Malwa converted Mandu's plateau into a route fortress; Jaunpur turned an eastern command into sovereignty; Kashmir under Zain-ul-Abidin used reconciliation and craft patronage to rebuild the valley; Ahom-Assam developed a distinct paik-service order outside Delhi's provincial lineage.

Culturally, adaptation is clearest in architecture. Bengal translated local roof forms into brick mosques; Gujarat integrated Jain-regional stone craft; Malwa used massive high-plinth forms; Jaunpur monumentalised the screen facade. Courts also supported Bengali literature, Kashmir's Sanskrit-Persian translations and Jaunpur's learned reputation.

However, adaptation should not be romanticised. Court inclusion coexisted with coercion; monumental evidence privileges elites; Malwa's faction and Jaunpur's exhaustion reveal institutional weakness. Regional rivalry also produced warfare and temple destruction. Nor were the states uniform: ports mattered more in Bengal and Gujarat, while valley ecology and service organisation distinguished Kashmir and Ahom-Assam.

Overall, the regional century widened India's repertoire of governance and cultural expression, but through competitive, hierarchical states rather than inclusive modern experiments.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the metaphor, tests it across six cases, includes counter-evidence and offers a qualified verdict.

**Unique scoring note:** The highest band requires at least one administrative adaptation and one cultural adaptation plus an explicit warning against homogenisation.

## B. Why regional states emerged

- Delhi's weakening released provincial resources but did not itself create viable states.
- Durable autonomy required land, ports or routes; military-revenue organisation; capitals; intermediaries; and legitimacy.
- Timur was a catalyst, not the sole cause.
- Regionalisation was polycentric state formation, not anarchy.
- Balance of power meant mutual checking among unequal and changing actors.

## C. Bengal recall card

- Geography: delta, rivers, climate and long communications.
- Ilyas Shah: 1342 consolidation; Ekdala; Kosi settlement; independent despite gifts.
- Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah: justice ideal, Hafiz and China.
- Husain Shah: 1494–1519; order, expansion and Hindu high officials.
- Economy: rice, silk, textiles, shipbuilding, Chittagong and re-export.
- Culture: Bengali patronage; Maladhar Basu/Gunaraja Khan.
- Architecture: Gaur-Pandua; brick, terracotta, broad arches, curved roof and local motifs.
- Limits: Odisha, Jaunpur/Lodis, Kamrup-Assam and Arakan frontiers.

## D. Gujarat recall card

- Muzaffar Shah: independent line, c. 1407.
- Ahmad Shah I: consolidation; Ahmedabad; noble control and administration.
- Architecture: local Jain-regional stone craft, brackets, screens and slender turrets.
- Begarha: 1459–1511; Girnar/Junagadh and Champaner.
- Political economy: fertile hinterland, crafts, ports, merchants, safe roads and inns.
- Portuguese distinction: Begarha resisted; Bahadur Shah later conceded in the Bassein-Diu context under Humayun's pressure.
- Heritage anchor: Champaner-Pavagadh as a fortified pre-Mughal capital with water systems and mixed building traditions.

## E. Malwa recall card

- Dilawar Khan → Hushang Shah → Malwa Khaljis.
- Mahmud Khalji: 1436–69, expansionist and deeply involved in Mewar conflict.
- Mandu: defensible plateau and route hinge.
- Architecture: massive forms, lofty plinths, coloured/glazed surfaces.
- Monuments: Jama Masjid, Hushang Shah's tomb, Hindola Mahal and Jahaz Mahal.
- Politics: faction invited Gujarat and Mewar intervention.
- Later: Medini Rai, Mahmud II and Rana Sanga accelerate fragmentation.

## F. Jaunpur recall card

- Malik Sarwar received title Malik-us-Sharq.
- Sharqi name derives from the title, not ethnicity.
- Major eastern Ganga-valley state; rival of Delhi and check on Bengal.
- Bahlul Lodi annexed it in 1484.
- “Shiraz of the East” is a qualified learned-reputation label.
- Atala Mosque; lofty screen facade and huge arch; prominent minarets not defining.
- Political defeat did not erase cultural legacy.

## G. Kashmir recall card

- Shah Mir founded the sultanate.
- Sikandar's coercive record requires chronicle caution.
- Zain-ul-Abidin/Bud Shah: 1420–70.
- Reversal: return, reversion, restored grants/libraries/temples, jizyah abolition.
- Administration: Sriya Bhatt and wider elite incorporation.
- Crafts: paper, shawls, stone work, bottle-making and gold-beating.
- Translation: *Mahabharata*, *Rajatarangini*, medicine and music into Persian.
- Rishi-Sufi field: interaction without doctrinal sameness.

## H. Ahom-Assam recall card

- Sukapha, traditional 1228; Charaideo early centre.
- Formation through incorporation, conflict, alliance and wet-rice expansion.
- Paik: rotational labour-military service of adult male subjects.
- Khel: functional/occupational organisation in the mature state.
- Buranji: court chronicle; valuable and biased.
- Moidam: royal burial ground; full architecture and UNESCO criteria belong to Art and Culture.
- Do not project later mature institutions unchanged into the thirteenth century.

## J. Economy, society and culture

- Agrarian surplus remained foundational even in commercial states.
- Bengal and Gujarat connected countryside production to maritime trade.
- Malwa and Jaunpur monetised inland routes and towns.
- Capitals concentrated officials, merchants, scholars and artisans.
- Persian interacted with Bengali, Assamese, Sanskrit, Kashmiri and other regional languages.
- Cross-community office-holding was politically important but socially limited.
- Religious accommodation and coercion could coexist.
- Architecture shows localisation, not two sealed civilisational styles.

- Court chronicle: sequence and ideology, not neutral society.
- Coin: sovereignty and mint, not a border map.
- Inscription: local act and title, not total practice.

- Traveller: routes and goods, not complete economy.

- Famous labels such as “Shiraz of the East” require explanation and restraint.
- A campaign, tribute, occupation and annexation are distinct.
