# The Deccan and the Mughals to 1657 - Complete Learner-First Live Edition

## Learning roadmap

The central problem is not simply why the Mughals marched south. It is why repeated victories, fort captures and treaties did not produce equally durable control. The learning sequence therefore moves from the political field to the mechanisms of conquest, resistance, accommodation and fiscal strain.

| Lesson | Learning dependency | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Deccan political geography, successor states, factions and control vocabulary | Foundation |
| 2 | Why Akbar moved south: Khandesh, routes, sovereignty and diplomacy | Foundation |
| 3 | Chand Bibi, Berar, Ahmadnagar, Asirgarh and the limits of Akbar's gains | Core |
| 4 | Malik Ambar's coalition and mobile-war mechanism | Core |
| 5 | Revenue reconstruction, forts, Khirki and the limits of attribution | Core |
| 6 | Jahangir, Khan-i-Khanan, Prince Khurram and limited settlements | Core |
| 7 | Maratha service networks and Shahji before a Maratha state | Core |
| 8 | Shah Jahan, Daulatabad and the 1636 Deccan settlement | Core |
| 9 | Provincial incorporation, jagirs, revenue and effective control | Advanced |

| 11 | Bijapur in 1657, southern resources and the collapse of the 1636 compact | Advanced |
| 12 | Sources, historiography, consequences and the 1657 boundary | Final synthesis |

The roadmap uses twelve lessons because four dependencies must remain distinct: first capture versus durable control, dynastic survival versus territorial possession, suzerainty versus annexation, and pre-1657 service politics versus the later Maratha-Mughal war.

## Visual first: one region, several overlapping political layers

```text
NORTH-WEST GATEWAY
Khandesh - Burhanpur - Asirgarh
          |
          v
NORTHERN DECCAN
Berar - Balaghat - Ahmadnagar - Daulatabad/Khirki
          |
     -----+----------------
     |                     |
BIJAPUR              GOLCONDA
western routes       Telangana/eastern routes
     |                     |
Konkan/horse trade   Karnataka/Coromandel trade
          \             /
       post-Vijayanagara field
```

The Deccan was not one state and not an isolated "South." It was a connected but difficult political field in which routes, forts, mobile troops, local landed interests and rival courts mattered together.

### 1. From Bahmani breakup to a competitive state system

The Bahmani kingdom fragmented into the Nizam Shahis of **Ahmadnagar**, Adil Shahis of **Bijapur**, Qutb Shahis of **Golconda**, Imad Shahis of **Berar** and Barid Shahis of **Bidar**. By the late sixteenth century, Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda were the principal powers. Ahmadnagar absorbed Berar in 1573; Bidar survived as a smaller contested court.

**Khandesh** requires separate treatment. Its Faruqi rulers were not Bahmani successors. Its location between the Narmada and Tapti, its capital Burhanpur and the fort of Asirgarh made it the Mughal gateway to the plateau and to the Agra-Surat commercial route.

### 2. Vijayanagara after 1565

The coalition victory near Talikota/Bannihatti in 1565 and the sack of Vijayanagara did not erase the empire or all southern authority. An Aravidu/Rayal line and Nayaka principalities remained. Their survival matters because Bijapur and Golconda later expanded into Karnataka and the Coromandel through a field of nominal allegiance, local forts and competing chiefs.

### 3. Rivalries were structural, not accidental

| Dispute | Why it mattered |
|---|---|
| Ahmadnagar-Bijapur over Sholapur | fertile territory and political prestige |
| Ahmadnagar ambitions in Berar | northern expansion and Mughal proximity |
| Bijapur-Golconda over Naldurg and later Karnataka | frontier, booty and southern expansion |
| Claims over Bidar | Bahmani succession prestige |
| Gujarat/Konkan interests | inland-maritime connection |

Marriage alliances could accompany war without ending rivalry. A treaty usually managed a balance temporarily; it did not create a permanent bloc.

### 4. Factions without communal flattening

**Deccani**, **afaqi/gharib**, **Habshi** and **Afghan** described shifting service and patronage networks. Many afaqis came from Iran or Khurasan; Habshis were of African origin; Deccanis included locally established groups. Sunni-Shia and Mahdawi conflicts sometimes overlapped with office competition, but sect did not predetermine every alliance.

Maratha families such as the Mores, Nimbalkars, Ghatges and Bhonsales served different courts. Deccani Brahmans worked in revenue administration and diplomacy. These actors were neither politically irrelevant subjects nor one united national force.

### 5. The vocabulary that prevents map-based errors

| Term | Exam-safe meaning |
|---|---|
| alliance | cooperation for a purpose without permanent subordination |
| tribute or indemnity | regular or one-time payment, not direct administration |
| suzerainty | superior status acknowledged while a subordinate court survives |
| khutba and sikka | symbolic claims through sermon and coinage; not proof of full control |
| occupation | possession of a fort or tract, possibly without secure countryside |
| annexation | direct imperial claim and administrative incorporation |
| durable control | reliable revenue, supply, local cooperation, garrisons and order |

The key analytical rule is:

```text
fort captured + ruler submits + tribute promised
                  does not necessarily equal
revenue collected + countryside governed + allegiance sustained
```

### 6. Connected but frictional

Merchants, pilgrims, Sufis, soldiers and job-seeking elites crossed the Vindhyas long before Akbar. Tughlaq use of Daulatabad and Gujarat-Malwa links intensified contact. Yet long communications, monsoon conditions, river crossings, fodder, plateau edges and fortified nodes made permanent occupation expensive.

**MCQ 3: C**

- **B — Incorrect:** A subordinate dynasty could survive after accepting the emperor's name in public forms.

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain why the Deccan should be understood as a connected but frictional political field rather than a simple Mughal-versus-southern-states frontier.

**Model answer:**
The late sixteenth-century Deccan was connected to northern India by commerce, pilgrimage, migration and earlier political movement, yet it remained difficult to dominate. Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda competed over Sholapur, Bidar, Berar and Naldurg; Khandesh separately controlled the Burhanpur-Asirgarh gateway. Post-Vijayanagara rulers and Nayaka principalities added a southern field of expansion.

Politics operated through changing Deccani, afaqi, Habshi, Afghan, Maratha and Brahman service networks rather than two religious blocs. Marriage, tribute, fort possession and alliance regularly crossed confessional lines.

At the same time, plateau geography, monsoon conditions, long supply routes, fodder requirements and fortified corridors imposed friction. A conqueror could hold a fort while local chiefs denied intelligence, supplies or revenue. Khutba, tribute or suzerainty therefore did not equal provincial administration.

The Deccan was consequently accessible enough to invite Mughal intervention but fragmented and logistically demanding enough to resist durable consolidation. Its history must be written through routes, resources, forts and negotiated allegiance, not a coloured map of uninterrupted imperial advance.

**Scoring note:** Require the state system, Khandesh gateway, cross-regional connections, fluid service groups, two logistical constraints and the control-versus-claim distinction.

## Visual first: pressure accumulated before invasion

```text
Malwa and Gujarat consolidation
          +
Khandesh gateway and Surat route
          +
all-India claim of suzerainty
          +
Deccani succession and factional conflict
          +
Portuguese pressure on shipping
          v
diplomatic probing -> 1591 missions -> military pressure after 1595
```

Akbar did not formulate one fixed southern conquest plan in 1562. Policy developed as northern consolidation, information and opportunity changed.

### 1. Early Khandesh contacts

Pir Muhammad Khan's 1562 invasion followed Khandesh's shelter to Baz Bahadur and failed. In 1564, Mubarak Shah sent gifts, accepted a marriage connection and ceded Bijaygarh and Handia. This did not prove a settled forward policy.

After Gujarat's conquest, an ambassador investigated Deccan conditions. In 1576, the new Faruqi ruler Raja Ali submitted, paid tribute and handed Muhammad Husain Mirza to the Mughals. Khandesh thereby became the first Deccan-area state clearly subordinate, but Bengal, Bihar and the north-west delayed sustained expansion.

### 2. Sovereignty and the Rajasthan analogy

Abu'l Fazl represented India from the Himalayas to the seas as one imperial field. The claim did not require every ruler's immediate removal. Akbar could seek a Rajasthan-like hierarchy:

```text
local dynasty remains
     + accepts Mughal over-lordship
     + maintains order
     + serves when called
     = subordinate rule rather than direct province
```

Imperial language about rescuing subjects from oppression legitimated expansion. It should be read as court ideology, not accepted as a neutral report of Deccani society.

### 3. Security, commerce and the Portuguese

Khandesh linked the northern empire to Burhanpur, Surat and the plateau. The Portuguese cartaz system affected shipping, including pilgrimage traffic. Akbar attempted accommodation but also resented dependence on Portuguese passes. His lack of a strong navy limited direct coercion at sea.

Wider influence over Deccan rulers could increase diplomatic leverage, but Portuguese pressure was one motive among several. Revenue, routes, succession openings and imperial prestige also mattered.

### 4. The 1591 diplomatic offensive

Akbar sent four missions to the principal Deccani rulers. Khandesh accepted suzerainty clearly. Other courts sent goodwill or presents without meaningful submission; Burhan Nizam Shah of Ahmadnagar responded brusquely.

Diplomacy here was coercive signalling. A gift might acknowledge communication while rejecting political subordination. When embassies failed, Ahmadnagar's 1595 succession crisis created an opening for armed intervention.

### 5. Strongest interpretation and qualification

**Interpretation:** Akbar pursued an all-India sovereignty claim through graduated instruments: intelligence, embassy, marriage, tribute, alliance and force.

**Objection:** This language merely disguised territorial expansion.

**Reply:** Expansion was real, but the repeated willingness to leave subordinate dynasties in place shows that direct annexation was not the sole available objective.

### UPSC application

- Reject a sudden 1595 beginning; show the 1560s-1591 buildup.
- Use Khandesh to link strategy and commerce.
- Present Portuguese power as contributing context, not the sole cause.
- Explain why diplomacy could be coercive without being annexation.
- Read Abu'l Fazl's welfare language as legitimation and evidence of imperial ideology.

### Mains practice - 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why did Akbar's diplomacy precede large-scale Mughal intervention in the Deccan?

**Model answer:**
Akbar's Deccan policy evolved after the consolidation of Malwa and Gujarat rather than beginning as a fixed conquest programme. Khandesh's submission in 1576 supplied a gateway through Burhanpur and Asirgarh, but eastern and north-western priorities delayed deeper intervention.

Diplomacy offered a lower-cost test of Akbar's all-India claim to suzerainty. The 1591 missions asked Deccani rulers to accept superior Mughal status while allowing their courts to survive, broadly resembling subordinate alliance rather than immediate annexation. The missions also gathered intelligence and signalled military capacity.

Commercial routes, Portuguese pressure on shipping, factional instability and concern for political order strengthened Mughal interest. Yet most courts returned courtesy and presents without submission. Ahmadnagar's 1595 succession crisis then provided the opening that diplomacy had not secured.

Thus embassies were an instrument of graduated coercion: they preceded war because hierarchy without costly occupation remained preferable if it could be obtained.

## Visual first: succession crisis became imperial entry

```text
Burhan Nizam Shah dies, 1595
          |
Ibrahim dies in Bijapur war
          |
rival claimants and noble coalitions
     /                         \
Miyan Manju invites Mughals    Chand Bibi backs infant Bahadur
     \                         /
         siege of Ahmadnagar
                  |
1596: Berar ceded + Bahadur recognised + suzerainty accepted
                  |
disputed implementation -> 1597 war -> renewed siege
                  |
1600 fort captured, but Nizam Shahi remnant survives
```

Chand Bibi's importance lies in political choice under constraint. She defended, negotiated and attempted to preserve a dynasty; neither solitary-hero mythology nor surrender language explains her role.

### 1. The succession crisis

After Burhan's death, Ibrahim Nizam Shah died fighting Bijapur over Sholapur. Miyan Manju, the Deccani-party peshwa, promoted a pretender and invited Mughal aid. Chand Bibi, sister of Burhan and widow of a Bijapur ruler, backed the infant Bahadur with Habshi support and appealed to Bijapur and Golconda.

Miyan Manju later recoiled from the intervention he had invited. This is a recurring mechanism: a faction uses imperial force against a rival, then discovers that the outsider's objective exceeds the original bargain.

### 2. The 1595-96 siege and treaty

Prince Murad and Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan led the Mughal campaign. A Bijapuri relief force strengthened Chand Bibi's defence. After about four months, the agreement:

- ceded Berar to the Mughals;
- recognised infant Bahadur under Chand Bibi's regency;
- accepted Mughal suzerainty.

The Mughals compromised partly because a strong Bijapur-Golconda presence made unlimited assault costly. The treaty preserved the Nizam Shahi court while conceding territory and hierarchy.

### 3. Why peace failed

Berar gave the Mughals a permanent northern foothold and alarmed the Deccani states. Some Nizam Shahi nobles rejected its transfer; others negotiated with the Mughals. A combined Ahmadnagar-Bijapur-Golconda force challenged Mughal possession in 1597 and was defeated in the Berar theatre.

The detailed OCR prints the battle place as **Sonepat**. It must not be relocated automatically to Haryana's Sonipat; variant modern renderings require source-specific caution.

### 4. Ahmadnagar fort, 1600

Continued raids on Berar produced another siege. Chand Bibi explored a settlement retaining Bahadur as a subordinate ruler with herself as guardian. A hostile faction accused her of treachery and killed her. Mughal forces then captured Ahmadnagar fort and adjacent territory in 1600; Bahadur was sent to Gwalior.

Her negotiation was not betrayal. It was an attempt to preserve dynastic authority when relief and internal unity had failed.

### 5. Asirgarh and Khandesh, 1601

Akbar moved toward Khandesh and sought Asirgarh, a powerful fort commanding the northern gateway. The detailed source emphasises a tight siege, pestilence and surrender. Other capture traditions should not displace this account without provenance.

Khandesh was annexed in 1601. Yet Murtaza Nizam Shah II, supported by remaining nobles and Bijapur, continued to rule part of Ahmadnagar. A 1601 compromise recognised remaining territory under conditions of loyalty.

### 6. Gains versus control

| Mughal gain | What remained unresolved |
|---|---|
| Berar | collection and security contested |
| Ahmadnagar fort | wider Nizam Shahi state survived |
| Balaghat claim | routes and countryside remained vulnerable |
| Asirgarh and Khandesh | plateau consolidation still required |
| marriage with Bijapur princess | no durable Bijapuri submission |

Akbar's return north amid Salim's rebellion and Daniyal's death left a substantial but unstable frontier.

- End with “foothold, not consolidated Deccan.”

### Mains practice - 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Assess the political significance of Chand Bibi's defence and the Mughal gains of 1595-1601.

**Model answer:**
The Ahmadnagar crisis began as a struggle among Nizam Shahi factions after the deaths of Burhan and Ibrahim in 1595. Miyan Manju invited Mughal aid, while Chand Bibi supported the infant Bahadur with Habshi and external backing.

Her four-month defence forced the 1596 compromise: Berar was ceded, Mughal suzerainty accepted and Bahadur recognised under her regency. This was neither total victory nor capitulation. Chand Bibi used resistance and negotiation to preserve dynastic space, while Bijapur-Golconda support constrained the Mughals.

The settlement failed because Berar gave the empire a strategic foothold and remained contested. After the 1597 Deccani defeat, renewed factional conflict led to Chand Bibi's murder and the Mughal capture of Ahmadnagar fort in 1600. Akbar then annexed Khandesh and secured Asirgarh in 1601.

These were substantial gains in forts, routes and territory. Yet Murtaza II's Nizam Shahi remnant survived, countryside control remained incomplete and Bijapur did not accept binding obedience. Akbar established a Deccan bridgehead, not a durable conquest of the region.

## Visual first: resistance was a system, not one man's cavalry trick

```text
Nizam Shahi claimant
        +
Malik Ambar as peshwa
        +
Habshi + Deccani + Afghan soldiers
        +
Maratha bargi cavalry + deshmukh intelligence
        +
Bijapur support when interests aligned
        +
forts + stores + revenue
        v
mobile war that denied Mughal consolidation
```

Malik Ambar's achievement came from combining legitimacy, finance, local knowledge and mobility. “Guerrilla warfare” captures one method but can hide the state structure that sustained it.

### 1. From enslavement to peshwa

Malik Ambar was born in Ethiopia, passed through enslavement and commercial networks and entered Deccan service. He rose in the household of the influential Habshi noble Chingiz Khan and later became the chief minister or **peshwa** of the restored Nizam Shahi regime.

His biography demonstrates African agency in the early modern Indian Ocean and Deccan worlds. It should not be romanticised into a modern abolitionist programme or reduced to outsider status.

### 2. Dynastic legitimacy and coalition

After Bahadur's removal, Malik Ambar supported Murtaza Nizam Shah II. The claimant supplied dynastic legitimacy; Ambar supplied command and coalition management. He recruited disbanded Deccani soldiers, Afghans, Habshis and large numbers of Maratha **bargis**, or light mounted auxiliaries.

Bijapur supported Ahmadnagar as a buffer. It provided the fort of Qandahar in Telangana for Ambar's family, stores and treasure, sent 10,000 horse and joined a 1609 marriage alliance involving Ambar's son.

### 3. How mobile warfare worked

```text
avoid battle on imperial terms
        ->
disperse and move rapidly
        ->
watch routes through local intelligence
        ->
raid stores and cut grain/fodder
        ->
force a large army to halt or retreat
        ->
recover countryside after garrisons are isolated
```

Mughal armies had heavy cavalry, siege artillery and substantial manpower. Those advantages required supply. A fort could be taken, but an army unable to feed men and horses could not convert the fort into wide control.

### 4. Why “guerrilla” needs qualification

The shorthand does not mean a uniquely modern national doctrine. Deccan campaigns combined:

- light-cavalry harassment;
- conventional battle;
- siege and fort defence;
- political defection;
- treaty and marriage;
- revenue collection and urban bases.

Mobile forces could deny consolidation but struggled to eliminate secure Mughal bases such as Burhanpur. Each side had different strengths.

### 5. Reversal by 1610

Khan-i-Khanan defeated Ambar near Nander in 1601 but then made a stabilising pact. After Akbar's death and Jahangir's distraction by Khusrau's rebellion, Ambar renewed war. With Bijapuri and Maratha help, he forced Mughal retreat; by 1610 most Akbari gains outside secure positions had been lost.

This reversal demonstrates that possession on a map was conditional on continued command unity, finance, supply and local cooperation.

- Connect cavalry to forts, intelligence and revenue.
- Avoid treating all Marathas or Habshis as a permanent bloc.
- Balance effective resistance with dependence on unstable alliances.

## Visual first: cultivation financed resistance

```text
measure land and village boundaries
          ->
replace revenue farming where possible
          ->
use progressive rates on new cultivation
          ->
work with deshmukhs and local officials
          ->
expand collection and order
          ->
pay troops, provision forts and sustain Khirki
```

A mobile army still needs a stationary fiscal base. Malik Ambar's importance therefore extends from tactics to reconstruction of a war-damaged state.

### 1. What the evidence supports

Later Marathi sources credit Ambar with measuring land, fixing village boundaries and standardising local measures. Documents refer to chain measurement and progressive taxation for newly cultivated land, with the full rate reached in the fifth year.

He is associated with replacing **ijara**, revenue farming or contractual collection, with a **zabti-type** assessment based more directly on measured cultivation. He paid attention to deshmukhs and other local figures connected with agriculture.

### 2. What the evidence does not support

The material is later and incomplete. It does not securely establish:

- a uniform one-third demand everywhere;
- a perfect copy of Todar Mal's system;
- simultaneous implementation across all Nizam Shahi territory;
- elimination of intermediaries;
- relief from every wartime burden.

The safest claim is functional: fiscal reconstruction and accommodation with local landed networks helped expand cultivation, order and military resources.

### 3. Khirki as an urban-logistical centre

Malik Ambar developed **Khirki**, later associated with Aurangabad, as a Nizam Shahi centre. Its waterworks and urban landscape show investment in settlement and logistical capacity. Material remains demonstrate capability; they cannot independently prove every administrative claim made in later narratives.

### 4. Fort networks and countryside

Forts protected rulers, stores and strategic passages. Bijapur's provision of Qandahar in Telangana gave Ambar a secure place for family and supplies. Yet a fort depended on surrounding cultivation and communications.

```text
fort without countryside -> isolated garrison
countryside without fort -> vulnerable collection
fort + supply + local cooperation -> staying power
```

### 5. Historiographical balance

One view celebrates Ambar as a defender and administrator. Satish Chandra also records a critical interpretation: ambition, harshness and conflict with Bijapur weakened the united Deccani front and prolonged wars that eventually damaged Ahmadnagar.

The fair verdict is dual. He restored state capacity and delayed Mughal consolidation, but his system did not create permanent interstate unity or save the dynasty after his death.

- Use “zabti-type” rather than identical Todar Mal transplantation.
- Connect fiscal reform to military endurance.
- Explain Khirki through water, stores and administration.
- Include Ambar's coalition and interstate limitations.

3. Chain measurement appears in documentary evidence.
4. New cultivation reportedly faced progressive rates.

6. Ijara means revenue farming or contractual collection.
7. A zabti-type system linked assessment more directly to cultivation.
8. A uniform one-third rate is not securely established.
9. Deshmukh cooperation connected state and countryside.
10. Khirki supported urban and logistical capacity.


Jahangir's record cannot be described as either helpless failure or straightforward conquest. Mughal armies won major victories, while imperial policy repeatedly stopped short of absorbing all Deccani states.

### 1. Why the 1611 plan failed

Jahangir sent armies under Khan-i-Jahan Lodi, with Raja Man Singh, and Abdullah Khan to converge on Daulatabad. Rivalry and weak coordination prevented concentration. Numerical scale could not substitute for unified command, communications and timing.

### 2. Khan-i-Khanan's political warfare

Reappointed to the Deccan, Khan-i-Khanan won over Habshi and Maratha nobles including Jagdev Rai, Babaji Kate and Udaji Ram. In 1616 he defeated a combined Ahmadnagar-Bijapur-Golconda force. Mughal troops occupied and burned Khirki, then withdrew.

This was not merely battlefield superiority. Recruitment fractured Ambar's coalition before force struck it.

### 3. Prince Khurram and the 1617 settlement

Jahangir sent Prince Khurram with a large army and moved to Mandu in support. Ambar restored Balaghat and delivered the key of Ahmadnagar fort. The detailed narrative places this in 1617; the concise telling attaches the culminating pressure to 1618. Use 1616-17 for the campaign sequence and state the source variation if exact dating is under examination.

Jahangir did not enlarge Akbar's annexations. His gracious farman called Ibrahim Adil Shah **farzand**, “son.” This expressed hierarchy and conciliation, not equality.

### 4. Recovery and the 1621 settlement

Malik Ambar recovered territory again. Another Mughal victory in 1621 compelled restoration, an additional belt adjoining Ahmadnagar and an indemnity. The detailed Part II account prints fifty lakh rupees; the concise volume prints five lakh. Unless identifying the edition, “a large indemnity” is safer than silently selecting one figure.

### 5. Bhaturi and the collapse of unity

Old disputes over Sholapur and Bidar shattered the Deccani front. Jahangir aligned with Bijapur to isolate Ambar. Ambar defeated combined Mughal-Adil Shahi forces at **Bhaturi** near Ahmadnagar in 1624, killed the Bijapuri commander Muhammad Lari, attacked Nauraspur and recovered Sholapur. His prestige peaked, but conflict with Bijapur weakened collective resistance.

### 6. Why restraint made sense

Jahangir hoped subordinate states would preserve order without imposing the cost of direct rule. Safavid correspondence favoured preservation of tribute-paying Deccani vassals rather than confrontation with the Mughals. This context does not make Shah Abbas the sole author of Jahangir's policy.

### 7. Malik Ambar's death

The standard chronology used here is **1626**. One detailed assigned OCR narrative prints 1627. The discrepancy must remain visible rather than being silently harmonised.

## Visual first: service mobility before sovereignty

```text
deshmukh and local landed base
          +
cavalry skill and local intelligence
          +
service under Ahmadnagar / Bijapur / Mughal commanders
          +
mansab, jagir, protection and opportunity
          v

```

Before Shivaji's mature state, Maratha chiefs were important landed, military and diplomatic actors within several regimes. Their service mobility must not be rewritten as a continuous national war.

### 1. The service world

Maratha **deshmukhs** held locally rooted revenue and landed influence. **Bargis** served as mounted auxiliaries. Families such as the Mores, Nimbalkars, Ghatges and Bhonsales entered the armies and administrations of Ahmadnagar and Bijapur; Maharashtrian Brahmans also worked in records and diplomacy.

Unlike major Rajput rulers, these chiefs generally did not command recognised kingdoms backed by unified clans. Their security depended on office, local following and bargaining with competing courts.

### 2. Mughal recruitment as a military instrument

Khan-i-Khanan recruited Maratha sardars to weaken Malik Ambar. Jahangir called the Marathas hardy and central to regional resistance. Mughal service offered rank and jagir; local commanders offered cavalry, intelligence and access to countryside.

Recruitment was therefore incorporation and counter-insurgency at once. It could produce service without permanent loyalty.

### 3. Shahji Bhonsale's changing alignments

The killing of Jadhav Rao, Shahji's father-in-law, helped push Shahji toward Mughal service. Shah Jahan granted him a mansab of 5,000 and Poona-region jagirs. When those jagirs were transferred to Fath Khan in 1632, Shahji defected.

He then entered Bijapuri service, harassed Mughal supplies and supported a Nizam Shahi claimant after Daulatabad fell. His force drew disbanded soldiers and reportedly expanded greatly.

Shahji was:

- a locally rooted sardar;
- a cavalry commander;
- a negotiator among courts;
- a holder of service assignments;
- a defender of a claimant when that served his coalition.

He was not yet the ruler of the later Maratha state, and his changes of employer were not proof of ideological inconsistency. They were normal features of a competitive service order.

### 4. What this contributed to later history

Service networks developed military skill, knowledge of forts and routes, revenue links and confidence in bargaining with large states. These were enabling conditions later used by Shivaji. They did not mechanically create his state: leadership, organisation, fiscal institutions and new political claims were still required.

## Visual first: extinction of one state, subordination of two others

Shah Jahan changed the treatment of Ahmadnagar while still avoiding direct annexation of Bijapur and Golconda.

### 1. Why policy hardened

Khan-i-Jahan Lodi failed to recover lost territory, rebelled and received Nizam Shahi asylum. To Shah Jahan, Ahmadnagar's continued challenge to Berar and Balaghat now threatened imperial authority and protected a rebel noble. He concluded that peace required ending Nizam Shahi independence.

He nevertheless offered Bijapur roughly one-third of Ahmadnagar for cooperation and invited Maratha chiefs into Mughal service. The method combined diplomatic isolation with force.

### 2. Fath Khan and Daulatabad

Bijapur initially cooperated but shifted when Mughal advance threatened the balance and promised territory was withheld. Within Ahmadnagar, Malik Ambar's son Fath Khan became peshwa, killed the Nizam Shah, installed a puppet and recognised Shah Jahan through khutba and sikka in 1632. He entered Mughal service; Shahji's Poona jagir passed to him.

Mahabat Khan besieged Daulatabad. Its surrender in **1633** ended the ruling Nizam Shahi line. It did not end resistance: Shahji raised another claimant and, with Bijapuri support and disbanded troops, controlled substantial territory and attacked supplies.

### 3. The Bijapur ahdnama of 1636

Bijapur agreed to:

- recognise Mughal suzerainty;
- pay twenty lakh rupees as indemnity;
- accept imperial arbitration in disputes with Golconda;
- avoid interference with Mughal-protected Golconda;
- help reduce Shahji or employ him away from the Mughal frontier.

In return it received a large share of former Ahmadnagar territory, described in the detailed source as yielding about twenty lakh huns annually. OCR rupee conversions should be treated cautiously.

### 4. The Golconda settlement

Golconda agreed to place Shah Jahan's name in the khutba instead of the Iranian ruler's, acknowledge loyalty and receive Mughal protection. Its former annual payment of four lakh huns to Bijapur was remitted; it was to pay two lakh huns annually to the Mughal emperor.

### 5. Settlement, not annexation

| Ahmadnagar | Bijapur | Golconda |
|---|---|---|
| ruling line extinguished; territory partitioned | court survived with territory, indemnity and obligations | court survived under tribute, khutba and protection |
| direct Mughal provinces in northern field | unequal subordinate autonomy | unequal subordinate autonomy |

Satish Chandra calls the treaties statesmanlike because they reduced direct costs and stabilised hierarchy. The objection is strong: force created the bargain, parties were unequal and compliance depended on continuing capacity. “Coercive suzerainty and buffer management” captures both aspects.

- State Bijapur and Golconda terms separately.
- Do not describe either surviving state as annexed.
- Evaluate “statesmanship” against coercion and enforceability.


The Deccan problem was administrative after it was military. A campaign could add territory while also adding commanders, garrisons and claims on revenue.

### 1. Instruments of incorporation

Mughal rule used a viceroy or subadar, provincial diwans, imperial correspondence, mansabs, jagirs, forts and **thanas**. Burhanpur served as a major headquarters and supply centre. Provincial arrangements included Khandesh, Berar and Ahmadnagar/Daulatabad-related territories; names and boundaries changed.

Recruiting Deccani, Habshi and Maratha elites supplied knowledge and cavalry. It also created new claims for rank and assignment.

### 2. Jama and hasil

**Jama** was assessed revenue; **hasil** was actual realisation. War damage, inflated estimates, local resistance and uncertain collection could make a Deccan jagir worth much less than its paper value.

Topic 21's evidence places some Deccan assignments at only three or four months of nominal value. This is an application, not the full later jagirdari-crisis story.

### 3. Forts, roads and countryside

Garrisons secured nodes, not every village. A subadar needed grain, fodder, remounts, money and intelligence. If local intermediaries withheld cooperation, an imperial army could guard a fort while failing to realise nearby revenue.

### 4. Subsidy and the expansion paradox

Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb corresponded sharply over Deccan deficits. The centre had used resources from Malwa and Surat to sustain southern administration. Shah Jahan's reluctance to continue subsidies strengthened the search for compensation from Golconda and Bijapur.

```text
new territory promises revenue
        +
new territory requires troops and administration
        =
gain only if real collection exceeds added cost
```

### 5. Strongest objection

**Objection:** Fiscal difficulty proves Mughal administrative failure.

**Reply:** The empire maintained provinces, forts and large campaigns for decades. The more precise verdict is uneven capacity: institutions worked, but their returns varied sharply with war, ecology and local cooperation.

Aurangzeb's later imperial Deccan war must not be projected backward. As viceroy he operated within Shah Jahan's authority and changing instructions.

### 1. First tenure, 1636-44

Aurangzeb became viceroy at about eighteen. His responsibility was to implement the new settlement, supervise Mughal territories and manage relations with Bijapur and Golconda. Evidence does not establish a completed 1630s design to annex both states.

### 2. Second tenure, 1652-57

Reappointed in 1652, Aurangzeb faced low realisation and disagreement with Shah Jahan over subsidies. With diwan **Murshid Quli Khan**, he attempted to improve cultivation and revenue collection. Exact success varied; administrative reform did not remove structural costs.

Aurangzeb favoured stronger action against Golconda and Bijapur. His case combined arrears, fiscal pressure, changing regional power and opportunities created by court conflict.

### 3. Why Golconda mattered

After 1636, Golconda and Bijapur expanded into Karnataka and the Coromandel. The region's textiles, indigo, ports and inland revenue enlarged their wealth. Golconda also possessed famous diamond-producing zones, but “diamond greed” alone cannot explain Mughal policy.

### 4. Mir Jumla changes the balance

Mir Jumla was Golconda's leading noble and a wealthy military-commercial power-holder with extensive Karnataka interests. His rupture with the Qutb Shahi court and movement into Mughal service created an intervention opening.

He was not merely a victim inviting rescue. His troops, wealth and territorial claims made him an actor who could alter bargaining among the emperor, viceroy and Qutb Shah.

### 5. The 1656 campaign and settlement

Aurangzeb pressed for annexation. Shah Jahan initially sought more limited compensation and submission. The settlement brought money and the **Ramgir** district to the Mughals. A further dispute concerned whether Mir Jumla's Karnataka jagir belonged to the emperor or Golconda.

The rupee-hun exchange-rate dispute and tribute arrears supplied a stated pretext. They mattered, but policy had been hardening for years because Mughal deficits and subordinate expansion changed the 1636 balance.

### 6. Emperor, viceroy and Dara

Aurangzeb later suspected Dara of blocking annexation. The assigned source finds no conclusive proof that Dara determined Shah Jahan's final decisions. Policy differences are secure; a decisive conspiracy is not.

## Visual first: a successful buffer settlement was gradually hollowed out

```text
1636 compact
  -> Bijapur/Golconda expand south under Mughal neutrality
  -> new wealth and powerful nobles
  -> Mughal deficit and compensation claims
  -> Shahji and Mir Jumla become intervention openings
  -> Golconda campaign, 1656
  -> Bijapur succession crisis and invasion, 1657
  -> former Nizam Shahi lands surrendered
  -> compact broken before succession war
```

The Mughals reopened the annexation question without resolving the cost of annexation.

### 1. Southern expansion after 1636

Freed from immediate northern attack, Bijapur and Golconda expanded from the Krishna toward Tanjore and into territories of Nayaka and Rayal-linked powers. Mughal diplomacy helped broker a 1646 arrangement dividing gains two shares to Bijapur and one to Golconda.

The arrangement was not stable. Rapid expansion increased treasure and territory but weakened internal cohesion and empowered Shahji, Mir Jumla and other regional commanders.

### 2. Trade and finance as causal context

Karnataka and Coromandel textiles and indigo connected inland conquest to export wealth. Konkan and western ports linked horse supply, customs and shipping to military capacity. Golconda's diamonds added court wealth.

These resources affected policy through finance:

```text
commercially valuable territory
        ->
stronger subordinate courts and nobles
        ->
Mughal demand for compensation and control
```

Trade did not mechanically cause war. Succession, arrears, treaty interpretation and strategic fear translated wealth into conflict.

### 3. Bijapur's vulnerability

Muhammad Adil Shah died in 1656. Succession uncertainty, tribute arrears and Bijapur's support for Golconda supplied Mughal grounds for intervention. Shah Jahan authorised full annexation if feasible, or a lesser settlement recovering former Ahmadnagar territory, imposing a large indemnity and obtaining sovereign recognition.

### 4. The 1657 result

The final terms stopped short of annexing Bijapur. The state surrendered the Nizam Shahi territories it had received in 1636. The episode nevertheless destroyed confidence that the older partition and protection arrangement would endure.

The campaign was interrupted by Shah Jahan's illness and the succession struggle. That is the chronological cutoff: the later choices of Emperor Aurangzeb, Shivaji's mature conflict and the annexations of 1686-87 belong elsewhere.

### 5. Competing verdicts

| Verdict | Evidence | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| necessary correction | deficits, arrears and altered balance | ignores Mughal role in enabling expansion |
| Aurangzeb's aggression | he pressed annexation | Shah Jahan authorised intervention and set alternatives |
| Shah Jahan's reversal | 1656-57 broke his 1636 design | subordinate growth created genuine new pressures |
| inevitable conquest | long expansion and imperial ideology | final settlements still avoided total annexation |

Satish Chandra judges Shah Jahan's reopening of the problem doubtful in wisdom. J.N. Sarkar's “psychological impossibility” formulation stresses the loss of trust. These are historical interpretations, not treaty clauses.

### 1. Source ladder

| Evidence | Best use | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Mughal chronicles | campaigns, appointments and imperial language | victory teleology and court hierarchy |
| Deccan chronicles such as Ferishta | regional courts, nobles and rival claims | patronage and retrospective narration |

Jahangir's image of shooting Malik Ambar represents imperial hostility and frustrated desire; it does not document a physical encounter.

### 2. Debate: all-India sovereignty or expansionism?

Both operated. Universal imperial language justified intervention, while strategic and fiscal interests shaped its timing. Repeated reliance on subordinate courts shows that sovereignty could be satisfied through hierarchy short of annexation.

### 3. Debate: Malik Ambar, defender or destabiliser?

He restored Nizam Shahi power, reconstructed revenue and made Maratha mobility effective against imperial supply. He also alienated allies and fought Bijapur, weakening a durable united front. His resistance delayed conquest; it did not permanently secure Ahmadnagar.

### 4. Debate: Jahangir, weak or restrained?

The 1611 failure reveals command weakness, but victories in 1616-21 reveal capacity. Refusal to enlarge annexations was a policy choice intended to limit commitments. Its weakness was reliance on unstable compliance.

### 5. Debate: 1636 statesmanship or coercive pause?

The settlement economised on direct rule and stabilised hierarchy. It was also unequal, force-backed and dependent on trust. Its later breakdown does not erase its short-term effectiveness.

### 6. Debate: who reopened the problem?

Aurangzeb pressed annexation, but Shah Jahan authorised campaigns and possible conquest. Satish Chandra assigns substantial responsibility to Shah Jahan's reversal. Aurangzeb's allegation that Dara decisively blocked annexation lacks conclusive proof.

### 7. Why repeated Mughal difficulty?

1. long communications and seasonal supply;
2. forts controlling routes but not all countryside;
3. mobile cavalry attacking grain and fodder;
4. local intermediaries bargaining over revenue;
5. factional rivalry inside both imperial and Deccani camps;
6. unstable buffers and cross-court service;
7. jama-hasil gaps and subsidy;
8. the contradiction between universal sovereignty and limited administrative capacity.

### 8. Consequences by 1657

The Mughals held a major northern Deccan territorial system and could compel treaties. Ahmadnagar's ruling dynasty had ended. Bijapur and Golconda remained autonomous but weakened subordinate courts. Maratha service groups had accumulated experience and local bases. The 1636 compact had been undermined, while direct annexation still promised costs greater than immediate gains.

The later Mughal-Maratha war was not predetermined. By 1657, however, the unresolved cost-control dilemma had been placed firmly on the imperial agenda.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks - 150 words

**Question:** Why did fort capture not necessarily produce durable Mughal control in the Deccan?

**Model answer:**
Forts such as Ahmadnagar, Asirgarh and Daulatabad controlled routes, stores and political symbols. Their capture gave the Mughals strategic bases and bargaining power, but did not automatically secure dependent countryside.

Durable rule required grain, fodder, communications, garrisons, revenue officials and cooperation from deshmukhs and mobile service groups. Malik Ambar's cavalry exploited this gap by avoiding imperial battle conditions, cutting supplies and recovering territory after Mughal columns withdrew.

Fiscal weakness reinforced the problem. A high jama could yield low hasil when war disrupted cultivation or local intermediaries resisted. A fort garrison could therefore survive while its assigned territory failed to finance the expected contingent.

Mughal artillery and administration produced real gains, so the issue was not incapacity. The Deccan showed that military possession became durable sovereignty only when logistical, fiscal and political control operated together.

**Scoring note:** Require two named forts, route/store function, supply-cutting, local intermediaries, jama-hasil and a qualified capacity verdict.

## 15 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Assess Malik Ambar's contribution to the political and military history of the Deccan.

**Model answer:**
Malik Ambar rose from Ethiopian enslavement and Deccan service to become peshwa of a restored Nizam Shahi regime. His first contribution was political: a dynastic claimant, Habshi-Afghan-Deccani soldiers, Maratha bargis, deshmukh networks and periodic Bijapuri support formed a resistance coalition.

His mobile cavalry dispersed, raided stores, cut supplies and exploited the gap between Mughal fort occupation and countryside control. By 1610, most Akbari gains outside secure positions had been reversed. Bhaturi in 1624 marked a late military peak.

Ambar was also a state-builder. Later Marathi evidence credits measurement, village-boundary settlement, a move from ijara toward zabti-type assessment and progressive rates for new cultivation. Khirki and its water system reflected urban-logistical investment. These claims require caution: uniform implementation and an exact one-third demand are not proven.

His limitations were serious. He alienated nobles, fought Bijapur and could not permanently expel the Mughals. His standard death date is 1626, though one assigned narrative prints 1627.

Ambar's lasting importance lies in delaying consolidation, rebuilding regional capacity and developing Maratha confidence and experience without predetermining a later state.

**Scoring note:** Require origin/office, coalition, operational mechanism, one reversal, fiscal measures with source caution, factional limit and non-inevitable legacy.

## 20 marks - 250 words

**Question:** Trace and evaluate the evolution of Mughal Deccan policy from Akbar to 1657.

**Model answer:**
Akbar moved from reconnaissance and diplomacy to selective conquest. Khandesh submitted in 1576; the 1591 missions sought suzerainty. Ahmadnagar's succession crisis produced the 1596 Berar settlement, Ahmadnagar fort's capture in 1600 and Khandesh-Asirgarh annexation in 1601. A Nizam Shahi remnant survived.

Malik Ambar then combined claimant legitimacy, mobile cavalry, local elites and fiscal reconstruction to reverse consolidation. Jahangir recovered territory through victories in 1616-21 but deliberately avoided unlimited annexation, preferring subordinate stability.

Shah Jahan changed course toward Ahmadnagar after Khan-i-Jahan Lodi's rebellion. Daulatabad fell in 1633; the 1636 treaties partitioned Ahmadnagar while retaining Bijapur and Golconda under tribute, protection, arbitration and service restrictions. This reduced direct costs but remained coercive.

Deccan jama-hasil weakness, southern expansion and powerful intermediaries later altered the balance. During Aurangzeb's second viceroyalty, Mir Jumla's defection enabled the 1656 Golconda campaign. Bijapur in 1657 surrendered former Nizam Shahi lands but was not annexed.

Policy thus moved among suzerainty, selective annexation and buffer management. By 1657 it had reopened wider conquest without solving the fiscal-logistical cost of durable rule.

## Policy by ruler

| Ruler | Dominant instrument | Major outcome | Central limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Akbar | diplomacy plus selective conquest | Khandesh, Berar and fort footholds | Nizam Shahi survival |
| Jahangir | campaigns plus restraint | territory restored, commitments limited | reversibility |
| Shah Jahan | dynastic extinction plus treaty hierarchy | 1636 settlement | coercion and fragile trust |
| Aurangzeb as viceroy | administration plus renewed pressure | 1656-57 concessions | operated under emperor; annexation unresolved |

## Control ladder

```text
embassy
 -> courtesy
 -> tribute
 -> suzerainty
 -> fort occupation
 -> territorial administration
 -> durable revenue and order
```

Movement along the ladder was reversible.

## Malik Ambar mechanism

```text
claimant legitimacy
 + multi-group coalition
 + bargi mobility
 + deshmukh intelligence
 + revenue reconstruction
 + forts and Khirki
 = resistance capacity

but

noble alienation + Bijapur rivalry + Mughal recruitment
 = coalition fragility
```

## 1636 versus 1656-57

| 1636 | 1656-57 |
|---|---|
| limit direct commitments | seek compensation and possible annexation |
| preserve two subordinate courts | exploit court and succession crises |
| partition Ahmadnagar | recover transferred territory |
| stabilise through arbitration | undermine confidence in arbitration |
| short-term cost control | renewed cost-control dilemma |

## Durable-control equation

```text
military victory
 + secure route
 + provisioned fort
 + realistic hasil
 + local cooperation
 + stable diplomacy
 = durable control
```

Absence of any one element could reverse a territorial gain.

## Chronology

- 1562: failed punitive Khandesh expedition.
- 1573: Ahmadnagar annexed Berar.
- 1576: Khandesh submitted and handed over Muhammad Husain Mirza.
- 1591: four Mughal missions; Khandesh alone clearly accepted suzerainty.
- 1595-96: Ahmadnagar succession crisis and Chand Bibi's defence.
- 1596: Berar ceded; Bahadur recognised; suzerainty accepted.
- 1597: combined Deccani challenge defeated in the Berar theatre.
- 1600: Chand Bibi killed; Ahmadnagar fort captured.
- 1601: Asirgarh fell, Khandesh annexed and Nizam Shahi remnant recognised.
- 1610: Malik Ambar had reversed most exposed Akbari gains.
- 1611: two-pronged Mughal operation failed through poor coordination.
- 1616: Khan-i-Khanan defeated the combined coalition; Khirki burned.
- 1617: Khurram's pressure produced restoration; concise account varies the culmination toward 1618.
- 1621: another Mughal victory and large, textually disputed indemnity.
- 1624: Malik Ambar won at Bhaturi.
- 1626: standard date of Malik Ambar's death; one OCR account prints 1627.
- 1629-33: Shah Jahan's Ahmadnagar offensive.
- 1632: Fath Khan's defection and puppet arrangement.
- 1633: Daulatabad fell; ruling Nizam Shahi line ended.
- 1633-36: Shahji sustained claimant resistance.
- 1636: separate Bijapur and Golconda treaties; Aurangzeb's first viceroyalty began.
- 1646: Mughal-mediated two-to-one division of southern gains.
- 1649: Shahji service question exposed treaty weakness.
- 1652: Aurangzeb's second viceroyalty began.
- 1656: Golconda campaign and settlement; Muhammad Adil Shah died.
- 1657: Bijapur surrendered former Nizam Shahi lands; succession crisis halted further policy.

## Political geography

- Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Berar and Bidar emerged from Bahmani fragmentation.
- Khandesh was Faruqi and controlled the northern gateway.
- Berar-Balaghat linked Khandesh with Ahmadnagar.
- Ahmadnagar-Daulatabad-Khirki formed a fortified political core.
- Sholapur, Bidar and Naldurg were recurrent interstate disputes.
- Karnataka and Coromandel became the major post-1636 expansion field.
- Konkan and western ports linked horse supply, customs and shipping to inland war.

## Actors and institutions

- Deccani, afaqi, Habshi and Afghan factions were fluid.
- Maratha deshmukhs joined local revenue authority to armed following.
- Bargis were mounted auxiliaries skilled in rapid movement.
- Peshwa was an established Ahmadnagar office before later Maratha usage.
- Mughal mansab and jagir could recruit regional elites without ensuring permanent loyalty.
- Khutba and sikka expressed sovereignty claims.
- Ahdnama means a formal treaty or covenant.

## Akbar's phase

- Motives combined sovereignty, security, routes, revenue, instability and Portuguese context.
- Portuguese pressure mattered, but naval weakness limited direct action.
- The 1591 missions preceded major war.
- Chand Bibi represented constrained political agency.
- Berar was the price of the 1596 settlement.
- Ahmadnagar fort's fall did not extinguish the dynasty.
- Asirgarh and Khandesh were strategic, not merely prestigious.
- Akbar left an unstable foothold.

## Malik Ambar

- Ethiopian/Habshi origin and enslavement must be stated precisely.
- Nizam Shahi legitimacy anchored his power.
- Coalition included Habshis, Afghans, Deccanis, Marathas and intermittent Bijapuri support.
- Mobility attacked Mughal supply rather than artillery frontally.
- Forts, stores and local intelligence supported cavalry.
- Revenue reconstruction involved measurement, boundaries and a zabti-type shift.
- Uniform one-third assessment is not securely established.
- Khirki embodied urban-logistical capacity.
- He delayed Mughal consolidation but weakened unity through rivalry.

## Jahangir

- 1611 showed command-coordination failure.
- 1616 combined recruitment and battlefield victory.
- 1617 restored claims without wider annexation.
- Farzand expressed conciliatory hierarchy.
- The 1621 indemnity amount differs across assigned narratives.
- Bhaturi showed continuing reversibility.
- Restraint was a policy choice with capacity limits.

## Shah Jahan and 1636

- Khan-i-Jahan Lodi's rebellion hardened policy.
- Ahmadnagar was isolated through offers and recruitment.
- Fath Khan recognised Shah Jahan in khutba and sikka.
- Daulatabad ended the ruling line, not all resistance.
- Bijapur accepted suzerainty, indemnity, arbitration and a Shahji restriction.
- Golconda accepted khutba, tribute and protection.
- The settlement combined partition with buffer management.
- It was cost-conscious, coercive and contingent.

## Fiscal and military mechanism

- Jama is assessment; hasil is realisation.
- Deccan jagirs could yield only a fraction of paper value.
- Poor hasil weakened contingent maintenance.
- Forts held nodes; local cooperation connected them to countryside.
- Artillery required transport, powder, time and supply.
- Monsoon, rivers, disease and fodder affected operations.
- Recruitment supplied knowledge while preserving defection risk.
- Expansion added revenue claims and administrative costs simultaneously.

## 1656-57 and boundary

- Southern expansion increased subordinate wealth and noble autonomy.
- Textile, indigo, port and diamond resources mattered through finance.
- Mir Jumla's defection created leverage against Golconda.
- Ramgir and compensation followed the 1656 settlement.
- Bijapur's succession and arrears supplied a 1657 opening.
- Shah Jahan authorised possible annexation or a lesser settlement.
- Bijapur surrendered former Nizam Shahi territory but survived.
- Dara's decisive role is unproven.
- The 1636 compact was broken before the succession war.
- Post-1657 annexations and the mature Mughal-Maratha conflict belong to Topic 23.

## Final analytical verdict

Mughal policy moved repeatedly among diplomacy, suzerainty, selective annexation, buffer management and renewed coercion. The empire possessed formidable military and institutional capacity, but Deccan geography, local power, fiscal realisation and unstable alliances made control negotiated and reversible. By 1657, the Mughals had widened their territorial and hierarchical claims without resolving whether direct annexation would produce resources or consume them.
