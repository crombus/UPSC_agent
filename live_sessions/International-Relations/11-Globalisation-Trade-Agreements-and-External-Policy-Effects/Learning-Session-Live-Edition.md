### Concept compass

| Impulse | Opens or controls | Contemporary expression |
|---|---|---|
| Globalisation | Cross-border exchange | Trade, capital, data, technology and knowledge flows |
| Sovereign nationalism | National policy space | Tariffs, screening, subsidies, border and technology controls |
| Co-presence | Selective openness | Liberalise useful flows while guarding strategic sectors |

Globalisation is the intensification of cross-border trade, capital, technology and information flows. Sovereign nationalism is the reassertion of national control over borders, strategic sectors and external dependence. They are **co-present forces**, not consecutive eras. A government may sign an FTA while screening investment, welcome technology while restricting sensitive exports, or seek foreign capital while subsidising domestic production.

⚠️ The analytical shift is from uniform liberalisation towards **selective interdependence**. Global links remain extensive, but states increasingly decide which dependencies are tolerable. India must therefore seek markets and technology without treating every external link as equally safe. Detailed tariff and macroeconomic effects remain with Economy Topic 20.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Globalisation integrates cross-border flows.
2. Sovereign nationalism reasserts national control.
3. The two forces operate simultaneously.
4. Selective openness is not autarky.
5. Strategic sectors receive greater policy attention.
6. An FTA can coexist with industrial policy.
7. India seeks opportunity and resilience together.
8. Economy Topic 20 owns tariff mechanics.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is the present order better described as selective interdependence than deglobalisation?

**Model answer:** Cross-border trade, finance, technology and information remain extensive, so globalisation has not disappeared. What has changed is the political treatment of dependence. States preserve beneficial exchange while using tariffs, screening, subsidies, sanctions and export controls in sensitive sectors. FTAs continue to expand even as national-security exceptions grow. Selective interdependence therefore captures simultaneous integration and control better than a binary claim of complete deglobalisation.

**Adjacent rubric:** definition 2 + co-presence 3 + instruments 3 + judgement 2 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating stronger state control in selected sectors with the end of all global exchange.

**Transition:** With the two impulses separated, the next lesson traces the channels through which they reach India.

### Transmission map

```text
FOREIGN POLICY OR REGULATION
     ├─ trade terms ───────→ market access
     ├─ investment rules ──→ capital and ownership
     ├─ technology controls→ capability access
     ├─ labour rules ──────→ mobility and welfare
     ├─ standards ─────────→ compliance and certification
     └─ climate measures ──→ carbon-linked export conditions
                          ↓
                    INDIAN RESPONSE
```

An external-policy measure matters when it changes incentives, permissions or costs inside India. A tariff affects access; an export control affects technology; a labour chapter affects mobility; a technical standard affects certification; and a climate measure can alter the conditions under which goods enter a market.

✅ The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism began its definitive regime on **1 January 2026**.[^CBAM] It illustrates a measure that is simultaneously environmental, trade-related and industrial. ⚠️ Its India-specific effect must be established sector by sector; neither automatic devastation nor automatic irrelevance should be assumed.

The examiner rewards a transmission chain: **external instrument → channel → exposed actor → domestic distribution → diplomatic response**.

2. Trade rules affect market access.
3. Investment rules affect capital and control.
4. Export controls affect capability access.
5. Labour provisions affect mobility.
6. Standards can become market-entry conditions.

8. Claims about impact require sector-specific evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Construct a transmission chain for a foreign climate-linked trade measure affecting India.

**Model answer:** A foreign climate regulation first changes the conditions of market entry, such as emissions reporting or carbon-related payment. Exporting firms then face measurement, verification and compliance requirements. Costs vary by sector, technology and firm capability, producing domestic winners and losers. India may respond through consultation, standards support, green upgrading, diversification or rules-based challenge. The chain prevents a climate measure from being analysed only as diplomacy or only as economics.

**Adjacent rubric:** instrument 2 + transmission 3 + distribution 2 + response 3 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling every climate-linked trade measure either purely environmental or necessarily disguised protectionism.

### Instrument matrix

| Instrument | Primary object | Typical logic | IR question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tariff/quota | Goods at border | Protect or bargain | Who bears market-access loss? |
| Sanction | State/entity/transaction | Coerce foreign-policy change | Is coercion effective and legitimate? |
| Export control | Sensitive item/technology | Prevent capability transfer | Can partners secure trusted access? |
| Industrial subsidy | Domestic production | Build capacity | Does support distort competition? |

Protectionism broadly shields domestic production through tariffs, quotas or local-content rules. Sanctions target countries, entities or transactions to pursue foreign-policy objectives. Export controls restrict sensitive goods, software or know-how. Industrial policy supports domestic capability through finance, procurement, standards or subsidies.

✅ The US India tariff cycle demonstrates why legal identity and dates matter: EO 14326 imposed an additional reciprocal duty effective 7 August 2025; EO 14329 created a separate Russian-oil-linked duty effective 27 August; EO 14384 removed that latter duty from 7 February 2026; EO 14389 directed termination of IEEPA additional duties in February 2026.[^USTARIFF][^EO14384] ⚠️ A short-lived measure may still alter bargaining, contracts and risk perception.

Topic 03 owns detailed supply-chain strategy; this lesson owns instrument distinction and external-policy transmission.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Tariffs target goods at the border.
2. Sanctions pursue foreign-policy coercion.
3. Export controls target capabilities and transfers.
4. Industrial policy builds domestic capacity.
5. One partner may use several legal instruments.
6. Each measure requires its own date and authority.
7. Temporary measures can affect expectations.
8. Topic 03 owns detailed supply-chain strategy.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Distinguish sanctions from export controls and explain one common effect on India.

**Model answer:** Sanctions restrict dealings with a target state, entity or transaction to exert foreign-policy pressure. Export controls restrict transfer of designated goods, software or knowledge to prevent capability acquisition. Their legal objects differ, although both can interrupt finance, technology access and supply relationships. For India, either may create compliance risk, force supplier diversification and complicate strategic partnerships. A sound response identifies the instrument before judging its effect.

**Adjacent rubric:** sanctions 3 + export controls 3 + common effect 3 + precision 1 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Using tariff, sanction and export control as interchangeable labels.

**Transition:** Once instruments are classified, the institutional baseline against which they operate becomes visible.

### Institutional dashboard

```text
WTO FUNCTIONS
rules baseline ── still operating
notifications ── transparency function
panels ───────── first-stage adjudication
appeal review ── blocked: no Appellate Body members
```

The WTO remains the reference framework for non-discrimination, bound commitments, notifications and dispute settlement. Its weakness is specific, not total. ✅ The Appellate Body lost the three-member quorum required for new appeals on **10 December 2019** and has had **zero members since 30 November 2020**.[^WTOAB] India is outside the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement.

An appeal “into the void” can prevent adoption of a panel report. That harms both complainants seeking enforcement and respondents seeking appellate correction. Meanwhile, consensus makes broad negotiation difficult, encouraging bilateral and plurilateral alternatives.

Topic 12 owns full WTO architecture. Here the institution is studied as the rules background for external-policy effects and agreement choices.

### REVISION NOTES

1. WTO weakness must be specified by function.
2. Baseline rules continue to matter.
3. Panels and appellate review are distinct stages.
4. The Appellate Body lost quorum in December 2019.
5. It has had zero members since November 2020.
6. India is outside the MPIA.
7. Appeal paralysis creates two-sided costs.
8. Topic 12 owns full institutional architecture.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why is it inaccurate to say that appellate paralysis makes the WTO irrelevant?

**Model answer:** Appellate paralysis disables binding second-stage review, but it does not erase negotiated rules, schedules, notifications, committees or panel proceedings. Governments and firms still organise expectations around WTO commitments, and members continue negotiations and disputes. The weakness is serious because an appeal into the void may block final resolution, yet relevance varies by function. The accurate description is a continuing rules baseline with impaired adjudication and constrained negotiating capacity.

**Adjacent rubric:** continuing functions 4 + appellate weakness 3 + balanced judgement 3 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating a non-functioning appellate stage as proof that every WTO function has ceased.

**Transition:** The WTO’s difficulty in producing comprehensive bargains helps explain why preferential agreements proliferate.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Integration ladder

```text
MULTILATERAL RULES (WTO baseline)
          ↓ stalled broad liberalisation
BILATERAL / PLURILATERAL AGREEMENTS
          ↓ preferential commitments
goods + services + investment + mobility + standards
          ↓
utilisation and adjustment determine outcomes
```

An FTA lowers barriers between parties. Labels such as ECTA, CEPA, CETA or TEPA can signal wider scope, but the text—not the title—determines obligations. Such agreements are not proof that globalisation has ended. They continue integration through smaller negotiating circles when multilateral liberalisation stalls.

Benefits are conditional. Rules of origin determine whether goods qualify. Standards, customs capacity, services schedules, safeguards and business awareness affect utilisation. Sensitive sectors may be excluded or phased. ⚠️ Signing an agreement creates neither automatic export growth nor equal gains.

The correct IR question is how an agreement changes partnerships, regulatory interfaces and strategic options; Economy Topic 20 retains detailed trade modelling.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Agreement labels do not determine legal scope.
2. The operative text controls obligations.
3. FTAs create preferential rather than universal access.
4. Rules of origin police preference eligibility.
5. Services and mobility need separate commitments.
6. Sensitive sectors may be phased or excluded.
7. Utilisation is distinct from signature.
8. Bilateral integration can coexist with WTO membership.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) How can FTA proliferation indicate both continuing globalisation and fragmentation?

**Model answer:** FTAs continue globalisation by lowering barriers, linking services and investment, and creating cross-border rules among participating states. Yet each agreement has its own origin rules, standards, exclusions and dispute procedures. Firms therefore face a patchwork rather than one universal rulebook, while non-parties may be disadvantaged. FTA proliferation expands selected integration but fragments the institutional landscape through which that integration occurs.

**Adjacent rubric:** integration 4 + fragmentation 4 + synthesis 2 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming that every agreement called comprehensive removes every barrier.

**Transition:** Before comparing agreements, the legal-status verbs must be placed in their proper order.

## Lesson 6: A press release is not entry into force

📚 Book context: canonical owner unit on negotiation, conclusion, signature, ratification and implementation queried

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

### Legal-status chain

```text
launch → negotiate → conclude text → sign
        → domestic approval/ratification → enter into force
        → implement → utilise → measure outcomes
             [a failure can occur at every arrow]
```

Trade diplomacy requires exact verbs. A launch begins bargaining; conclusion records a negotiated outcome; signature authenticates and expresses political commitment; ratification or domestic approval satisfies constitutional requirements; entry into force activates legal obligations; utilisation and outcomes follow later.

✅ India-EFTA TEPA was signed on 10 March 2024 and entered into force on 1 October 2025.[^EFTA] ✅ India-UK CETA was signed on 24 July 2025 and entered into force on 15 July 2026.[^UK] ✅ India-EU FTA negotiations were concluded at the 16th summit on 27 January 2026, but conclusion did not itself make the agreement operative.[^EU]

⚠️ Every current-affairs answer should date-stamp the legal stage rather than use “deal done” language.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Launch begins negotiation.
2. Conclusion is not signature.
3. Signature is not entry into force.
4. Domestic procedures can intervene.
5. Entry into force activates obligations.
6. Implementation differs from utilisation.
7. Outcomes require later evidence.
8. Current claims must name stage and date.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why does legal-stage precision matter in evaluating a trade agreement?

**Model answer:** Each legal stage creates a different degree of commitment. Negotiation or conclusion may establish political direction without operative rights; signature authenticates the text but may await domestic procedures; entry into force activates obligations. Implementation, utilisation and economic outcomes occur later. Collapsing these stages exaggerates certainty and can convert a negotiating announcement into a false claim about market access. Precise verbs therefore protect both legal accuracy and policy evaluation.

**Adjacent rubric:** stage chain 5 + evaluation reason 3 + conclusion 2 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reporting concluded negotiations as an agreement already in force.

**Transition:** With the status chain secured, India’s agreement portfolio can be read without mixing announcements and obligations.

### Utilisation funnel

```text
PREFERENCE OFFERED
   ↓ can the product qualify?
RULE OF ORIGIN
   ↓ can compliance be proved?
CERTIFICATE + CUSTOMS
   ↓ can the buyer accept it?
STANDARD / CONFORMITY
   ↓ can the firm deliver competitively?
ACTUAL UTILISATION
```

A preferential tariff is useful only if the exporter qualifies and claims it. Rules of origin prevent simple trans-shipment. Product standards, testing, customs documentation, awareness and logistics can determine whether legal access becomes commercial access. Small firms may face proportionately higher fixed compliance costs.

⚠️ Utilisation therefore connects external policy with domestic capability. Government may negotiate access, but firms require information, certification infrastructure and predictable procedures. Liberal rules without enforcement invite circumvention; excessively complex rules suppress legitimate use.

This is also a federal issue: production clusters, ports, skills and regulatory agencies are distributed across states. A coherent agreement strategy must include subnational implementation rather than end at signature.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Preference must be claimed to be useful.
2. Rules of origin prevent trans-shipment.
3. Origin proof creates compliance costs.
4. Standards can obstruct nominal access.
5. Testing capacity affects exporters.
6. Small firms face higher fixed burdens.
7. States shape implementation capability.
8. Utilisation is an outcome distinct from legal coverage.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why can a high-coverage FTA still produce low utilisation?

**Model answer:** Formal coverage does not remove practical barriers. Exporters may fail origin tests, lack certification, face complex customs procedures, or remain unaware of preferences. Standards and logistics can outweigh the tariff advantage, especially for small firms with high fixed compliance costs. Importers may also prefer established suppliers. Utilisation therefore depends on usable rules, administrative capacity and firm competitiveness, not the percentage of tariff lines nominally covered.

**Adjacent rubric:** origin 3 + non-tariff constraints 3 + firm capacity 2 + judgement 2 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating tariff-line coverage as a complete measure of agreement success.

**Transition:** Practical access also depends on the standards written into modern agreements.

### Distribution map

```text
AGREEMENT CHANGE
 ├─ exporting sectors → scale, jobs, investment
 ├─ import-competing sectors → adjustment pressure
 ├─ consumers → price/variety effects
 ├─ workers → transition and skill effects
 └─ states → uneven cluster, port and revenue exposure
POLICY TASK: identify → consult → sequence → compensate/upskill → review
```

National gains can conceal concentrated losses. Exporters may gain scale; import-competing producers may face rapid adjustment; consumers may gain choice; workers may confront displacement; and states differ in industrial structure, ports, skills and fiscal exposure.

⚠️ Distribution is central to foreign-policy durability. An agreement that ignores visible losses may provoke backlash and weaken India’s negotiating credibility. Consultation should not become a veto by every incumbent, but it must reveal exposure and implementation needs.

### Adaptive portfolio loop

```text
OBJECTIVES: growth | resilience | equity | sustainability | autonomy
       ↓ choose instrument mix
WTO + FTA + standards diplomacy + diversification + domestic capability
       ↓ monitor indicators
utilisation | concentration | adjustment | compliance | dispute exposure
       ↓ review and recalibrate
```

The strategic choice is not openness or autonomy in the abstract. India needs a portfolio matched to sectoral conditions. Multilateral rules protect predictability; FTAs create preferential access; standards diplomacy reduces regulatory friction; diversification manages concentration; domestic capability increases choice.

⚠️ Adaptive statecraft uses indicators and review triggers. Preference utilisation, supplier concentration, export complexity, compliance cost, worker adjustment and dispute exposure reveal different dimensions. A policy may be retained, modified or sunset as evidence changes.

The final principle is **open where exchange builds capability, guarded where dependence creates intolerable risk, and cooperative where rules cannot be shaped alone**. This is a decision framework, not a slogan of self-sufficiency or unrestricted liberalisation.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Strategy should be portfolio-based.
2. WTO and FTAs serve different functions.
3. Standards diplomacy is a strategic instrument.
4. Diversification manages concentration risk.
5. Domestic capability expands external choice.
6. Indicators should match objectives.
7. Review triggers enable adaptation.
8. Neither autarky nor unconditional openness is sufficient.

### Concept check

**Question:** (20 marks) Formulate an adaptive external-economic strategy for India.

**Model answer:** India should combine WTO engagement for baseline predictability, carefully sequenced FTAs for market and services access, standards diplomacy for interoperability, and diversification for critical dependencies. Domestic infrastructure, skills, technology and adjustment support must convert legal access into capability. Sectoral risk should be measured through utilisation, concentration, substitutability, compliance and distribution indicators. Policies should include review and sunset triggers. India should remain open where exchange raises productivity, guard dependencies with severe disruption costs, and build coalitions where unilateral rule-taking would constrain development.

**Adjacent rubric:** portfolio 6 + domestic capability 4 + indicators/review 5 + calibrated principle 5 = **20 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Reducing strategic autonomy in trade to permanent protection of every domestic producer.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks

**Question:** Elucidate why sovereign nationalism does not imply the end of globalisation.

**Model answer (100 words):** Sovereign nationalism reasserts control over strategic sectors, borders and external dependence through tariffs, subsidies, screening, sanctions and export controls. Yet states continue trading, attracting capital, sharing technology and signing preferential agreements. The present order therefore reflects selective interdependence rather than autarky. Multilateral liberalisation has weakened, but bilateral and plurilateral integration continues. Governments seek the gains of exchange while limiting vulnerabilities in critical technologies, energy and supply chains. India illustrates this duality: it pursues FTAs and WTO engagement, yet preserves sensitive-sector safeguards and diversification options. Sovereign nationalism changes the terms, sectors and institutions of globalisation; it does not erase cross-border interdependence.

**Adjacent rubric:** definition 2 + manifestations 3 + continuing integration 3 + India/synthesis 2 = **10 marks**.

## 15 marks

**Question:** Evaluate FTAs as instruments of India’s external economic policy.

**Model answer (118 words):** FTAs can widen goods and services access, attract investment, support mobility and create regulatory cooperation. They also help India pursue integration when multilateral liberalisation is stalled. Their value, however, depends on legal stage, rules of origin, standards, customs capacity and firm utilisation. Gains are uneven: competitive exporters and consumers may benefit while import-competing sectors, workers and states face adjustment. A patchwork of agreements can also raise compliance costs and divert trade. India should therefore judge each agreement against a counterfactual and a portfolio strategy: strategic fit, usable preferences, diversification, distribution and preservation of justified policy space. Consultation, phased commitments, standards infrastructure and post-entry review are essential. FTAs are enabling frameworks, not automatic guarantees of exports or strategic alignment.

**Adjacent rubric:** benefits 4 + limitations 4 + India evaluation framework 5 + judgement 2 = **15 marks**.

## 20 marks

**Question:** Analyse how trade, technology and climate measures transmit geopolitical competition into India’s domestic policy choices.

**Model answer (150 words):** Geopolitical competition increasingly travels through economic networks. Tariffs alter market access; sanctions and export controls interrupt transactions or capability acquisition; industrial subsidies redirect investment; and carbon-related standards condition entry into major markets. These measures affect Indian sectors differently according to concentration, substitutability, technology intensity and compliance capacity. Domestic consequences include pressure on firms, workers, consumers, states and development priorities.

India needs a layered response. It should verify each measure’s legal basis, map exposure, maintain critical flows and use bilateral or rules-based consultations. Medium-term policy should diversify suppliers and markets, strengthen testing and carbon-accounting systems, develop feasible technologies and support worker and firm adjustment. WTO engagement preserves a baseline, while FTAs and regulatory cooperation can create alternative access. Coalitions can press for equitable climate finance, transparent standards and development-sensitive rules. The objective is not isolation but risk-adjusted openness: gain markets and knowledge while reducing intolerable dependencies and retaining legitimate policy space.

**Adjacent rubric:** transmission channels 6 + domestic effects 4 + response portfolio 7 + judgement 3 = **20 marks**.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Core thesis

- Globalisation and sovereign nationalism are co-present; the contemporary form is selective interdependence.
- Integration continues through trade, capital, information, technology and preferential agreements.
- Control appears through tariffs, screening, sanctions, export controls, subsidies and standards.
- India must combine openness, resilience, equity, sustainability and strategic autonomy.

## 2. Transmission and instrument discipline

- Use the chain: external measure → channel → exposed actor → domestic distribution → response.
- Channels include trade, investment, technology, labour, standards and climate.
- Tariffs protect or bargain at the border; sanctions coerce targets; export controls restrict capabilities; industrial policy builds domestic production.
- Every measure requires its own legal authority, date, scope and termination status.
- A temporary measure can influence contracts and negotiations beyond its legal duration.

## 3. WTO baseline and reform

- WTO remains the baseline for rules, schedules, notifications, committees and disputes.
- Appellate Body lost quorum on 10 December 2019 and has had zero members since 30 November 2020.
- India is not an MPIA participant; appeal paralysis creates costs for complainants and respondents.
- Consensus protects participation but can impede agreement.
- Fisheries Subsidies was adopted on 17 June 2022, entered into force on 15 September 2025, and was accepted by India on 20 July 2026.
- Agriculture reform must balance producer livelihood, consumer food security and trade effects.
- Bali’s public-stockholding protection is interim and conditional; India seeks a permanent solution.
- Topic 12 owns full institutional architecture.

## 4. Agreements and legal stages

- Agreement titles do not determine obligations; texts do.
- Correct chain: launch → conclude → sign → approve/ratify → enter into force → implement → utilise → evaluate.
- UAE CEPA entered into force on 1 May 2022; Australia ECTA on 29 December 2022.
- EFTA TEPA: signed 10 March 2024; in force 1 October 2025.
- UK CETA: signed 24 July 2025; in force 15 July 2026.
- EU FTA negotiations concluded 27 January 2026; conclusion was not entry into force.
- New Zealand FTA signed 27 April 2026.
- India announced RCEP non-entry on 4 November 2019.
- A portfolio should be judged by strategic fit, usable access, diversification, distribution and policy space.

## 5. Utilisation and deep rules

- Rules of origin determine qualification and prevent trans-shipment.
- Standards, testing, customs, logistics and awareness determine actual preference use.
- Modern agreements cover services, investment, digital rules, mobility, labour and environment.
- Deep rules should be reciprocal, transparent, feasible and compatible with legitimate regulation.
- Market access on paper is not commercial utilisation.

## 6. Distribution, federalism and legitimacy

- Aggregate gain may coexist with concentrated sectoral or regional loss.
- Map exporters, import competitors, workers, consumers and states separately.
- Adjustment can include phasing, safeguards, skills, credit, standards support and social protection.
- Consultation supplies information and legitimacy but should not become an incumbent veto.
- Domestic durability increases international credibility.

## 7. Geoeconomics, supply chains and climate

- Geoeconomic leverage rests on central nodes in finance, technology and logistics.
- Effectiveness depends on coalition breadth, substitutability, enforcement and target adaptation.
- De-risking reduces concentration while preserving exchange; it differs from decoupling and autarky.
- Vulnerability depends on concentration, criticality, substitutability, lead time and buffers.
- EU CBAM entered its definitive regime on 1 January 2026.
- Climate-linked trade measures require analysis of purpose, design, equity, compliance and sectoral impact.
- India’s 2022 NDC commitments must not be confused with achieved outcomes.

## 8. Advanced evaluation

- Do not attribute every post-agreement change to the agreement.
- Use counterfactuals, preference utilisation, trade creation/diversion, composition and distribution.
- Standards power can externalise domestic regulation through large-market effects.
- Responses include alignment, interoperability, challenge and credible alternatives.
- Compound shocks require separate legal diagnosis and coordinated short-, medium- and long-term action.
- Adaptive strategy uses indicators, review and sunset triggers.

## 9. Exam traps

- “Waning” is not “ended.”
- An FTA is not inherently anti-globalisation.
- Conclusion is not signature or entry into force.
- Coverage is not utilisation.
- WTO impairment is not total irrelevance.
- Public-stockholding protection is not an unconditional permanent exemption.
- Climate-linked measures are neither purely environmental nor automatically protectionist.
- Resilience is not maximum domestic production.

## 10. Ready answer formula

Define the trend precisely; classify the instrument; trace the transmission channel; show domestic distribution; use one dated Indian or institutional anchor; acknowledge a counterpoint; and conclude with a calibrated portfolio of WTO engagement, agreements, standards capacity, diversification, adjustment and review.
