### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a political map alone insufficient for analysing India's West Asia policy?

**Model answer:** Because India must overlay borders with sea lanes, chokepoints, energy infrastructure, diaspora concentrations and conflict corridors.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not equate every West Asian crisis with an automatic closure of Hormuz or Bab-el-Mandeb; disruption has degrees.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (10 marks; no more than 150 words):** Explain why geography makes West Asia structurally important to India.

**Responsive model:** West Asia is India's near extended neighbourhood across the Arabian Sea. Its importance arises from the concentration of hydrocarbon suppliers, trade and investment partners, Indian workers, ports and strategic sea lanes. The Persian Gulf exits through the Strait of Hormuz, while Red Sea traffic depends on Bab-el-Mandeb and Suez. Conflict can therefore transmit to India through freight, insurance, energy prices, evacuation needs and diplomatic pressure. Yet geography does not prescribe alignment with one camp. It rewards a differentiated policy: maritime awareness, diversified supply, country-specific partnerships, support for lawful navigation, humanitarian diplomacy and resilient citizen-protection systems. Geography is thus the structural base of policy, not a substitute for political judgement.

**Adjacent rubric (10 marks):** geographic logic 3 + Indian interests 3 + differentiated policy 3 + conclusion 1 = 10

### Concept check

**Question:** What distinguishes portfolio diplomacy from equidistance?

**Model answer:** Portfolio diplomacy builds unequal, issue-specific cooperation according to Indian interests; equidistance mechanically keeps the same distance from all actors.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not describe multi-alignment as having no preferences or principles; choices vary by issue.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (15 marks; no more than 250 words):** Assess the logic of India's non-exclusive engagement with West Asia.

**Responsive model:** India's West Asia policy is best understood as a non-exclusive portfolio. It combines energy and investment ties with Gulf states, defence and technology cooperation with Israel, connectivity interests with Iran, and support for Palestinian statehood. This reduces dependence on a single regional axis and creates issue-specific coalitions. It also serves multiple Indian interests: secure supplies, maritime access, citizen welfare, trade, food security and strategic autonomy. However, the portfolio is not costless. Israel-Iran confrontation, sanctions, Gaza-related humanitarian concerns and US pressure can force difficult choices. India should therefore distinguish principles from instruments: oppose terrorism, protect civilians, support dialogue and sovereignty, while using quiet diplomacy, diversified supply, consular readiness and project-level safeguards. The approach succeeds when simultaneous partnerships produce usable outcomes without creating vetoes over Indian choice.

### Energy trilemma

```text
                    SECURITY
             reliable volume and routes
                    /       \
                   /         \
          AFFORDABILITY ---- SUSTAINABILITY
          price and terms     transition and emissions

Policy test = supplier + route + contract + reserve + demand-side resilience
```

*Visual reading:* this energy trilemma supplies the specific reasoning frame for energy security: the trilemma and the import baseline.

### Verified anchors

- ✅ Fact — PPAC's FY2024-25 baseline records crude-oil import dependence at 88.2%.
- ✅ Fact — The canonical owner records about 88.7% for FY2025-26 as a provisional PPAC baseline.
- ✅ Fact — Import dependence measures imported crude as a share of consumption; it is not West Asia's supplier share.

### Analytical build

1. Energy security is a trilemma because the cheapest short-run barrel may not be the safest route or the most sustainable long-run choice.
2. Diversifying suppliers can lower concentration risk even while aggregate import dependence stays high.
3. Contracts differ: term supply offers predictability, while spot purchases offer flexibility but can expose buyers to volatility.
4. Strategic reserves buy time; they do not permanently replace production, imports or demand management.
5. Refinery configuration constrains switching because crude grades are not perfectly interchangeable.
6. Economy owns detailed price, forex and transition mechanics; this topic owns the foreign-policy bargaining consequences.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can supplier diversification improve security without reducing the import-dependence ratio?

**Model answer:** Because it spreads source concentration while total imported crude can remain the same share of domestic consumption.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not confuse a diversified import basket with energy independence.

### Responsive Mains practice

**Question (15 marks; no more than 250 words):** How should India integrate energy security into its West Asia diplomacy?

**Responsive model:** India should treat energy security as a portfolio rather than a single supplier relationship. With crude import dependence at 88.2% in FY2024-25, foreign policy must combine reliable Gulf partnerships with source diversification, flexible contracts, maritime-route protection and strategic reserves. Diplomacy should seek predictable volumes and investment across refining, petrochemicals, storage and cleaner energy, while retaining the capacity to use competitive spot opportunities. Chokepoint risk requires coordination on safe navigation and alternative logistics; sanctions risk requires legal and financial due diligence. Domestic transition, efficiency and production remain essential because diplomacy cannot remove aggregate dependence. The objective is not withdrawal from West Asian energy, but greater bargaining resilience: no single supplier, route, currency channel or geopolitical crisis should be able to impose intolerable costs on India.

India therefore needs a layered response: diversified suppliers and crude grades, adequate inventories, flexible procurement, shipping information, naval and diplomatic coordination, and de-escalatory engagement with all regional actors. Hormuz also illustrates why supplier diversification alone is incomplete; route concentration can persist across several Gulf suppliers. The correct objective is resilient continuity, not the unrealistic elimination of all maritime exposure.

India should therefore use a two-level design—GCC dialogue for shared norms and regional projects, and country-specific mechanisms for investment, labour, logistics and security. # ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks — 150 words

**Question:** Explain why supplier diversification alone cannot guarantee India's West Asian energy security.

**Model:** Supplier diversification reduces exposure to one producer but may leave several hidden concentrations. Gulf suppliers can share the Strait of Hormuz; similar crude grades may not be interchangeable with every refinery configuration; contracts may depend on the same currency, banks, insurers or shipping capacity. A crisis can therefore disrupt a diverse supplier list through one route or commercial channel. India needs a four-part portfolio: source diversity, route awareness, term-and-spot contract flexibility and usable reserves. It also needs refinery compatibility, lawful payment options, shipping information and demand-side resilience. Domestic production, efficiency and transition gradually reduce the volume exposed. Diversification is thus necessary, but security depends on whether alternative barrels can be legally purchased, physically transported, technically processed and delivered in time.

**Rubric (10 marks):** limitation of supplier count 3 + correlated risks 3 + integrated response 3 + judgement 1 = 10.

## 15 marks — 250 words

**Question:** “India's West Asia policy is an exercise in differentiated interdependence.” Discuss.

**Model:** India depends on West Asia through energy, trade, investment, sea lanes, citizens and connectivity, but the dependence is neither uniform nor one-way. Saudi Arabia and the UAE combine energy with capital and strategic councils; Qatar is especially important for LNG; Oman adds Arabian Sea geography and a trade framework. Israel contributes defence, agriculture, water and innovation. Iran offers connectivity through Chabahar but carries sanctions risk. Palestine remains central to India's two-state and humanitarian position. India therefore uses differentiated instruments—CEPAs, strategic councils, project minilaterals, corridor arrangements, consular systems and political dialogue—rather than one regional template.

Interdependence creates leverage and vulnerability. Gulf partners value India's market, skills and demand, while India needs reliable supply and citizen access. Conflicts can raise freight costs, compress diplomatic space and trigger evacuation. India should preserve non-exclusive relations, diversify energy and routes, state consistent principles, and evaluate initiatives from agreement to outcome. Differentiated interdependence captures both mutual value and the need for sovereign choice.

**Rubric (15 marks):** concept 3 + country differentiation 5 + vulnerabilities 3 + instruments and judgement 4 = 15.

## 20 marks — 250 words

**Question:** Formulate an integrated Indian strategy for a decade of recurrent conflict and connectivity competition in West Asia.

**Model:** India should organise strategy around resilience, plural relationships and delivery. First, energy policy must diversify sources, routes, contracts and reserves while expanding efficiency and clean-energy partnerships. Second, India should retain non-exclusive political ties with Gulf states, Israel, Iran and Palestine, anchored in opposition to terrorism, civilian protection, sovereignty, dialogue and a two-state horizon. Third, connectivity should be a portfolio: pursue bankable IMEC segments, protect lawful Chabahar operations and strengthen maritime logistics without declaring any single route transformative before use.

Fourth, economic statecraft should convert CEPAs, sovereign capital and technology cooperation into realised trade, local capability, food-security and clean-energy outcomes. Fifth, citizen protection requires current registries, labour safeguards, rehearsed evacuation options and after-action reviews. Sixth, scenario cells should integrate mission, energy, shipping, aviation and sanctions indicators with pre-authorised thresholds.

External-power competition should be met selectively: India need not match military deployments, but should offer dependable projects, market access and diplomatic continuity. A public scorecard should measure concentration risk, operation, utilisation, citizen reach and relationship usability under pressure. The goal is sustained access and sovereign choice, not alignment or regional primacy.

- UAE: CEPA in force from 1 May 2022; high-density trade, logistics, investment, technology and minilateral relationship.
- Qatar: strategic partnership established through the 18 February 2025 Joint Statement; LNG remains central.
- Oman: CEPA signed 18 December 2025 and officially recorded as in force from 1 June 2026; Arabian Sea geography adds value.
- Agreements create frameworks; utilisation and realised projects establish outcomes.

## 4. Citizens and social linkage

- Large Indian communities create interdependence and a duty of protection.
- Remittances matter, but Topic 09 owns full diaspora and consular architecture.
- Citizen protection chain: prevention, registration, communication, negotiated access, movement, reception and review.
- Operation Sindhu: MEA recorded 4,415 evacuated by 27 June 2025—3,597 from Iran and 818 from Israel.
- An evacuation count is an output, not a complete measure of readiness, speed, equity or safety.

## 5. Israel and Palestine

- Full India-Israel diplomatic relations date from 1992.
- Bilateral depth includes defence, agriculture, water, innovation, trade and political dialogue.
- Diversity makes ties durable, but regional conflict and India's independent positions remain constraints.
- India supports a sovereign, independent, viable and united Palestinian state living beside Israel within secure and recognised borders.
- India recognised Palestine in 1988 and maintains a Representative Office in Ramallah.
- Two-state policy combines Palestinian self-determination and Israeli security.
- The Arab Peace Initiative was an Arab proposal endorsed by the Arab League; Israel did not sign it.
- Abraham Accords normalisation and the Arab Peace Initiative are distinct.

## 6. Iran and Chabahar

- Iran offers geography, civilisational ties, regional relevance and connectivity potential.
- Sanctions operate through banks, insurance, shipping, investment and risk appetite.
- India and Iran held their 20th Joint Commission Meeting on 8 May 2025.
- The Chabahar ten-year terminal contract was signed on 13 May 2024 between IPGL and Iran's PMO.
- A port becomes a corridor only with hinterland links, customs, finance, security and regular cargo.
- The US revoked the 2018 Chabahar sanctions exception effective 29 September 2025.
- Chabahar has strategic option value, but it is not sanction-proof or automatically transformative.

## 7. Functional platforms and corridors

- I2U2: India, Israel, UAE and US; functional minilateral, not defence alliance.
- Its 14 July 2022 Joint Statement prioritised water, energy, transport, space, health and food security.
- Proposed food parks and renewable-energy projects must be judged by delivery milestones.
- IMEC principles MoU: 9 September 2023, eight parties.
- IMEC envisages India-Gulf and Gulf-Europe components; it is not yet an end-to-end operational corridor merely because an MoU exists.
- Compare I2U2, IMEC and Chabahar by function, legal status, finance, operation, use and outcome.
- A portfolio can preserve option value because the initiatives serve different sectors and geographies.

## 8. Conflict, balancing and external powers

- India combines condemnation of terrorism with civilian protection, humanitarian access, dialogue and a two-state political horizon.
- Iran-Israel escalation affects citizens, flights, shipping, sanctions and energy expectations.
- External actors include the US, China, Russia and Europe, but regional states retain agency and hedge across partners.
- India should compete selectively through markets, projects, skills and continuity rather than imitate every military deployment.
- De-escalation, consular readiness and commercial resilience are mutually reinforcing.
- India can preserve channels without claiming mediation leverage it does not possess.

## 9. Implementation and advanced evaluation

- Match tools to problems: reserve for interruption, compliance guidance for sanctions, consular planning for citizens, project governance for corridors.
- Use the truth ladder: announcement -> agreement -> finance -> construction -> operation -> use -> outcome.
- Stress tests must include correlated shocks across suppliers, routes, contracts and intermediaries.
- Legal optionality is not commercial optionality.
- A risk dashboard needs thresholds, owners and executable alternatives.
- Evaluate future policy through energy resilience, realised economic depth, citizen safety, principled consistency, autonomy and project delivery.

## 10. Final answer line

India's West Asia policy should pursue resilient pluralism: dependable but non-exclusive partnerships, diversified energy and connectivity options, citizen protection, consistent humanitarian and political principles, and implementation discipline that converts access into outcomes without surrendering sovereign choice.
