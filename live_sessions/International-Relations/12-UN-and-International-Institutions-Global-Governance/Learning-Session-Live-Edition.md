### Institutional identity matrix

| Institutional type | Legal basis | Typical authority | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| UN principal organ | UN Charter | Charter-assigned function | UNGA, UNSC, ICJ |
| Specialised agency | Separate constitution linked to UN | Sectoral standard-setting/programmes | WHO, UNESCO |
| Treaty body/regime | Specific convention | Monitors or implements treaty | UNFCCC process |
| Independent organisation/court | Separate treaty | Mandate defined by members | WTO, ICC |

Global governance is the collective management of cross-border problems through law, institutions, norms and cooperation. “UN body” is not one legal category. Principal organs derive authority from the Charter; specialised agencies have separate constitutions and relationship agreements; treaty bodies depend on particular conventions; and institutions such as the WTO or ICC are legally distinct.

⚠️ Classification determines membership, voting, legal effect, finance and accountability. A declaration may carry political authority without being a treaty. A court may bind consenting parties while lacking general enforcement machinery. Before evaluating performance, identify the institution’s legal basis and assigned task.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Global governance combines law, institutions and cooperation.
2. UN bodies do not share one legal status.
3. Principal organs derive authority from the Charter.
4. Specialised agencies have separate constitutions.
5. Treaty bodies depend on particular conventions.
6. Independent organisations have distinct memberships.
7. Legal basis determines power and accountability.
8. Classification must precede evaluation.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Why must an answer classify an international institution before judging its effectiveness?

**Model answer:** Effectiveness depends on the authority an institution was actually given. A Charter organ, specialised agency, treaty body and independent court differ in membership, voting, finance, jurisdiction and enforcement. Judging WHO as if it were a world government or the General Assembly as if it issued binding Security Council decisions creates a false failure test. Classification identifies the correct mandate, available instruments and implementation chain, enabling fair evaluation.

**Adjacent rubric:** classification 3 + mandate link 3 + examples 2 + judgement 2 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every organisation associated with the UN as a subordinate department with identical powers.

**Transition:** The taxonomy established, Lesson 2 places authority inside the Charter architecture.

### Charter architecture

```text
UN CHARTER PURPOSES
peace and security | friendly relations | cooperation | human rights
                         ↓
SIX PRINCIPAL ORGANS
UNGA ─ UNSC ─ ECOSOC ─ ICJ ─ Secretariat ─ Trusteeship Council
                         ↓
state consent + assigned powers + political limits
```

✅ The UN Charter establishes six principal organs: General Assembly, Security Council, Economic and Social Council, International Court of Justice, Secretariat and Trusteeship Council.[^CHARTER] The organisation has **193 members**.[^MEMBERS] Sovereign equality, peaceful settlement, non-use of force and cooperation structure the order, while Article 2(7) protects domestic jurisdiction subject to Charter enforcement powers.

The UN is not a central government. It combines universal membership with differentiated authority: the Assembly deliberates, the Council can adopt binding peace-and-security decisions, the Court applies law within jurisdiction, and the Secretariat administers and exercises diplomatic initiative.

### REVISION NOTES

1. The Charter states UN purposes and principles.
2. The UN has six principal organs.
3. Membership currently stands at 193.
4. Sovereign equality is a Charter principle.
5. The UN is not a world government.
6. Authority is differentiated by organ.
7. Domestic jurisdiction has Charter qualifications.
8. State cooperation remains indispensable.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) How does the Charter combine sovereign equality with differentiated institutional power?

**Model answer:** All members possess sovereign equality in the Charter order and participate in the General Assembly. Yet the Charter assigns different powers to different organs and states. The Security Council has primary peace-and-security responsibility, while five permanent members possess veto power; the ICJ acts only within legal jurisdiction; the Secretariat administers rather than legislates. The system therefore combines formal equality with functional hierarchy intended to secure both legitimacy and major-power participation.

**Adjacent rubric:** sovereign equality 3 + differentiated organs 4 + rationale 2 + conclusion 1 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming sovereign equality means every state possesses identical authority in every UN organ.

**Transition:** The Charter map now separates the Assembly’s universal voice from the Council’s concentrated power.

The General Assembly is the UN’s universal deliberative organ. It discusses Charter matters, adopts the budget, elects members to several bodies and participates in appointments. Most resolutions are recommendations, but repeated and broadly supported practice can shape expectations, political legitimacy and, with additional evidence, the development of norms.

✅ Security Council reform is discussed through intergovernmental negotiations in the Assembly’s informal plenary.[^IGN] ⚠️ Tharoor’s “renewed, not retired” philosophy includes strengthening the Assembly beyond rhetorical declaration. ### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Assess the significance and limits of General Assembly resolutions.

**Model answer:** General Assembly resolutions express the views of the universal membership, set agendas, authorise programmes and can consolidate political legitimacy. Repeated resolutions may contribute evidence relevant to norm development, but they do not automatically create binding law. Their effect depends on wording, voting pattern, subsequent state practice and implementation by states or competent organs. The Assembly is therefore influential in representation and norm entrepreneurship, yet institutionally limited in command and enforcement.

**Adjacent rubric:** significance 4 + legal limit 3 + conditions 2 + judgement 1 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Calling every General Assembly resolution legally binding merely because a large majority supported it.

### Collective-security fork

```text
DISPUTE / THREAT
   ├─ Chapter VI orientation → peaceful settlement and recommendations
   └─ Chapter VII finding → sanctions / authorisation / binding measures
                       ↓
          15-member Council voting
          P5 veto can block substance
```

✅ The Security Council has **15 members**: China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and United States as permanent members, plus ten members elected for two-year terms.[^UNSC] It has primary responsibility for international peace and security. Chapter VI supports peaceful settlement; Chapter VII permits binding measures after the relevant determination.

The veto was designed to keep major powers inside collective security. ⚠️ It can prevent direct institutional confrontation among them, yet also block action and produce selective enforcement. Elected members contribute agenda, negotiation and legitimacy, but do not possess equivalent structural power.

### REVISION NOTES

1. The UNSC has 15 members.
2. Five permanent members possess veto power.
3. Ten elected members serve two-year terms.
4. The Council has primary security responsibility.
5. Chapter VI concerns peaceful settlement.
6. Chapter VII permits coercive measures.
7. The veto is both stabiliser and blocker.
8. Elected members still shape deliberation.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Explain the veto’s dual character in the UN security system.

**Model answer:** The veto reflects the judgment that collective security cannot operate against a united opposition from a major power. By keeping the principal powers inside the institution, it can prevent formal UN confrontation and preserve participation. Yet the same device blocks action during major-power rivalry, creates selectivity and weakens representative legitimacy. It is therefore both a constitutional stabiliser and a source of paralysis, making reform politically desirable but procedurally difficult.

**Adjacent rubric:** stabilising logic 4 + blocking effect 4 + synthesis 2 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Presenting the veto only as an arbitrary privilege without explaining its original institutional rationale.

**Transition:** Concentrated political authority needs an administrative and diplomatic engine: the Secretariat.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Distinguish Secretariat reform from Security Council reform.

**Model answer:** Secretariat reform concerns administration, mandate delivery, staffing, budgeting and organisational efficiency. The Secretary-General can initiate such changes within delegated authority and member-approved resources. Security Council reform changes representation, categories of membership, size or veto arrangements and therefore implicates Charter amendment and member-state ratification. UN80 may improve implementation, but it cannot alter who sits permanently on the Council. The two tracks address effectiveness and legitimacy through different legal mechanisms.

### Court comparison

| Feature | ICJ | ICC |
|---|---|---|
| Legal position | UN principal judicial organ | Separate Rome Statute court |
| Subjects | States in contentious cases | Individuals accused of specified crimes |
| Jurisdiction | Consent-based bases | Statute and admissibility rules |
| Outputs | Judgments and advisory opinions | Criminal decisions |

International law draws from treaties, custom and general principles, supplemented by judicial decisions and scholarship. Compliance rests on consent, reciprocity, domestic incorporation, reputation, institutions and, sometimes, collective measures.

The ICJ decides disputes between states where jurisdiction exists and gives advisory opinions when authorised bodies request them. Advisory opinions are authoritative but not binding as contentious judgments are between parties. ✅ On **23 July 2025**, the ICJ delivered its advisory opinion on states’ obligations concerning climate change.[^ICJ]

The ICC prosecutes individuals under the Rome Statute and is not a UN principal organ. India is not a State Party to the Rome Statute.

### REVISION NOTES

1. Treaties and custom are major sources of law.
2. ICJ contentious jurisdiction requires a legal basis.
3. ICJ cases are between states.
4. Advisory opinions are authoritative but non-binding.
5. ICC prosecutes individuals.
6. ICC is separate from the UN principal organs.
7. India is not a Rome Statute State Party.
8. Compliance has legal and political mechanisms.

### Concept check

**Question:** (10 marks) Differentiate an ICJ advisory opinion from a contentious judgment.

**Model answer:** A contentious judgment resolves a dispute between states that fall within the Court’s jurisdiction and is binding between those parties for that case. An advisory opinion answers a legal question referred by an authorised UN organ or agency; it is authoritative but not formally binding on states. Both clarify law and can influence conduct, but their jurisdictional basis and legal effect differ. Political follow-through and domestic implementation remain important for either output.

**Adjacent rubric:** contentious judgment 4 + advisory opinion 4 + implementation 2 = **10 marks**.

**Misconception to avoid:** Confusing the ICJ’s state-to-state jurisdiction with the ICC’s prosecution of individuals.

**Transition:** Legal authority often lacks its own enforcement arm; peacekeeping demonstrates operational dependence on consent and contributions.

**Question:** (10 marks) Evaluate the CTC’s effectiveness without attributing powers it does not possess.

**Model answer:** The CTC made counter-terrorism duties a sustained Security Council agenda, monitors implementation of resolution 1373 and uses CTED assessments to identify capacity gaps and facilitate assistance. It has adapted attention to financing, borders and emerging technologies. However, it does not investigate or prosecute and depends on national implementation, reporting quality and political cooperation. Its effectiveness is therefore strongest in norm diffusion, assessment and capacity-building, but uneven in measurable prevention and enforcement.

Specialised agencies are autonomous organisations linked to the UN through agreements. Their constitutions define membership, governing bodies, finance and mandate. The proper accountability chain includes member oversight, technical independence, audit, transparency, affected-population feedback and measurable results.

**Question:** (10 marks) Why does technical expertise not eliminate political dependence in specialised agencies?

**Model answer:** Expertise enables agencies to define standards, collect data and advise governments, but member states control legal authority, access, finance and domestic implementation. Voluntary funding may also steer priorities, while contested evidence can become politicised. Agencies therefore need scientific independence and transparent methods, yet cannot operate outside interstate consent. Their performance should be judged by technical quality, equitable agenda-setting, implementation support and accountability rather than an unrealistic expectation of sovereign command.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## 10 marks

**Question:** Distinguish legitimacy from effectiveness in international institutions.

**Model answer (83 words):** Legitimacy concerns whether authority is representative, consent-based and exercised through fair procedures. Effectiveness concerns whether an institution produces relevant outputs and improves outcomes relative to a realistic alternative. The two can diverge: universal participation may slow decisions, while a smaller technical body may act quickly but lack broad ownership. Accountability and equity mediate the tension through transparency, review and fair distribution. Evaluation should therefore identify the mandate, affected actors and time horizon rather than treat speed or popularity as a complete performance measure.

**Adjacent rubric:** legitimacy 3 + effectiveness 3 + trade-off 2 + evaluation 2 = **10 marks**.

## 15 marks

**Question:** Evaluate India’s case and strategy for reform of the UN Security Council.

**Model answer (108 words):** India’s claim rests on population, democratic legitimacy, economic and diplomatic capacity, peacekeeping contribution and the under-representation of developing regions. It seeks expansion in permanent and non-permanent categories and supports Africa’s claim. The case is strengthened by responsible participation, but no contribution creates automatic legal entitlement.

Strategy requires more than G4 coordination. India must accommodate African priorities, engage states favouring elected-seat expansion, reassure regional competitors and press for genuine text-based negotiation. Working-method reforms and greater troop-contributor participation can deliver interim gains. The decisive constraint remains Charter amendment and ratification by all P5 members. India should therefore combine principled advocacy with coalition-building and avoid presenting process milestones as completed reform.

**Adjacent rubric:** case strengths 5 + constraints 4 + coalition strategy 4 + judgement 2 = **15 marks**.

## 20 marks

**Question:** International institutions remain indispensable but structurally dependent on states. Analyse.

**Model answer (147 words):** International institutions aggregate information, create rules, coordinate action, settle disputes, mobilise finance and supply legitimacy. The UN Charter organs, WHO, WTO, courts and financial institutions address problems that no state can manage alone. Their authority, however, is delegated. States determine membership, finance, jurisdiction, implementation and, in the Security Council, veto outcomes.

This dependence produces uneven performance. WHO can issue standards but relies on reporting and national health systems; the ICJ clarifies law but lacks general coercive machinery; peacekeeping depends on mandates, contributions and political consent; Bretton Woods resources do not automatically ensure representative voice. Yet state dependence is also a source of legitimacy because cooperation cannot sustainably bypass sovereign consent.

Reform should therefore target specific constraints: representative governance, predictable finance, transparent delegation, capacity support, proportionate compliance and periodic review. India’s “renewed, not retired” approach is appropriate—participate within existing institutions while building coalitions for structural and working-method reform.

**Adjacent rubric:** indispensable functions 5 + state dependence 5 + examples 4 + reform 4 + judgement 2 = **20 marks**.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Global-governance taxonomy

- Principal organs derive authority from the UN Charter.
- Specialised agencies have separate constitutions and relationship agreements.
- Treaty bodies and regimes derive authority from named conventions.
- WTO and ICC are legally distinct from UN principal organs.
- Political declarations and compacts do not automatically become treaties.
- Always verify membership, mandate, voting rule, jurisdiction and legal effect.

## 2. Charter architecture

- The UN has 193 members and six principal organs.
- UNGA supplies universal deliberation, budget and elections; most resolutions are recommendatory.
- UNSC has 15 members: five permanent and ten elected.
- Chapter VI and Chapter VII roles must be distinguished.
- ECOSOC coordinates economic, social and development work.
- ICJ decides inter-state disputes within jurisdiction and gives advisory opinions.
- Secretariat administers and exercises good offices; UN80 is administrative, not Council reform.
- Trusteeship Council remains a principal organ although operations are suspended.

## 3. International law and compliance

- Treaties, custom and general principles are core sources of international law.
- ICJ contentious judgments bind parties to the case; advisory opinions are authoritative but non-binding.
- ICC prosecutes individuals under the Rome Statute and is not an ICJ substitute.
- India is not a Rome Statute State Party.
- Compliance combines consent, reciprocity, domestic law, reputation and institutional pressure.

## 4. Peace and security machinery

- Peacekeeping rests on Council mandate, voluntary contributions and political strategy.
- Consent, impartiality and limited force are core principles.
- India’s first all-women FPU deployment to Liberia began in 2007.
- CTC was established by resolution 1373 on 28 September 2001.
- CTED supports assessment and technical assistance; its mandate runs to 5 January 2029.
- The ISIL/Al-Qaida sanctions committee handles listings; FATF is separate.

## 5. Specialised agencies

- WHO’s WHA, Executive Board and Secretariat have distinct roles.
- WHO coordinates and sets standards but does not command national health policy.
- Pandemic Agreement adopted 20 May 2025; PABS negotiations continued in 2026.
- Adoption is not signature, ratification or entry into force.
- UNESCO works across education, science, culture and communication.
- US withdrawal notification announced in 2025 takes effect only at end-December 2026.
- Funding concentration creates programme and legitimacy vulnerability.

## 6. Finance and trade institutions

- IMF supports monetary cooperation and temporary balance-of-payments needs.
- World Bank supports development finance and knowledge.
- IMF quota shares affect resources and voting power.
- The 16th review’s 50 per cent equiproportional increase did not change relative shares.
- IDA21 mobilised USD 100 billion for FY2025–28; finance expansion is not voice reform.
- WTO functions must be separated: rules, monitoring, negotiation, panels and appeals.
- Economy owns operational lending and trade mechanics.

## 7. UNSC reform and India

- India’s case combines representation, capability, contribution and legitimacy.
- India supports expansion in permanent and non-permanent categories.
- Its April 2026 position backed a 26-member Council, African claims and veto parity.
- G4 comprises India, Brazil, Germany and Japan.
- African and Uniting for Consensus positions prevent a single Global South model.
- IGN continues, but continuation is not Charter amendment.
- The 28 July 2026 decision carried negotiations into the eighty-first Assembly session.[^GA2026]
- Charter change requires demanding approval and ratification including all P5.
- “Renewed, not retired” captures reform rather than abandonment.

## 8. Contemporary governance gaps

- Pact for the Future adopted 22 September 2024.
- Global Digital Compact and Declaration on Future Generations are its annexes.
- BBNJ entered into force on 17 January 2026.
- ICJ delivered its climate advisory opinion on 23 July 2025.
- Health, climate, oceans, AI and digital governance share fragmentation and capacity gaps.
- India links safety and rights with development finance, technology and representation.

## 9. Evaluation and advanced design

- Evaluate legitimacy, effectiveness, accountability, equity and adaptability separately.
- Institutional design chooses membership, voting, delegation, finance, compliance and review.
- No design maximises speed, representation and sovereignty simultaneously.
- Crisis interoperability requires role clarity, lawful data, finance and review.
- Coalition strategy should match each institution.
- Process evidence must not be reported as completed structural reform.

## 10. Exam traps and answer formula

- UNGA recommendation is not a binding UNSC decision.
- ICJ is not ICC; CTC is not the sanctions committee; WHO is not a world government.
- More funding is not necessarily more voting voice.
- Peacekeeping contribution does not automatically yield a permanent seat.
- Adoption is not entry into force; announced withdrawal is not completed withdrawal.
- Answer formula: classify → mandate → instrument → output → implementation limit → India → reform → calibrated verdict.
