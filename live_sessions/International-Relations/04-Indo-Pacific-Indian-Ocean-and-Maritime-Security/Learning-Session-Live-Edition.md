### Why the term matters

The **Indian Ocean Region (IOR)** is a geographic and institutional space centred on the Indian Ocean and its littoral and island states. The **Indo-Pacific** is a wider strategic frame connecting the Indian and Pacific Oceans through trade, security, ecology and institutional interaction. It is not a new ocean, a treaty area or a fixed military bloc.

✅ **Fact / canonical doctrine:** India's official formulation emphasises a **free, open and inclusive Indo-Pacific**, respect for sovereignty and international law, freedom of navigation, peaceful dispute settlement and ASEAN centrality. ⚠️ **Analytical inference:** the concept lets India treat events east of Malacca and west of the Persian Gulf as connected without declaring every issue part of a China-containment project.

### Three nested maps

| Map | Central question | This topic's depth |
|---|---|---|
| Indian Ocean | How are India's immediate sea lanes, islands and littorals secured? | Full |
| Indo-Pacific | How do two ocean systems connect through rules, partnerships and competition? | Full maritime application |
| Bilateral/major-power relations | How does India manage a specific power? | Cross-link to Topics 01 and 03 |

China appears here as an external maritime actor, not as a substitute for the complete India-China relationship owned by Topic 03. Quad appears as a maritime mechanism, while Topic 10 owns its full institutional history and non-maritime agenda. Strategic autonomy is applied here but taught fully in Topic 01.

### Strongest objection and reply

**Objection:** Indo-Pacific is merely a geopolitical label that militarises Asia.
**Reply:** military competition is one layer, but the frame also contains shipping, fisheries, disaster response, marine ecology, digital information-sharing and international law.
**Residual risk:** inclusive language cannot by itself prevent bloc perceptions; India must show inclusion through public goods and partner choice.


### Revision notes

1. The Indian Ocean Region and the Indo-Pacific are related but not identical.
2. The Indo-Pacific links the Indian and Pacific Oceans through flows, rules and security.
3. India's official vocabulary is free, open and inclusive.
4. ASEAN centrality distinguishes India's approach from a closed alliance frame.
5. The Indo-Pacific is neither a treaty nor a precisely bounded legal zone.
6. Indian Ocean applications receive full depth in this topic.
7. Full China and grouping profiles remain cross-owned by Topics 03 and 10.
8. A strong answer separates geographic fact from strategic interpretation.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Explain why the Indo-Pacific should be understood as a connected strategic space rather than a military bloc. (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** The Indo-Pacific connects the Indian and Pacific Oceans through shipping, energy flows, production networks, ecological systems and security externalities. For India, the frame links its immediate Indian Ocean interests with Malacca, Southeast Asia and the western Pacific. It therefore supports cooperation on navigation, disaster relief, maritime domain awareness, fisheries and international law, not merely naval balancing. India's official approach stresses a free, open and inclusive region, sovereignty, peaceful dispute settlement and ASEAN centrality. These principles distinguish a strategic geography from a closed alliance. Yet military competition remains real, and inclusive language can be read as balancing if delivery is dominated by a few powers. India must therefore combine capable partnerships with regional institutions and public goods. The Indo-Pacific is best treated as an interconnected policy theatre whose order remains contested, not as a single organisation or anti-China coalition.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 2 marks definition + 3 marks connective mechanisms + 3 marks Indian principles + 2 marks qualification = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the Indo-Pacific wider than the Indian Ocean but narrower than a universal foreign-policy label?

**Model answer:** It joins the Indian and Pacific maritime systems around specific flows, rules and security interactions; it does not absorb every bilateral or continental issue.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating Indo-Pacific, Quad and Indian Ocean Region as interchangeable names.

### Sea lane before warship

A **sea lane of communication (SLOC)** is a route used by commercial and naval shipping. A **chokepoint** is a narrow passage through which a large flow is concentrated. India's maritime interest begins with reliable movement of energy, merchandise, food, fertiliser, containers and people.

✅ **Canonical fact:** the owner records that more than half of India's trade traverses the Strait of Malacca. Use this as a source-grounded dependence claim, not as a timeless percentage for every category of trade. Hormuz links Gulf energy to the Indian Ocean; Bab-el-Mandeb and Suez connect Asia-Europe traffic; the Cape route is a longer alternative when the Red Sea corridor is unsafe.

### How disruption travels inland

| Maritime event | First effect | Wider effect |
|---|---|---|
| attack or elevated risk | slower sailing / rerouting | higher insurance and freight |
| port congestion | vessel waiting | inventory and production delay |
| chokepoint closure | capacity shock | energy and commodity stress |
| cable damage | data-routing loss | financial and communication risk |

⚠️ **Inference:** vulnerability depends on concentration, duration, spare shipping capacity, inventories and substitute routes. A temporary risk premium is not the same as a complete closure; a longer route is not automatically unavailable.

### Strategic response ladder

India can diversify suppliers and routes, build reserves and ports, escort or coordinate shipping where lawful, share information, strengthen coastal and island infrastructure and support de-escalation. Sea power is only one part of maritime resilience.

**Objection:** chokepoints make naval control decisive.
**Reply:** control matters, but commercial resilience, diplomacy, insurance, port capacity and alternative routing shape the actual economic outcome.
**Residual:** alternatives often cost more and can themselves become congested.


### Revision notes

1. SLOC means sea lane of communication.
2. A chokepoint concentrates flows in a narrow passage.
3. Malacca links the Indian Ocean with East Asian production and markets.
4. Hormuz is central to Gulf energy flows.
5. Bab-el-Mandeb connects the Indian Ocean to the Red Sea and Suez.
6. Cape diversion raises time, fuel, freight and insurance costs.
7. Disruption, diversion and complete closure are different claims.
8. Maritime resilience combines naval, economic, infrastructure and diplomatic tools.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** How do maritime chokepoints convert distant conflict into an Indian economic-security problem? (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** Chokepoints concentrate shipping into narrow passages, so violence or uncertainty can disrupt flows even without a formal blockade. Hormuz affects Gulf energy movement; Bab-el-Mandeb and Suez connect Asia with Europe; Malacca connects the Indian Ocean with East Asian markets. A threat causes vessels to slow, reroute or pay higher war-risk insurance. Longer voyages consume more fuel and ship capacity, raising freight costs and delaying inventories. These effects can transmit to Indian energy prices, exports and industrial inputs. The response cannot be purely naval. India needs route and supplier diversification, strategic reserves, efficient ports, maritime-domain awareness, lawful security coordination and diplomacy aimed at de-escalation. Alternative routes such as the Cape reduce dependence on one corridor but add cost and congestion. Thus chokepoints turn geographic concentration into economic vulnerability, while resilience depends on both security provision and commercial adaptation.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 2 marks mechanism + 3 marks chokepoint examples + 3 marks transmission chain + 2 marks response = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is route diversion not equivalent to a chokepoint closure?

**Model answer:** Diversion preserves movement through a costlier and longer route, whereas closure removes passage capacity at the chokepoint; their economic severity differs.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming every security incident stops all shipping or can be solved only by naval deployment.

### Four layers of interest

India's maritime interests are economic, legal, security and developmental at once. The coastline hosts ports, cities, refineries, naval and coast-guard facilities and fishing communities. Maritime zones contain resources and regulatory responsibilities. Island territories extend surveillance and logistics reach. Undersea cables carry digital traffic.

| Layer | Indian interest | Typical vulnerability |
|---|---|---|
| coast and ports | trade, industry, communities | attack, cyclone, congestion |
| territorial sea / EEZ | safety, resources, enforcement | illegal fishing, pollution |
| islands | awareness, logistics, connectivity | remoteness, ecology, capacity |
| wider commons | navigation and trade | piracy, coercion, route disruption |

Andaman and Nicobar's location near eastern approaches gives India observation and logistics value, but geography does not create automatic sea control. Lakshadweep is relevant to western approaches and island development, yet ecological carrying capacity and community consent constrain infrastructure choices.

### Maritime strategy is not naval strategy alone

The Navy deters and operates at distance; the Coast Guard performs law-enforcement, search-and-rescue and pollution roles; ports and shipping authorities manage commerce; coastal states and police matter near shore; diplomacy secures access and cooperation. ⚠️ **Inference:** India's advantage comes from connecting these actors, not from treating a larger fleet as a complete maritime policy.

**Objection:** island infrastructure necessarily improves security.
**Reply:** useful runways, jetties, communications and logistics can improve reach.
**Residual:** poorly designed projects may damage ecosystems, displace livelihoods or create expensive assets without sustained use.


### Revision notes

3. The EEZ creates resource rights and governance duties, not full sovereignty.
4. Andaman and Nicobar improve eastern awareness and access.
5. Lakshadweep matters to western approaches and island development.
6. Undersea cables make seabed security economically important.
7. Navy, Coast Guard, ports, states and diplomacy have distinct roles.
8. Island infrastructure must be tested for use, ecology, resilience and local benefit.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** India's maritime strategy is broader than fleet expansion. Explain. (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** Fleet capability is necessary for deterrence, presence and distant response, but India's maritime interests also include trade, ports, energy terminals, fisheries, islands, coastal communities, undersea cables and marine ecology. These require differentiated institutions. The Navy handles war-fighting and naval diplomacy; the Coast Guard performs constabulary, search-and-rescue and pollution functions; port and shipping bodies sustain commerce; coastal states and police secure the near shore; diplomacy enables access, information-sharing and common rules. Andaman and Nicobar and Lakshadweep extend awareness and logistics, but infrastructure creates value only when it is usable, resilient, ecologically responsible and locally legitimate. A complete strategy therefore connects military capacity with law enforcement, economic resilience, island development, environmental protection and regional partnership. Measuring strategy only by ship numbers would ignore the systems that maritime power is meant to protect.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 2 marks thesis + 4 marks interest layers + 2 marks institutional differentiation + 2 marks island qualification = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** What is lost when maritime security is reduced to naval strength?

**Model answer:** Commercial resilience, coast-guard enforcement, port continuity, island welfare, cable protection, ecology and diplomacy disappear from the analysis.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating maritime strategy with combat-fleet procurement.

### A vision, not a treaty

✅ **Verified fact:** SAGAR was articulated in Mauritius on 12 March 2015 as **Security and Growth for All in the Region**. It connects India's security with the safety, capacity and prosperity of regional partners. It creates no collective-defence clause and no automatic right of access.

✅ **Verified current fact:** on 12 March 2025 at Port Louis, the Prime Minister announced **MAHASAGAR — Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions**. The official formulation links trade for development, capacity-building, technology-sharing, concessional finance and mutual security. The same address continued to affirm SAGAR. Therefore MAHASAGAR widens the frame; it does not erase SAGAR.

### The delivery test

| Declaration | Instrument | Outcome test |
|---|---|---|
| shared security | training, patrol assets, information | partner can detect/respond |
| shared growth | connectivity, credit, trade | local use and benefit |
| regional solidarity | HADR and evacuation | speed, consent and effectiveness |
| wider cooperation | technology and finance | transparent, viable implementation |

⚠️ **Inference:** doctrine gains credibility only when partners experience reliable delivery without political domination or unsustainable projects. Topic 01 owns the full evolution of strategic autonomy and India's world-view; here those principles are applied to maritime policy.

**Objection:** MAHASAGAR is merely a rebranding.
**Reply:** its stated geographic and developmental reach is wider.
**Residual:** without project and capability outcomes, a broader slogan can produce broader expectations rather than broader influence.


### Revision notes

1. SAGAR expands to Security and Growth for All in the Region.
2. It was articulated in Mauritius on 12 March 2015.
3. SAGAR is a policy vision, not a treaty or alliance.
4. Security and development are deliberately linked.
5. MAHASAGAR was announced at Port Louis on 12 March 2025.
6. MAHASAGAR expands to Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions.
7. MAHASAGAR extends rather than replaces SAGAR.
8. Doctrine must be evaluated through partner capacity, implementation and consent.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish SAGAR from MAHASAGAR and assess the test of their credibility. (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** SAGAR, articulated in Mauritius on 12 March 2015, links Security and Growth for All in the Region. It frames Indian maritime policy around partner security, capacity-building, connectivity, disaster response and shared prosperity rather than a formal alliance. MAHASAGAR, announced at Port Louis on 12 March 2025, means Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions. It broadens the vision toward the Global South through trade, technology-sharing, concessional finance, sustainable capacity and mutual security. It is an extension, not a replacement, because the announcement continued to reaffirm SAGAR. Their credibility cannot be measured by slogans or summit counts. It depends on delivered infrastructure, usable partner capability, timely HADR, transparent finance, ecological responsibility and respect for partner sovereignty. A wider doctrine raises expectations; influence grows only if implementation remains reliable and locally beneficial.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 3 marks SAGAR + 3 marks MAHASAGAR distinction + 2 marks instruments + 2 marks delivery qualification = 10.

### From principle to work programme

✅ **Verified fact:** India announced the **Indo-Pacific Oceans Initiative (IPOI)** at the 14th East Asia Summit in Bangkok on 4 November 2019. Its seven pillars are maritime security; maritime ecology; maritime resources; capacity-building and resource-sharing; disaster-risk reduction and management; science, technology and academic cooperation; and trade, connectivity and maritime transport.

IPOI is voluntary and partner-led. It is neither a treaty nor a centralised funding institution. This flexibility lowers entry costs but can make implementation uneven.

### Why ASEAN centrality matters

ASEAN centrality means that inclusive ASEAN-led forums remain a central convening platform. The East Asia Summit supplies leaders-level strategic dialogue; the ASEAN Regional Forum supports wider security dialogue and confidence-building. India's policy presents IPOI and the ASEAN Outlook on the Indo-Pacific as complementary.

| Broad forum | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| ASEAN-led architecture | legitimacy and inclusiveness | consensus can be slow |
| minilateral mechanism | speed and capability | may trigger bloc perceptions |
| bilateral partnership | tailored delivery | fragmented coverage |

⚠️ **Inference:** India uses institutional layering because no single mechanism can provide legitimacy, speed, capacity and local ownership simultaneously.

**Objection:** ASEAN centrality is rhetorical when major powers use smaller groups.
**Reply:** minilaterals can deliver specific public goods while ASEAN-led forums preserve broad participation.
**Residual:** if smaller mechanisms routinely bypass regional consultation, complementarity can become hollow.

Its voluntary, partner-led design allows states to cooperate without joining an alliance or accepting a central command. India also presents IPOI as complementary to the ASEAN Outlook on the Indo-Pacific. IPOI therefore operationalises inclusion when partner-led projects are funded, coordinated and connected to ASEAN-centred regional architecture.

### Different zones, different legal powers

✅ **Treaty fact:** UNCLOS permits a coastal state to establish a territorial sea up to 12 nautical miles from lawful baselines. Sovereignty extends there, subject to the right of innocent passage for ships of all states. Passage is innocent while it is continuous and not prejudicial to the coastal state's peace, good order or security.

The contiguous zone extends up to 24 nautical miles for specified enforcement relating to customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws. The EEZ may extend to 200 nautical miles. In it, the coastal state has sovereign rights over resources and specified jurisdiction, but other states retain navigation and overflight freedoms under UNCLOS. **Sovereign rights are not full sovereignty.**

| Zone | Coastal-state position | Other-state position |
|---|---|---|
| territorial sea | sovereignty | innocent passage |
| contiguous zone | limited preventive/enforcement powers | navigation |
| EEZ | resource rights and specified jurisdiction | navigation, overflight and lawful uses |
| high seas | no national sovereignty | shared freedoms subject to law |

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Why must a maritime-security answer distinguish territorial-sea sovereignty from EEZ sovereign rights? (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** UNCLOS assigns different legal powers to different maritime zones. In the territorial sea, extending up to 12 nautical miles, the coastal state exercises sovereignty, although ships of all states retain innocent passage subject to treaty conditions. In the exclusive economic zone, extending up to 200 nautical miles, the coastal state has sovereign rights over natural resources and specified jurisdiction over matters such as installations and marine protection. It does not possess complete territorial sovereignty: other states retain navigation, overflight and related lawful freedoms. Confusing the two can turn a resource dispute into an exaggerated claim of territorial control or wrongly deny navigation rights. Legal precision also clarifies enforcement choices and escalation risks. UNCLOS therefore balances coastal-state interests with shared maritime mobility rather than granting one uniform bundle of powers across all waters.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 3 marks zone distinction + 3 marks rights of coastal state + 2 marks other-state freedoms + 2 marks significance = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does an EEZ not become national territory merely because the coastal state controls resources?

⚠️ **Advanced inference:** the best maritime strategy creates an escalation ladder with credible off-ramps. It signals capability and resolve while preserving room to revise attribution.

**Objection:** visible restraint invites incremental coercion.
**Reply:** restraint can coexist with denial, presence, evidence release and targeted cost imposition.
**Residual:** adversaries may exploit thresholds, requiring pre-communicated red lines and rapid coalition consultation.


### Revision notes

1. Ambiguous incidents require competing hypotheses.
2. Evidence thresholds reduce both underreaction and premature escalation.
3. Cable repair ships and commercial traffic may need protection.
4. Deterrence by denial reduces the expected gain from coercion.
5. Redundancy is a security capability.
6. Partner consent remains necessary for access.
7. An escalation ladder should include off-ramps.
8. Restraint can coexist with visible denial and targeted costs.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** How can India deter grey-zone maritime coercion without becoming trapped in premature escalation? (20 marks / 250 words)

**Model response:** India should combine persistent awareness, denial and a graduated response ladder. It must first maintain competing hypotheses for ambiguous activity—accident, crime, grey-zone pressure or conflict preparation—and establish evidence thresholds using vessel identity, repeated patterns, communications, cyber indicators and proximity to critical infrastructure.

At lower levels, India can intensify surveillance, notify partners, escort repair or commercial vessels and issue lawful warnings. Persistent coercion can trigger coordinated presence, public attribution where evidence permits, diplomatic pressure and targeted economic costs. An armed attack requires proportionate defence and partner support.

Deterrence by denial is central: redundant cables, alternative routes, protected repair capability, distributed logistics and stronger partner patrol capacity reduce the coercer's expected gain. Consent-based access and crisis communication prevent protective action from appearing expansionist.

Clear red lines, rapid consultation and visible off-ramps preserve flexibility. Restraint should not mean passivity; it should make escalation evidence-based, reversible where possible and focused on protecting civilian flows.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 4 marks ambiguity framework + 5 marks response ladder + 4 marks denial/resilience + 4 marks escalation control + 3 marks verdict = 20.

### Concept check

**Question:** How does deterrence by denial differ from deterrence by punishment?

**Model answer:** Denial reduces the adversary's chance of achieving its objective; punishment threatens costs after or during the attempt.

**Misconception to avoid:** Believing credible deterrence always requires immediate military retaliation.

### Avoid the label trap

“Net security provider” is a strategic-studies label often used for India's aspiration. Without a dated official doctrinal source, it should not be presented as a completed or uncontested status. India can provide net benefits in HADR, awareness, training or anti-piracy while still lacking capacity in other contingencies.

### Evaluate the chain

**Announcement -> finance -> construction -> operation -> use -> outcome -> durability**

Each step can fail. An exercise proves activity, not alliance. A donated patrol craft proves transfer, not sustained readiness. A chairship proves convening role, not institutional control. A logistics agreement proves a legal route, not crisis access. A common picture proves information, not enforcement.

### Adaptive portfolio

India should combine:
- sea-lane resilience and capable naval presence;
- Coast Guard and partner constabulary capacity;
- IFC-IOR and diversified sensors;
- lawful, consent-based logistics;
- island and port resilience;
- IORA/IONS convening;

**Reply:** qualitative evidence can be incorporated.
**Residual:** some outcomes are counterfactual; absence of crisis does not prove which policy prevented it.

1. Net security provider should be treated as an aspiration unless officially and specifically sourced.
2. Maritime performance must be evaluated through an implementation chain.
3. Chairship means convening advantage, not control.
4. Transferred assets require crews, fuel, spares and doctrine.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Construct an evaluation framework for India's Indo-Pacific and Indian Ocean maritime strategy. (20 marks / 250 words)

**Model response:** India's maritime strategy should be evaluated across seven dimensions. First, awareness: partner counts and sensors must translate into verified alerts and action. Second, access: logistics agreements and ports should be tested for services and crisis availability. Third, capacity: fleet size matters less than readiness, endurance, maintenance and trained personnel. Fourth, partnership: meetings, credit and transferred assets must produce local use, trust and sustained capability. Fifth, law: India should apply UNCLOS and treaty-status distinctions consistently. Sixth, resilience: alternate routes, reserves, cables, ports and repair capacity should preserve flows during shocks. Seventh, sustainability: blue-economy projects must improve livelihoods without eroding ecosystems.

The implementation chain is announcement, finance, construction, operation, use, outcome and durability. Chairships, exercises or common pictures prove only intermediate stages. “Net security provider” should remain a qualified aspiration rather than a presumed status.

A sound portfolio combines naval deterrence, coast-guard cooperation, MDA, consent-based logistics, island resilience, inclusive institutions, transparent connectivity and crisis off-ramps. Success is reliable regional benefit plus sovereign Indian choice.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Practice 1 — 10 marks / 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish a rules-based maritime order from a major-power sphere of influence.

**Model answer:** A rules-based maritime order allocates rights and duties through accepted law and institutions. UNCLOS distinguishes territorial-sea sovereignty, EEZ resource rights, navigation freedoms and dispute procedures. It allows coastal and smaller states to make claims without accepting the hierarchy of the strongest navy. Institutions such as IMO create common safety and environmental standards, while IORA enables inclusive dialogue.

A sphere of influence, by contrast, assumes that a dominant power may restrict partner choice because of geography or capability. It treats access and alignment as entitlements rather than negotiated outcomes.

Rules are not self-enforcing: powerful states may interpret them selectively, and institutions need capacity and compliance. Yet the remedy is more consistent law, adjudication, transparency and partner capacity—not exclusive control. India's interest lies in combining credible maritime power with respect for sovereignty, navigation and consent.

**Rubric:** 3 marks conceptual distinction + 3 marks legal/institutional evidence + 2 marks limitation + 2 marks India-focused conclusion = 10.

## Practice 2 — 15 marks / 250 words

**Question:** “Maritime domain awareness is a necessary but insufficient public good.” Analyse with reference to India's regional architecture.

**Model answer:** Maritime domain awareness combines detection, identification, analysis and information-sharing about activities affecting security, economy, safety or environment. India's IFC-IOR, established at Gurugram in 2018, provides a regional fusion and liaison platform. IPMDA extends commercially available, unclassified data through existing centres, while the 2026 IPMSC and Common Operating Picture announcements deepen collaboration.

MDA is a public good because piracy, illegal fishing, dark shipping, distress and pollution cross borders; one state's report can improve every partner's picture. Liaison officers and shared alerts can reduce uncertainty and support coordinated action.

It remains insufficient for four reasons. Sensors may miss, spoof or misidentify vessels. Data governance and vendor concentration create sovereignty and dependency concerns. Legal authority differs across territorial seas, EEZs and high seas. Most importantly, smaller partners may lack ships, aircraft, fuel, trained crews or prosecutorial capacity to act.

India should therefore link IFC-IOR alerts with coast-guard training, patrol and maintenance support, clear protocols, diversified sensors and legal cooperation. Effectiveness should be measured by verified alerts, response time and action enabled, not partner count alone. Awareness becomes security only when a trusted picture reaches a lawful and sustainable response.

**Rubric:** 3 marks concept + 4 marks Indian architecture + 4 marks insufficiency + 2 marks reforms + 2 marks verdict = 15.

## Practice 3 — 20 marks / 250 words

**Question:** Formulate an Indian maritime strategy that reconciles deterrence, inclusive regional leadership, blue-economy sustainability and strategic autonomy.

**Model answer:** India needs a portfolio strategy rather than a fleet-only doctrine. First, credible naval readiness, island infrastructure and diversified logistics should deter coercion and protect sea-lane continuity. Second, Coast Guard cooperation, IFC-IOR, IONS and partner training should address piracy, illegal fishing, pollution and search-and-rescue at lower escalation levels.

Third, regional leadership must remain inclusive. IORA, ASEAN-centred forums, SAGAR and partner-specific arrangements should supply public goods without claiming an exclusive sphere. Island states must retain agency; Indian influence should rest on transparent finance, reliable delivery and consent.

Fourth, UNCLOS consistency, IMO implementation and BBNJ preparedness should connect power with law. Fifth, blue-economy policy should protect fish stocks, ecosystems and coastal livelihoods while building resilient ports, shipping and energy. Sixth, strategic autonomy requires diversified suppliers, indigenous maintenance, data control and non-exclusive access.

For crises, India needs persistent awareness, competing hypotheses, proportional response ladders, communication and off-ramps. Performance should be tested through the chain from announcement to finance, operation, use, outcome and durability.

The objective is not uncontested dominance. It is sufficient denial capability, reliable regional benefit, lawful freedom of action and resilient maritime flows in a plural, contested order.

**Rubric:** 4 marks deterrence/capacity + 4 marks inclusive leadership + 3 marks law + 3 marks blue economy + 3 marks autonomy/crisis control + 3 marks integrated verdict = 20.

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

## 1. Concept and geography

- Indo-Pacific connects the Indian and Pacific Oceans through flows, rules, ecology and security; it is not an organisation.
- India's official frame is free, open and inclusive, with sovereignty, law and ASEAN centrality.
- Indian Ocean interests begin with SLOCs and chokepoints but extend to ports, islands, cables, fisheries and climate.
- Malacca, Hormuz and Bab-el-Mandeb concentrate risk; diversion, disruption and closure must be distinguished.

## 2. Doctrine

- SAGAR: Security and Growth for All in the Region; Mauritius, 12 March 2015.
- MAHASAGAR: Mutual and Holistic Advancement for Security and Growth Across Regions; Port Louis, 12 March 2025.
- MAHASAGAR extends rather than replaces SAGAR.
- IPOI: announced 4 November 2019; seven voluntary pillars; complements ASEAN Outlook.
- Topic 01 owns full strategic-autonomy doctrine; here it means sustainable sovereign maritime choice.

## 3. Law and order

- Territorial sea: up to 12 nm, sovereignty subject to innocent passage.
- Contiguous zone: up to 24 nm for specified enforcement.
- EEZ: up to 200 nm; resource sovereign rights, not full sovereignty.
- India accepted the 7 July 2014 Annex VII Bangladesh maritime-boundary award.
- BBNJ entered into force 17 January 2026; India signed 25 September 2024 but was not yet a ratified party at the cut-off.
- Senkaku/Diaoyu is an East China Sea sovereignty dispute.

## 4. Threats and agencies

- Traditional: interstate coercion; grey zone: ambiguous state-linked pressure; non-traditional: piracy, terrorism, trafficking, IUU fishing, pollution and disasters.
- Navy: deterrence, war-fighting, distant presence and naval diplomacy.
- Coast Guard: constabulary enforcement, SAR, coastal coordination and pollution response.
- Sagar Prahari Bal: raised 2009 for naval-base and vulnerable-point protection.
- Awareness, authority, assets, prosecution and recovery form one response chain.

## 5. Maritime domain awareness

- IFC-IOR: Indian Navy, Gurugram, established 2018.
- November 2025 official baseline: 25 partner countries and liaison officers from 15.
- IPMDA: Quad, Tokyo, 24 May 2022; unclassified commercial AIS/RF data through existing centres.
- IPMSC: launched 26 May 2026; newer surveillance collaboration with initial IOR focus.
- COP expansion does not equal common command.
- Data value depends on identity, law, patrol capacity, governance and funding.

## 6. Institutions and partnerships

- IORA: 23 members, 12 dialogue partners, Ebène secretariat; India chair 2025-27.
- IONS: voluntary naval symposium from 2008; India chair from 20 February 2026.
- ReCAAP addresses piracy/armed-robbery cooperation.
- CSC status terms—member, observer, guest and decision to join—must not be collapsed.
- Maldives Joint Vision: 7 October 2024; concrete economic and maritime partnership instrument.
- HADR builds trust only with consent, local command and sustained recovery.

## 7. Capability architecture

- LEMOA 2016 supports case-by-case reciprocal logistics; not bases or alliance.
- COMCASA 2018 supports secure communications; BECA 2020 supports geospatial exchange.
- MILAN began in 1995; broad inclusive naval diplomacy.
- Malabar builds high-end interoperability among the four Quad navies but no defence obligation.
- AUKUS: announced 15 September 2021; nuclear-powered, conventionally armed submarines plus advanced capabilities.
- Supplier diversification supports autonomy only with interoperability, spares, maintenance and data control.

## 8. IMO and blue economy

- IMO Convention entered into force in 1958.
- SOLAS concerns safety; MARPOL pollution.
- 0.50% global sulphur cap outside ECAs from 1 January 2020.
- Hong Kong ship-recycling convention in force 26 June 2025.
- 2023 GHG Strategy aims at net zero by or around 2050.
- Blue economy combines fisheries, ports, shipping, tourism, energy and biodiversity with ecological sustainability and equitable livelihoods.

## 9. Advanced evaluation

- Indian Ocean order is contested because island states retain agency and external powers remain present.
- Quad's asymmetric alliance ties enable flexibility but limit automatic cohesion.
- Commercial data lowers classification barriers but creates vendor, governance and funding risks.
- Deterrence by denial uses redundancy, resilience and protected continuity.
- “Net security provider” is a qualified aspiration, not a presumed completed status.
- Evaluate through announcement -> finance -> construction -> operation -> use -> outcome -> durability.

## 10. Final answer line

India's maritime influence will rest not on alliance imitation or exclusive control, but on sufficient deterrent capacity, lawful domain awareness, trusted island partnerships, resilient trade routes, sustainable ocean development and the ability to offer inclusive regional public goods while preserving sovereign choice.
