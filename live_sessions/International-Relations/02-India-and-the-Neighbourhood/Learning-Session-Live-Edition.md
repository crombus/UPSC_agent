```text
Land borders, maritime proximity and security relevance create different ties.
```

### Start with geography, then add strategy

India's neighbourhood is not a uniform ring of small states. Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar are land neighbours; Sri Lanka and Maldives are maritime neighbours. The canonical owner also treats Afghanistan as a **near-neighbour** because developments there affect Indian security and connectivity even though present access is interrupted by territory under Pakistan's control.

The analytical unit is therefore **proximity plus interdependence**. A neighbour can affect border security, river flows, trade routes, migration, energy exchange, ethnic ties, maritime awareness or India's access to wider regions. Each channel has its own institutions and risks.

### Three boundaries prevent duplication

1. China appears here only where a border neighbour or an external actor in another neighbour affects neighbourhood policy; Topic 03 owns the full China relationship.
2. Maldives and Sri Lanka are taught as neighbours; Topic 04 owns wider Indo-Pacific architecture.
3. SAARC, BIMSTEC and other groupings appear only as neighbourhood instruments; Topic 10 owns their complete institutional comparison.

**Objection:** a broad definition makes the neighbourhood limitless.
**Reply:** retain a disciplined test—direct land/maritime proximity or a canonical near-neighbour whose security and connectivity effects are immediate.
**Residual:** strategic relevance can change, so scope must not become a pretext for claiming a sphere of exclusive control.


### Revision notes

1. India's neighbourhood includes land and maritime relationships.
2. Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar are land neighbours.
3. Sri Lanka and Maldives are maritime neighbours.
4. Afghanistan is treated as a strategic near-neighbour in the owner.
5. Proximity creates security, water, market, mobility and cultural interdependence.
6. Every neighbour retains agency; geography does not create Indian entitlement.
7. China, Indo-Pacific and grouping detail are cross-linked, not duplicated.
8. A neighbour-specific answer should identify the operative channel of interdependence.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Define India's neighbourhood as a strategic policy space rather than a mere map. (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** India's neighbourhood begins with land and maritime proximity but becomes strategic through interdependence. Land borders connect security, migration, rivers, trade and ethnic communities; maritime proximity adds sea-lane, disaster-response and coastal-security concerns. Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar are land neighbours, while Sri Lanka and Maldives are maritime neighbours. Afghanistan remains a near-neighbour because its political and security trajectory affects India despite interrupted territorial access. This space is not homogeneous: each relationship has a different history, power balance and domestic politics. Nor does proximity confer a sphere of control. Sound neighbourhood policy therefore combines differentiated bilateral instruments, regional cooperation and respect for partner agency, while cross-linking rather than duplicating the full China, Indo-Pacific or grouping modules.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 2 marks scope + 3 marks interdependence channels + 3 marks differentiation/agency + 2 marks ownership-qualified conclusion = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a list of bordering countries insufficient to define neighbourhood policy?

**Model answer:** Because policy responds not only to location but to the specific security, river, economic, mobility, cultural and maritime interdependencies created by proximity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating all neighbours as one geopolitical bloc with identical interests.

### Doctrine-to-instrument ladder

```text
POWER ASYMMETRY
      |
      v
REASSURANCE
  non-reciprocity toward smaller neighbours
  respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity
  non-interference
  no territory used against another
  bilateral peaceful settlement

DELIVERY
  grants · credit · connectivity · trade · energy · HADR · exchanges
      |
      v
TEST
  does cooperation survive delay, dispute and change of government?
```

### Two ideas, not one slogan

**Neighbourhood First** is a policy priority: nearby states receive sustained political attention, development partnership, connectivity and first-responder support. **The Gujral Doctrine** is a normative method for managing asymmetry, especially through non-reciprocal accommodation of smaller neighbours alongside sovereignty, non-interference, security restraint and peaceful bilateral settlement.

Non-reciprocity does not mean India has no interests. It means India may provide a benefit without demanding an equal concession at the same moment because long-run stability is itself a return. The doctrine seeks reassurance, not charity.

### Where doctrine becomes policy

Power trade with Nepal and Bhutan, connectivity with Bangladesh, crisis assistance to Sri Lanka, disaster support to Maldives and development projects across the region translate priority into observable instruments. Yet a grant or summit is an input; the result depends on access, local ownership, maintenance and political durability.

**Objection:** non-reciprocity rewards free-riding and cannot handle security threats.
**Reply:** it is calibrated, not unconditional. Cooperative public goods may be asymmetric, while terrorism, territorial coercion or misuse of territory triggers safeguards.
**Residual:** neighbours may still read generosity as influence, so consultation and transparency remain essential.


### Revision notes

1. Neighbourhood First is a priority framework, not a treaty.
2. The Gujral Doctrine is associated with five South Asia principles.
3. Its distinctive element is non-reciprocity toward smaller neighbours.
4. Non-reciprocity seeks long-run stability rather than immediate barter.
5. Sovereignty, territorial integrity and non-interference remain safeguards.
6. Peaceful bilateral settlement does not exclude useful regional forums.
7. Policy instruments include connectivity, development, trade, energy and HADR.
8. Doctrine is judged by delivered and durable outcomes, not declarations.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** How does the Gujral Doctrine address asymmetry in India's neighbourhood? (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** India's size can make smaller neighbours fear dependence even when cooperation is mutually beneficial. The Gujral Doctrine responds through non-reciprocal accommodation: India may extend benefits without insisting on equal, immediate concessions. Its other principles—respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-interference, denial of territory for hostile use and peaceful bilateral settlement—convert power into reassurance. Connectivity, grants, energy trade and people-to-people exchange operationalise this approach. However, non-reciprocity is not unconditional passivity. Security threats, ecological limits and domestic accountability require safeguards. Its success therefore depends on consultation, transparent delivery and benefits visible to partner societies. Properly applied, the doctrine treats asymmetry as a reason for restraint and public-goods provision rather than dominance.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 3 marks doctrine + 3 marks asymmetry mechanism + 2 marks instruments + 2 marks qualification = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is non-reciprocity different from ignoring reciprocity altogether?

**Model answer:** It relaxes demand for equal immediate concessions to build long-run trust, but India still protects legitimate security, ecological and accountability interests.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equating non-reciprocity with unlimited concessions regardless of conduct or capacity.

### Asymmetry matrix

| Dimension | India's structural weight | Neighbour's leverage/agency | Policy implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| market | larger demand and finance | transit, resources, local consent | predictable access, avoid coercive dependence |
| security | stronger aggregate capacity | border terrain, intelligence, denial/permission | joint mechanisms and restraint |
| water/energy | upstream/downstream roles vary | geography and domestic vetoes | basin logic plus federal consultation |
| connectivity | capital and network scale | route access and implementation | co-design, last-mile completion |
| politics | diplomatic reach | electoral narratives and hedging | society-wide, non-partisan engagement |

### The smaller side is not powerless

**Asymmetric interdependence** means capabilities are unequal but both sides possess leverage. India may offer market access, finance, technology or emergency capacity; a neighbour may control a transit route, river position, border cooperation, port access, local legitimacy or the pace of implementation.

This explains why a one-template policy fails. Bhutan's hydropower partnership, Bangladesh's connectivity and river agenda, Nepal's open-border and power relationship, Sri Lanka's crisis and maritime setting, Maldives' island geography, Myanmar's conflict-affected corridors, Pakistan's adversarial security context and Afghanistan's access constraints demand different mixes.

### From hierarchy to differentiated partnership

Policy should ask: What is the shared public good? Who bears the cost? Which domestic actor can veto delivery? What reassurance does the partner need? What safeguard does India require? The answer determines whether the tool should be a grant, credit line, power purchase, transit protocol, capacity programme, humanitarian aid or security mechanism.

**Objection:** differentiation encourages inconsistency.
**Reply:** principles should remain consistent—agency, consultation, non-coercion and delivery—while instruments vary with the problem.
**Residual:** calibrated policy can still look selective unless criteria and commitments are transparent.


### Revision notes

1. Asymmetry is unequal capacity, not one-sided dependence.
2. Smaller neighbours retain geographic, political and implementation leverage.
3. Neighbour agency is an analytical variable, not a courtesy phrase.
4. Different problems require different mixes of grants, trade, energy and security tools.
5. Consistent principles can coexist with differentiated instruments.
6. Local consent affects both legitimacy and project durability.
7. Domestic veto players must be mapped before promising delivery.
8. Transparency prevents differentiation from appearing arbitrary.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Structural asymmetry requires differentiated rather than uniform neighbourhood policy. Examine. (15 marks / 250 words)

**Model response:** India's economic, demographic and military weight creates dependence anxiety, but asymmetry is not unilateral control. Neighbours retain leverage through geography, transit, river position, border cooperation, ports, domestic legitimacy and implementation consent. Uniform policy therefore misdiagnoses distinct relationships. Bhutan requires hydropower and development partnership with diversification safeguards; Nepal combines open-border mobility, power trade and political sensitivity; Bangladesh links connectivity and commerce with rivers and border management; Sri Lanka and Maldives add maritime and crisis-response dimensions; Myanmar combines Act East connectivity with conflict and border security; Pakistan is constrained by terrorism and unresolved disputes; Afghanistan by access and political uncertainty.

Differentiation should not mean inconsistent principles. India should apply common standards—respect for agency, consultation, non-coercive security, transparent finance, delivery discipline and people-centred benefits—while choosing instruments closest to each problem. This converts hierarchy into partnership. The residual risk is perceived selectivity; publishing criteria, consulting local stakeholders and completing commitments can reduce it.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 3 marks asymmetry concept + 5 marks differentiated country logic + 4 marks common principles + 2 marks limitation + 1 mark verdict = 15.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can a materially smaller neighbour exercise leverage over India?

**Model answer:** Through route access, river geography, border cooperation, ports, local consent, domestic narratives and the ability to delay or redirect implementation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Assuming material size removes the smaller state's bargaining power or policy agency.

### Border-system cross-section

```text
SOVEREIGN LINE
     |
     +-- security: terrorism · insurgency · trafficking · illegal arms
     +-- economy: formal trade · informal exchange · labour mobility
     +-- society: kinship · pilgrimage · language · grazing · markets
     +-- ecology: rivers · forests · disasters · disease
     |
     v
SMART MANAGEMENT = legal clarity + surveillance + gates + local trust
```

### A border is not only a fence

Neighbourhood borders are simultaneously sovereign boundaries and lived spaces. Security threats demand monitoring and coordinated enforcement, but indiscriminate closure can damage legitimate trade, kinship and livelihoods, driving activity underground.

India's border relationships differ. The India-Nepal open border enables exceptional mobility but requires cooperative policing. Bangladesh combines a dense settled border with trade, migration narratives and security cooperation. Myanmar's terrain links insurgency, communities and Act East routes. Pakistan's border is shaped by cross-border terrorism and military risk. Bhutan presents a cooperative security setting. A sound answer therefore avoids one border-management formula.

### Four-part design

1. Clarify legal crossing and documentation rules.
2. Build interoperable posts, customs and communication.
3. Coordinate intelligence and law enforcement for specific threats.
4. Protect lawful mobility, border markets and community trust.

**Objection:** livelihood language weakens sovereignty.
**Reply:** lawful access and local legitimacy improve intelligence and reduce incentives for clandestine networks.
**Residual:** sudden crises may require temporary restrictions, but these need proportionality, review and humanitarian safeguards.

> **Cross-link:** operational counter-terrorism and insurgency detail belongs to Internal Security; this lesson explains the bilateral-policy interface.


### Revision notes

1. Borders are sovereign lines and social-economic systems.
2. Threat-specific management is superior to uniform securitisation.
3. Open or porous borders require cooperation, not conceptual denial.
4. Legal trade and mobility can reduce clandestine incentives.
5. Integrated check posts combine security and facilitation.
6. Local communities are sources of legitimacy and information.
7. Crisis restrictions should be proportionate and reviewable.
8. Internal Security owns operational doctrine; IR owns cooperation and diplomacy.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Why should India manage neighbourhood borders as systems rather than barriers? (10 marks / 150 words)

**Model response:** India's borders carry terrorism, trafficking and insurgency risks, but they also sustain trade, kinship, pilgrimage, labour and ecological connections. A barrier-only approach can displace lawful activity into informal channels and alienate communities whose cooperation improves security. Border systems therefore require legal clarity, integrated check posts, surveillance, customs coordination, intelligence sharing and rapid communication, combined with protected lawful mobility and local consultation. The instrument must vary: the open India-Nepal border differs from the Pakistan security frontier or the conflict-affected Myanmar boundary. Temporary restrictions may be necessary during crises, but should be proportionate and reviewed. Effective management does not trade sovereignty for livelihoods; it uses facilitation and trust to make enforcement more targeted and legitimate.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 2 marks dual character + 4 marks mechanisms + 2 marks differentiation + 2 marks qualified conclusion = 10.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can easier lawful crossing strengthen rather than weaken border security?

**Model answer:** It channels legitimate movement through visible, documented routes, freeing enforcement to target genuinely suspicious flows and improving community cooperation.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating every cross-border movement as equivalent to a security threat.

### Connectivity status staircase

```text
POLITICAL ANNOUNCEMENT
      -> agreement / MoU
      -> financing and design
      -> land, permits and construction
      -> segment completion
      -> customs and operating protocol
      -> traffic and maintenance
      -> economic and social outcome

A corridor is only as useful as its weakest missing link.
```

### Connectivity is a service, not concrete

A bridge or port has strategic value only when feeder roads, customs, security, pricing, schedules and maintenance allow regular use. This is the **status-chain discipline**: announcement, agreement, finance, construction, operationalisation, utilisation and outcome are separate claims.

Bangladesh connectivity can improve Northeast access; Nepal links combine roads, rail, pipelines and bridges; Bhutan's proposed rail links need construction before they can be called operational; Myanmar's Kaladan project demonstrates partial delivery because the waterway and Sittwe Port can advance while a road segment lags. Digital payment and power links show that connectivity is not purely physical.

### Network gains and network risks

Connectivity lowers transport cost, widens markets and creates constituencies for peace. It can also distribute costs unequally, expose security vulnerabilities or become stranded when domestic conflict interrupts a segment. Assessment must include traffic, time, cost, reliability, local benefit and resilience.

**Objection:** incomplete projects are strategic failures.
**Reply:** segments may yield value, but official language must not equate partial use with corridor completion.
**Residual:** faster construction without local consent can create durable political opposition.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Connectivity is the operational core of Neighbourhood First, but not every project creates integration. Analyse. (15 marks / 250 words)

**Model response:** Connectivity translates diplomatic intent into lower travel time, market access, energy exchange and people-to-people contact. Rail and inland-water links with Bangladesh can improve Northeast access; bridges and pipelines deepen Nepal ties; Bhutan rail proposals can diversify access; Myanmar corridors connect Act East to territory. Yet infrastructure is not integration by itself. A project moves through announcement, agreement, finance, construction, operationalisation, utilisation and outcome. Missing feeder roads, customs protocols, security, maintenance or local consent can break the chain. Kaladan illustrates why a functioning waterway and port must be distinguished from an incomplete corridor.

Evaluation should track traffic, logistics cost, reliability, border-processing time, local jobs, ecological effects and distribution of gains. Joint operating institutions and transparent status reporting matter as much as capital spending. Connectivity succeeds when a completed, governed network delivers regular shared benefit; ceremonial openings or isolated segments should not be mistaken for integration.

**Question:** When does a completed bridge fail to become functional connectivity?

**Model answer:** When feeder links, border procedures, security arrangements, schedules, maintenance or user demand are missing, so the asset does not deliver regular movement and shared benefit.

**Misconception to avoid:** Using 'inaugurated', 'operational' and 'economically integrated' as interchangeable statuses.

### Economic partnership balance sheet

| Instrument | Shared gain | Frequent risk | Better metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| trade access | scale and cheaper inputs | imbalance, non-tariff friction | diversified trade and clearance time |
| grant project | public good and visibility | delay, maintenance gap | service use and local ownership |
| line of credit | infrastructure finance | debt/procurement concern | viability and transparent terms |
| power trade | revenue and grid balancing | dependence and project risk | reliable supply, price and ecology |
| capacity building | institutional ties | elite concentration | skills retained and broadly accessed |

### Development is diplomacy only when it works

Economic instruments create mutual stakes in stability. India offers market access, grants, credit, training and project execution; neighbours provide trade, transit, energy, labour and strategic access. Cross-border power trade with Nepal and Bhutan can produce revenue and grid benefits, while Bangladesh commerce and connectivity can link subregional markets.

Gross trade is an incomplete indicator. Ask whether trade is diversified, border clearance is predictable and smaller firms participate. Likewise, a credit announcement is not a completed project, and a completed asset is not automatically a maintained public service.

### Avoid the donor-recipient frame

The better frame is **co-produced development**: partner priorities, local labour and institutions, transparent finance, maintenance and feedback. This respects agency and reduces the political vulnerability of a project associated only with one government.

**Objection:** strategic development assistance disguises influence.
**Reply:** all external finance has strategic effects; legitimacy comes from demand-responsiveness, transparent terms and durable local benefit.
**Residual:** even beneficial projects can deepen dependence, so diversification and periodic evaluation matter.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Assess development partnership and economic interdependence as instruments of India's neighbourhood policy. (15 marks / 250 words)

**Model response:** Economic interdependence gives neighbouring societies a material stake in stable relations. Market access, grants, lines of credit, capacity building and power trade can lower costs, create public goods and connect border regions. Bhutan and Nepal illustrate energy complementarities; Bangladesh shows how commerce and connectivity can support subregional production; Indian projects across Sri Lanka, Maldives and Afghanistan demonstrate sectoral development cooperation.

However, aggregate trade or announced finance can mislead. Persistent imbalance, non-tariff barriers, delayed construction, tied procurement, weak maintenance or benefits concentrated near elites can generate dependence narratives. India should therefore co-design projects around partner priorities, publish status and financing terms, include local institutions and labour, and assess utilisation, service quality, trade diversification, payment reliability and local distribution. Strategic value follows credible delivery rather than expenditure alone. Economic partnership is most durable when the neighbour experiences agency and recurring benefit, while India gains reliable connectivity, markets and regional stability.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Distinguish regime-oriented and society-oriented neighbourhood diplomacy. Which is more durable? (15 marks / 250 words)

**Model response:** Regime-oriented diplomacy depends heavily on a particular leader or governing coalition for access, narrative and project support. It may deliver decisions quickly, but commitments can be repudiated when power changes. Society-oriented diplomacy spreads the relationship across civil services, legislatures, provinces, businesses, universities, border communities and users. Projects become harder to reject when their benefits are locally understood and institutionally embedded.

Formal government-to-government channels remain essential for sovereignty, finance and security; the two approaches are complementary rather than substitutes.

### Non-reciprocity decision gate

```text
       |
```

### Generosity is not costless

Non-reciprocity is easiest when India can provide a grant, market opening or capacity programme at manageable cost. It is harder where the resource is finite, the concession affects an Indian state, or security externalities are high.

Water is the clearest case: an allocation has seasonal and distributive costs, so goodwill cannot substitute for hydrology, federal consent and ecology. Trade access may produce adjustment costs. Security accommodation may create moral hazard if hostile conduct faces no consequence.

### Bounded non-reciprocity

India should identify the public good, disclose domestic cost, consult affected actors, phase implementation, protect vulnerable groups and review spillovers. Conditionality should target the risk without humiliating the partner.

**Objection:** safeguards empty the doctrine of generosity.
**Reply:** sustainable generosity requires domestic legitimacy; otherwise a later reversal destroys trust.
**Residual:** diffuse long-run stability benefits are hard to compare with visible short-run costs.


### Revision notes

1. Non-reciprocity is a strategy, not a blank cheque.
2. Its feasibility varies by fiscal, ecological and security cost.
3. Finite shared water is a hard case.
4. Indian states and affected groups shape domestic legitimacy.
5. Security concessions can create moral hazard.
6. Phasing and review can preserve an asymmetric public good.
7. Targeted safeguards need not become humiliating reciprocity.
8. Unsustainable generosity risks abrupt reversal and greater distrust.

### Mains drill

**Mains prompt:** Critically examine the limits of non-reciprocity in India's neighbourhood policy. (15 marks / 250 words)

**Model response:** Non-reciprocity can reassure smaller neighbours by allowing India to provide market access, grants or capacity without demanding equal immediate concessions. It treats regional stability as a long-run return. Its limits appear when costs are concentrated or risks are non-divisible. Shared water involves finite seasonal allocation, ecology and Indian state interests; trade concessions may impose adjustment costs; security accommodation can create moral hazard; large finance creates fiscal and accountability concerns.

India should therefore practise bounded non-reciprocity. It must identify the regional public good, disclose domestic costs, consult affected federal and social actors, phase support, protect vulnerable groups and monitor spillovers. Conditions should address concrete risk rather than become a demand for symbolic equality.

This does not dilute the doctrine. A concession that lacks domestic legitimacy may later be reversed, damaging trust more than calibrated support would. The unresolved challenge is valuation: diffuse future stability is difficult to compare with visible present cost, requiring transparent political judgment.

**Unique scoring rubric:** 3 marks value of doctrine + 5 marks limits/cases + 4 marks safeguards + 2 marks counterpoint + 1 mark verdict = 15.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is water a harder domain for non-reciprocity than capacity building?

**Model answer:** Water is finite, seasonal and distributive, with ecological and federal costs; training or capacity support is usually less rival and easier to expand.

**Misconception to avoid:** Treating any safeguard or review clause as proof that non-reciprocity has been abandoned.

## 5. Water, federalism and people

- Shared rivers involve timing, quality, floods, sediment, ecology and allocation.
- The Union negotiates internationally; states supply knowledge, consent and implementation.
- Teesta is the standard federal-diplomacy illustration.
- Joint data and flood warning can preserve cooperation below a grand bargain.
- Regime-oriented policy is exposed to leadership change.
- Society-oriented policy builds ties through institutions, users, education, health, trade and culture.
- Understanding domestic politics differs from interfering in outcomes.
- People-to-people ties are ballast, not a substitute for statecraft.

## 6. Country recall

- **Bangladesh:** connectivity/Northeast access + trade/security + rivers/federal sensitivity.
- **Nepal:** open border + transit/power + cultural depth + sovereign sensitivity.
- **Bhutan:** high-trust development/hydropower + diversification/ecology.
- **Sri Lanka:** 2022 first response + maritime/development + domestic reform and agency.
- **Maldives:** island resilience/first response + transparent maritime capacity + political cycles.
- **Myanmar:** Act East/border security + conflict/humanitarian constraints + incomplete corridors.
- **Pakistan:** terrorism/deterrence + escalation control + exact water-treaty status.
- **Afghanistan:** security/humanitarian/development legacy + constrained access + engagement-recognition distinction.

## 7. Regional, humanitarian and external-power dimensions

- Regional forums provide voice and reassurance.
- Bilateral mechanisms often deliver specific projects.
- BBIN illustrates subregional functional cooperation and implementation gaps.
- SAARC and BIMSTEC are bounded here; Topic 10 owns full comparison.
- First response requires speed, consent, distribution, local handover and resilience.
- Humanitarian support may produce goodwill but should remain needs-based.
- External-power activity should be classified as ordinary diversification, governance risk or direct threat.
- India earns preference through proximity, public goods and delivery—not automatic primacy.

## 8. Advanced qualifications

- Regional-framework preference can coexist with intensive bilateral engagement.
- Society orientation improves, but cannot guarantee, continuity across political transition.
- Water and security are hard cases for non-reciprocity.
- Historical intervention and contemporary non-interference form a genuine tension.
- Close political analysis is not automatically interference.
- Scenario planning is disciplined when it states baseline, assumptions, signals and counterfactuals.
- Use a dashboard rather than one success index.
- Preserve reversibility and learning where uncertainty is high.
