# Landforms of Glaciation / Himalayan Glaciers and GLOF — Mains-Focused Live Edition

This topic connects glacier formation/dynamics, erosional and depositional landforms, Himalayan glacier
geography and glacial-lake outburst risk. It separates measured glacier change from terminus movement,
lake inventory from hazard and hazard from downstream risk.

**Current evidence cutoff: 5 October 2026.** Current controls include MoES/MoJS glacier evidence,
GSI's inventory of record, NRSC's Glacial Lake Atlas, CWC monitoring, NDMA/NIDM guidelines, NGRMP,
MoES's April 2026 warning-system update, MHA's September 2026 preparedness review, official South
Lhonak assessments and the 2025 peer-reviewed event reconstruction.

**Scope.** Geography owns cryosphere process, landforms, glacier/rivers mapwork, change measurement and
the GLOF hazard-to-risk chain. Detailed response operations, avalanche doctrine, dam economics,
transboundary diplomacy and Siachen strategy require their own subject evidence.

## Lesson 1 — From snow to flowing glacier ice

```text
fresh/settled snow -> multi-year firn -> denser polycrystalline glacier ice
 accumulation + metamorphism + recrystallisation
                       ↓
              stress sufficient for flow
```

### 1. Glacier criterion

A glacier is a perennial body of land ice that moves under gravity through deformation, basal motion
and/or subglacial-bed deformation. Flow—not permanence, area or one thickness threshold—separates a
glacier from a persistent snowfield.

Snow densifies through compaction, vapour transfer, melt/refreezing where present and recrystallisation.
Rates and density thresholds vary with climate and depth; textbook fixed time/density ladders are not
universal. Firn is multi-year snow more compact than seasonal snow but more porous than glacier ice.
*Névé* is an older/overlapping term, not a mandatory separate stage.

### 2. Glacier ice is not an impermeable block

Glacier ice is polycrystalline and relatively low-permeability at grain scale, yet crevasses, moulins and
englacial/subglacial drainage conduct water. Its brittle surface and ductile deeper response vary with
stress, temperature and timescale.

### 3. Geomorphic significance

Ice fills and couples to much of a valley cross-section, carries debris on/within/beneath it and follows
the gradient of its free surface rather than river base-level rules. Landforms still depend on basal
conditions, rock, sediment and time; ice presence alone does not guarantee strong erosion.

Revision notes:

- Glacier = perennial land ice that flows.
- Persistent snowfield may not be a glacier.
- Snow metamorphoses to firn and glacier ice.
- Firn is multi-year transitional snow.
- Névé is overlapping historical usage.
- No universal thickness/time/density threshold applies.
- Glacier ice is polycrystalline, not absolutely impermeable.
- Crevasses/moulins route meltwater.
- Surface may fracture while deeper ice deforms.
- Geomorphic work depends on motion and basal conditions.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is a permanent snow patch not automatically a glacier?

**Model answer:** Permanence only shows that some snow survives ablation. A glacier must contain
sufficient connected ice and stress to move through internal deformation, basal motion or bed
deformation. A small perennial patch may remain static, while a moving glacier can be debris-covered
and appear snow-free at its terminus. Confirm motion/ice structure rather than inferring from longevity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Permanence, whiteness or size alone does not define a glacier.

### Mains Practice 1

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why glacier ice is an unusual geomorphic agent.

**Model (about 150 words):** Glacier ice is a solid that deforms and flows under sustained stress. It
occupies much of a valley, carries debris at several levels and can move across locally rising bedrock
because flow follows the ice-surface gradient. Yet its geomorphic work is selective: internal
deformation can occur with little bed motion, while basal sliding/bed deformation promotes erosion.
Crevasses and drainage pathways also make the body mechanically and hydrologically heterogeneous.
Thus landforms follow ice dynamics, substrate and debris—not ice presence alone.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Specific mass balance | Water-equivalent gain/loss per unit area over stated period |
| ELA | Altitude where annual specific balance is zero on one glacier |
| Firn line | End-of-season visible firn/bare-ice boundary; conditional ELA proxy |
| Climatic snowline | Regional persistent-snow concept under stated climate/period |
| Terminus/snout | Lower ice margin produced by ice delivery versus frontal loss |

### 1. Balance and boundary

State whether balance is surface or total and define glacier/basin boundary and balance year. Gains can
include snowfall/redistribution, avalanches and retained refreezing; losses include melt, sublimation and
frontal losses where relevant.

A positive/negative balance year does not cause immediate terminus advance/retreat. Geometry,
thickness, slope, debris and dynamic adjustment create lag. Ice normally continues down-glacier while a
terminus retreats because frontal loss exceeds ice delivery.

### 2. ELA and firn line

ELA is a glacier/year balance property. Firn line may approximate ELA on suitable glaciers but debris,
superimposed ice, snow redistribution and incomplete melt can separate them. Snout altitude is not
snowline or ELA.

### 3. Debris effects

Thin debris commonly lowers albedo and enhances melt; sufficiently thick debris insulates, while ice
cliffs and ponds concentrate local melt. No universal critical thickness or response time applies.

### 4. Current values

MoES (31 July 2025) reports cumulative Indian Himalayan mass loss about **−26 m w.e. (1975–2023)**;
upper Chandra mean annual balance **−0.51 ± 0.28 m w.e. a⁻¹** and cumulative **−3.54 m w.e.
(2015–2022)**. The official release includes the negative signs.

Revision notes:

- Mass balance needs quantity, boundary, method and period.
- ELA is annual zero-balance altitude.
- Firn line is a surface observation, conditional proxy.
- Climatic snowline is regional; snout is geometric.
- Positive/negative year does not imply immediate margin response.
- Retreat does not reverse ice flow.
- Thinning, area loss and retreat differ.
- Dynamic response is lagged and geometry-dependent.
- Debris effects vary with thickness and ice cliffs/ponds.
- Preserve official signs and uncertainty.

### Concept check

**Question:** Can a glacier have negative mass balance and a stationary or advancing terminus?

**Model answer:** Yes, temporarily. Terminus position reflects delayed ice delivery and frontal loss, so
a large or surging glacier can remain stationary or advance while current balance is negative. Conversely,
a glacier may keep retreating after forcing improves. Establish mass balance, flow/surge state,
geometry and response lag before treating margin motion as climatic balance.

**Misconception to avoid:** Terminus motion is not an instantaneous mass-balance meter.

### Mains Practice 2

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish ELA, firn line, climatic snowline and snout.

**Model (about 150 words):** ELA is the altitude where one glacier's annual specific balance is zero.
The firn line is the visible end-of-season boundary between firn and bare ice and only sometimes proxies
ELA. Climatic snowline is a regional climate-dependent surface of persistent snow, while the snout is the
lower glacier margin set by ice delivery and loss. Debris, superimposed ice, redistribution and dynamic
lag separate them. Their altitude figures therefore cannot be substituted.

## Lesson 3 — Glacier motion, thermal regime and surging

Book context: Leong pp.51–52; Majid pp.2.33–2.34 (local-scan pp.55–56), paraphrased.
CA search: current Indian documents report surge-type glaciers but do not prescribe one mechanism.
CA found: use surge classification as dynamic evidence, not climatic counter-evidence.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Motion component | Control | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Internal deformation | Driving stress, ice temperature/fabric | Flow even where bed is frozen |
| Basal sliding | Water pressure, roughness, effective pressure, thermal state | Variable basal speed/erosion |
| Soft-bed deformation | Sediment strength, pore pressure, drag | Fast flow and subglacial bedforms |

### 1. Thermal regimes

Cold-based ice is frozen to much of its bed and commonly has low basal motion/erosion; temperate/warm-
based ice is near pressure melting; polythermal glaciers contain both. These are spatial/temporal
regimes, not whole-glacier labels inferred safely from one landform.

### 2. Velocity and fracture

Velocity is often greater away from valley walls and near the surface, but tributaries, bends, bed
geometry and basal conditions produce complex fields. Crevasses reflect tensile/shear stress; a
bergschrund separates moving glacier ice from ice/snow attached near a headwall. Seracs form where
intersecting crevasses fragment steep ice.

### 3. Surges

Surges are short-lived flow accelerations after quiescent storage, involving glacier geometry,
thermal state and subglacial hydrology. No single switch from channels to a water film explains every
surge. A surge-driven advance is not evidence of positive climatic mass balance.

Revision notes:

- Flow combines deformation, sliding and/or bed deformation.
- Cold-based does not mean motionless or zero erosion absolutely.
- Polythermal glaciers contain contrasting regimes.
- Basal water pressure affects effective pressure/sliding.
- Velocity fields are not simple universal arrows.
- Crevasses map stress, not simply melting.
- Bergschrund is a headwall/moving-ice separation.
- Seracs are blocks between crevasses.
- Surge is dynamic instability, not favourable climate.
- Terminus advance can accompany regional loss.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a surging glacier not disprove regional warming-driven mass loss?

**Model answer:** A surge rapidly transfers ice stored upstream to lower elevations through an internal
dynamic instability involving geometry, thermal and subglacial hydrological conditions. The terminus
may advance even while the glacier's total mass balance is negative. Evaluate mass/volume change over a
stated period and distinguish surge phase from climatic forcing.

**Misconception to avoid:** Advance describes margin position; it does not establish positive climatic balance.

### Mains Practice 3

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why glacier flow and glacier erosion are not synonymous.

**Model (about 150 words):** Internal deformation allows ice to flow even where the bed is largely
frozen and basal motion is small. Strong bed erosion generally requires relative ice/bed or deforming-bed
motion, erosive debris, suitable rock discontinuities and water-pressure conditions. Consequently a thick
long-lived glacier can preserve some surfaces while eroding other reaches intensely. Thermal regime,
substrate and debris determine geomorphic work, not flow alone.

## Lesson 4 — Ice-mass types and grounded versus floating ice

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Leong pp.51–52; Majid p.2.33 (local-scan p.55), paraphrased.
Current evidence: MoES identifies global sea-level contribution from grounded Himalayan ice loss.
CA found: no magnitude inferred from typology alone.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Ice mass | Diagnostic |
|---|---|
| Cirque/valley glacier | Flow strongly constrained by mountain relief |
| Piedmont glacier | Valley ice spreads into a lobe beyond confinement |
| Ice field | Interconnected upland ice; relief still influences flow |
| Ice cap | Dome-like ice flowing outward, largely overriding relief |
| Ice sheet | Continental-scale grounded ice system (Greenland/Antarctica) |
| Ice shelf | Floating extension fed by grounded ice |
| Iceberg/sea ice | Floating ice; different origins/dynamics |

Melting already-floating ice has negligible direct sea-level effect because it already displaces water,
subject to small density/salinity nuances. Grounded ice transferred to the ocean raises sea level. Ice-
shelf loss matters indirectly by reducing buttressing and potentially accelerating grounded discharge;
it is not correct simply to say shelf collapse itself adds its full volume.

India's cryosphere is predominantly mountain glacier ice; whether small ice-field-like complexes meet a
formal classification depends on definition. Avoid absolute claims that India has/no representatives
without an inventory standard.

Revision notes:

- Classify by scale, geometry, bed/relief control and flotation.
- Valley glaciers follow relief.
- Ice fields remain relief-influenced.
- Ice caps/sheets build their own surface gradients.
- Piedmont ice spreads beyond valleys.
- Ice shelves are floating extensions.
- Floating melt has negligible direct sea-level effect.
- Grounded discharge adds ocean mass.
- Shelf buttressing makes indirect effects important.
- Typology alone gives no sea-level magnitude.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can ice-shelf collapse matter if floating ice melt barely raises sea level directly?

**Model answer:** A shelf is mechanically coupled to grounded ice at the grounding zone and margins.
Removing it can reduce buttressing, allowing grounded ice to accelerate and cross into the ocean. That
grounded transfer raises sea level. The effect depends on shelf geometry/pinning and glacier response;
shelf loss is an indirect dynamic mechanism, not direct addition of the floating shelf's displaced volume.

**Misconception to avoid:** “No direct rise” does not mean “no consequence.”

### Mains Practice 4

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish relief-controlled mountain ice from self-controlled ice masses.

**Model (about 150 words):** Cirque and valley glaciers are steered strongly by hollows and valleys;
piedmont glaciers spread after leaving confinement. Ice fields bury parts of upland relief but retain
bed influence. Ice caps and sheets generate broad surface domes and outward flow that can override local
bed topography. The distinction predicts flow pattern and landform scale, while grounded/floating status
separately controls sea-level effects.

Book context: Leong pp.52–53; Majid p.2.34 (local-scan p.56), paraphrased.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Mechanism | Requirements | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Abrasion | Relative motion, basal debris, contact stress | Polish, striations, grooves, flour |
| Quarrying/plucking | Discontinuities, stress/water-pressure changes, ice–rock coupling | Block removal, rough lee faces |
| Subglacial meltwater | Pressure gradients and sediment tools | Channels, potholes, sorted deposits |

Quarrying is not simply water refreezing on every low-pressure lee side; regelation operates mainly
around small obstacles and modern quarrying includes fracture propagation and transient water pressure.
Abrasion does not require one “intermediate pressure” rule and striation orientation needs corroboration
where ice flow rotates or bedforms are inherited.

Roches moutonnées classically show smooth/abraded stoss and steeper/quarried lee faces, but asymmetry can
vary with structure and bedform evolution. Use an assemblage of striations, grooves and form rather than
a single compass rule. Crag-and-tail combines resistant crag with protected/depositional lee material.

Revision notes:

- Abrasion wears with basal tools.
- Quarrying removes jointed blocks.
- Basal motion/bed deformation enables strong erosion.
- Cold-based erosion is low, not universally zero.
- Regelation is not the universal quarrying mechanism.
- Water-pressure transients matter.
- Striations indicate local former flow, with corroboration.
- Rock flour is not a basin erosion-rate measure.
- Roche moutonnée orientation is classic but not infallible.
- Bulk Himalayan sediment has multiple sources.

### Concept check

**Question:** What can aligned striations and asymmetric bedrock bumps establish?

**Model answer:** Together they can reconstruct a local former ice-flow direction and show basal
abrasion/quarrying, especially if the smooth stoss and rough lee orientation is consistent. They do not
date glaciation, quantify erosion or prove one thermal regime. Structural control, inherited marks and
later weathering should be checked.

**Misconception to avoid:** One bump or scratch is not a complete glacier history.

### Mains Practice 5

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why glacial erosion is spatially selective.

**Model (about 150 words):** Erosion varies with basal thermal/hydrological state, sliding or soft-bed
motion, debris/tools, rock discontinuities, effective pressure, ice thickness/velocity and time.
Abrasion dominates where debris moves against the bed; quarrying favours fractured rock and suitable
water-pressure/stress conditions. Cold-based or debris-poor reaches may preserve surfaces while nearby
warm-based trunks overdeepen. Selectivity therefore reflects coupled ice, water, sediment and geology.

## Lesson 6 — Cirques, arêtes, cols and horns

Progress: 6 / 19  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Upland glacial assemblage and palaeo-environmental inference

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: Leong p.53; Majid pp.2.33–2.34 (local-scan pp.55–56), paraphrased.
CA search: no current official evidence required for morphology.
CA found: process lesson.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
adjacent cirque/valley-head hollows enlarge -> intervening ridge narrows (arête)
ridge saddle lowered/breached -> col
erosion on several flanks -> pyramidal horn
cirque basin after ice loss -> tarn where outlet/rock-sediment geometry retains water
```

A cirque is an amphitheatre-like glacier-head hollow formed by combined ice erosion, headwall retreat,
rockfall/frost processes and glacier dynamics. The older deterministic snowpatch→bergschrund→rotational
abrasion sequence is a teaching model, not universal. A rock lip is common but not required and a tarn's
outlet may include rock, sediment or moraine.

**Arête** is French for ridge/edge, not fish-bone. Arêtes and horns are residual forms between enlarged
basins/valleys; a horn does not require exactly three cirques. A col is a saddle that may be glacially
modified, but not every mountain pass is a breached arête. A nunatak is exposed rock surrounded by ice;
weathering/vegetation trimlines may help infer former ice surface but need corroboration.

Revision notes:

- Cirque formation is multi-process and geology-dependent.
- Bergschrund may aid headwall processes but is not compulsory.
- Cirque rock lip is common, not universal.
- Tarn occupies a glacial hollow; dam material varies.
- Arête means ridge/edge.
- Arête is residual between adjacent glacial basins/valleys.
- Col is a saddle; origin must be established.
- Horn forms by erosion on several sides; no fixed cirque count.
- Nunatak is rock surrounded by ice.
- Trimline interpretation needs independent evidence.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “a horn forms where exactly three cirques meet” too rigid?

**Model answer:** A horn is a residual pyramidal peak produced where glacier-head/valley erosion steepens
several flanks. Three cirques are a useful diagram, but real horns reflect variable basin number,
structure, pre-existing relief and non-glacial slope processes. Diagnose surrounding glacial hollows,
arêtes and geology rather than count exactly three.

**Misconception to avoid:** Teaching geometry is not a universal genetic rule.

### Mains Practice 6

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Show how the upland glacial suite forms one related assemblage.

**Model (about 150 words):** Glacial erosion and headwall/slope retreat enlarge neighbouring valley-head
hollows. The narrowing residual between adjacent hollows becomes an arête; a lowered saddle through the
ridge can form a col; erosion on several sides may leave a horn. After deglaciation, a cirque basin may
hold a tarn where outlet geometry retains water. Lithology, structure and pre-glacial relief qualify the
ideal sequence, so the assemblage is related without being mechanically identical everywhere.

Book context: Leong pp.53–54; Majid p.2.34, paraphrased. Verified PYQ: 2023 GS-I Q6.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Form | Process interpretation |
|---|---|
| Glacial trough | Widening/deepening/truncation by ice plus slope processes |
| Truncated spur | Former projecting valley-side ridge cut by ice erosion |
| Hanging valley | Tributary floor left above more deeply eroded trunk |
| Rock/ribbon basin | Differential overdeepening and/or lithologic/structural control |
| Fjord | Marine-inundated glacial trough, often overdeepened and locally sill-controlled |

A glacier occupies much of a valley rather than one fluvial channel, but trough shape also reflects
pre-glacial valley, lithology, tributary ice and postglacial modification. Ice can erode closed basins
because motion follows ice-surface driving stress across locally rising beds; “it escapes base level” is
an imprecise river analogy.

Fjords form where glacial troughs extend below sea level and are inundated during/after deglaciation and
relative sea-level change. Mouth sills are common where erosion/deposition differed near the margin but
not compulsory. Fjords are not caused universally by west-coast westerlies. Rias are drowned fluvial
valleys; distinguish using the full glacial landform and bathymetric assemblage, not an absolute “middle
deeper than mouth” test.

Scenic character follows steep walls, hanging-valley waterfalls, deep water near mountain relief,
truncated spurs and surrounding glacial uplands.

Revision notes:

- Trough formation combines ice erosion and slope response.
- Not every trough starts as a simple V-valley.
- Hanging valleys reflect differential erosion/ice thickness.
- Rock basins can be lithologically/structurally controlled.
- Fjord = marine-inundated glacial trough.
- Relative sea-level history matters.
- Sills are common, not universal.
- West-coast/westerly association is not universal cause.
- Ria is drowned fluvial valley.
- India lacks a marine fjord coast but has Himalayan trough analogues.

### Concept check

**Question:** Is a shallow mouth sill necessary to identify every fjord?

**Model answer:** No. Many fjords have entrance thresholds from differential erosion, deposition or
bedrock, but sill prominence varies and some have complex outlets. Identification rests on a marine-
inundated glacial trough with associated overdeepening, steep walls, hanging valleys/truncated spurs and
glacial history. Bathymetry helps but no single sill rule is compulsory.

**Misconception to avoid:** Fjord diagnosis uses an assemblage, not one depth comparison.

### Mains Practice 7

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain fjord formation and picturesque character.

**Model (about 150 words):** A glacier widens and deepens a coastal mountain valley, commonly producing
an overdeepened trough and locally a bedrock/depositional threshold. Deglaciation and relative sea-level
rise inundate the trough. The resulting inlet is visually dramatic because steep walls rise directly
from deep water, tributary hanging valleys produce waterfalls, spurs are truncated and glacial peaks/
ridges frame the valley. Fjords are not restricted causally to west coasts, and a mouth sill is common
rather than universal.

| Term | Correct use |
|---|---|
| Till | Sediment deposited directly by glacier ice; commonly poorly sorted/unstratified but may be deformed or contain lenses |
| Moraine | Ice-marginal/subglacial debris landform or assemblage; may contain till and water-laid/reworked sediment |
| End/terminal moraine | Former margin/advance position, not necessarily all-time maximum |
| Recessional moraine | Stillstand or readvance during recession |

| Erratic | Clast foreign to local bedrock; source/transport evidence when provenance known |

Lateral/medial moraines describe debris position; counting medial bands does not always count tributaries
because debris emerges, merges and is buried. “Englacial moraine” is better called englacial debris
until deposited. Moraines can be compacted, ice-cored, washed, stratified or reworked; “ice never
compacted it” and “inherently weak” are false absolutes.

Drumlins can contain till, stratified sediment and bedrock; formation hypotheses include bed deformation,
erosion/deposition and meltwater-related processes. They are common under former ice sheets but not
absolutely restricted to one setting, and absence in India requires mapped evidence rather than assertion.

Revision notes:

- Till is a sediment facies; moraine is a landform/assemblage.
- Direct till is commonly poorly sorted, not invariably structureless.
- Moraines can include reworked/water-laid sediment.
- End moraine records a margin episode.
- Recessional ridges may mark stillstand/readvance.
- Englacial debris is not yet a landform.
- Medial debris bands do not count tributaries infallibly.
- Drumlin composition/genesis vary.
- Erratic provenance can indicate transport.
- Dam stability cannot be inferred from “moraine” alone.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a stratified ridge still be a moraine?

**Model answer:** Moraine is interpreted from position, geometry and glacier-margin history, not one
sediment texture. Meltwater or lake deposition can sort/bedd parts of an ice-marginal ridge, and later
reworking can add stratified lenses. Field diagnosis combines landform relation to former ice, clast
provenance/fabric and internal sedimentology.

**Misconception to avoid:** Moraine and homogeneous unsorted till are not synonyms.

### Mains Practice 8

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Distinguish till, moraine and drumlin.

**Model (about 150 words):** Till is sediment deposited directly by ice, commonly poorly sorted. A
moraine is a debris landform/assemblage marking glacier margins or bed and may contain till plus
meltwater-reworked material. A drumlin is a streamlined subglacial bedform with varied internal
composition and uncertain genesis, aligned broadly with former flow. Thus material, landform position
and process history are separate diagnostic dimensions.

### Concept check

**Question:** What evidence distinguishes a kame terrace from a lateral moraine?

**Model answer:** A kame terrace is primarily meltwater-laid sediment between ice and valley wall, so it
commonly shows bedding/sorting, channel structures and ice-contact collapse. A lateral moraine records
marginal debris accumulation and commonly has till fabric/provenance. Both can be mixed/reworked;
diagnose internal sedimentology, geometry and relation to former ice/meltwater.

**Misconception to avoid:** Position beside a valley wall does not uniquely determine origin.

### Mains Practice 9

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why “ice deposits unsorted; water deposits sorted” is useful but incomplete.

**Model (about 150 words):** Direct till is commonly poorly sorted because ice releases mixed debris,
whereas meltwater sorts by hydraulic conditions and creates bedding. Yet ice-marginal deposits can be
washed or deposited into water, till can contain lenses and glaciofluvial floods/debris flows may be
poorly sorted. The rule is a first diagnosis; landform position, fabric, structures and process context
must corroborate it.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
QUATERNARY: 2.58 Ma to present
  Pleistocene -------------------------- 11.7 ka | Holocene
repeated glacial/interglacial oscillations; regional advances not one global fourfold ladder
```

The older claim that Quaternary spans about one million years is obsolete. The Holocene begins about
11.7 ka, not an undefined “Recent” at 12,000 years. De Terra–Paterson's four-glacial/three-interglacial
Indian scheme is historiographic and must not control modern chronology or date Karewas, Pir Panjal
uplift, Narmada alluvium or Kashmir terraces.

Moraines, trimlines, erratics, striations, troughs and cirques establish former ice extent/flow but do not
date themselves. Correlation requires geomorphic mapping, sedimentology and absolute/relative dating.
Late-glacial global sea level was roughly 120 m below present at the Last Glacial Maximum, not a fixed
100 m throughout every glacial. Relative sea level also includes local isostatic/tectonic motion.

Karewa succession records complex lacustrine/fluvial deposition with tectonic, climatic and
catchment-history influences; it is not a simple second-interglacial or varve archive.

Revision notes:

- Quaternary begins ~2.58 Ma.
- Holocene begins ~11.7 ka.
- Present is an interglacial within Quaternary.
- Four-glacial Indian ladder is historiography.
- Landforms prove former ice, not age alone.
- Moraines require independent dating/correlation.
- LGM sea level roughly 120 m below present.
- Eustasy differs from isostatic/tectonic land movement.
- Karewas are complex lacustrine/fluvial deposits.
- Do not tie tectonic events mechanically to named glacials.

### Concept check

**Question:** What can ancient moraines below a present Himalayan snout establish?

**Model answer:** They establish that a glacier margin formerly extended farther down-valley and may
help reconstruct relative extent/flow. They do not by themselves provide age, duration, climate cause
or correlation with a named global glacial. Dating needs appropriate material/surfaces, stratigraphy and
regional correlation.

**Misconception to avoid:** A mapped moraine is an extent marker, not a self-dating event.

### Mains Practice 10

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain how glacial landforms reconstruct palaeoclimate with limits.

**Model (about 150 words):** Moraines and trimlines reconstruct former ice limits/thickness; striations
and erratics indicate flow/provenance; cirques/troughs show former glacial occupation. Assemblages beyond
present ice therefore demonstrate colder or differently balanced past conditions. But landforms can be
reworked, inherited and diachronous. They establish presence and relative sequence more readily than
calendar age or forcing. Modern dating, sedimentology and regional correlation are required.

### Concept check

**Question:** How can wetter and sunnier conditions on one slope push snowline in opposite directions?

**Model answer:** More solid precipitation/avalanche input raises accumulation and tends to lower the

aspect, shading, slope, wind redistribution and varied hypsometry. Climatic snowline is a regional
climate concept, while ELA/firn line belong to individual glacier balance/surface and snout marks the
terminus. Old range-wide figures and sector snout altitudes therefore describe different quantities.
Use sector-specific, dated observations and state the line measured.

Glaciers can buffer runoff by storing precipitation and releasing melt later, but timing differs:
western/upper Indus basins receive major westerly snow and relatively weak monsoon; Ganga/Brahmaputra

“Peak water” describes a modelled phase where enhanced melt runoff first rises and later declines as ice
area/mass shrinks. Its timing/magnitude is glacier- and basin-specific, modified by rainfall, snow,
groundwater, debris, reservoirs and demand. No Indian peak-water date is asserted.

Impacts include shifts in runoff seasonality/interannual variability, local dry-season/drought-year
reliability, glacial-lake/slope/sediment changes and a small global sea-level contribution. Do not claim
rivers will cease or that glacier loss alone causes Indian water stress.

- Glacier hydrology is basin/season-specific.
- Monsoon and westerly sectors differ.
- Seasonal snow differs from glacier ice.
- Groundwater/reservoirs also buffer flow.
- Melt is not universally anti-correlated with rainfall.
- Peak water is model-dependent and not universally dated.
- Annual total and lean-season reliability differ.
- Point-estimate retreat ranking ≠ water-vulnerability ranking.
- No unsupported melt-share/runoff projection.
- Link glacier evidence to a named basin.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can glacier loss matter even where monsoon rain supplies most annual flow?

**Model answer:** Annual volume can remain monsoon-dominated while glacier/snow storage affects timing,
late-season flow and drought-year buffering in upper reaches. Consequences depend on basin altitude,
rainfall/snow regime, groundwater, reservoirs and demand. The defensible claim is altered reliability
and variability—not automatic basin drying or a fixed national melt share.

**Misconception to avoid:** Small annual share can still have seasonal value, but magnitude needs data.

### Mains Practice 15

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** How will Himalayan glacier loss affect India's water resources?

**Model (about 150 words):** Effects are basin- and time-dependent. Enhanced melt may temporarily raise
some glacier-fed runoff before declining as ice mass shrinks; seasonal timing and drought-year buffering
may change before annual totals. Upper Indus-type basins can be relatively more snow/ice-dependent,
while Ganga/Brahmaputra flows are strongly monsoonal. Glacier loss also expands/changes lakes, sediment
and high-mountain hazards. Rainfall, groundwater, reservoirs, abstraction and demand mediate outcomes;
rivers will not simply disappear.

- Cohort change ≠ lake proliferation.
- Area monitoring ≠ full hazard/risk assessment.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can two moraine-dammed lakes of equal area have very different hazard?

**Model answer:** Area does not establish volume/depth, freeboard, dam width/slope/material/seepage,
buried ice, outlet condition or exposure to avalanche/landslide waves. Glacier connection and upstream
slopes affect triggers; valley geometry and downstream assets affect consequences. Bathymetry, dam and
slope investigation plus routing are required.

**Misconception to avoid:** Dam label and surface area do not rank hazard automatically.

### Mains Practice 16

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why glacial-lake inventories are screening tools, not danger lists.

**Model (about 150 words):** Inventories establish existence, location, area, type and change under a
stated threshold/method. They usually lack bathymetry, freeboard, dam geotechnics, upstream-slope
instability, trigger probability, routing and downstream exposure. Monitoring cohorts also change over
time. Thus inventories prioritise field investigation and repeat observation; hazard/risk classification
requires additional physical and societal evidence.

## Lesson 17 — Multi-mechanism GLOFs and South Lhonak

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Evidence: LS Starred Q68 (7 Dec 2023), NRSC satellite study, Sikkim PDNA and 2025 Science study
`10.1126/science.ads2659`.
CA found: current reconstruction identifies frozen lateral-moraine collapse, displacement wave and breach.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
LONG-TERM CONDITIONING: glacier/permafrost/lake/slope change
            +
IMMEDIATE TRIGGER: slope/ice/moraine collapse, inflow/rain, quake or internal dam change
            ↓
WAVE / OVERTOPPING / OUTLET EROSION / PIPING / ICE-DAM DRAINAGE
            ↓
SUDDEN RELEASE + SEDIMENT ENTRAINMENT + SECONDARY FAILURES
            ↓
RISK shaped by valley routing, exposure, vulnerability, warning and response
```

A GLOF is sudden release from a glacier-related lake; it need not involve a moraine dam or complete dam
breach. Overtopping may be a release/failure mechanism after a displacement wave rather than the initial
trigger. Some events have rainfall precursors; some are sediment-rich/debris-flow-like; neither is
universal.

### South Lhonak evidence

The December 2023 Parliament answer documents lake-area monitoring, the failed 2016 pilot sensor, no
operational EWS **as of that reply**, response and reported casualties; it does not establish the full
trigger physics. NRSC and Sikkim PDNA provide official event/impact assessments.

The 2025 peer-reviewed reconstruction identifies collapse of a frozen lateral moraine into South Lhonak,
a displacement wave, overtopping/breach and a sediment-rich downstream cascade, with long-term glacier/
permafrost/lake change as conditioning. Preserve study-specific volumes/heights with that paper and
distinguish conditioning from immediate trigger.

Revision notes:

- GLOF is sudden glacier-related lake release.
- Moraine breach is only one mechanism.
- Conditioning differs from immediate trigger.
- Displacement wave can cause overtopping.
- Overtopping/piping/outlet erosion are failure processes.
- Ice-dam drainage may recur without moraine breach.
- Sediment/debris-flow behaviour is event-specific.
- Risk includes routing, exposure, vulnerability and warning.
- South Lhonak 2023 status evidence must retain dates.
- Current trigger reconstruction: frozen lateral-moraine collapse.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “warming caused the South Lhonak GLOF” incomplete?

**Model answer:** Warming-related glacier/permafrost and lake changes contributed long-term
conditioning, but the reconstructed immediate cascade involved failure/collapse of a frozen lateral
moraine into the lake, a displacement wave, overtopping/breach and downstream sediment mobilisation.
Risk and losses also depended on valley routing, infrastructure, warning and vulnerability. Separate
background forcing, immediate trigger, release mechanism and consequences.

**Misconception to avoid:** Long-term climatic influence is not the same as the event's immediate trigger.

### Mains Practice 17

**Question (15 marks, 150 words):** Explain the hazard-to-risk chain of a Himalayan GLOF.

**Model (about 150 words):** Glacier/permafrost/lake change may enlarge or destabilise a high-mountain
system. Immediate rock/ice/moraine collapse, intense inflow, quake or dam/outlet degradation can then
produce a displacement wave, overtopping, piping, ice-dam drainage or breach. Released water entrains
sediment and may trigger secondary failures in a confined valley. Risk becomes high where roads,
settlements and hydropower occupy the path and warning/evacuation are weak. Each link requires separate
evidence; warming alone does not specify the event.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Q1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Explain why a retreating glacier is normally still flowing forward.

**Model answer:** Glacier ice continues downslope through deformation and basal/bed motion. The terminus
is a moving boundary between ice delivery and frontal ablation. It retreats when loss at the front
exceeds arriving ice, remains stationary when they balance and advances when delivery exceeds loss.
Terminus response may lag mass-balance change and can be altered by debris or surge. Retreat therefore
describes margin position, not reversal of ice flow or direct measurement of total mass.

## Q2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish glacial erosion from glacial deposition through field evidence.

**Model answer:** Abrasion/quarrying leave polish, striations, grooves, quarried bedrock, cirques and
troughs; flow direction needs an assemblage rather than one form. Direct ice deposition produces till
and moraine complexes, commonly poorly sorted but sometimes reworked/stratified. Subglacial drumlins are
streamlined bedforms with varied composition, while eskers, kames and outwash are meltwater deposits.
Material, internal structures and landform position must be read together.

## Q3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain the formation of fjords and their picturesque character.

**Model answer:** A glacier occupies, widens and deepens a coastal mountain valley, often excavating
reaches below sea level and leaving steep walls, truncated spurs and tributary hanging valleys.
Deglaciation and relative sea-level rise then inundate the trough. Differential erosion/deposition may
leave a mouth threshold, but a sill is not compulsory and fjords are not caused universally by
west-coast westerlies. Their scenic character follows deep water close to steep relief, waterfalls from
hanging valleys, sinuous enclosed reaches and surrounding glacial ridges/peaks. Distinguish rias by the
full bathymetric and glacial-landform assemblage, not one depth rule.

## Q4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** How will Himalayan glacier loss affect India's water resources?

**Model answer:** Effects vary by basin and season. Himalayan rivers combine rainfall, seasonal snow,
glacier melt, groundwater and reservoirs; upper Indus-type basins can be relatively more snow/ice-
dependent, while Ganga/Brahmaputra annual flow is strongly monsoonal. Enhanced melt may temporarily
increase some glacier runoff before declining as ice shrinks, but no uniform Indian peak-water date is
established. The more defensible concerns are changes in timing, interannual variability and drought-
year buffering, plus lake, sediment and high-mountain hazard changes. Rainfall, demand, storage and
regulation mediate outcomes; rivers will not simply disappear.

## Q5 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Analyse the geographical causes and risk dimensions of Himalayan GLOFs.

**Model answer:** Glacier/permafrost/lake change can enlarge or destabilise high-mountain systems, but
GLOFs arise through several mechanisms: moraine overtopping/piping/outlet erosion, ice-dam drainage,
supraglacial/englacial connection or cascading failure. Immediate triggers may include rock/ice/moraine
collapse, displacement waves, intense inflow or earthquakes. Released water often entrains sediment and
secondary failures in narrow valleys. Risk depends additionally on flow routing, settlements, roads,
hydropower, vulnerability and warning/evacuation. Lake inventories screen candidates; bathymetry, dam/
slope investigation and downstream modelling establish hazard/risk. South Lhonak illustrates frozen
lateral-moraine collapse, displacement wave, breach and downstream cascade rather than a one-cause
warming narrative.

## Q6 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate India's glacier and glacial-lake monitoring architecture.

**Model answer:** India combines GSI/MoES/MoJS field glacier studies, NRSC inventories and CWC seasonal
satellite monitoring with NDMA guidelines and the NGRMP's assessment, monitoring/EWS, site mitigation
and capacity building. MoES's April 2026 multi-hazard architecture adds forecasts/DSS, while MHA's
September 2026 review stresses flow paths, alerts, evacuation and district action. Strengths are wide
remote-sensing coverage, dated inventories and growing institutional coordination. Limits include
transboundary terrain, cloud/snow/shadow, sparse bathymetry/geotechnics, high-altitude maintenance and
unpredictable slope/dam failures. Monitoring supports prioritisation and warning but cannot guarantee a
specific event prediction; operational performance must be measured, not inferred from programme
announcement.
