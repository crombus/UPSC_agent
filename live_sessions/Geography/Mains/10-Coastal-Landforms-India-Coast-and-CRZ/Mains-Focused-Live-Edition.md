# Coastal Landforms / India Coast and CRZ - Mains-Focused Live Edition

A coast is not a line on a map. It is a zone where energy from the sea meets the land, and where the sea, the rock and the sediment settle into a balance that keeps changing. The twelve lessons follow one argument from the bottom up. Waves carry energy in from the ocean. That energy is bent and concentrated by the shape of the sea floor. It erodes rock, moves sediment along the shore and builds new forms. Sea level and land movement then decide whether the shore advances or retreats. Finally, people build on this zone, which is why India's coastline is measured, regulated and defended. Each lesson rests on the one before it, and each ends with a concept check, a short retrieval prompt and an original Mains question with a model answer.

## Lesson 1 - Why wave energy, not wind alone, shapes the coast

Start with a puzzle. Two coasts face the same ocean and the same wind, yet one has cliffs and the other has beaches. Why does the same sea do different things in different places? The answer begins with the wave, and with the fact that a wave is mostly a moving pattern of energy, not a moving mass of water. Water particles in a deep-water wave move in small circles and return close to where they began. The energy, however, travels a long way. When that energy reaches shallow water near the shore, the wave is squeezed. It slows, its height grows and it steepens until it breaks.

Three things set how much energy a wave carries. The first is wind speed. The second is how long the wind blows. The third is fetch, the distance of open water over which the wind acts. A wave raised by a local wind is steep and choppy. A swell that has travelled from a distant storm is smoother and more regular, and it often arrives at a coast with a long, organised rhythm. Both carry energy. A coast experiences whichever waves reach it, and it is the energy arriving at the shore, not the wind overhead, that does the geomorphic work.

The first key idea is refraction. A wave approaching a coast at an angle bends, so that its crests line up more and more with the contours of the sea floor. The bending concentrates energy on any projecting headland, where the wave front is squeezed into a narrower stretch of water. It spreads energy in a bay, where the front fans out. The same deep-water waves therefore deliver unequal energy per metre of coast. That is why a headland is commonly attacked more than a neighbouring bay, and why a bay tends to fill with sediment while the headland loses rock.

Two terms need to be fixed before the rest of the sequence. A **coast** is the zone of land and sea that is shaped by their interaction, and it has width in both directions. The **shoreline** is the line where water meets land at a particular moment; it moves with the tide and the season. The **coastline** is a line drawn on a map to represent the shore at some scale. Its length depends on the scale of the map, because a finer map follows more bends and gives a longer line. A statement about length therefore always needs its scale and its method.

Tides matter here in two ways. The first is the water level on which waves act: the astronomical rise and fall moves the zone of breaking waves up and down the beach. The second is the tidal range, the vertical difference between high and low water. Range sets the width of the intertidal zone, the strip between the low tide line and the high tide line where waves and tides both act, so a large-range coast spreads wave attack across a wider strip. A high tide can lift waves onto the upper beach or onto structures that are normally out of reach. The mechanics of tides and ocean circulation belong to the lesson on the oceans.

**Limits of the model.** Refraction is a first-order model. It assumes a fairly regular wave train and a sea floor that changes gradually. Real coasts have reefs, submarine channels and river plumes that bend waves in extra ways. The model still identifies the controlling gradient, which is the energy per metre of coast, and it explains why the pattern of headland and bay repeats along many shores. The model does not say how much more a headland erodes, because rock strength is a second control. A soft headland can retreat faster than a hard bay, even though it receives less energy. Energy explains the first-order pattern; rock and structure explain the exceptions, and Lesson 2 takes up that second control.

**A rival view and its reply.** A common objection is that waves simply push water up the beach, so the water itself must be the agent of erosion. The reply is that a wave carries energy, and the work is done when that energy is spent: breaking on rock, lifting material, or carrying sand along the shore. The water that does the work is local and often returns to the sea. Energy, not the mass of water, is the quantity to track.

**Exam use.** A Mains question on coastal erosion or deposition is asking for process. Begin with the energy that arrives at the coast, then the way its distribution changes with the sea floor, then the landforms that follow. A list of landform names without the energy logic is the most common weakness in coastal answers.

Revision notes:

- Wave energy, not wind alone, decides where a coast is attacked.
- Water particles in deep water move in circles; the energy moves forward.
- Wind speed, duration and fetch control the energy of a locally generated wave.
- Swell from distant storms arrives smoother and more regular than local waves.
- Waves slow, steepen and break as they reach shallow water.
- Refraction bends wave crests to follow the sea-floor contours.
- Refraction concentrates energy on headlands and spreads it in bays.
- Coast is a zone; shoreline is a moving line; coastline is a map line whose length depends on scale.
- Tide is the water level on which wave energy acts.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two headlands and a bay face the same deep-water waves from the same direction. Why can the headlands lose rock while the bay gains sediment, even before rock strength is considered?

**Model answer:** Refraction bends the wave crests as they reach the shallow water in front of the headlands, so the wave energy arriving per metre of headland is greater than the energy arriving at the bay. In the bay, the crests fan out and the energy is spread over a longer stretch of shore. Headlands therefore receive more energy to do erosive work, and the bay receives less energy, which lets sediment settle. The explanation is about the distribution of energy along the coast; rock strength then modifies the result.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that headlands are attacked only because they are made of harder rock. Rock resistance matters, but the first-order reason headlands and bays differ is that the same waves deliver unequal energy along the coast. Do not say that waves erode because they are strong. A large wave on a deep, sheltered coast may do little work, while a modest swell on a shallow shelf can break hard. Strength depends on the energy that reaches the shore, which depends on the sea floor as well as the wave.

### Pause and retrieve

Pause and retrieve: a wave that has crossed open water from a distant storm arrives at a bay that is sheltered by headlands. Name two properties of that wave that differ from a local wind wave, and say which part of the coast will receive the smaller share of its energy. (Answer: the swell is smoother and more regular, and the bay receives the spread-out, lower energy.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain how wave refraction produces an alternation of headlands and bays on an initially straight coast.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Waves approaching a coast obliquely slow down and bend as the sea floor shallows, so their crests tend to align with the depth contours. Where the sea floor projects seaward as a headland, the wave front converges and energy per metre of shore rises; where the floor recedes into a bay, the front diverges and energy falls. Over time, the headland receives more wave work and loses rock, while the bay receives less energy and accumulates sediment that its weaker waves can settle. Repeated along a coast, this produces a sequence of projecting headlands and recessed bays. Rock strength and structure can alter the outline, but refraction explains why the alternation forms at all.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for stating that refraction bends crests toward the depth contours; 2 for convergence on headlands and divergence in bays; 3 for linking energy per metre to erosion on headlands and deposition in bays; 2 for the time sequence of headland retreat and bay infilling; 1 for noting rock structure as a modifier.

```text
ENERGY ARRIVES AT THE FOOT OF A CLIFF
        │
        ▼
  HYDRAULIC ACTION     air and water compressed into joints
  ABRASION             sand and pebbles grind the rock face
  SOLUTION             limestone or chalk dissolves
  WEATHERING           salt, wetting and drying loosen the surface
        │
        ▼
  NOTCH cut at the cliff foot
        │
        ▼
  overhang collapses, cliff retreats landward
        │
        ▼
  SHORE PLATFORM left at the foot, widening as the cliff retreats
```

Lesson 1 showed where wave energy concentrates. This lesson shows what that energy does to rock. It helps to separate two groups of processes. The first group wears the rock; the second group removes what has been worn. Weathering loosens the rock by salt crystallisation, by alternate wetting and drying, and by chemical attack. Erosion then takes the loosened material away. Hydraulic action is the force of water and trapped air, which compresses into joints and cracks as each wave strikes. Abrasion is the grinding of rock by sand and pebbles carried by the waves, which works like sandpaper on a cliff face. Solution dissolves limestone and chalk, so it matters only on those rock types.

Attrition is the process most often misunderstood. It is the breaking and rounding of the pebbles and sand that the waves carry. It acts on the load, not directly on the cliff. A pebble that has been rounded by attrition has already been worn; attrition shapes the sediment that abrasion then uses. Keeping this distinction clear prevents a common error, the claim that attrition erodes the cliff.

The sequence of a cliff coast begins with the notch. Waves strike the foot of the cliff most strongly at the level of the sea surface, and hydraulic action and abrasion cut a recess there. The overhang above the notch is unsupported and eventually collapses. The cliff then retreats landward, and the base of the cliff is left as a flat rock bench that is covered by the sea at high tide. This bench is the shore platform. As the cliff retreats the platform widens, because the zone attacked by waves is spread across a broader bed.

The platform has a feedback that matters for later lessons. A wider platform means that waves break further out, and the breaking waves lose much of their energy on the flat, shallow bench before they reach the cliff. The cliff is then attacked less, so retreat slows. The platform therefore tends to limit its own erosion. A wide platform is a sign of a coast that has been attacked for a long time, not of a coast that is retreating quickly.

Where a headland has joints, the waves work along them. A cave forms where the joints are widened by hydraulic action and abrasion. If the cave is cut through the headland from both sides, it becomes an arch. When the arch roof collapses, the remaining pillar is a stack. Further erosion reduces the stack to a stump, a low remnant near sea level. The usual sequence is cave, arch, stack, stump: a stack usually forms after an arch has collapsed. A stack offshore is therefore a clue that an arch may once have existed, not proof of it.

Two smaller features complete the cliff list. A geo is a narrow inlet cut into a cliff, usually where the roof of a sea cave has collapsed or where waves have widened a fault or joint. A blowhole is a vertical opening from a cave roof up to the surface; when a wave drives air and water up through it, spray is pushed out of the opening. Both are local expressions of the same cave-and-joint process rather than a separate sequence.

Structure controls the pattern. Rock beds that dip seaward, and joints that run across the cliff line, make the cliff unstable and encourage collapse. Beds that dip landward or are strongly cemented resist the notch and make a steep cliff that holds for longer. A coast with alternating hard and soft beds develops a series of headlands, caves and bays, because erosion finds the soft beds first.

**Where the model stops.** The sequence is a model of a rocky coast with resistant rock and a steady supply of wave energy. Many coasts do not follow it neatly, because beaches of sand and shingle may hide the platform, and because storms can remove material in a single event. India has fewer high sea cliffs than many temperate coasts, and much of its erosion takes place at beach level. The model is therefore a guide to mechanism, not a census of Indian cliffs.

**A critic's point.** A critic may say that the platform should be cut by the cliff's own collapse, so waves play little part. The reply is that collapse is the last step of a process that starts with wave attack at the notch. The notch is cut by waves before any collapse, so collapse is a consequence of marine erosion, not an alternative to it.

**Answer shape.** A question on landforms of marine erosion is usually asking for a sequence with its mechanism. State the process at each stage, then the landform, then the condition that moves the sequence on. Naming the forms without the process is a loss of marks.

Revision notes:

- Weathering loosens rock; erosion removes it.
- Hydraulic action compresses air and water into joints.
- Abrasion grinds the rock with carried sand and pebbles.
- Solution acts only on soluble rock such as limestone and chalk.
- Attrition rounds the load; it does not directly erode the cliff.
- The notch at the cliff foot starts cliff retreat.
- The shore platform widens as the cliff retreats.
- The platform's shallow breaking zone slows further erosion.
- The usual sequence is cave, arch, stack, stump; a stack usually forms after the arch collapses.
- Seaward-dipping beds and joints encourage collapse; resistant beds hold steep cliffs.

### Concept check

**Question:** A stack stands offshore from a headland. Which landform came before it, and what process produced the change from that landform to the stack?

**Model answer:** The stack most likely came after an arch. Waves widened the joints in the headland into a cave, the cave was cut through to form an arch, and then the arch roof collapsed, leaving the pillar standing in the sea. The stack is the remnant of that collapse. Further erosion would reduce the stack to a stump. The usual logic is cave, arch, stack, stump, and the stack is usually the product of an arch collapsing.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not place the stack before the arch. A stack usually forms only after an arch has existed and its roof has fallen. Do not treat solution as a general marine process. It acts only where the rock is soluble, such as limestone or chalk. Do not say that a wide platform means rapid erosion; the platform usually slows the erosion of the cliff behind it.

### Predict before reading on

Predict: a shore platform is exposed at the base of a retreating cliff. Explain why the platform widens over time, and say what happens to the energy of a wave that breaks on it before reaching the cliff. (Answer: retreat spreads the attacked zone across a broader bed, and the breaking wave loses energy on the flat shallow platform, slowing further cliff attack.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain how wave attack at a cliff foot produces a shore platform, and why the platform can slow the erosion of the cliff that formed it.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Waves attack the foot of a cliff most strongly at sea level. Hydraulic action and abrasion cut a notch there, the overhang above it collapses, and the cliff retreats landward. The base left behind is a shore platform. As the cliff retreats the platform widens, because waves attack a broader area. On a wide platform, waves break further from the cliff and lose much of their energy on the shallow bench, so less energy reaches the cliff foot. The platform therefore tends to slow the erosion of the cliff that formed it. A wide platform signals sustained erosion, not rapid present retreat.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the notch and collapse sequence; 2 for the platform as the exposed base; 2 for widening with retreat; 3 for the energy-loss feedback on the platform; 1 for the distinction between sustained and rapid erosion.

## Lesson 3 - Sediment transport: swash, longshore drift and the littoral cell

```text
WAVE APPROACHES AT AN ANGLE
        │
        ▼
  SWASH up the beach   (wave runs up obliquely)
        │
        ▼
  BACKWASH down the beach  (returns straight down the slope)
        │
        ▼
  net zigzag movement along the shore  =  LONGSHORE DRIFT
        │
        ▼
  LITTORAL CELL: sources → transport → sinks, bounded by headlands,
  canyons or structures that cut the transport path
```

Cut rock does not vanish. It becomes sand and shingle, and waves carry it along the shore. Most sand and shingle along a shore are carried by waves that approach at an angle. The swash runs up the beach obliquely, carrying sediment up the slope. The backwash returns straight down the slope under gravity, taking sediment back toward the sea along the steepest line. Each cycle moves the sediment a little further along the shore than the one before, in a zigzag. The net result, summed over many waves, is longshore drift, a steady movement of sediment parallel to the coast in the direction of the dominant oblique waves.

The direction of drift is fixed by the approach of the waves. Waves arriving from the north-west move sediment toward the south-east, and waves arriving from the south-west move it toward the north-east. Where the wave climate changes with the seasons, drift can reverse seasonally. A permanent net direction on a given reach is the result of the dominant wave direction over the long run, and that direction is the key to reading any shoreline change.

The idea that organises the rest of this lesson is the **littoral cell**. A littoral cell is a stretch of coast within which sediment is produced, transported and deposited, and it is bounded by features that stop the transport, such as a headland or a submarine canyon. Its logic is a budget. Sediment enters from sources such as rivers, eroding cliffs and offshore banks. It moves along the coast and leaves at sinks such as deep water, a canyon or a dune system. Where input exceeds output, the coast advances. Where output exceeds input, it retreats. The budget therefore links a local change to supply and transport elsewhere in the same cell.

The most important consequence follows from the budget. A structure that interrupts drift, such as a breakwater, groyne or harbour wall, traps sediment on its updrift side. The beach on that side advances. The coast beyond the structure receives no replacement sediment, and it continues to lose sand to the waves and currents. That downdrift coast therefore erodes. This is the downdrift transfer of erosion, and it is the single most important reason that coastal defence must be planned across a whole cell and not site by site.

Now apply the logic to a case. Suppose a breakwater is built at a river mouth, and some years later the beach north of it has grown seaward while a village beach to the south has lost much of its sand. Accretion on the north side means that sediment has been trapped there. The trapped sediment arrived from the north, because a structure traps material on its updrift side. The net drift must therefore run from north to south: sediment travels south along the coast, is intercepted by the breakwater, and piles up on the north. The starved reach is the south side, which is downdrift, exactly as the logic predicts. Notice that the direction is inferred from where the material piles up, not from any single measurement, and that the inference is strongest when the shoreline pattern is clear.

A second cause must also be kept open. A reduction in the river's sediment supply, from an upstream dam, a catchment change or sand extraction in the channel, would also starve the cell and erode its downdrift end. Shoreline shape cannot separate the two causes unless sediment flux data are available. A good answer therefore names the breakwater as one candidate and reduced supply as another, and states what evidence would decide between them.

Sand extraction from the beach or the nearshore channel is a direct withdrawal from the budget. Sand taken from the transport path would otherwise have reached a sink or a neighbouring beach, so the effect appears downdrift and at the sinks, often years later. Coastal sand mining is therefore one of the most direct human entries into the sediment budget, and the 2019 Mains question on its threats is supported by this mechanism.

**Assumptions and limits.** Longshore drift assumes a dominant oblique wave approach. Where waves approach from many directions, net drift is small or unclear. The littoral cell is a useful unit, but its boundaries are not always sharp, because sediment can leak across them in storms. The budget also assumes that sediment is conserved, which holds over a cell but not for a single beach. Sediment budgets are difficult to measure. A morphological reading gives the direction and the likely cause, but a sediment-flux record would be needed to quantify the share of each cause. The residual is therefore an honest statement of uncertainty, not a gap to be filled with a confident number.

**The strongest objection.** A critic may argue that a breakwater that makes the north beach grow is simply a good thing. The reply is that the growth on one side is paid for by erosion on the other. The budget is conserved, so the sand gained in one place is sand lost in another. The correct question is not whether a structure helps one beach, but where the transferred erosion falls and who bears it.

**Examiner's lens.** A question on sediment transport or on coastal protection is asking for the cell logic: sources, transport, sinks and the downdrift consequence of an interruption. A protection answer that does not state the downdrift transfer is incomplete.

Revision notes:

- Swash runs up the beach obliquely; backwash returns down the slope.
- The zigzag of swash and backwash produces longshore drift.
- The direction of drift follows the dominant oblique wave approach.
- A littoral cell is a unit of sources, transport and sinks bounded by barriers.
- Input above output advances the coast; output above input retreats it.
- A structure traps sediment on its updrift side.
- The starved coast beyond the structure is downdrift and erodes.
- Accretion on one side of a structure indicates the drift direction.
- A reduction in river sediment can starve a cell as well as a structure can.
- Protection must be planned across the cell, not site by site.

### Concept check

**Question:** Beach A, north of a new breakwater, has grown seaward. Beach B, five kilometres south, has lost much of its sand. Which way does the net longshore drift run, and what is the other cause that the shoreline shape alone cannot exclude?

**Model answer:** Sediment is trapped on the updrift side of a structure, so accretion on the north side means that material arrived from the north. The net drift therefore runs from north to south, carrying sediment toward the breakwater and the south coast beyond it. Beach B is downdrift and starved, as the budget predicts. The other cause that the shape cannot exclude is a reduction in the river's sediment supply, through an upstream dam, catchment change or channel sand extraction. That would also starve the cell and erode the downdrift end, so the two causes can only be separated with sediment-flux data.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not read the accreting beach and the eroding beach as two problems with two remedies. They are one budget, with an obstruction in its path; building a second defence on the eroding beach only moves the deficit further downdrift. Do not say that longshore drift moves sediment only in one fixed direction everywhere. The direction depends on the local wave approach, and it can reverse with the seasons. Do not treat the accreting and eroding beaches as two separate problems with two separate remedies; they are one budget.

### Map task

Map task: a groyne is built on a beach that receives sand from the north. Which beach gains sand, which loses it, and in which direction does the lost sand travel? (Answer: the north side gains; the south side loses; the net drift runs south and the starved reach is downdrift.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain why a groyne that protects one beach can starve the coast beyond it, and state one condition under which the transfer is smaller.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast in the direction of the dominant oblique waves. A groyne projecting across the drift path traps sediment on its updrift side, so that beach gains. The sediment is not created by the groyne; it is removed from the transport path that would otherwise have carried it to the downdrift coast. That coast therefore receives less sand and erodes. The transfer is smaller where the drift is weak or where the downdrift coast receives a replacement supply from a river or a cliff, because the budget then has an additional input. The groyne protects one reach by moving the deficit, not by removing it.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the trapping mechanism on the updrift side; 2 for the removal of sediment from the transport path; 3 for the downdrift starvation and its link to the budget; 2 for a correct condition that reduces the transfer; 1 for the statement that the deficit is moved, not removed.

```text
SEDIMENT ARRIVES BY LONGSHORE DRIFT
        │
        ├── piles up at the shore ............ BEACH (berm, sorted sand or shingle)
        │
        ├── extends from land into water ..... SPIT (ridge attached at one end)
        │        └── tip recurves ............ recurved spit
        │
        ├── spit or ridge crosses a bay ...... BAR
        │        └── water behind it ......... LAGOON
        │
        ├── ridge joins island to mainland ... TOMBOLO
        │
        └── ridge parallel to the coast ...... BARRIER (may enclose a lagoon)
```

Sediment that stops moving builds something. The shape it takes, and what happens to the water behind it, is the subject here. The forms that build on a coast are not a random list. Each one records the same two decisions: where the sediment is deposited, and what happens to the water behind it. A useful way to learn them is to ask, for each form, two questions. Is it attached to land at one end or both? Does it enclose water?

A beach is the simplest form. It is an accumulation of sand or shingle along the shore, shaped by the waves that work it. Waves that break and surge up the beach tend to build a steeper, coarser profile and to deposit material above the water line in a flat-topped ridge called a berm. Waves that break and draw back strongly tend to remove sand from the beach face and carry it offshore, leaving a flatter, finer profile. The sorting is also a process: heavier and coarser grains settle first and finer grains are carried further. A beach is therefore a record of the waves that built it. Two beaches on the same coast can look different because they are built by different waves.

A spit is a ridge of sand or shingle that extends from the land into the water. It forms where longshore drift meets a change in the coastline, such as a bay mouth or an estuary, and the drifting sediment is deposited in the calmer water beyond the point of change. A spit is attached to the land at one end and open at the other. Its end often curves, forming a recurved spit, because waves wrapping around the tip of the spit are refracted back toward the land and deposit material along a hook. The recurve is a clue to the direction of the waves that built the spit.

A bar is a spit or ridge that grows across a bay or an inlet, joining the two sides rather than extending from one. A bar that closes the mouth of a bay can enclose the water behind it. That enclosed water is a lagoon. The bar and the lagoon are thus linked: the bar is the sediment that closes the mouth, and the lagoon is the water it encloses. A lagoon is therefore not a landform built directly by waves, but a water body that a depositional ridge has cut off from the open sea.

A tombolo is a spit or bar that connects an island to the mainland. Waves and currents refract around the island and deposit sediment in the sheltered zone behind it, so the sediment builds a link. A tombolo tying an island to the shore is a distinct form, and it must not be confused with a spit, which is attached to land at one end only, or with a barrier, which runs parallel to the coast.

A barrier is a long ridge of sand or shingle that runs parallel to the coast, often some distance offshore, and that encloses a lagoon or marsh between itself and the land. Barriers are common where sediment is abundant and the coast is low. Their behaviour in storms and over time is one reason why low coasts are sensitive to sea-level change, a theme taken up in Lessons 6 and 8.

A lagoon needs particular care, because a common error is to treat it as a freshwater lake. A coastal lagoon is a body of water enclosed behind a bar or spit, and it is often brackish, because it receives both river water and sea water. Its salinity changes with the season, with the opening of the lagoon mouth and with rainfall. Some lagoons are temporary, drying in the dry season and filling again in the wet. The east coast of India has well-known lagoons. Chilika in Odisha and Pulicat on the Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu border lie behind sandy barriers and are both brackish and seasonal in character. The ecology of these lagoons and their status as wetlands is taught in the lakes and wetlands lesson; here the point is their depositional origin.

**Limits.** The classification assumes that the form has a single dominant process. In practice a coast can carry several forms at once, and a spit can grow into a bar, and a bar can later become a barrier. Names are therefore descriptions of a stage, not permanent categories. The distinction between these forms is also sometimes a matter of scale: a feature a few hundred metres long and a kilometre long may belong to the same family. Whether a given lagoon was enclosed by a bar that grew from a spit, or by a barrier that was reworked by storms, often cannot be settled from the landform alone. The residual is a historical question that needs dating and stratigraphy, and this lesson does not claim to settle it.

**A sceptic's point.** A critic may say that the names are arbitrary, since many forms look alike from the air. The reply is that the forms differ in their attachment and in what they enclose, and those differences tell you the process. A spit is attached at one end; a bar joins two shores; a tombolo links an island; a barrier runs along the coast; and a lagoon is the water they hold. Each name carries a different causal history.

**In the answer.** A question on depositional coastal landforms asks for the process of formation and the distinction between forms. Name the form, state how it is attached or what it encloses, and then give the drift or refraction process that built it. A description that calls a lagoon an erosional feature is a basic error.

- A beach is sand or shingle shaped by the waves that built it.
- A berm is a flat ridge above the water line built by swash.
- Sorting places coarse grains first and fine grains further offshore.
- A spit is attached to land at one end and extends into the water.
- A recurved spit hooks because waves refract around its tip.
- A bar joins two shores across a bay or inlet mouth.
- A lagoon is the water enclosed behind a bar or spit.
- A tombolo connects an island to the mainland.
- A barrier runs parallel to the coast and can enclose a lagoon or marsh.

**Model answer:** The ridge is a recurved spit: it grows out from the land, and its free end curves back because waves refract around its tip and deposit material along the hook. Where it curves to enclose water, the enclosed water is a lagoon. The spit is the depositional form that extends from the land, and the lagoon is the water it cuts off from the open sea. A lagoon is therefore the result of the depositional ridge, not a separate erosional feature.

Do not say that a lagoon is always freshwater; a coastal lagoon is commonly brackish. Do not say that a tombolo separates an island from the mainland; it connects them.

```text
SAME COAST, TWO DIRECTIONS OF RELATIVE CHANGE

  SUBMERGENT: land sinks or sea rises
        valleys flood  ──►  RIA (drowned river valley, branching inlets)
                       ──►  FJORD (drowned glacial trough, steep walls)
                       ──►  DALMATIAN (drowned ridges parallel to the coast)

```

Picture the same coastline after the land has sunk by a few metres, and then after it has risen. The shoreline would look different in each case, even with the same sea. A coast is a boundary between two moving surfaces, the sea and the land. Its character depends on which of them has moved relative to the other. The phrase that matters is **relative sea level**: the level of the sea measured against the land it meets. If the land sinks, or the sea rises, the relative sea level rises and the coast is submergent. If the land rises, or the sea falls, the relative sea level falls and the coast is emergent. The distinction depends on relative movement, not on wave energy, and that is the most common confusion in this topic.

A submergent coast is one that has been drowned. Its valleys and hollows have been flooded by the sea, so the land's own features now meet the water in a new pattern. Three landforms are the classic signatures. A **ria** is a drowned river valley. Its inlets branch like the arms of a tree, because the river system that cut the valley had many tributaries. A **fjord** is a drowned glacial trough. It is steep-sided and often deep, because glaciers carved a U-shaped valley that the sea then filled. A **Dalmatian** coast is one where long ridges parallel to the shore have been drowned, so that the sea fills the valleys between them and the ridges remain as long, narrow islands and channels. The type locality of the Dalmatian pattern is the Adriatic coast of the Dinaric region.

Two of these forms are easily confused, and the confusion is a common trap. A fjord is not a drowned river valley. A drowned river valley is a ria. The test is the shape of the valley before flooding: a river-cut valley has branching tributaries and a gradually graded profile, while a glacial trough has a U-shaped cross-section, steep walls and often a sill or threshold near its mouth. The fjord belongs to the glacial landforms lesson, which explains how the trough is cut. Here it is enough to know that the sea has filled a trough that ice had already made.

An emergent coast is one that has been raised, or whose sea has retreated. Its marks are different. Old wave-cut platforms and beaches stand above the present sea level as **raised beaches** and terraces. Straight, cliffed coastlines are common, because the sea has not had time to cut new embayments in the rising land. Offshore water near the coast is often shallow for some distance, because the sea floor was lifted with the land.

The relative sea-level model has a limit that must be stated plainly. Real coasts rarely fit one type over their whole length. A coast can drown in one sector and rise in another, because the land moves differently from place to place. The types are regional tendencies. The India case shows this clearly. The west coast is generally narrow and estuarine, with rivers that have drowned their mouths, and the east coast is broad and deltaic, built by large rivers. Describing either coast as uniformly submergent or emergent would be a simplification that a careful examiner would challenge.

**What the classification assumes.** The classification assumes that the landform reflects a recent relative change. Some drowned landforms may have been drowned long ago and then reworked. A ria, a fjord and a Dalmatian coast can also occur in combination with tectonic folding, and their origin should be checked against the local geology. Whether an apparently drowned coast was drowned by sea-level rise, by land subsidence or by a combination is often an open question. The residual is a claim that the classification alone does not resolve, and the next lesson shows why.

**A rival reading.** A critic may say that a fjord and a ria are both drowned valleys, so the distinction is pedantic. The reply is that the two valleys were formed by different agents, a river and a glacier, and that difference determines their shape, their depth and their sill. Naming the agent is what makes the form intelligible.

**What examiners test.** A question on emergent and submergent coasts asks for the relative movement and the landform signature. Name the direction of relative change first, then give the landform and the reason it carries that signature. A question about a ria or a fjord asks you to name the agent that carved the valley, not only its shape.

Revision notes:

- Emergent and submergent describe relative movement between land and sea.
- A submergent coast has been drowned: its valleys are flooded.
- A ria is a drowned river valley with branching inlets.
- A fjord is a drowned glacial trough, steep-sided and U-shaped.
- A Dalmatian coast has drowned ridges parallel to the shore.
- An emergent coast has raised beaches and terraces and often straight cliffs.
- Relative sea level combines ocean-level change with land movement.
- Classification is regional; a single coast can change type along its length.
- The west and east coasts of India differ in their dominant form, and both are tendencies.

### Concept check

**Question:** A valley system on a coast has branching inlets and a gradually sloping profile. A second has a U-shaped cross-section, steep walls and a sill near its mouth. Name the drowned form of each and state the agent that carved each valley.

**Model answer:** The first is a ria: its branching inlets and gradual profile indicate a river-cut valley system that the sea has flooded. The second is a fjord: its U-shaped cross-section, steep walls and sill indicate a glacial trough that the sea has filled. The agent is a river for the ria and a glacier for the fjord. The shape of the valley before flooding is what identifies the agent.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not call every deep inlet a fjord. A fjord requires a glacial trough; a deep inlet cut by a river is a ria or an estuary. A fjord is not a drowned river valley. Emergent and submergent are not wave-energy classes; they describe relative land and sea movement. Do not state that every Indian coast is one of these types.

### Pause and retrieve

Pause and retrieve: a coast is marked by raised beaches and a straight cliffed shoreline. Is it submergent or emergent, and what has happened to the relative sea level? (Answer: emergent; the relative sea level has fallen because the land has risen or the sea has retreated.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain how the relative movement of land and sea produces contrasting coastal landforms, using one submergent and one emergent example.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Relative sea level is the sea level measured against the land it meets. Where the land sinks or the sea rises, valleys flood and the coast is submergent: a river-cut valley becomes a ria with branching inlets, and a glacial trough becomes a fjord with steep walls. Where the land rises or the sea falls, former shore platforms and beaches stand above the present sea as raised beaches and terraces, and the coast is emergent, often straight and cliffed. The two directions leave different signatures because one floods valleys that already exist and the other lifts marine features above the sea. The relative movement, not wave energy, is what separates the two.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for defining relative sea level; 2 for the submergent process and its landform; 2 for the emergent process and its landform; 2 for the ria and fjord distinction by agent; 2 for noting regional variation.

```text
GLOBAL CHANGE IN OCEAN VOLUME                LOCAL CHANGE IN LAND LEVEL
  thermal expansion (water warms)              subsidence (compaction, pumping)
  mountain glaciers and ice caps               glacial isostatic adjustment
  ice sheets (Greenland, Antarctica)           ocean dynamics (winds, currents)
  land water storage (pumping, dams)           gravitational fingerprinting
        │                                              │
        └──────────────► RELATIVE SEA LEVEL ◄──────────┘
                         (what floods a particular coast)
```

A coast can look stable while the relative sea level beneath it is changing. This lesson asks what makes relative sea level change, and why it does not change by the same amount everywhere. The first step is to revise a common picture. Many people imagine sea-level rise as a bath filling evenly. The reality is that the sea surface is not a flat plane with a single level, and that the land beneath the coast is also moving. What a coast experiences is the difference between the two.

The global part of the change comes from adding or redistributing water in the ocean. Four components are usually listed. Thermal expansion means that water expands as it warms, so a warmer ocean occupies more volume. Mountain glaciers and ice caps lose mass by melting and transfer it to the ocean. The large ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica are the largest long-term uncertainty, because their discharge depends on processes that are hard to predict. Land water storage can work in either direction: groundwater pumped out of aquifers and eventually reaching the sea adds water to the ocean, while water held back by reservoirs does the opposite.

The local part is set by movement of the land and by the ocean itself. Land subsidence is especially important on coasts built of soft sediment. Deltas compact under their own weight, and withdrawal of groundwater or fluid can make the surface sink. A delta can therefore sink faster than the global rise, so that the relative sea level at its shore rises more quickly than the average. Glacial isostatic adjustment is a slow movement of the land still recovering from the weight of ice that once covered it. Some land is rising, and some is still sinking, as a result. Ocean dynamics concern the way winds, currents and water density hold the sea surface higher in some regions than others. Gravitational fingerprinting reflects the fact that a large ice mass attracts water toward itself. When the ice melts, the sea near it falls slightly, while the sea far from it rises more than average.

The last point is a useful reversal. Sea level near a melting ice sheet can fall, while sea level far from it rises more than average. The fingerprint is a pattern, not a uniform change. This is why a single global number cannot describe the change at a given coast.

A precise statement of the limits is needed. This lesson does not quote a global or Indian rate of sea-level rise, nor a projection for a particular year. Rates depend on the period, the measurement method and the scenario, and a rate quoted without them is easily misused. The usable evidence for a given coast is the record from a tide gauge at that coast, read with the land movement at that point. The history of the sea is also relevant. In the late last glacial period, global sea level stood roughly 120 to 125 metres below its present level, the figure varying by place and method. The rise began about 20,000 years ago, and sea level came close to its present level only around 6,000 to 7,000 years ago. That long history explains why the drowned coasts of the present were formed by a sea that has been moving for a very long time.

**Example and its limit.** The Ganga-Brahmaputra delta is a useful Indian case. Its surface is low and its sediment is compacting, and extraction of groundwater adds to the sinking. Sea-level change alone cannot explain its vulnerability, because the sinking and the loss of new sediment matter just as much. The limit is that subsidence differs from one part of the delta to another, so a single rate cannot be applied to the whole.

**The objection to test.** A critic may say that if the global mean is rising slowly, local risk must be small. The reply is that the relevant quantity is relative sea level at the coast, which may rise much faster than the global mean because of subsidence. A slow global rise on a sinking delta is still a fast local change.

The contribution of each component at a given coast in a given decade is not settled by the classification. It requires measurement of the land and the sea at that place. The residual is an empirical question, and an honest answer names it as one.

**Answer use.** A question on sea-level change asks for the components, the local modifiers and the difference between global and relative change. A strong answer names subsidence as a co-driver for deltas, rather than treating sea-level rise as a uniform rise everywhere.



Revision notes:

- Relative sea level combines the ocean-level change with land movement.
- Thermal expansion means that warmer water occupies more volume.
- Glaciers, ice caps and ice sheets add water to the ocean when they melt.
- Land water storage can add water through pumping or remove it through reservoirs.
- Subsidence, from compaction or fluid withdrawal, can dominate on deltas.
- Glacial isostatic adjustment is a slow recovery of land from ice loading.
- Ocean dynamics can hold the sea surface higher or lower in some regions.
- Gravitational fingerprinting makes sea level fall near a melting ice sheet and rise more elsewhere.
- Global rates must be quoted with their period and method; local tide-gauge records are the evidence.
- In the late last glacial period sea level was roughly 120 to 125 metres lower than today.

### Concept check

**Question:** Global mean sea level rises by a modest amount, yet a delta in the same ocean experiences a much faster rise at its shore. Explain the difference using the components of relative sea level.

**Model answer:** The shore experiences relative sea level, which combines the global ocean-volume change with the vertical movement of the land. The delta's land is sinking through compaction, sediment loading and possibly groundwater extraction, so the land contributes an additional fall relative to the sea. The shore's relative rise is therefore the global change plus the local subsidence, and the subsidence can exceed the global signal. The delta is not experiencing a different ocean; it is experiencing a sinking floor.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the global mean as the level that floods a particular coast. Local land movement can make the local rise much larger or smaller than the global average.

### Prediction task

Predict: a melting ice sheet lowers the sea level near it. Why does the sea far from the ice rise more than the global average, and what does this imply for a single number for global sea-level rise? (Answer: gravitational fingerprinting; the pattern is uneven, so one global number cannot describe the change at a given coast.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain why the same global sea-level change can produce very different relative changes at two coasts, identifying two local modifiers.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** Relative sea level at a coast combines the global change in ocean volume with the vertical movement of the land and local ocean conditions. Two modifiers commonly dominate. Subsidence, through compaction and fluid withdrawal, lowers the land on soft delta sediments, so a delta can experience a rise much greater than the global mean. Glacial isostatic adjustment makes some land rise and other land sink as it recovers from past ice loading. Gravitational fingerprinting and ocean dynamics add further regional variation, so a coast far from a melting ice sheet can experience a larger rise than the average. The two coasts therefore differ because their land and local conditions differ, not because the global change differs.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for relative sea level as the combined quantity; 3 for subsidence and its effect on a delta; 2 for glacial isostatic adjustment; 2 for fingerprinting or ocean dynamics; 1 for the explicit statement that the global change is the same.

```text
CYCLONE OVER THE SEA
        │
        ▼
  wind stress pushes surface water toward the coast      (dominant)
  low central pressure lets the sea surface rise          (lesser)
        │
        ▼
  water piles up on a shallow, funnel-shaped shelf

```

A flood can arrive from the sea rather than from a river. That is the storm surge: the coastal flood that a cyclone can drive ashore. It is not heavy rain, and it is not a tsunami. A storm surge is an abnormal rise of sea water pushed onto the coast, and it is the principal cause of loss of life and property in many tropical cyclone landfalls. This lesson explains how the surge forms and why its damage varies so widely from one coast to another.

The dominant cause is wind stress. A cyclone's winds blow across the sea surface and drag the surface water along with them. Where the wind blows toward the coast, the water is pushed shoreward and piles up against the land. This is the main contributor, and it explains why the side of a cyclone where the wind blows onshore is the most dangerous. Low central pressure is a real but lesser cause. Lower air pressure allows the sea surface beneath the storm to rise slightly, because there is less weight of air on the water. The effect is much smaller than the wind effect, so an answer that leads with pressure has reversed the order of importance.

Four features of the coast then amplify the surge. A shallow, gently shelving sea floor cannot let the piled water escape downward or seaward, so the water rises higher. A funnel-shaped or converging coastline squeezes the water into a narrowing space and raises it further. Coincidence with high tide decides whether a given surge overtops defences. The surge rides on top of the tide, so the same surge can be harmless at low water and damaging at high water. Wave set-up and run-up add more height, because breaking waves push water above the surge level on the shore. Low, flat land then determines how far the water travels inland, because nothing halts its penetration.

The most useful idea is the **compounding rule**. Surge, tide, wave run-up and river discharge can arrive together, and the damage depends on their coincidence. A moderate storm at high tide during a river flood can cause more damage than a stronger storm at low tide. This is the same non-linearity that Lesson 8 applies to coastal cities: a modest change in the level of any driver can turn a rare joint event into a frequent one.

**India example and its limit.** The Bay of Bengal has a broad, shallow northern shelf, and its deltaic coasts are low and densely settled. These features combine to make the north of the bay especially exposed to surge. The stretch from Odisha through West Bengal to Bangladesh is commonly cited as especially surge-prone for this reason. The limit is that surge height depends on a particular cyclone's track, intensity and timing, so the coast's exposure is a matter of configuration and not of a fixed height. This lesson does not quote surge heights, return periods or casualty figures; those belong to the disaster-management treatment of cyclones and are not verified here.

**Boundary: tsunamis.** The 2025 Mains question on tsunamis asks for formation and consequences. Formation is a different process: sea-floor displacement by an earthquake or landslide launches a long-period wave train, and its origin is taught in the tectonics treatment and its warning systems in disaster management. The coastal consequences follow the same logic as a surge, the water reaching land and flooding it, but the source is different. A storm surge is a wind-driven rise of the water surface; a tsunami is a wave from the sea floor. Keeping those two sources apart is the approach this lesson gives to the coastal part of such an answer. Warning and evacuation are disaster-management content, and this lesson supplies only the coastal mechanism.

**A reasonable doubt.** A critic may argue that a surge is just a large wave, so its mechanism is the same as wave erosion. The reply is that a surge is a rise of the whole water level driven by wind and pressure over a wide area, not a breaking wave. It floods land that normal waves never reach, so its geomorphic and human effects differ in kind.

The relative weight of wind stress, pressure and shelf shape at a given landfall depends on the storm and the coast. The general mechanism is secure; the magnitude at a particular place needs modelling and observation that this lesson does not supply.

**Exam framing.** A question on coastal hazards asks for the mechanism, the amplifiers and the reason that exposure varies. Begin with wind stress, then the lesser role of pressure, then the amplifiers and the coincidence with the tide. Stating that deltas are the most exposed, with the reasons, is a strong closing point.

Revision notes:

- A storm surge is an abnormal rise of sea water pushed onto the coast by a cyclone.
- Wind stress is the dominant cause; low pressure is lesser.
- A shallow, gently shelving shelf amplifies the surge.
- A funnel-shaped coast squeezes water and raises it.
- The surge rides on the astronomical tide; coincidence with high tide determines overtopping.
- Wave set-up and run-up add height on top of the surge.
- Low, flat land determines how far the water penetrates.
- Compounding: damage depends on the coincidence of surge, tide, waves and river flow.
- Deltaic coasts combine shallow shelves, low elevation and dense settlement.
- Tsunami formation and warning belong to other treatments.

### Concept check

**Question:** A moderate cyclone makes landfall at high tide during a river flood. A stronger cyclone makes landfall at low tide on a different stretch of coast. Explain which event may flood more land, and say which mechanism decides the answer.

**Model answer:** The moderate cyclone may flood more land. Its surge rides on a high astronomical tide and meets river discharge, so the water level at the shore is much higher than the surge alone. The stronger cyclone's surge arrives at low tide and may not overtop defences or reach far inland. The deciding mechanism is the coincidence of the drivers, not the peak wind speed alone: because the surge adds to the tide and the river, the joint level can exceed the stronger event's level at a different time.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not rank cyclones only by their wind speed. The flood depends on how the surge combines with the tide, river flow and the shape of the coast at the point of landfall. Do not say that a surge is caused mainly by low pressure. Do not say that a surge is the same as heavy rainfall flooding. Do not quote a surge height from memory.

### Quick recall

Quick recall: a cyclone's winds blow onshore across a shallow shelf. Which contributor is dominant, which is lesser, and what does the shallow shelf do to the water? (Answer: wind stress is dominant; low pressure is lesser; the shelf stops the water from escaping and so raises it.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Explain why the same cyclone can produce very different flood extents on two coasts of a bay, using the amplifiers of a storm surge.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** A storm surge is driven mainly by wind stress pushing sea water toward the coast, with low pressure adding a smaller rise. The water is then amplified by the shape of the coast. On a shallow, gently shelving shelf the water cannot escape, so it piles higher. On a funnel-shaped stretch the water is squeezed into a narrowing space. Two coasts of the same bay may differ in these features, and in their elevation: a low, flat deltaic coast lets the water penetrate far inland, while a higher coast stops it. Timing matters too, because a surge arriving at high tide reaches much higher than one arriving at low tide. The two coasts therefore differ in shelf, shape, elevation and tidal coincidence, not in the storm's wind alone.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for wind stress as the dominant driver and pressure as lesser; 2 for the shelf amplifier; 2 for the funnel shape; 2 for low elevation and inland penetration; 2 for tidal coincidence.

Take one rise in relative sea level and apply it to five different coasts. The answer is not the same everywhere, and that difference is why sea-level change is a planning problem rather than a single number. A coast's risk depends on how low it lies, how its land moves, how much sediment reaches it, how many people live on it and how its water is used. Five coastal types show the range.

A **delta** is the highest combined risk. Its land is low and its sediment is compacting. The river that built it may now deliver less sediment, because dams, sand extraction and changed catchments trap material upstream. The delta is also densely settled and farmed, and its groundwater is used heavily. A rising relative sea level therefore meets a sinking, starved and crowded surface. The Ganga-Brahmaputra delta is the Indian case, and the running-water treatment covers its river origin. This lesson treats only its coastal risk.

A **low coral island** faces what the risk literature calls existential exposure. Its elevation is very small, so a modest rise can cover much of its land. Its freshwater is held in a thin lens of groundwater floating above salt water, and a rising sea shrinks the lens and admits salt. Its retreat space is limited, because the island is surrounded by sea. The reefs and island geography of such islands are taught in the islands lesson.

A **sandy barrier or beach coast** faces shoreline retreat. The beach migrates landward as the sea rises, rolling back over its own sediment. Hard structures built to stop this interrupt longshore drift, as Lesson 3 showed, and so shift the erosion to neighbouring beaches.

An **estuarine city** faces compound flooding. Its drainage must discharge into a sea that stands higher at high tide, and storm surge and heavy rain can arrive together. The damaging event is then not the sea-level rise alone but the coincidence of surge, high tide and rainfall. A modest permanent rise turns a formerly rare joint event into a frequent one. This non-linearity is the central analytical point of the lesson.

A **rocky cliffed coast** faces the lowest inundation risk, because its elevation protects it. Undercutting and mass failure continue, so it is not safe, but flooding is not its main threat.

Two transmissions of risk matter as much as inundation, and they often act first. Saline intrusion moves salt water inland through aquifers and up estuaries when the sea rises and freshwater pressure falls. It degrades drinking water and soils years before any land is permanently lost. Its mechanism is set by the freshwater head that holds the salt water back, and the same aquifer may be depleted by pumping, which also draws salt water in. A seawater-intrusion problem therefore has more than one cause, and the remedy must address the cause that is operating. The groundwater mechanism is taught in the lesson on weathering and groundwater, and the intrusion question in the environment treatment of coastal ecology.

Inundation is the permanent loss of land to the sea. On a rising coast it usually comes last, after saline intrusion and compound flooding have often already changed the land. This ordering is the reason that the early impacts of sea-level change are often hidden: the water supply fails before the shore is lost.

**Remedies for intrusion.** The remedy follows the pathway. The first aim is to restore the freshwater head that holds salt water back: managed recharge of aquifers in the wet season, and limits on withdrawal so that pumping does not draw salt water inland. Where an estuary or a coastal aquifer is threatened, engineered barriers or controlled freshwater releases can hold the salt wedge back, and monitoring of wells and salinity shows whether a remedy is working. Each measure has a cost, and its success depends on the source of the water and on the pumping pattern. The policy and governance of groundwater belong to the environment treatment; this lesson supplies the mechanism that a remedy must address.

**Where the ranking stops.** The ranking of coastal types is qualitative. A delta may be much safer in one stretch than another, depending on its sediment and subsidence, and a city's drainage may be good enough to resist compound flooding for a long time. The ranking identifies the dominant risk for each type, not its certain outcome. Which of several causes dominates saline intrusion on a given aquifer requires measurement of water levels and salinity. The residual is an empirical question that the classification cannot settle.

**The rival argument.** A critic may argue that a rocky coast is therefore safe. The reply is that a rocky coast is protected from inundation only; it still suffers undercutting and collapse, which are real losses for the people and assets on the cliff top.

**Application in answers.** A question on sea-level impacts asks for the differentiated consequences: the coastal types, their dominant risks, the early transmissions and the reasons they differ. A strong answer names subsidence and sediment starvation for deltas, the freshwater lens for islands, and compound flooding for cities.

India has two coasts that face the same ocean system in different ways. The lessons so far explained the processes; this lesson applies them to the Indian peninsula, and uses the Rann of Kachchh to show how a marsh can form without a single simple cause. A useful first step is to remember the lesson on coastal types. The west and east coasts of India are not mirror images, and the contrast between them is a tendency, not a rule.

The west coast runs nearly north to south from Gujarat to Kanniyakumari, and it is generally narrow. It is narrow because the Western Ghats stand close to the Arabian Sea, so there is little room for a wide coastal plain. Its coastline is cut by estuaries and creeks. The Narmada and the Tapi form estuaries rather than deltas on the west coast, because their mouths are drowned and their sediment is not built out into a broad delta. The Kerala backwaters, including the large lake of Vembanad, are a distinctive feature of the south-west. The coast is commonly described in sectors: the Gujarat coast, the Konkan, the Karnataka coast and the Kerala or Malabar coast. Its deep natural harbours are more common than on the east, which matters for port geography. Harbour suitability follows the coast type: a drowned coast offers deep inlets that can serve as natural harbours, while an emergent or deltaic coast, with its shallow shelf and silt, depends more on artificial or silt-managed ports.

The east coast runs from the Ganga delta to Kanniyakumari, facing the Bay of Bengal. It is broad and alluvial, because large east-flowing rivers have built deltas on it. Its sectors are commonly named the Utkal plain and Northern Circars in the north and the Coromandel coast in the south. The Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Cauvery are the principal deltaic systems. The east coast also has lagoons, including Chilika in Odisha and Pulicat on the Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu border. Because its rivers build out and its coast is low, it has fewer natural harbours, and many of its ports depend on managing silt.

The contrast has a caveat that should be stated openly. The west coast is commonly described as submergent, with drowned estuaries, and the east coast as deltaic and emergent in parts. But textbooks differ on how to classify the western margin of the peninsula, and some describe it as emergent in its upland parts. This conflict is not resolved here. The safe statement is that the two coasts differ in the dominant landforms and the river mouths, and that each contains exceptions to the regional description.

**The Rann of Kachchh.** The Great Rann of Kachchh is an extensive seasonal saline marsh and mudflat in the north-west of Gujarat. Its surface is flat, wet in the monsoon and salt-encrusted in the dry season. Its landscape records a sea that once covered parts of the region as a shallow gulf, and that has since retreated and been filled with sediment. The Rann's history is not a single sea-level event. Relative marine regression, sediment infilling, tectonic movement of the land and aridity have all contributed, and the Allah Bund, a bund-like embankment, is part of the local record. Its formation is linked in published accounts to tectonic movement, a link to be checked against the primary study. Attributing the Rann to a fall of sea level alone would be a simplification.

The Rann teaches a general point about marshes. Flat, wet and salty ground can be produced by several processes, and these must be separated. A natural saline marsh or mudflat, a mangrove estuary, a salt pan made by people and other swamps are not interchangeable merely because they all look flat and wet. The process behind each determines its origin and its future.

**The 2023 Prelims demand on marshlands.** A 2023 Prelims question concerns the processes that build marsh on low-gradient coasts. The official key is not available to this lesson, and no option or answer is recorded here. The mechanism that bears on it is taught in this lesson: where a low-gradient coast is repeatedly raised and lowered in relative sea level, the shoreline moves back and forth across the flat, and the sediment and salt left behind can build extensive marsh. The Rann illustrates a combined version of that process.

**Limits of the framework.** The west-east contrast assumes that the rivers, the land and the sea combine in the broad pattern described. Exceptions exist in every sector. The Rann's origin is the subject of published research, and the account here stays at the level of process, without dates for any event. The relative weight of tectonics, sediment and sea level in the Rann is still a subject of study, and this lesson does not settle it.

**A common rejoinder.** A critic may say the two coasts are simply the same ocean seen from two sides. The reply is that the same ocean produces different coasts because the land behind it is different: the west has a narrow, steep hinterland, and the east has large rivers that build deltas.

**Answer strategy.** A question on India's coasts asks for the contrast and its reasons, with at least one example from each coast. A question on marshes or salt flats asks you to name the process and to separate it from other causes.

- Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Cauvery form east-coast deltas.
- Chilika and Pulicat are east-coast lagoons.

```text
ONE COAST, THREE MEASUREMENTS

  method A: national mapping, 1990-2018 shoreline analysis  ──► a length, plus a change split
  method B: an older official series, different base map   ──► a longer or shorter length
  method C: a later official revision on a finer basis     ──► a much longer length

```

A coastline has no fixed length: measure it with a finer scale and it grows. This lesson applies that to a real national question: how long is India's coast, and how much of it is eroding? The answer requires two separate skills. The first is to read a length with its method and scale. The second is to read a change statistic as a share of a particular length in a particular period, not as an annual rate.

Consider the measurement problem first. A coastline is irregular at every scale. A finer map follows more inlets, headlands and bays, so the same shore gets longer as the scale gets finer. Two official measurements of India's coast can therefore differ for reasons of method, not of physical change. A length cannot be compared with another length unless the scale, the base map and the date of the survey are the same.

The official figures show this clearly. A national shoreline analysis for 1990 to 2018 gives a mainland length of about 6,907 km. An older official series gave about 7,517 km, using an older measurement method. A 2025 official remeasurement, based on the high-water line and carried out through national survey and hydrographic agencies, gives about 11,099 km. Two questions are needed before these numbers can be compared. First, what does each length measure: the mainland alone, or the mainland with islands? The official release does not make clear whether that figure covers the mainland alone or includes islands. Second, what method produced it? A finer line follows more inlets and bends. The 2025 figure is therefore a different measurement, not evidence that the coast has grown. The lesson's rule holds: always ask what a length measures before reading any change into it. The 2025 figure is stated as reported and was not independently confirmed here.

Now the change statistic. The same 1990 to 2018 analysis classifies the mainland shoreline into three groups: about one-third (33.6 per cent) eroding, about two-fifths (39.6 per cent) stable, and about a quarter accreting. The printed share is 26.9 per cent. With 33.6 and 39.6, that printing sums to 100.1, so the rounding is not exact, and whether another printing gives a different second decimal could not be verified here. The lesson therefore states the accreting share as about a quarter.

The same classification gives state-level pictures, and these need the same care. For 1990 to 2018, West Bengal and Puducherry are reported as having especially high eroding shares. That is a dated statement about a period, not a current ranking of states.

Three readings of this statistic must be kept apart. First, the shares are shares of a length in a period; they are not annual rates. A coast that is "33.6 per cent eroding" over nearly three decades is not losing a third of its shore every year. Second, the shares describe classes of shoreline, not total losses of land: a stable stretch may have been eroded and rebuilt, and an accreting stretch may be building on sediment from elsewhere. Third, erosion and accretion happen at the same time on the same coast. A national share hides both, and that is why a single figure cannot say whether a particular village is safe.

The drivers of the change are the subject of earlier lessons, and they should be combined here. Sea-level rise matters, but it does not explain a local erosion reach on its own. Sediment supply, dams that trap river sediment, sand extraction, storms, currents, coastal structures and subsidence all interact. A hard structure can cause accretion on one side and erosion on the other, as Lesson 3 showed. A measurement period can also change the picture: a stretch that eroded in one decade may have accreted in another.

**Limits of the measure.** The shoreline classes assume that the shoreline could be classified consistently over the period, and that the imagery and methods were comparable. Changes in method between surveys limit how far the periods can be compared. The lesson does not claim a regional or annual rate, and it does not attribute the national pattern to any single cause. The mapping cannot say how much land a given shore has lost in a given year, nor how much of the eroding length is due to natural cycles and how much to human causes. That residual needs site-level monitoring.

**A critic's worry.** A critic may say that if a third of the coast is eroding, the national problem is grave, and that a national average is therefore misleading in the other direction. Both points are right in part. The reply is that the statistic is a classification over a long period, and that its value is in showing that erosion is widespread and local, not in announcing a national loss rate. The planning question is where the eroding stretches are and what their cause is.

**Using the statistic.** A question on India's coastline asks you to interpret a length and a change statistic with their method and period. Quote the figure with its series and year, state whether it is a share or a rate, and say why a single national figure cannot show the local picture.

```text
THE SHORE, SEEN BY THE LAW
  sea ── high water line (HWL) ── high tide line (HTL) ── regulated zone ── land
           shore changes daily        regulatory reference      categories I-IV
                                      (set by spring tide)       no-development zone (conditional)

  PROTECT ──────── ACCOMMODATE ──────── RETREAT ──────── ECOSYSTEM-BASED
  sea walls        raise, flood-proof     set back,       mangroves, dunes,

```

Once the coast is understood as a system, the next question is who decides where people may build, and what can be done about erosion and flooding. Two questions follow. What lines and categories does the law use to decide where building is allowed? And, once erosion and flooding are accepted as real, what are the options for responding to them, and what does each one cost?

Begin with the lines. The **high water line** is the line the sea reaches at high water, and it moves with the tide and the season. It is a physical line, and it is not fixed. The **high tide line** is the regulatory reference used in the coastal framework, defined in relation to the high tide at spring tides. The exact wording of that definition should be checked against the gazette text before it is quoted. The distinction matters because a regulation that measures from a moving line gives different results from one that measures from a defined reference. A careful answer names which line a rule uses and why.

The main instrument for the coastal zone is the 2019 coastal regulation notification, notified as G.S.R. 37(E) dated 18 January 2019. It replaced the earlier regime of 2011 and places the coast in four categories. CRZ-I covers ecologically sensitive areas and has two divisions: CRZ-IA, the most sensitive areas, and CRZ-IB, the intertidal zone between the low tide line and the high tide line. CRZ-II covers developed land within urban limits. CRZ-III covers relatively undisturbed stretches of land, divided into CRZ-IIIA, the more densely populated rural stretches, and CRZ-IIIB, the less densely populated ones. CRZ-IV covers the water area and its tidal stretches. Published commentary on the 2019 rules gives 50 metres for CRZ-IIIA and 200 metres for CRZ-IIIB, measured from the high tide line. This lesson could not check those distances against the gazette text, so they should be verified there before any answer quotes them. The categories are the reason a single rule cannot apply to the whole coast, which is a common trap.

The **no-development zone** is the area in which construction is restricted. Where a default is stated, it is set for the category: for example 50 metres for CRZ-IIIA and 200 metres for CRZ-IIIB, to be checked against the gazette. An approved coastal zone management plan for a stretch can modify that default. The distance therefore cannot be read from one figure for the whole coast: it comes from the category and the plan together. The category defaults above are the starting point, and the plan is the condition that can change them.

The **hazard line** is a different kind of line. It marks the zone exposed to coastal hazards, and it is an input to planning and disaster management. It is not, by itself, a construction setback. Confusing the two is a common error, and the remediation in the final section corrects it.

The second half of the lesson is the response ladder. A coast that is eroding or flooding has four broad responses, and each has a characteristic trade-off.

**Protect** uses sea walls, groynes, breakwaters and embankments. It is effective locally and is the response people most often expect. Its cost is that it interrupts sediment transport and commonly transfers erosion to the downdrift coast, as Lesson 3 showed. It is also expensive to build and maintain, and it can encourage further building behind the defence.

**Accommodate** raises buildings on plinths, flood-proofs them, redesigns drainage, installs warning systems and uses insurance. It is cheaper and reversible, and it does not stop land loss. It keeps people in place while accepting the water.

**Retreat** sets buildings back, relocates settlements or buys out properties, and enforces setback regulation. On rapidly eroding coasts it is the only durable option, and it is politically and socially the hardest.

**Ecosystem-based** responses restore mangroves, dunes, reefs and marshes. These attenuate wave energy, trap sediment and can keep pace with a moderate rise. They need space, sediment and time, and they fail against extreme surge alone. The ecology of mangroves and the policy of blue carbon are taught in the environment treatment; here the point is their coastal-defence function.

The principle that governs the ladder is the sediment cell. Protecting one stretch while starving the next is a common result, so coastal management is increasingly planned across the cell rather than property by property. Hard defence is therefore not automatically the responsible choice. It is one tool whose cost falls somewhere else.

The blue economy links this coast to economic use. Fisheries, aquaculture, ports, tourism, marine renewable energy and biotechnology all depend on the coast's condition. Erosion and salt intrusion threaten those uses directly, which is why coastal regulation and resilience are described together. The blue economy is an applied outcome of the coastal system, not a separate geography.

**Limits of the response options.** The response ladder assumes that the sites can be classified. In practice a coast may need several responses at once, and a settlement that cannot be moved may need protection where a village nearby can be relocated. The ladder is a set of options with trade-offs, not a formula. Where a community cannot be moved and cannot be protected affordably, the choice between accommodation and partial protection is a value judgement about risk and cost, and the analysis cannot settle it alone.

**A cost-benefit challenge.** A critic may say that sea walls always protect the coast. The reply is that they protect the stretch they cover and transfer the erosion beyond it; the honest comparison is between the costs and where they fall.

**Writing the answer.** A question on coastal regulation asks for the framework, the lines, and the trade-off among responses. A question on coastal protection asks you to recommend nothing absolutely: state the downdrift transfer, the sediment-cell principle, and the response best suited to the site. The 2022 Mains question on the causes, effects and management of coastal erosion is answered by combining this lesson's causes and ladder with the environment treatment of policy and ecology.

Revision notes:

- The high water line moves with the tide; the regulatory reference is a defined line.
- The 2019 coastal regulation notification is the main instrument.
- It classifies the coast into categories with different rules.
- The no-development zone is conditional on category and an approved plan.
- The hazard line is a planning input, not a construction setback.
- Protect, accommodate, retreat and ecosystem-based are the four responses.
- Protection commonly transfers erosion downdrift.
- Accommodation keeps people in place but does not stop land loss.
- Retreat is the only durable option on rapidly eroding coasts.
- Ecosystem-based responses attenuate waves but need space and sediment.

### Concept check

**Question:** A village on an eroding stretch asks for a sea wall. Name the main downdrift consequence you would tell it, and state which two other responses could be combined with, or used instead of, the wall.

**Model answer:** The main consequence is that the wall interrupts sediment transport and commonly transfers erosion to the neighbouring coast downdrift, so the village's gain may be another settlement's loss. The two other responses are accommodation, through raised plinths, drainage and warning systems, which keeps people in place without stopping land loss, and retreat, through setbacks or relocation, which is the only durable option where erosion is rapid. The choice should be made across the sediment cell, not for the village alone.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat a sea wall as the responsible default. It is one response, and its cost falls on the coast beyond it. Do not say that the no-development zone is a fixed distance. Do not treat the hazard line as a construction setback. Do not say that sea walls stop erosion everywhere. Do not describe the coastal framework as applying uniformly to the whole coast.

### Decide

Decide: a rule sets a construction limit at a fixed distance everywhere on the coast. What is wrong with this, and what should the limit depend on? (Answer: the category sets a default distance, and an approved coastal zone management plan can modify it for the stretch.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Argue why a coastal planner should consider retreat and ecosystem-based responses before choosing a sea wall on an eroding shore.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** A sea wall protects the stretch it covers but interrupts longshore drift, so erosion commonly moves to the downdrift coast, and it encourages building behind it. Retreat avoids that transfer, and it is the only durable option where erosion is rapid. Ecosystem-based responses such as mangroves, dunes and marshes attenuate waves and trap sediment without starving the coast beyond, though they need space, sediment and time, and fail against extreme surge alone. A planner should therefore compare the wall's local benefit with its downdrift cost and the feasibility of the other responses across the sediment cell.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the downdrift transfer of a wall; 2 for encouragement of building behind it; 2 for retreat as the durable option; 2 for ecosystem-based benefits and limits; 2 for planning across the sediment cell.

## Lesson 12 - How to answer a coastal question that asks for both resources and hazards

Progress: 12 / 12 | Stage: Advanced | Subtopic: Directive decoding, the 2023 coastline demand and the answer architecture

```text
ONE COASTAL CONFIGURATION, TWO FACES

  a coast with a shallow shelf, a long shoreline and river mouths
        │
        ├── RESOURCE face: ports, fisheries, minerals, tourism, marine energy
        │
        └── HAZARD face: surge, erosion, saline intrusion, compound flooding
        │
        ▼
  the same configuration produces both; the answer must show the link
```

Most coastal questions ask for two things at once: what the coast offers, and what it puts at risk. This final lesson turns the mechanisms into an answer. The 2023 question asks for a balanced assessment of a coastline's resources and of its preparedness for hazards. The answer depends on seeing that the two faces of the coast come from the same configuration, and then on writing an argument that connects them without listing facts.

Decode the directive first. "Comment and highlight" asks for an evaluation of a claim and for the specific features that matter most. It is not a request for a complete list. A strong answer identifies the resource potential of the coastline and the hazards it faces, comments on how they relate, and highlights the few features that carry the argument. The 2023 Mains question on India's coastline resource potential and hazard preparedness, at 15 marks and 250 words. The question's wording is not reproduced here, and no solved answer is given in this file.

The answer architecture has five moves. First, set the context with a dated and qualified statement about the coastline, such as its measured length and the period of any erosion statistic, so the examiner sees a source-aware opening. Second, develop the resource dimension, tying each resource to the coastal configuration that makes it possible: deep natural harbours on the west, deltaic land and fisheries on the east, and the shelf and lagoon systems that support fishing and tourism. Third, develop the hazard dimension through the mechanisms of Lessons 2 to 8: erosion through the sediment budget, surge through the shelf and tide, and saline intrusion and compound flooding through the coastal settlement pattern. Fourth, link the two dimensions through the configuration, showing that the features that make a coast valuable also make it exposed, so resources and hazards share a cause. Fifth, conclude with a graded judgement: preparedness depends on matching the response to the mechanism, and the coast's capacity to adjust is part of its value.

The link sentence carries the argument. A good link states that the shelf that makes a coast productive for fishing is the same shelf that amplifies a surge, or that the delta that makes farmland and ports possible is the same delta that sinks and floods. Without a link, the answer is two lists. With it, the answer is an argument.

**Reading a coastal map: a procedure.** Map work is the fastest way to show an examiner that the link is real. Work through five steps. First, read the contours or depth lines and find the headland, the bay and the shelf. Second, place the drift arrows: the dominant wave approach gives the net direction, and the updrift side of any structure is the side that gains sediment. Third, mark the high water line and the high tide line, and note whether any building lies inside the regulated zone. Fourth, overlay the coastal category and the hazard line, and check whether a resource such as a port or a fishery sits inside the zone. Fifth, write the link sentence from the configuration you have drawn.

```text
            N
            ^
   LAND     |                              SEA
            |  PORT + sea wall             ~~~
            |  updrift (north): accretion  ~~~
            |                              ~~~  HEADLAND
            |                                |
            |                                v   one drift arrow:
            |                                |   net drift runs north to south
            |                                |
            |  BAY + FISHERY               ~~~
            |  downdrift (south): starved bay
            |
   overlay: CRZ category | hazard line | port and fishery locations
```

**Worked example.** A small port sits north of a headland, in the sheltered water of a deep inlet, and a fishery lies in the bay to the south. Drift runs from north to south. A sea wall built at the port would trap sand on its north side and starve the bay's southern beach, which feeds the fishery. On the map this is the updrift accretion zone north of the port and the downdrift starved bay to the south. The inlet's shelter, which makes the port possible, also concentrates surge in the inlet. The link sentence therefore says that the sheltered, deep-water configuration makes the port and the fishery possible and also exposes them, so the response must protect the port without starving the bay. The map turns a list of features into an argument.

**Limits of the architecture.** The architecture assumes that the examiner expects a balanced argument within the word limit. It does not require every mechanism; it requires the relevant ones, chosen to carry the link.

**A rejoinder.** A critic may argue that the question asks for a list of resources and hazards, and that linking them is over-reaching. The reply is that a list without a link does not answer a question that asks for an assessment. The link is the assessment.



**Application.** The architecture applies to any coastal question that asks for both an opportunity and a risk. The same five moves work for a question on the blue economy, on ports or on coastal tourism.

Revision notes:

- "Comment and highlight" asks for evaluation and for the key features.
- The 2023 coastline demand asks for resources and hazard preparedness together.
- Resources and hazards often come from the same coastal configuration.
- The answer needs a context, two dimensions, a link and a graded conclusion.
- The link sentence is what turns a list into an argument.
- Dates and sources must accompany any statistic.
- A map sketch shows how resources and hazards sit on the configuration.
- Hazard sections must name the mechanism, not just the hazard.
- Preparedness means matching the response to the mechanism.
- The answer should close with a graded judgement.

### Concept check

**Question:** A coastal answer names a shallow shelf as a fishing asset and then, separately, as a hazard. Why is this a weak answer, and what link would make it strong?

**Model answer:** It is weak because it treats the shelf as two unrelated facts. A strong answer states the link: the same shallow, gently shelving shelf that concentrates fish and supports a fishery also prevents storm-surge water from escaping, so it amplifies the surge. Naming the shared configuration turns two lists into one argument, and the conclusion can then judge how to keep the resource while managing the hazard.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that listing a resource and a hazard side by side answers an assessment question. The examiner is looking for the connection between them. Do not turn a comment question into a list. Do not present a dated statistic as current. Do not write a hazard section that names no mechanism.

### Build the link

Build the link: an answer lists ports and cyclones as separate points. What single sentence would turn them into an argument? (Answer: a link stating that the sheltered, deep-water configuration that makes a port possible can also expose it to surge, so the same features carry both resource and hazard.)

### Mains practice (10 marks, 150 words or fewer)

**Practice prompt:** Write the link paragraph for a question that asks you to assess a river-delta coast for both its agricultural value and its flood risk.

**Model (150 words or fewer):** The delta's fertile, low and flat land makes it agriculturally valuable, because river sediment builds soils and the flat surface supports irrigation and cultivation. The same low, flat and sinking configuration makes it exposed. Its elevation lets surge and tide penetrate far inland, its subsidence raises relative sea level above the global rise, and its dense settlement increases the number of people and assets at risk. Resource and hazard therefore share one cause, the delta's form. A preparedness response must protect the soils and settlements together, by matching drainage, flood defence and retreat to the mechanisms that threaten each area.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 2 marks for the agricultural value and its cause; 2 for the flood exposure and its cause; 3 for the shared configuration as the link; 2 for the subsidence and settlement factors; 1 for a matched response.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cumulative check 1 - Lessons 1, 2 and 5

**Question:** Two headlands face the same waves, and one has an arch while the other has a drowned valley. Explain how energy, rock and drowning produce these different forms.

**Model answer:** Refraction delivers more energy to both headlands than to the bay between them, so the headlands are attacked. Where a headland's rock has open joints, waves widen them into caves and arches, and collapse leaves stacks. A drowned valley is a separate history: the land has sunk or the sea has risen, and a river or glacial valley has been flooded. The arch reflects wave work on rock; the drowned valley reflects a change in relative sea level.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat every indented coast as wave-cut or every inlet as drowned. Energy and rock control the arch; relative sea level controls the drowned valley.

### Cumulative check 2 - Lessons 3, 4 and 10

**Question:** A sediment budget shows accretion on one side of a structure and erosion on the other. Why is this one problem, and why must a shoreline statistic be read with its period?

**Model answer:** The structure traps sediment on its updrift side and removes it from the transport path, so the downdrift side starves. The gain and the loss are one budget. A shoreline statistic must be read with its period because it classifies a long span of shore; it does not show a yearly rate, and erosion and accretion can coexist on the same coast.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat the two sides as separate problems with separate remedies.

### Cumulative check 3 - Lessons 5, 6 and 8

**Question:** Why can a delta and a rocky coast in the same ocean face very different relative sea-level risks, and how does classification help explain it?

**Model answer:** Relative sea level at a coast combines the global change with the land's vertical movement. The delta sinks and loses sediment, so its relative rise exceeds the global mean, and its dense settlement and groundwater use magnify exposure. The rocky coast stands high, so its inundation risk is low. Classification by coastal type, and by relative movement rather than wave energy, shows why the same global rise matters unequally.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that the global mean sea-level rise is the rise at every coast.

### Cumulative check 4 - Lessons 7, 9 and 12

**Question:** How can one coastal configuration be both a resource and a hazard, and how should a balanced answer show this?

**Model answer:** A shallow shelf can support fisheries and ports, and the same shelf amplifies a storm surge by stopping water from escaping. A deltaic plain gives farmland and soils, and it floods and sinks. A balanced answer names the configuration, shows both faces, and links them through the shared feature before concluding with a graded judgement on preparedness.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat resources and hazards as two unrelated lists.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

These tasks are new: each asks for a different skill from the lesson practice (evaluation of a claim, a map-based prediction, and an integrated argument with a recommendation). Models are original and are not official solutions.

## 10-mark evaluation - a stable share and the claim of safety

**Question:** Evaluate the claim that a coast whose shoreline is classed as largely stable is safe from sea-level change. Answer in 150 words.

**Model answer (about 100 words):** The claim treats stability as safety, but the stable share is a classification over 1990 to 2018 and describes a period, not the future. A stable stretch can lie beside an eroding one, and its stability may rest on a sediment supply that dams or extraction are now cutting. Stable stretches are also exposed to saline intrusion, compound flooding and changes in relative sea level, which a shoreline class does not measure. The claim should be rejected: stability in one period is evidence of past balance, not of safety against the forces now acting on the coast.

**Rubric (10 marks):** 3 for the period-bound meaning of the stable class; 3 for the sediment supply that may be changing; 2 for the risks a shoreline class does not measure; 2 for a conditional conclusion.

## 15-mark map-based prediction - a headland, bay and river mouth

**Question:** A coast has a headland to the north of a river mouth, a bay to the south and a shallow shelf offshore. Predict where sediment will accumulate, where erosion is likely, and how a sea wall built at the headland would change the pattern. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (about 175 words):** Assuming drift runs from the headland toward the bay, refraction focuses wave energy on the headland and spreads it into the bay, so the headland is more exposed and the bay receives sediment. Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast, and the river mouth adds more. Accumulation is therefore likely in the bay and on the river-mouth side, and erosion on the headland, which loses rock and sediment to the water. If the headland is walled, the wall stops the headland's loss locally, but it interrupts the drift that reaches the bay. Sediment that would have reached the bay is trapped on the wall's updrift side, so the bay starves and erodes, while the wall's own protection may be undercut at its foot. The shallow shelf shapes the wave energy throughout: it promotes refraction and can hold surge water high. A wall therefore moves the deficit rather than removing it, and a balanced prediction says where that deficit falls. The sediment-cell view, not the single site, is the basis for choosing between the wall and other responses.

**Rubric (15 marks):** 3 for refraction and the energy distribution; 3 for the drift and river-mouth accumulation; 3 for erosion on the headland; 3 for the downdrift consequence of the wall; 2 for the shelf's role; 1 for the sediment-cell conclusion.

## 20-mark integrated argument - sediment supply and the sequence of management

**Question:** Discuss the coastal consequences of a sustained fall in river sediment supply for a deltaic coast, and assess how management across the sediment cell should be sequenced. Answer in 250 words.

**Model answer (about 195 words):** A sustained fall in river sediment supply changes the budget of the whole cell. Dams, extraction and changed catchments trap material upstream, so the delta receives less to build its surface and to replace what waves and currents remove at its margin. The consequences follow in sequence. First, where input falls below output, the delta's shoreline begins to retreat. Second, a sinking delta loses its margin faster when relative sea level rises, so subsidence and sediment starvation compound each other. Third, the early transmissions reach people before the land is lost: saline intrusion degrades aquifers and soils, and compound flooding becomes more frequent. Sequencing therefore matters. Management should begin by measuring the sediment budget across the cell, because the cause may lie upstream, and then address the causes that can be controlled, such as extraction and reservoir releases. Where erosion is already active, accommodation protects people while the budget is diagnosed, and selective protection is justified only for essential assets whose downdrift cost has been assessed. Planned retreat is reserved for the most exposed settlements. The sequence is an analytical order, not a fixed recipe, and the verdict depends on the flux data for each delta.

**Rubric (20 marks):** 4 for the budget consequence of falling supply; 4 for compounding with relative sea level; 3 for intrusion and compound flooding as early transmissions; 4 for the sequencing logic; 3 for the downdrift-cost test before protection; 2 for stating the data limit.
