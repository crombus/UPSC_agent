# Weather Elements, Severe Weather and Fujiwhara / India Jet Stream and Western Disturbances - Mains-Focused Live Edition

Weather is what the sky is doing right now, and what it does over the next few days. Climate is the average of that behaviour over decades. Almost every weather question in the Geography syllabus is a question about one chain of cause and effect: the sun heats unevenly, the air responds with pressure differences and winds, the winds carry moisture, and the moisture condenses into cloud and rain. Those links, followed in order, let you explain a heatwave, a cloudburst, a cyclone's track or a winter shower in Punjab without memorising each event separately. Sixteen lessons build the chain from the surface outward. The first six give you energy, temperature, the vertical atmosphere and pressure. The next six add rotation, the global circulation, local winds, moisture, clouds and air masses. The last four take up the severe systems, the upper-air jets and the western disturbances that decide India's winter.

Read the diagram as a relay. Each box is the cause of the next. If an early link changes, every later link changes with it. Most mistakes in weather answers come from skipping a link, for example describing rain as if it appeared without a wind bringing the moisture.

Start with the question the lesson answers. Why does the same sky look so different on two days a week apart? The answer is that weather is the state of the atmosphere at one place over a short time, described by a small set of measurable elements. Climate is the long-run pattern of those elements. A cold January day in Delhi is weather. The fact that Delhi's winters are cool and dry is climate. Confusing the two is the most common error in the first question of any weather paper, and it is worth correcting before anything else.

The five core elements are temperature (with insolation as its driver), atmospheric pressure, wind, humidity and precipitation. Cloud cover is a sixth element that records the other five in visible form. Each element has a standard instrument. A thermometer records air temperature in a shaded screen. A barometer records pressure. An anemometer records wind speed and a wind vane its direction. A hygrometer or psychrometer records moisture. A rain gauge records precipitation depth. Measuring each element at many stations at the same moment gives a map, and the map is the raw material of forecasting.

Forecasting works at several scales, and the scales should not be mixed. A station observation is one point at one time. A synoptic system is a pressure system spanning hundreds of kilometres, such as a depression or a front. An upper-air wave is a pattern in the westerly winds several kilometres above the ground. A seasonal outlook is a statement about the average of a season. A nowcast looks minutes or hours ahead, a forecast days ahead, and a seasonal outlook months ahead. Each uses different data and has different skill. A nowcast can warn of a storm cell over one town; a seasonal outlook cannot say when that cell will form.

Forecasts today depend on numerical models that solve the equations of motion on a grid. A model's resolution is the spacing of that grid. A finer grid resolves smaller features, such as a convective cell, but needs more computing power and is still limited by how well the atmosphere is observed. This matters for the severe weather in Lessons 13 and 14, which are small enough to sit between the grid points of a coarse model.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks about weather elements and the processes that produce weather. One item in the PYQ list (Prelims 2026) concerns the resolution objective and the developing institution of a national forecasting system. This lesson teaches what resolution means; the institution is a fact for official forecasting material.

**Objection and reply.** A student may say that weather is just "whatever the forecast says", so the forecast is the phenomenon. The reply is that a forecast is an estimate of the elements above. The elements exist whether or not anyone predicts them, and a wrong forecast is judged against the elements that were actually measured.

**India-centric example and its limit.** In the pre-monsoon season, the northwest Indian plains heat up quickly under clear skies, and the measured surface temperature climbs day by day. That rise is weather, observed at stations. Whether the season is hot overall is climate. The limit of the example is that a single hot week does not change the climate of the region, and a single cool week does not disprove a warming trend.

**UPSC application.** Prelims asks about instruments, scales and the basic definitions. Mains asks you to trace a phenomenon through its causal chain, so the diagram above is a template for the answer. Trap: calling any change in the sky a "climate change" in an answer on a single event.

Revision notes:

- Weather is short-term and local; climate is the long-run pattern of the same elements.
- The five core elements are temperature, pressure, wind, humidity and precipitation; cloud records them.
- Each element has a standard instrument; a map needs many stations read at one moment.
- Scales differ: station, synoptic system, upper-air wave, seasonal regime. Do not mix them.
- Forecast skill falls as the lead time grows; nowcasts are local and short, seasonal outlooks are broad and coarse.
- Numerical model resolution is the grid spacing; finer grids resolve smaller features but need more data and computing.
- The causal chain runs from unequal heating to rain. Every weather answer should show it.

Retrieve before moving on: a forecast says heavy rain will fall over a town in the next two hours. Which scale does this belong to, and which element would you measure first to check it?
*Answer pointer:* It belongs to nowcasting, and the first element to check is rainfall intensity at nearby gauges and radar, supported by the humidity and wind data that feed the storm.

Next, the chain begins with sunlight. Lesson 2 asks how much energy the surface actually receives, how much is returned, and what the atmosphere does with the rest.

### Concept check

**Question:** A town records a very cold week in January. A climatologist says this does not disprove warming. Explain the distinction that makes that claim valid.

**Model answer:** Weather is the state of the atmosphere over days or weeks at one place, while climate is the average and variability of the elements over decades. One cold week is a sample from the weather distribution; a warming trend is a shift in the distribution's average over a long period. A cold week therefore cannot overturn a long-run trend, and a warm trend does not make every single week warm.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that an unusual single event proves or disproves a long-run pattern. Climate is inferred from many years of observations, and one event is one draw from that distribution.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Trace how uneven heating of the Earth's surface leads to rainfall, naming each intermediate element and the instrument that records it. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Sunlight heats the surface unevenly, so air over warmer ground expands and rises while air over cooler ground is denser. The resulting temperature contrast creates pressure differences, measured by a barometer across stations, and air flows from high to low pressure, which an anemometer records as wind. The wind carries water vapour, measured by a hygrometer, from the source. When moist air is cooled, either by rising or by contact with a cooler surface, its vapour condenses into cloud droplets, which grow into rain recorded by a gauge. Each link depends on the previous one, so a failure at any link interrupts rainfall.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Uneven heating as the origin, 2 marks. Temperature contrast and pressure difference, 2 marks. Wind as the movement from high to low pressure, 2 marks. Moisture transport and cooling to condensation, 2 marks. Named instrument or measurement for at least three elements, 2 marks.

```text
          |
          +--> reflected by clouds, aerosols, bright surfaces  --> back to space
          |
          +--> absorbed by atmosphere and surface
                     |
                     v
              EARTH WARMS, EMITS LONG WAVE (infrared)
                     |
                     +--> escapes directly to space
                     |
                     +--> absorbed by greenhouse gases and cloud
                               |
                               v
                     re-emitted in all directions, some back down
```

The arrows show a balance. Energy arrives as shortwave, leaves as longwave, and the atmosphere decides how much of each path is taken. The figure "about 100 units" is a relative bookkeeping convention, not a measured total, and it is used only to keep the proportions in view.

The intuition starts with a question. If the sun heats the Earth, why is the planet not steadily getting hotter, and why is the atmosphere not simply heated directly by sunlight? A thermostat helps. Incoming energy must be matched by outgoing energy over a long period, or the temperature keeps changing. The energy budget is the accounting of that matching. Insolation is the arriving side of the account, measured as solar radiation received at the top of the atmosphere or at the ground.

Insolation depends on the angle of the sun. When the sun is overhead, its rays are concentrated on a small area and pass through the least atmosphere, so the surface receives the most energy per unit area. When the sun is low, the same beam is spread over a wider area and passes through more air. This is why the tropics receive more energy than the poles on average, and why the Earth has a surplus in low latitudes and a deficit in high latitudes. Winds and ocean currents move heat poleward to close the gap. Cloud, latitude, slope aspect and altitude then modify the amount reaching any given spot.

Albedo is the share of incoming radiation that a surface or the atmosphere reflects. It is expressed as a fraction or percentage. Fresh snow and clouds have high albedo and reflect much sunlight back to space, which cools the surface beneath. Dark ocean and dark asphalt have low albedo, absorb more, and warm. Clouds are the largest reflector in the system, and this matters because the same cloud can also trap longwave, a point developed in Lesson 11.

Aerosols are tiny solid or liquid particles suspended in the air, such as dust, sea salt, soot and sulphate droplets. They act on the energy budget in two ways. Many reflect shortwave, so they cool the surface below, and some absorb it, so they warm the air around them. Some aerosols also act as nuclei on which cloud droplets form, which changes cloud albedo indirectly. The net effect depends on the particle type, its height and the surface beneath it. This is why a dust layer over a bright desert and a soot layer over dark ocean can have opposite effects.

Now the outgoing side. The Earth emits energy as longwave, or infrared, radiation, because it is much cooler than the sun. Longwave is absorbed strongly by water vapour, carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases, and by cloud. Those gases and clouds re-emit the energy in all directions, including back toward the surface. That return flow is the greenhouse effect. It is a natural process that keeps the surface warmer than it would be with no atmosphere, and it is not in itself a problem. Its enhancement by human emissions is a separate question, which the syllabus treats in its climate topics.

The key physical point is that the atmosphere is largely transparent to incoming shortwave and much less transparent to outgoing longwave. Sunlight reaches the surface, which warms and emits longwave, and that longwave is then partly trapped. The atmosphere is therefore warmed mainly from below, by energy the surface has absorbed. A tempting picture has the atmosphere absorbing sunlight directly as its main heating route. The reply is to trace the path: the surface absorbs first, then emits, then the air is warmed by contact and by absorbed longwave.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks about the causes of atmospheric heating and the Earth's energy balance. The PYQ list links four Prelims items here: 2023 Q62 (insolation and infrared radiation), 2024 Q1 (solar and terrestrial heating; greenhouse gases), 2025 Q26 (dust distribution) and 2025 Q29 (carbon dioxide and atmospheric temperature).

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that clouds, being white, must cool the Earth in every case. The reply is that cloud albedo cools by reflecting shortwave, but cloud also traps longwave, so the net effect depends on the cloud's height, thickness and time of day. Lesson 11 returns to this.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Dust from the Thar and the Arabian plains is carried over the Indo-Gangetic plain in pre-monsoon months, and dust reduces the shortwave reaching the ground there. The limit is that dust also absorbs and emits, so its effect on surface temperature is not simply cooling everywhere. The example illustrates the aerosol mechanism; it does not measure the net effect for a given day.

**In the answer.** Mains answers on heat, drought and monsoon all start from this budget. Trap: attributing the greenhouse effect to ozone or to sunlight alone, or treating albedo as the same process as greenhouse trapping. Albedo governs reflected shortwave; the greenhouse effect governs trapped longwave.

Revision notes:

- The energy budget balances incoming shortwave against outgoing longwave over long periods.
- Insolation is greatest where the sun is overhead, and the tropics have a surplus while high latitudes have a deficit.
- Albedo is the reflected fraction of incoming shortwave; clouds and fresh snow are the strongest reflectors.
- Aerosols reflect or absorb depending on type, height and surface; their effect is not uniformly cooling or warming.
- The greenhouse effect traps outgoing longwave; it is natural and keeps the surface warmer than an atmosphere-free Earth would be.
- The atmosphere is warmed mainly from below, through the surface's emitted longwave and contact heating.
- Albedo and greenhouse trapping are different processes; keep them separate in every answer.

Predict first: a bright desert and a dark ocean receive the same insolation. Which one will reflect more shortwave, and what does that do to its surface temperature?
*Answer pointer:* The bright desert reflects more shortwave, so it absorbs less and its surface warms less from the same insolation.

Next, the budget tells you how much energy the Earth receives unevenly. Lesson 3 asks how that uneven heating appears on a map, and why land and sea do not warm the same way.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two surfaces at the same latitude receive equal insolation. One is fresh snow and the other is dark forest. Which warms more, and what mechanism decides it?

**Model answer:** The dark forest warms more. Its low albedo means it absorbs most of the incoming shortwave, while fresh snow reflects most of it. The surface temperature responds to absorbed energy, not to incoming energy alone, so albedo is the decisive control.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that equal insolation means equal warming. Reflection removes energy before it reaches the surface's heat store.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain how albedo and the greenhouse effect each affect the surface temperature of the Earth, and why they must be kept separate in analysis. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Albedo controls how much incoming shortwave is reflected back to space before it can be absorbed. High-albedo surfaces such as snow and clouds therefore absorb less and stay cooler. The greenhouse effect controls how much outgoing longwave is trapped by greenhouse gases and cloud, returning energy to the surface. Albedo acts on the way in, and greenhouse trapping acts on the way out. Keeping them separate matters because a cloud can do both: it reflects shortwave and traps longwave, so the net effect depends on which process dominates for that cloud type, height and time of day.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Albedo defined as the reflected share of shortwave, 2 marks. Its effect on absorbed energy and surface temperature, 2 marks. Greenhouse trapping of outgoing longwave, 2 marks. The two processes distinguished by direction, 2 marks. Cloud example showing a net effect that depends on conditions, 2 marks.

Reading the map: an isotherm is a line joining places with the same temperature. Where the lines are close together, the temperature changes rapidly. Where they bend, the bend tells you which surface is warmer or colder than its surroundings at that latitude.

Start with an everyday observation. Coastal cities often feel milder in winter and less extreme in summer than inland cities at the same latitude. Why would the ocean and the land behave differently when both receive the same sunlight? The answer is the thermal properties of the two surfaces, not any difference in insolation.

Water has a much higher specific heat than dry land. That means more energy is needed to raise a given mass of water by one degree. Water also mixes its heat through a deep layer, because sunlight penetrates some tens of metres and currents and waves stir the warmed water down. Dry land absorbs heat in a thin skin and has no mixing, so its surface temperature rises and falls sharply. Over a day, the land surface may swing many degrees while the sea surface changes little. Over a season, the same contrast appears in larger amounts. Evaporation also takes energy from the ocean and sends it into the air as latent heat, which lowers the surface temperature of the water further.

Now apply this to the two seasons. In January in the Northern Hemisphere, the continents are cold and the oceans are relatively warm, so the isotherms bend. Over a cold continent, the lines bend toward the equator, which means the cold air extends farther south than its latitude would suggest. Over a warm ocean, the lines bend toward the pole, which means warm conditions extend farther north. The bend is a signature of the land-sea contrast, not of a difference in latitude alone. In July the picture reverses: hot continents push the isotherms toward the pole, and cool oceans pull them toward the equator. The January and July patterns are the reverse of each other because the land-sea contrast is reversed.

Read the specific cases carefully. The January bend over the North Atlantic and the Asian coasts is strongest where warm ocean currents flow close to a cold continent. The bend is not caused by altitude or by mountains alone, though relief can modify it. The direction of the bend tells you whether the surface is warmer or colder than its latitude would suggest.

The seasonal lag is the next idea. The sun's heating peaks at the solstice, but the hottest day of the year usually comes weeks later, and the coldest day comes after the winter solstice. The reason is that the surface and the air must keep receiving more energy than they lose before temperatures peak. Oceans, with their large heat capacity, lag more than land. This is why the warmest month over a continental interior is often July, while over coasts or oceans it can be later.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks about temperature distribution and the factors controlling it. The PYQ list links three Prelims items here: 2023 Q64, 2024 Q4 and 2025 Q27, all on seasonal and January isotherm patterns.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that the January bend simply reflects latitude. The reply is that latitude alone would produce straight, roughly east-west isotherms. Bends that change direction between land and sea show that the surface type, not latitude alone, controls the pattern.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Mumbai and Chennai have narrower seasonal temperature ranges than inland Nagpur or the northwestern plains at similar latitudes, because the sea moderates them. The limit is that the peninsula's coastal climate also depends on monsoon winds and altitude, so the land-sea effect is one control among several.

**UPSC application.** Map questions often ask you to explain a distribution from its bends. A useful habit is to name the surface first, then the bend, then the cause. Trap: reading a bend as a mountain effect or as a pure latitude effect.

Revision notes:

- An isotherm joins places of equal temperature; closely spaced lines mean rapid change.
- Land and sea receive the same insolation but heat differently, because of specific heat, mixing and evaporation.
- January isotherms bend toward the equator over cold land and toward the pole over warm ocean.
- The July pattern is the reverse, because continents heat more than the sea.
- A bend's direction tells you whether the surface is warmer or colder than its latitude would suggest.
- Peak seasonal temperature lags the solstice, and it lags more over oceans than over land.
- Coastal stations have smaller seasonal ranges than interior stations at the same latitude.

Read the map: a map shows the January isotherms bending sharply northward over a warm current off a continent. What does that tell you about the surface and its effect on the air above?
*Answer pointer:* The surface is warmer than its latitude suggests, and the warm ocean is raising the air temperature above it relative to the cold land farther inland.

Next, you know how the surface temperature changes across land and sea. Lesson 4 asks what happens above the surface, where temperature changes with height.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why do isotherms bend northward over a warm ocean in January, when the same latitude over land shows colder air?

**Model answer:** The ocean stores and releases heat with high specific heat and deep mixing, so its surface stays warmer than the adjacent cold land through winter. The warm surface warms the air above it, which pushes the warm temperatures northward on the map, while the cold continent holds cold air south of its usual latitude. The bend reflects the surface contrast, not latitude alone.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that a bend in isotherms is caused by altitude or that it simply follows latitude. The bend reflects the contrast between a warm surface and a cold one at the same latitude.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why coastal and continental interiors at the same latitude have different annual temperature ranges, and what the seasonal lag tells us about heating and cooling. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Water has a high specific heat, mixes heat through a deep layer, and loses energy by evaporation, so the sea's surface temperature changes little over a year. Land has a low specific heat, heats only a thin surface skin and does not mix, so its temperature swings widely. Coastal stations therefore have smaller ranges than continental stations at the same latitude. The seasonal lag follows from the same contrast: peak temperature comes weeks after the solstice because the surface must keep gaining energy, and the ocean, with more stored heat, lags longer than land. An Indian example is the milder annual range of the coast relative to the interior, though monsoon winds also contribute.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Specific heat and mixing explained for water, 2 marks. Land heating in a thin skin with no mixing, 2 marks. Coastal versus interior range conclusion, 2 marks. Seasonal lag tied to the heating balance, 2 marks. Indian example with the limit that monsoon winds also matter, 2 marks.

```text
 HEIGHT      TEMPERATURE PROFILE (typical tropospheric part)
 (km)

 16-17  -  ------------------- TROPOPAUSE (equator; ~8-9 km at poles)
           |  temperature stops falling
 10      -  |
           |  temperature falls with height
           |  (average environmental lapse rate ~6.5 C per km)
  0      -  ~~~~~~~~ SURFACE (warmest air of the troposphere) ~~~~~~~
```

Read the profile from the bottom up. Temperature falls as you climb through the troposphere and stops falling at the tropopause, the boundary above which the stratosphere begins. The numbers are typical, not exact.

Begin with a simple question. Why does the air at the top of a mountain feel colder than at its base, even on the same day? The air is not heated from above. Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface, and the surface warms the air touching it. The air higher up is farther from that heat source and, as it rises, it expands and cools. So temperature falls with height because of the way the air is heated from below, and because of the physics of rising air.

The lapse rate is the rate at which temperature falls with height, measured in degrees Celsius per kilometre. It is a property of the actual atmosphere at a given time and place, and it is called the environmental lapse rate. Averaged over the troposphere, it is roughly 6.5 degrees per kilometre. The environmental lapse rate varies from day to day and place to place, and it is the number that matters for stability, which Lesson 5 takes up.

Two reference rates describe what a rising parcel of air does, as distinct from the environment around it. A parcel of unsaturated air cools by expansion at about 9.8 degrees per kilometre. This is the dry adiabatic lapse rate. A parcel that has risen far enough to be saturated releases latent heat as its vapour condenses, which offsets part of the cooling. Its rate is the saturated, or moist, adiabatic lapse rate, which is smaller and varies with temperature, typically between about 4 and 7 degrees per kilometre. The two parcel rates are not the same as the environmental rate, and the difference between them decides stability.

The troposphere is the lowest part of the atmosphere, extending from the surface to the tropopause. It contains almost all the weather, because almost all the water vapour sits in it and because its heating from below drives the convection that makes clouds. Its depth is not uniform. The tropopause lies at roughly 16 to 17 kilometres over the equator and at roughly 8 to 9 kilometres near the poles. Warm air expands, so a tropical column is deeper. This is the reason the tropical troposphere is thicker than the polar one, and it is a direct consequence of temperature, not of any difference in the total mass of air.

The tropopause matters because it caps the convection and weather of the troposphere. Above it, temperature stops falling and then rises through the stratosphere. The rising temperature there is caused by absorption of ultraviolet radiation by ozone. Ozone is not the subject of this lesson, but the reversal explains why the tropopause acts as a lid. Rising air in a storm is stopped at the tropopause, which is why cumulonimbus clouds spread out in an anvil shape at that level.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks about the structure of the atmosphere. The PYQ list links two items: Mains 2022 GS-I Q17 (the troposphere and weather processes; 15 marks) and Prelims 2024 Q2 (troposphere thickness and convection).

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that the lapse rate is a fixed law, so every rising air parcel cools at 6.5 degrees per kilometre. The reply is that 6.5 is an average of the environment, while a rising parcel cools at its own rate: dry, about 9.8, or moist, smaller. Confusing the two produces wrong stability conclusions.

**India-centric example and its limit.** In the Himalaya, the same altitude difference produces a much colder air mass than the plains at equal latitude, because the column above the high ground contains less air and the environmental lapse rate carries the cooling. The limit is that the lapse rate varies, so the 6.5 figure is a guide for a station, not a rule for a given afternoon.

**In the answer.** Mains answers on the troposphere and weather start from the tropopause and the depth of the convective layer. Trap: treating the stratosphere as the weather layer, or assuming the tropopause is at the same height everywhere.

Revision notes:

- The lapse rate is how fast temperature falls with height in the actual air.
- The environmental lapse rate averages about 6.5 degrees per kilometre over the troposphere.
- A dry rising parcel cools at about 9.8 degrees per kilometre; a saturated one cools more slowly, around 4 to 7.
- The troposphere holds almost all weather and water vapour; its heating is from below.
- The tropopause is deeper in the tropics (roughly 16 to 17 km) than at the poles (roughly 8 to 9 km).
- Warm air expands, so the thicker tropical troposphere is a thermal effect.
- The tropopause caps convection, which spreads cumulonimbus anvils at that level.

Calculate, then check: a mountain station and a plains station are at the same latitude and on the same day. Why is the mountain station colder even though it is not farther from the sun?
*Answer pointer:* Temperature falls with height through the troposphere, so the higher station sits in a colder part of the same air column, cooled by the environmental lapse rate.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is the tropical troposphere deeper than the polar troposphere, and what does that imply for the height of the tropopause?

**Model answer:** Warm air expands and is less dense per unit height than cold air, so the column of air over the tropics extends higher before it reaches the same pressure. The tropopause therefore sits higher at the equator, around 16 to 17 kilometres, than at the poles, around 8 to 9 kilometres. The difference is due to the thermal structure of the column, not to a difference in total air mass.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that the tropopause is at one fixed height everywhere. Its height follows the temperature of the column beneath it.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Calculate the temperature at 3 kilometres above a plains station whose surface temperature is 30 degrees Celsius, first using the average environmental lapse rate and then for a dry parcel rising from the surface. Explain what the difference tells you about stability. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Using the average environmental lapse rate of about 6.5 degrees per kilometre, the air at 3 kilometres is about 30 minus 19.5, or about 10.5 degrees Celsius. A dry parcel lifted from the surface cools at about 9.8 degrees per kilometre, so at 3 kilometres it is about 30 minus 29.4, or about 0.6 degrees Celsius. The parcel is then far colder than the environment at that height, so it sinks back and the dry air is stable. The comparison of the two rates decides stability; the surface temperature alone does not.

```text
 NORMAL (temperature falls with height)           INVERSION (stable)

 HEIGHT    TEMP ->                   HEIGHT    TEMP ->
   high      cold                      high      cold
    ^         ^                         ^         ^
    |        /                          |       /
    |       /  falls with height        |      /  rises with height
    |      /                            |  ___/   (warm lid)
    |     /                             | /  cold surface air trapped
    +----/-- warm at ground            +--/-- cold ground
```

The left panel is a normal profile, where temperature falls with height. The right panel is an inversion, where temperature rises with height across a layer. Read the inversion as a lid: warm air sits on top of cold air, and vertical motion is suppressed.

Start with a puzzle. On a clear winter morning in the plains, the smoke from fires and vehicles often hangs close to the ground and does not rise. Why would air near the surface stay put, when hot air normally rises? The answer is the stability of the air column, and inversions are the most visible case.

A temperature inversion is a layer in which temperature increases with height, reversing the normal fall. The cause is usually radiation. On clear, calm, dry nights the ground loses heat by longwave emission faster than it receives it, so the surface cools. The air touching the ground cools too, and the cold dense air stays at the bottom while the air above stays warmer. Long winter nights, cloudless skies, dry air and light winds all strengthen this, because each allows more loss from the surface and less mixing. Cloud blankets the surface, so cloudy nights rarely produce strong inversions.

Now the stability idea. Stability describes whether a parcel of air, displaced upward, tends to keep rising or to return. Compare the parcel's temperature with that of the surrounding air at the new height. If the parcel is warmer and lighter, it keeps rising, and the air is unstable. If it is colder and denser, it sinks back, and the air is stable. Because a rising parcel cools at a known rate, the stability of the column depends on the comparison between the environmental lapse rate and the parcel rates from Lesson 4.

Here is the correction most students need. A layer that is heated from below is not automatically unstable. Heating the surface warms the air beneath it, which can produce instability, but whether the air actually becomes unstable depends on the environmental lapse rate measured against the dry adiabatic rate of about 9.8 degrees per kilometre and the moist rate of about 4 to 7 degrees. If the environment cools with height more slowly than the dry rate, a dry parcel rising from the surface ends up colder and denser than its surroundings, so it sinks back. The surface heating alone does not decide the outcome. The profile does. An inversion is the extreme stable case, because the environment warms with height, so no rising parcel can be warmer than its surroundings at the new level.

Stability has three cases, and the distinction matters for storms. Absolute stability holds when the environmental lapse rate is smaller than the moist rate, so air stays stable whether or not it is saturated. Absolute instability holds when the environmental rate exceeds the dry rate, so rising air is warmer than its surroundings even when dry. Conditional instability is the middle case. Dry air is stable, but if a parcel is lifted until it saturates, latent heat release can make it unstable. Lesson 13 uses this middle case to explain why tropical cyclones need a trigger.

The valley inversion is a local form. At night, cold dense air drains down slopes and collects in valley floors and frost hollows, where it is trapped beneath warmer air on the slopes. The valley floor is then colder than the hillside above, which is the opposite of the normal pattern. Frost and fog form first in the cold pool, and this is why orchards and crops on valley bottoms are sometimes more at risk of frost than those on slopes.

The subsidence inversion is a different cause. Air descending in a high-pressure system warms by compression as it sinks, and it can warm more than the air beneath, creating a stable warm layer. A descending stable layer of this kind can cap a polluted urban column for days, which is why persistent anticyclonic conditions in winter are associated with smog episodes. Use this as a mechanism, not as a fixed count of events.

**Where this sits in the syllabus.** The stability and inversion mechanism supports the weather-element and vertical-structure demands in Lessons 4 and 10, and it is the base for pollution and fog questions in the wider syllabus. No PYQ item in the list is assigned to this lesson.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that an inversion always traps pollution, so strong smog means strong inversion. The reply is that the inversion is necessary for trapping but not sufficient. The pollution must be emitted into the trapped layer, and wind speed, emission rate and the depth of the mixed layer all matter.

**India-centric example and its limit.** The Indo-Gangetic plain in winter often has calm, clear, cold nights and occasional persistent anticyclonic spells. Under those conditions, radiation and subsidence inversions can keep emissions close to the ground, which is one reason winter air quality in the region is poor. The limit is that the same inversion also forms in other seasons and that emissions, not the inversion alone, set the severity.

**UPSC application.** Mains answers about pollution or frost begin with stability, not with the emission source alone. Trap: calling an inversion an instability, or treating every heated surface layer as unstable.

Revision notes:

- An inversion is a layer where temperature increases with height; it is the extreme of stability.
- Radiation inversions form on long, clear, calm, dry nights, when the ground loses heat fast.
- Cloud cover suppresses radiation inversions by blanketing the surface.
- Valley inversions form when cold dense air drains into hollows and pools beneath warmer slope air.
- Subsidence inversions form when descending air warms by compression and caps the air below.
- Stability depends on the environmental lapse rate compared with the parcel rates, not on heating alone.
- Absolute stability, absolute instability and conditional instability are the three cases.
- An inversion traps smoke and fog; its strength alone does not set how bad the pollution gets.

Name the case: two nights are equally clear and calm, but one has a strong inversion and one does not. What single weather element, if it differed, would most likely explain the difference?
*Answer pointer:* Cloud cover. A cloudy night blankets the ground and reduces radiative loss, which weakens the surface cooling that starts a radiation inversion.

Next, the inversion showed how stability can keep air still. Lesson 6 asks what sets air moving at all, which requires looking at pressure and the force that pushes air from one place to another.

### Concept check

**Question:** A layer of air is heated strongly from the ground. Does this make the air unstable? Explain using the lapse-rate comparison.

**Model answer:** Not necessarily. Heating from below makes instability possible, but the outcome depends on whether the environmental lapse rate exceeds the dry rate of about 9.8 degrees per kilometre or the moist rate. If the environment cools more slowly than the dry rate, a parcel rising from the heated surface becomes colder and denser than its surroundings and sinks back. Instability requires the actual profile to be steep enough, so the heating itself is not decisive.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat a heated surface layer as automatically unstable. Stability is set by the lapse rate of the environment compared with the parcel's rate of cooling.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why a winter temperature inversion traps smoke near the surface, and why the inversion's presence is necessary but not sufficient for a severe pollution episode. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** A temperature inversion is a layer in which temperature rises with height, so it is strongly stable. Air beneath it cannot rise, because any parcel that lifts becomes colder and denser than its surroundings and sinks back. It is not sufficient, because the emission rate, wind speed and the depth of the trapped layer decide how concentrated the pollution becomes.
```text
            1006 hPa     1002 hPa     998 hPa     994 hPa
 isobars:   ----------   ----------   ----------   ----------
                 HIGH  ---->   wind flows down the gradient   ---->  LOW

   Pressure gradient force points FROM high TO low.
   Steeper gradient (isobars closer together) = stronger force = faster wind.
```

Read the isobars as contour lines on a hill. Water runs downhill, and air runs downhill in pressure. The closer the lines, the steeper the slope and the faster the flow.

Start with a question. A pot of water with a hole in its base drains because the water is higher than the outlet. Why does air move at all, when it is invisible and has no outlet? The answer is that air is pushed by differences in pressure. Pressure is the weight of the air column above a point, which is why it falls with height. Because it falls with height, a point high in the air is at lower pressure than a point below it. Differences in pressure across a horizontal distance create the force that moves air horizontally.

An isobar is a line joining places of equal pressure, usually drawn on a weather map at intervals of a few hectopascals. The pressure gradient is the rate at which pressure changes across distance, and the pressure gradient force acts from high pressure toward low pressure, perpendicular to the isobars. The gradient force is what starts the wind, but it is not the only force, and Lesson 7 shows how the others modify its direction. For now, the rule is simple: wider spacing gives a weak wind, and closer spacing gives a strong one.

Pressure falls quickly with height. It roughly halves about every 5.5 kilometres of ascent, which is why mountain stations have much lower pressure than sea-level stations and why an instrument reading must be corrected for altitude. Near the surface, the main causes of pressure differences are thermal. Warm air is less dense, so a column of warm air is lighter and produces a surface low beneath it. A column of cold air is denser and produces a surface high. This is the thermal low and thermal high. A dynamic high or low arises from motion of the air itself, as when air descends and piles up at the surface in a subtropical high, which Lesson 8 takes up.

The thermal low over northwestern India in summer is the Indian example that makes this concrete. The plains heat intensely in the pre-monsoon and monsoon months, the air over them becomes light, and a surface low forms. Air is drawn toward it from the surrounding seas and higher ground. The limit is that this thermal low is one component of the monsoon system and does not explain the monsoon by itself, a point Lesson 15 and the monsoon topic develop.

Winter gives the reverse example. Cold, dense air over the high ground of Central Asia and the Tibetan plateau forms a high, and surface winds blow outward from it. The same gradient logic applies: pressure is high where the air is cold and dense, and the outflow is what carries cold air southward toward India. The limit is that this high is shaped by terrain and by large-scale circulation, so it is best described as a seasonal feature rather than a fixed block.

**Worked example.** Two stations 200 kilometres apart record 1006 and 1002 hPa. The gradient is 4 hPa over 200 kilometres, or 2 hPa per 100 kilometres. Doubling that gradient would double the force on the air, so the wind would be stronger, but only if the Coriolis and friction balance were unchanged.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for pressure and wind systems. No PYQ item in the list is assigned to this lesson; the concept is required by Lessons 7 to 9.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that air should flow straight from high to low, so isobars alone predict the wind. The reply is that the gradient gives the initial direction, but friction and Earth's rotation turn the wind. The gradient sets how strong the push is, and the other forces set where the wind goes.

**In the answer.** In Mains answers, pressure gradient is the first link in any wind explanation. Trap: describing wind as flowing from high to low without stating the gradient and its steepness, or assuming that pressure rises with height.

Revision notes:

- Pressure is the weight of the air column above a point, and it falls rapidly with height.
- An isobar joins places of equal pressure; the pressure gradient is the rate of change across distance.
- The pressure gradient force points from high to low and is perpendicular to isobars.
- Closer isobars produce stronger winds.
- Thermal lows form over warm, light air; thermal highs over cold, dense air.
- Dynamic highs and lows come from the motion of air, such as subsidence.
- The summer thermal low over northwest India helps draw the monsoon but does not explain it alone.

*Answer pointer:* The tightly packed station, because the steeper pressure gradient produces a stronger pressure gradient force.

Next, the gradient explains why air is pushed. Lesson 7 asks why it does not move in a straight line, and it introduces the Coriolis effect, which turns the wind.

### Concept check

**Question:** A station lies in a region where the pressure gradient is steep but the surface wind is weak. Give two physical reasons why this could happen.

**Model answer:** Friction near the surface slows the wind, which weakens the Coriolis deflection and lets the gradient turn the flow. Local sheltering by terrain or buildings can also reduce the wind a station measures. The gradient sets the push; friction and local sheltering can reduce the observed wind.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that a steep gradient always produces a strong measured wind. Friction and local obstacles can reduce the wind at the surface.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why a summer thermal low can draw air toward northwest India, and what limits the explanation as a full account of the monsoon. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Intense heating of the dry plains makes the air over them warm and light, so surface pressure falls and a thermal low forms. Air is then pushed by the pressure gradient from the surrounding higher-pressure regions toward the low, which draws moisture-bearing winds inland. The mechanism is thermal and direct. It does not explain the monsoon by itself, because the monsoon also depends on the upper-air circulation, the seasonal shift of the belts and the moisture supply from the oceans. The thermal low is one component of a larger system.

## Lesson 7 - The four forces and the Coriolis effect

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: not available
CA search: not run
CA found: none verified (no search run, 24 October 2026)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
UPPER AIR (no friction): geostrophic balance

   LOW  <----- pressure gradient force ----- HIGH
        (gradient points from HIGH toward LOW)

   Coriolis deflects the moving air to its RIGHT (Northern Hemisphere).
   When the two forces balance, the wind runs ALONG the isobars,
   with LOW pressure on its LEFT.

SURFACE (friction): friction slows the wind, Coriolis weakens,
   and the gradient turns the flow across the isobars toward the LOW.
```

Read the upper-air diagram as a balance. The push toward low pressure is matched by the deflection to the right, so the wind runs along the isobars. Friction at the surface removes part of the wind, and the balance then tilts the wind across the isobars.

Start with a question. If pressure differences push air toward low pressure, why does the wind on a weather map usually blow parallel to the isobars aloft, and why does a low spin? The answer lies in the Coriolis effect, which is an apparent deflection caused by Earth's rotation. It is not a push but a consequence of observing motion from a rotating frame.

The four forces are the pressure gradient force, the Coriolis force, friction and the centrifugal or curvature effect. The pressure gradient force starts the motion and always points from high to low pressure. The Coriolis force acts on any moving object and deflects it to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. Its size is proportional to wind speed and to the sine of latitude. It is zero at the equator and largest at the poles. Ferrel's law gives the same deflection rule in words: moving air is deflected to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. Friction acts at the surface and slows the wind, which in turn weakens the Coriolis force. Curvature matters around cyclones and anticyclones, where the flow turns and an additional balance is needed.

In the upper troposphere, friction is small, and the two main forces balance each other. The pressure gradient pushes toward the low; the Coriolis force deflects the air to the right, which cancels that push when the wind is steady. The wind then blows parallel to the isobars, with low pressure on its left in the Northern Hemisphere. This balance is called geostrophic. It is an approximation, and it works best away from the equator and over large, smooth distances.

At the surface, friction reduces the wind speed. With the Coriolis force now weaker, the balance no longer holds, and the pressure gradient turns the wind across the isobars toward low pressure. This is why surface winds spiral inward into lows and outward from highs, counterclockwise and clockwise respectively in the Northern Hemisphere. This surface spiral is the flow that carries air into a cyclone, as Lesson 13 shows.

The latitude effect has a direct consequence for storms. Because the Coriolis force is zero at the equator, a converging low cannot begin to rotate there. A tropical cyclone therefore needs to form some distance poleward of the equator, and the usual threshold quoted is about five degrees of latitude. This is a condition for genesis, not a rule for all storms, and it links to the genesis conditions in Lesson 13.

The Coriolis effect is often mistaken for a force that causes water to swirl down a drain or that changes the direction of a ball across a pitch in a few seconds. Both are too small or too local to be governed by it. For atmospheric motion over hundreds of kilometres, it is decisive.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for the forces that control winds. The PYQ list links one Prelims item here: 2024 Q14, on the characteristics of the Coriolis force.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that the Coriolis force must set water and air rotating in the same direction everywhere. The reply is that the Coriolis force depends on latitude and is zero at the equator, so its effect on rotation direction is set by the hemisphere and by the balance of forces, not by a single fixed spin.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Cyclones in the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere because of the Coriolis deflection acting on converging surface winds. The limit is that the sign of rotation depends on the hemisphere, and small or short-lived vortices near the equator do not follow the same balance.

**UPSC application.** Mains answers on winds, cyclones and the inward spiral of lows need the four forces in the right order. Trap: saying the Coriolis force starts the wind, or applying it at the equator.

Revision notes:

- The four forces are pressure gradient, Coriolis, friction and curvature.
- The pressure gradient force starts motion and points from high to low pressure.
- The Coriolis force deflects moving air to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere.
- Coriolis strength depends on latitude (zero at the equator) and on wind speed.
- Aloft, gradient and Coriolis balance, so the wind runs parallel to isobars (geostrophic balance).
- At the surface, friction weakens the wind and the flow crosses isobars toward low pressure.
- Surface winds spiral inward into lows, counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere.
- Cyclones need roughly 5 degrees of latitude or more to acquire rotation.

Cover and recall: a wind blows exactly parallel to the isobars at 5,000 metres, but near the ground it crosses them. What force has changed, and why?
*Answer pointer:* Friction at the surface has reduced the wind speed, which weakens the Coriolis force and lets the pressure gradient turn the wind across the isobars.

Next, the forces explain the wind at one place and one level. Lesson 8 asks how the same forces arrange winds into a global pattern of belts.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a surface wind in the Northern Hemisphere blow inward and counterclockwise around a low, when the upper-air wind blows parallel to the isobars?

**Model answer:** The pressure gradient pushes air toward the low in both cases. Aloft, Coriolis deflects the air to the right until the two forces balance, so the wind runs along the isobars. At the surface, friction slows the wind, Coriolis weakens, and the gradient turns the flow across the isobars toward the low. The inward and counterclockwise spiral is the surface result of friction disturbing the upper-air balance.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that Coriolis causes the inward flow. The gradient causes the inflow; Coriolis, weakened by friction, determines how far the wind turns.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain why a tropical cyclone cannot form on the equator, using the Coriolis force, and state what the statement does and does not tell us about the storms that do form. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** The Coriolis force depends on the sine of latitude and vanishes at the equator. A converging low there cannot acquire rotation, because the deflection that turns inflow into a vortex is absent. This is why cyclones need a minimum latitude, usually quoted at about 5 degrees. The statement describes a necessary condition for rotation, not the full set of conditions for a storm. Warm sea surface, low wind shear and an initial disturbance are also required. The Coriolis result explains the absence of storms at the equator, and it does not by itself predict where a storm will form or how strong it will become.

```text
 POLE       ~60 N        ~30 N         EQUATOR        ~30 S        ~60 S        POLE
 (high)     (low)        (high)        (low, ITCZ)    (high)       (low)        (high)
   -- polar easterlies -->  <-- westerlies --  -- trades -->  <-- trades --  -- westerlies -->  <-- polar easterlies --
   air sinks at the poles and 30 degrees; air rises at 60 degrees and at the equator
```

Read the diagram from the equator outward. Air rises at the equator, sinks near 30 degrees, rises near 60 degrees and sinks at the poles. Each rising limb is a low, each sinking limb a high. The surface winds blow between them.

Start with a puzzle. The equator receives far more energy than the poles, yet the air does not simply rise at the equator and flow straight to the poles at high altitude. Why does the circulation break into belts? The answer combines the energy gradient with Earth's rotation. Warm equatorial air rises, flows poleward aloft, and is deflected by the Coriolis effect. Cold polar air sinks and flows equatorward. The flows set up cells, and the cells set up belts of high and low pressure.

The Hadley cell is the tropical cell. Warm air rises in the equatorial low, which is the zone where the trade winds converge, often called the intertropical convergence zone. It spreads poleward aloft and sinks near 30 degrees, creating the subtropical high, where descending air is dry and clear; these subtropical highs are also called the horse latitudes. Surface air then flows back toward the equator as the trade winds. The Ferrel cell is the mid-latitude cell, driven by the other two cells rather than by direct heating, and it produces the subpolar low near 60 degrees. The polar cell is the high-latitude cell of sinking cold air at the poles.

The surface winds follow from the pressure belts. The trade winds blow from the subtropical highs toward the equator, turning to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, so they are north-easterly there. The westerlies blow from the subtropical highs toward the subpolar lows, and they are south-westerly in the Northern Hemisphere. The polar easterlies blow from the polar highs toward the subpolar lows and are cold and east-moving. Note the direction: a trade wind blows toward the equator, not away from it, and a westerly blows toward the pole, not from it.

The belts move with the sun. As the sun's direct overhead position shifts between the tropics, the equatorial low and the subtropical highs migrate north and south, following the thermal equator. The migration is not a uniform five degrees everywhere. Continents and oceans distort the belts. Over land the highs and lows are often stronger or weaker and shift by different amounts, which is why the simple three-cell picture is an idealisation.

A persistent anticyclone over a region produces the same subsidence on a smaller scale. Descending air warms by compression and suppresses cloud, so the surface receives intense sunlight and heat builds beneath it. This heat-dome pattern is a circulation phenomenon rather than a surface one, and it is the link between the permanent subtropical high and an episode of prolonged heat.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for global pressure belts and planetary winds. No PYQ item is assigned to this lesson. The Indian monsoon is taught in the monsoon topic and is referred to here only as a seasonal reversal.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that the three cells are a real structure visible in every season. The reply is that the cells are a model of mean circulation. Individual weather systems, monsoon flows and jets depart from the model, especially over continents and in the upper air.

**India-centric example and its limit.** The subtropical high near 30 degrees north sits over the northwestern deserts and the Thar, and its descending dry air is one reason those areas are arid. The limit is that the Thar's aridity also depends on distance from the sea, relief and the monsoon's reach, so the belt explains a tendency rather than the whole climate.

**In the answer.** Mains answers on deserts, winds and seasonal migration start from the belts. Trap: confusing the equatorial low with a high, or reversing the direction of the trade winds.

Revision notes:

- The three cells are Hadley (tropical), Ferrel (mid-latitude) and polar.
- Rising air forms lows at the equator and near 60 degrees; sinking air forms highs near 30 degrees and at the poles.
- The equatorial low is the zone where the trades converge, often called the intertropical convergence zone.
- The Doldrums are the calm equatorial low, not a high.
- Trade winds blow toward the equator; westerlies blow toward the pole.
- Belts migrate with the sun's overhead position, but not uniformly.
- Continents distort the belts, so the three-cell picture is an idealisation.

Next, the global pattern is set by the large cells. Lesson 9 asks how smaller winds, shaped by the land and the slopes, give the local winds that matter for India's hot season.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why do the trade winds blow from the north-east in the Northern Hemisphere, when they are said to blow toward the equator?

**Model answer:** The trade winds blow from the subtropical high toward the equatorial low, so their direction is equatorward. Coriolis deflects the flow to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, turning a southward flow into a north-easterly one. The name describes the origin of the wind, and the equatorward direction describes its movement.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that a wind named for a direction blows from the opposite side of the destination. Name winds by origin, and check the direction of movement separately.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain how differential heating and Earth's rotation together produce the subtropical high, and why the desert belts sit near 30 degrees. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Warm air rises at the equator and flows poleward aloft. Coriolis deflects that poleward flow, and by about 30 degrees the air has been turned enough to accumulate and sink. Descending air warms by compression and suppresses cloud, creating the subtropical high. Surface air at this belt is therefore dry and clear. The desert belts sit near 30 degrees because the subsiding dry air limits rainfall there. The mechanism is dynamic, not only thermal, because the sinking depends on the circulation rather than on local heating. The belt explains a tendency, and it is modified by continents, relief and distance from the sea.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Equatorial heating and poleward flow aloft, 2 marks. Coriolis deflection and accumulation near 30 degrees, 2 marks. Subsidence, compression and cloud suppression, 2 marks. Link to desert belts, 2 marks. Limit from continental distortion or relief, 2 marks.

```text
 WINDWARD SLOPE                    LEE SLOPE

```

Read the diagram as a bank account of moisture. The windward slope spends water as rain, and that rain releases heat into the air. The leeward slope has no water left to release, so the same air warms faster on the way down than it cooled on the way up.

Start with a question. Why does a wind coming down a mountain on one side feel hot and dry, when the same air coming up the other side felt cool and damp? The puzzle has a single explanation, and it is the same explanation whether the mountain is the Alps, the Rockies or a Himalayan ridge.

Before the mountain winds, consider the sea breeze. On a sunny day, land heats faster than the sea, so the air over the land warms, rises and leaves a surface low. Cooler air flows in from the sea. At night the land cools faster than the water, and the wind reverses, flowing from land to sea. The sea breeze is therefore a daytime onshore wind, and the land breeze is a night-time offshore wind. Both are local, thermally driven winds, and both are weaker than the large-scale flow they sit inside.

Mountain and valley winds use the same logic on slopes. By day, the slopes heat, the air against them warms, and it rises up the slope as an anabatic, or upslope, wind. By night, the slopes lose heat, the air against them cools and drains down the slope as a katabatic, or downslope, wind. A valley wind is named for its origin in the valley but blows upslope in the day, which is a common point of confusion.

The Foehn wind is a different mechanism. Moist air is forced up the windward side of a mountain range. As it rises, it cools at the moist adiabatic rate, roughly 6 degrees per kilometre in this case, because latent heat is released as vapour condenses and falls as rain or snow. Having lost much of its moisture, the air crosses the ridge and descends the lee side. Now it has no condensation to offset the warming, so it warms at the dry adiabatic rate of about 9.8 degrees per kilometre. The air reaches the foot of the lee slope warmer and drier than the air that was at the same height on the windward side. The asymmetry of the two rates is the mechanism.

A common error is to say that a dry Foehn is impossible. A dry Foehn is possible when little rain falls on the windward side, so the air descends with little moisture removed and still warms at the dry rate on the way down. The mechanism is the descent, not the rain itself.

Chinook is the Foehn-type wind on the eastern slopes of the Rocky Mountains. The mechanism is the same, and the name is regional. Its effect is a sharp, rapid warming that can melt snow quickly. The Alpine Foehn is the European name for the same family of winds.

The Loo is different. It is a hot, dry, gusty wind of the northwestern and northern Indian plains in the late pre-monsoon months. Its heat comes from intense surface heating of a dry plain, not from descent over a mountain. There is no windward rainout and no leeward descent. The hot dry air is carried across the plain by the prevailing flow. Grouping it with the Foehn is tempting because both are hot and dry, but the causes differ. The Loo is typically strongest in the afternoon. The precise hours vary from day to day and place to place, so it should not be taught as a fixed clock window.

Here is the comparison that the Mains question tests.

| Feature | Loo | Foehn | Chinook |
|---|---|---|---|
| Region | Northwestern and northern Indian plains | Leeward valleys of the Alps | Eastern slopes of the Rocky Mountains |
| Season | Late pre-monsoon, hot season | Chiefly winter and spring | Chiefly winter and early spring |
| Mechanism | Surface heating of dry plain | Windward cooling, then leeward adiabatic warming | Same as Foehn, regional name |
| Mountain role | None | Essential | Essential |
| Typical impact | Extreme heat, heat stress, crop drying | Rapid warming, snowmelt, gusty wind | Rapid warming, fast snowmelt |

The table shows the classification the answer must make first. Chinook and Foehn share a mechanism and differ only in name. Loo has a different cause. Grouping them as hot local winds is accurate at the level of effect, but not of cause.

**Bridge to disaster management.** Heat stress and cold waves are important impacts of these winds and of the circulation, but their operational thresholds and warning systems are set by the disaster-management topic. The thresholds are not given here.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for local winds and their climatic effects. The PYQ list links one Mains item here: 2026 GS-I Q14 (Loo, Chinook and Foehn comparison; 15 marks). The approach is to classify first, then compare region, mechanism and impact.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a hot wind must come from over a desert. The reply is that Foehn winds come from cool, moist air on the windward side and become hot only through descent. Warmth alone does not identify the source.

**India-centric example and its limit.** The Loo is the Indian example. It is strongest in the afternoon, in the hot dry months, over the northwest plains. The limit is that the hours and strength vary, and the Loo is a local wind, not a planetary one.

**UPSC application.** Mains answers on local winds must classify before comparing. Trap: calling the Loo a Foehn wind, or claiming all three occur only in winter.

- Sea and land breezes reverse with the daily temperature contrast between land and water.
- Anabatic winds rise slopes by day; katabatic winds drain slopes by night.
- A valley wind blows upslope by day, even though it is named for the valley.
- Foehn and Chinook warm by descent: windward cooling releases latent heat; leeward warming is at the dry rate.
- A dry Foehn can occur when little rain falls on the windward side.

- The Loo is a surface-heating wind of the northern plains, not a Foehn wind.

Decision point: a wind warms sharply as it reaches a lowland valley after crossing a range, and the air at the lowland is drier than the air on the far side at the same height. Which mechanism explains this, and which two rates produce the warming?
*Answer pointer:* The Foehn mechanism: the air cooled slowly while condensing on the windward side and warmed faster on the descent, at the dry rate.

Next, these winds show how heat and moisture can move with the air. Lesson 10 turns to the moisture itself and asks how much water the air can hold, and what a cloudy night does to dew.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two winds both reach a plain hot and dry. One is a Foehn wind that descended a range, and the other is the Loo that formed over the plain. Why is classifying them by mechanism more useful than grouping them by temperature?

**Model answer:** Temperature and dryness are effects that both winds share, so grouping by effect hides the cause. The Foehn gains its heat from adiabatic descent after moisture has been removed on the windward side. The Loo gains its heat from intense surface heating over a dry plain, with no descent. A correct explanation names the cause, which determines where the wind forms, when it occurs and what precedes it.

If it does not, and the air was heated over a dry plain, the wind is a Loo. The second test is the place: the northern Indian plains point to a Loo, the Alps to a Foehn and the Rockies to a Chinook. Read the diagram as a capacity problem. Air has a maximum amount of vapour it can hold at a given temperature. Cooling lowers that maximum. When the actual vapour content reaches the maximum, the air is saturated and condensation begins.

Start with a common experience. On a humid day, the air feels heavy even when the temperature is not extreme, and cold water poured into a glass fogs the outside within seconds. Both observations depend on the same idea: the capacity of air to hold water vapour depends on its temperature.

Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapour in a given volume of air. Specific humidity, the mass of vapour per unit mass of air, is unaffected when a parcel expands or is compressed without condensation, which is why forecasters use it in many calculations. It is a direct measure of how much water is present. Relative humidity is the ratio of the actual vapour pressure to the saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature, expressed as a percentage. Relative humidity tells you how close the air is to saturation, not how much water it contains. A cold, dry air mass can have a high relative humidity with very little vapour, because its capacity is also small.

Saturation vapour pressure rises steeply with temperature. Roughly, it doubles for each rise of 10 degrees in the range of ordinary surface temperatures. Warm air can therefore hold much more vapour than cold air. This explains why the tropics are humid and why winter air over the plains feels dry even when a cold day is damp in appearance.

The dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure and moisture, for it to become saturated. At the dew point, relative humidity is 100 per cent. Further cooling causes condensation: dew on surfaces, fog in the air near the ground or cloud higher up, depending on the surface and height involved. The dew point is a property of the air's moisture content, so two air masses at the same temperature can have very different dew points.

Now the cloudy night. Dew forms on the ground when the surface cools to or below the dew point of the air touching it. On a clear, calm night, the ground loses longwave radiation to space freely, cools rapidly, and reaches the dew point, so dew forms. On a cloudy night, the cloud acts as a blanket. It returns much of the longwave radiation to the ground, which slows the surface cooling, and the surface often stays above the dew point. Dew is therefore scarce on cloudy nights, even when the air is moist. The moisture is present; the cooling is absent.

Water vapour also changes with height. Most of it lies in the lowest few kilometres, because the lower troposphere is warmer, more humid and closer to the moisture source of the surface. The amount falls quickly with altitude, so high-altitude air is much drier than air at the surface. This is one reason why high mountain stations have dry air and little vapour, even when the surface is humid below. The share of water vapour in the air is variable: it is close to zero in very cold, dry air and reaches a few per cent by volume in hot, humid tropical air.

**Worked dew-point calculation.** Take air at 25 degrees Celsius with a relative humidity of 60 per cent. Using the Magnus approximation, the saturation vapour pressure is about 6.112 x exp(17.67 x 25 / (25 + 243.5)), or about 31.7 hPa. The actual vapour pressure is 0.60 x 31.7, or about 19.0 hPa. Solving the same approximation for the temperature at which 19.0 hPa is saturation gives a dew point of about 16.7 degrees Celsius, which is about 8 degrees below the air temperature. The assumptions are constant pressure, the approximation's usual range (roughly -40 to 50 degrees Celsius at sea-level pressure) and ideal vapour behaviour. The dew point is therefore the temperature the air must reach by cooling, not a fixed property of the air's temperature.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for humidity and condensation. The PYQ list links two Prelims items here: 2019 Q44 (cloudy-night dew) and 2024 Q12 (water vapour with altitude).

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a humid day must produce dew at night. The reply is that dew depends on the surface reaching the dew point, not on the air being humid. A humid, cloudy night often produces less dew than a drier clear night, because the clouds limit the cooling.

**India-centric example and its limit.** On the Konkan coast in the post-monsoon period, nights can be humid and still produce little dew under cloud. The limit is that local relief, wind and the surface's own cooling all modify the dew, so the cloud effect is one control among several.

**In the answer.** Mains answers on humidity start with saturation and the dew point rather than with the amount of moisture alone. Trap: equating high relative humidity with high absolute humidity, or assuming that humid air always forms dew.

Revision notes:

- Absolute humidity measures the mass of vapour per volume; relative humidity measures closeness to saturation.
- Saturation vapour pressure rises steeply with temperature.
- The dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated at constant pressure and moisture.
- Relative humidity of 100 per cent corresponds to the dew point.
- Dew forms when the surface cools to the dew point of the touching air.
- Cloud cover slows surface cooling by returning longwave radiation, so cloudy nights produce less dew.
- Water vapour is concentrated in the lowest kilometres and falls quickly with altitude.
- The share of water vapour in the air is variable, from near zero to a few per cent by volume.

Spot the error: two nights have air at the same moisture content. One is clear and calm, the other overcast. Which night is more likely to produce dew, and what causes the difference?
*Answer pointer:* The clear, calm night, because the ground cools freely to the dew point, while cloud slows the cooling on the overcast night.

Next, the moisture is ready to condense, but condensation needs the right conditions to form visible cloud and rain. Lesson 11 asks which clouds form, how they affect the energy budget in opposite directions, and how rain is produced.

### Concept check

**Question:** A cold morning air mass has a relative humidity of 90 per cent. Does it hold more water than a warm afternoon air mass with a relative humidity of 60 per cent? Explain.

**Model answer:** Not necessarily. Relative humidity measures closeness to saturation, while absolute humidity measures the amount of vapour. The cold air's saturation capacity is small, so even a high relative humidity may correspond to very little vapour. The warm air can hold far more vapour in total, so it may contain more water even at a lower relative humidity.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat relative humidity as a measure of water content. The same vapour content can produce very different relative humidities at different temperatures.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** A cool plateau station and a humid coastal station both record clear, calm nights with the same fall in temperature. Explain which one is more likely to show dew, and whether dew depends on the humidity of the air alone. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Dew depends on whether the surface reaches the dew point, and the dew point depends on the moisture in the air. The coastal air holds more vapour, so its dew point is higher and it needs less cooling to become saturated. The plateau air holds little vapour, so its dew point is low and its surface must cool much further before dew forms. Clear, calm skies allow the surface to cool by longwave loss, so the coastal surface is more likely to reach its higher dew point. Humidity sets the threshold; cooling decides whether the threshold is crossed.

The heights are typical ranges and differ between the tropics and the poles. Read the right-hand column as the test. Each cloud is assessed on its effect, not its appearance, and the effect depends on height and thickness.

Begin with the strongest objection to the cloud question: a white cloud must cool the ground beneath it. That objection is partly right, and a thin high cloud shows why whiteness alone is the wrong test. A thin wispy cirrus looks harmless. Yet the thin cloud high in the sky can warm the ground beneath more than the thick cloud can cool it. Why would the appearance mislead? The answer lies in the two halves of the energy budget from Lesson 2. A cloud reflects incoming shortwave, which cools the surface. It also absorbs and re-emits outgoing longwave, which warms the surface by returning energy to it. The net effect is the balance of these two.

Cloud families are named by form and height. Cirrus is high, wispy and made of ice crystals. Alto prefixed clouds are middle-level layers. Strato clouds are low, stratiform sheets. Cumulus is heaped and rising, and cumulonimbus is the towering, vertically deep cloud of thunderstorms, reaching the tropopause. The prefix tells you the level and the suffix the form. The families are a description of the cloud, not a measure of rain.

The radiative sign is the direction of the cloud's net effect on surface temperature. A thick, low cloud is bright and reflects a large share of sunlight. Its trapping of longwave is small, because it sits low and the outgoing energy it intercepts is already close to the surface temperature. The net effect is cooling, especially by day. A thin, high cirrus is almost transparent to sunlight, so it reflects little shortwave. It is cold at its top, so it emits little longwave to space and returns more to the surface. The net effect is warming, especially at night. A deep cumulonimbus reflects strongly by day and traps heavily, so its net effect depends on its thickness and the time of day.

Consider the day and night cycle. By day, the cooling effect of reflection is strongest, so thick clouds reduce daytime heating. At night there is no sunlight to reflect, so only the trapping of longwave acts, and clouds warm the surface relative to a clear night. This is why cloudy nights are warmer than clear ones, a fact the dew lesson in Lesson 10 depended on. The same cloud therefore cools in the afternoon and warms overnight.

Rain needs lift. Air must rise, cool to saturation and form droplets large enough to fall. There are three main ways of producing that lift, and each has a distinct geography. Convectional rain forms when the surface is strongly heated, so the air above it becomes buoyant and rises in showers and thunderstorms. It is common in equatorial regions and in the afternoon of hot days. Orographic rain forms when moist air is forced up a mountain barrier. It falls on the windward slope, and the lee side receives less, which produces a rain shadow. Frontal or cyclonic rain forms when a warm air mass is lifted over a colder one, or when air converges into a low, as in a depression or a front. Lesson 12 develops the fronts.

The mountain question in the Mains record is a special case of orographic rain. The orientation of a range matters because the barrier works best when it is roughly perpendicular to the moist wind. A range parallel to the wind offers little obstruction, so the lifting is weak and the rain shadow is small. The alignment therefore controls both the amount of rain on the windward side and the dryness behind the range. The limit is that orientation is one control. The moisture supply, the height of the barrier, and the stability of the air all matter, so the same alignment can produce different rainfall in different seasons.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for clouds and precipitation. The PYQ list links two items: Prelims 2022 Q81 (high and low clouds and Earth's temperature) and Mains 2021 GS-I Q14 (mountain-range alignment; 15 marks).

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that all clouds cool the Earth because they are white. The reply is that thin high cloud is nearly transparent to sunlight and traps longwave, so its net effect is warming. Whiteness is not the right test; the balance of reflection and trapping is.

**India-centric example and its limit.** The Western Ghats, which run roughly parallel to the west coast, receive heavy rain on the windward western slopes during the southwest monsoon, and the Deccan plateau to their east lies in a rain shadow. The orientation and the height of the ghats are both necessary to the pattern. The limit is that the monsoon's moisture supply and seasonal wind direction also set the amounts, so the barrier explains the contrast without giving the total.

**UPSC application.** Mains answers on rain begin with the lift mechanism, then the geography of the lift. Trap: treating every rain as convectional, or ascribing cloud cooling without mentioning the time of day.

- Cloud families are named by height and form; the prefix gives the level.
- A cloud's radiative effect depends on the balance between shortwave reflection and longwave trapping.
- Thick low cloud cools the surface, especially by day.
- Thin high cloud warms the surface, especially at night.
- Deep convective cloud depends on thickness and time of day.
- Cloudy nights are warmer than clear ones, because clouds trap outgoing longwave.
- Rain needs lift; the three mechanisms are convectional, orographic and frontal or cyclonic.
- Orographic rain is strongest on the windward side; the lee side is a rain shadow.
- Barrier orientation matters: a range perpendicular to the moist wind lifts it most effectively.

Trace it: a region has two cloud types, a dense low stratus and a thin high cirrus. Which one cools by day more, and which one warms more by night?
*Answer pointer:* The dense low stratus cools more by day through reflection, and the thin high cirrus warms more by night through longwave trapping.

Next, rain is produced by lift, and lift often comes from the meeting of two different air masses. Lesson 12 asks what those air masses are and what happens at the boundary between them.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can a thin high cloud warm the ground at night, even though it looks harmless, and why does the answer depend on time of day?

**Model answer:** A thin high cloud transmits most sunlight, so it removes little incoming energy by day. At night, it intercepts outgoing longwave from the ground and returns part of it, and because it is cold and high, it emits less to space than the surface would. The surface therefore stays warmer. By day, the cloud's small reflection effect is small, so the net effect is mild. The sign of the effect depends on whether reflection or trapping dominates at that hour.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not judge a cloud's effect by its appearance or whiteness. The net effect depends on height, thickness and whether sunlight or longwave is the dominant process.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** In March a hilly district records heavy hail and short intense storms in the afternoon, while a neighbouring plain receives steady rain over a whole night. Identify three observations that would separate a convective, hail-producing event from a frontal rain event, and give the physical reason for each. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** The first observation is the duration and form of the rain: short, intense showers from towering cloud point to convection driven by surface heating, while steady rain over a whole night points to widespread lift at a front. The second is the cloud type: hail needs a strong updraft in a cumulonimbus, because the updraft holds ice aloft long enough for it to grow, whereas sheet-like stratiform cloud does not sustain that growth. The third is the timing and wind: afternoon onset over heated hills fits surface-driven convection, while a passage of a front shows a wind shift and a pressure change. Each observation tests the lift behind the rain; none is decisive alone.

```text

  COLD AIR  <--- front --->  WARM AIR          WARM AIR   |   COLD AIR
                                                          |
   ground  ____________________________         ground  ___|___________________
```

Read the diagrams as a boundary. The front is the surface along which two air masses meet. The warm air is lifted over or pushed up by the cold air, and lifting is what makes cloud and rain.

Start with the weather of a typical winter depression in the mid-latitudes. A band of cloud arrives, rain steadies for hours, then the wind swings and the sky clears behind a sharper squall. Why does one storm produce such different weather in sequence? The answer is that the storm passes two boundaries, and each boundary lifts air differently.

An air mass is a large body of air that has taken on the temperature and humidity of its source region. Source regions are extensive, uniform surfaces. Cold, dry continental air forms over high latitudes of land, cold and moist maritime air forms over high-latitude seas, warm and humid tropical air forms over subtropical oceans, and hot dry tropical air forms over deserts. The name combines the thermal and moisture character: polar or tropical for temperature, continental or maritime for moisture. An air mass keeps its character while it travels, and it is changed most at its edges.

A front is the narrow zone where two air masses of different character meet. Because cold air is denser, it acts as a wedge, and warm air is forced to rise over it or to slide up it. The lift is the source of cloud and rain. Four kinds of front are usually distinguished: warm, cold, stationary and occluded.

A warm front forms when warm air advances over cold air. The cold air is the wedge, and the warm air slides up it gently, over a long slope. The cloud is extensive and stratiform, the rain is steady and widespread, and the front moves slowly. A cold front forms when cold air advances under warm air. The cold air undercuts the warm air and lifts it sharply. The cloud is narrower and taller, the rain is heavier and often showery, and the front moves faster, so the weather change is abrupt.

A common error is to think every front is a temperature inversion. Inversions can occur near fronts, but a front is defined by the contact and contrast of two air masses. Its lift comes from the wedge geometry, not from a stable warm lid in every case. Students who memorise "front equals inversion" will be wrong for many cold fronts.

An occluded front forms when a cold front catches up with a warm front and lifts the warm air off the ground. A stationary front is a boundary that barely moves and so produces prolonged cloud and rain over the same area. Both are minor features of a maturing depression and need not be memorised beyond their meaning.

The mid-latitude depression, or extra-tropical cyclone, develops along a front. A small wave forms on the boundary between cold and warm air. Its low-pressure centre deepens as the cold air undercuts the warm air to its west and south, and the wave then matures into a closed circulation with a warm sector between the two fronts. This development is called cyclogenesis. It is driven by the temperature contrast across the front and by the upper-air flow that steers it. This is different from a tropical cyclone, which is fuelled by latent heat over a warm ocean and has no fronts in the same sense. Lesson 13 compares the two directly.

A timeline makes the sequence concrete. It shows what one station records as a warm front and then a cold front pass over it.

```text
FRONT PASSAGE AT A FIXED STATION (time runs left to right)

  ahead of warm front  |  warm front passes  |  warm sector  |  cold front passes  |  behind cold front
  cirrus, thickening  |  steady rain from  |  mild, humid,  |  narrow band of     |  clearing, cooler,
  cloud, then steady  |  stratiform cloud  |  drizzle, high  |  showers, sharp     |  drier air
  rain begins         |  temperature rises |  temperature   |  wind veer          |
```

In the Northern Hemisphere the wind veers, turning clockwise, as each front passes. The warm sector is the interval between the two fronts, which is why it is warm and humid.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a cold front and a warm front produce the same kind of cloud. The reply is that the geometry differs: the warm front slides gently up a long slope and produces extensive stratiform cloud, while the cold front lifts air sharply and produces narrow, towering cloud.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Western disturbances bring fronts and frontal cloud to northwest India in winter, producing steady rain and snow over the hills. The limit is that the western disturbance is a mobile trough in the upper westerlies, not simply a front on a surface chart, and it needs the upper-air support explained in Lessons 15 and 16.

**In the answer.** Mains answers on winter rain or on temperate storms start with the air masses and the type of front. Trap: calling a warm front the same as a cold front, or treating every frontal cloud as the same.

Revision notes:

- An air mass takes its temperature and humidity from its source region.
- Air masses are named by temperature (polar, tropical) and moisture (continental, maritime).
- A front is the boundary between two air masses; lift at the front produces cloud and rain.
- A warm front slides over a long slope and brings extensive stratiform cloud and steady rain.
- A cold front undercuts warm air and brings narrow, tall cloud and showers.
- A front is not defined as an inversion; inversions occur only in some frontal situations.
- An occluded front forms when a cold front overtakes a warm front.
- Cyclogenesis is the formation and deepening of a frontal low.
- A mid-latitude depression is driven by temperature contrast across a front, not by latent heat over ocean alone.

Stop and apply: a cold front and a warm front pass over the same station in one day. Which front brings the more abrupt weather change, and what in its geometry explains that?
*Answer pointer:* The cold front, because its cold wedge lifts warm air steeply, producing narrow tall cloud and a quick shift in wind and temperature.

Next, fronts and depressions are the mid-latitude story. The tropical cyclone works through a different engine, with the ocean as its fuel. Lesson 13 starts there.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a cold front usually bring a sharper weather change than a warm front, when both lift air?

**Model answer:** The cold front is a dense cold wedge advancing under warm air, so it lifts the warm air steeply and in a narrow zone. This produces tall, narrow cloud, showers and a quick shift in wind and temperature. The warm front slides up a gentle slope, lifting air over a wide area and producing extensive, stratiform cloud and steady rain. The difference is the slope of the lift, which follows from the geometry of the two air masses.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not assume that all frontal lift is the same. The geometry, not the mere presence of a front, determines the cloud type and the speed of change.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain how a mid-latitude depression develops along a front, and why its weather changes in sequence as it passes. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** A mid-latitude depression begins as a wave on the boundary between cold polar air and warmer air further south. The boundary is a front, and the wave deepens as the cold air undercuts the warm air, a process called cyclogenesis. Two fronts then form around a warm sector: the warm front, which slides gently over cold air and brings extensive stratiform cloud and steady rain, and the cold front, which lifts warm air steeply and brings narrow showers and a sharp change. The sequence follows the passage of these boundaries over a station. The depression is driven by the temperature contrast across the front, not by heat released over ocean, which separates it from a tropical cyclone.

Read the diagram from the ocean upward. Warm sea evaporates into the inflow, the inflow spirals inward, and the moist air rises in the eyewall, releasing latent heat. The calm eye is where air subsides and warms.

Start with a question. A mature cyclone releases an enormous amount of energy in a few days. Where does the energy come from? The answer is the ocean. A tropical cyclone is a heat engine whose fuel is the latent heat released when water vapour condenses. The warm ocean supplies that vapour through evaporation, and the storm converts the released heat into rotational kinetic energy.

The engine needs conditions. The first is sea-surface temperature of about 26 to 27 degrees Celsius or more, not merely at the surface but through a sufficient depth of the upper ocean. Depth matters because the storm's own winds mix cooler water up from below. A shallow warm layer would be cooled by that mixing and would shut off the fuel. The second is latitude beyond about five degrees, because the Coriolis effect from Lesson 7 is needed to give the converging inflow a rotation. The third is low vertical wind shear, because strong shear tilts and tears the developing warm core. The fourth is a pre-existing disturbance that supplies an initial convergence. The fifth is a deep, moist mid-troposphere, since dry air entering at mid-levels kills convection. The sixth is upper-level divergence, which carries air away aloft so that surface pressure keeps falling.

Genesis can be checked in order, as a decision sequence:

```text
GENESIS CHECK, IN ORDER
  Warm layer about 26-27 C or more, through a deep layer?   no -> no storm
    | yes
  Latitude beyond about 5 degrees?                          no -> no rotation
    | yes
  Vertical wind shear weak?                                 no -> system torn apart
    | yes
  Pre-existing disturbance present?                         no -> no genesis
    | yes
  Moist mid-levels and upper outflow?                       no -> convection killed
    | yes
  GENESIS POSSIBLE (intensity still set by the ocean and shear)
```

The conditional instability from Lesson 5 explains the trigger. Dry air is stable, but moist air lifted to saturation releases latent heat and becomes warmer than its surroundings. The storm needs an initial lift to start that process, which is why a pre-existing disturbance is required.

The eye needs its own explanation. Its air is calm, and it is warmer than the surrounding air at upper levels. The warmth is produced by subsidence: air sinks in the eye, compresses and warms, while the eyewall outside is cloudy and rising. This is the reason a tropical cyclone is called warm-cored, a feature that separates it from the mid-latitude depression of Lesson 12, which is driven by a temperature contrast across a front. Eye temperature is therefore a sign of the vertical structure of the storm, not a sign of its heat from the sea surface alone.

Sea-surface temperature matters because it sets the energy available. A warmer ocean raises the potential intensity ceiling and the rain rate, because more evaporation feeds the inflow. It also deepens the warm layer, so the storm's mixing does not shut off the fuel as quickly. It can extend the region that meets the temperature threshold. The limit is important: a warmer ocean does not straightforwardly increase the number of cyclones, because genesis also needs low shear and a disturbance, and those may change in the opposite direction. The Mains question on sea-surface temperature asks for this distinction between intensity and frequency.

Damage comes mainly from storm surge, from rain and inland flooding, and from wind. Surge is the wind-driven rise of water against the coast, most dangerous where the continental shelf is shallow and the coast funnels the water. Surge and flooding are widely reported as the main causes of loss of life, which means the wind speed alone is a poor guide to risk. A cyclone weakens after landfall because its warm-ocean fuel is cut off and surface friction over land disrupts the inflow, and it can re-intensify if it returns over warm water. The same storm is called a cyclone in the Indian Ocean, a hurricane in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific and a typhoon in the western Pacific: these are regional names for one phenomenon, not different phenomena.

The Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea differ. The Bay is very warm and receives large freshwater discharge from the Ganga-Brahmaputra and Irrawaddy systems. That fresh buoyant layer resists mixing and keeps the surface warm. The Bay's head is shallow and funnel-shaped, and it fronts a dense, low-lying deltaic population. Its historical lethality therefore reflects exposure and vulnerability as well as frequency. The Arabian Sea is also warm, but its approaches are less funnel-shaped and its exposed population is less dense over much of the coast, so the western coast carries significant exposure without the same concentration of deaths. The 1999 Odisha super cyclone is a standard illustration: the same storm on a steeper, sparsely settled coast would have caused far less loss.

Now the Fujiwhara effect. Two sufficiently close cyclonic vortices steer each other. Each circulation advects the other, and the pair rotates about a common centre between them. In the Northern Hemisphere the mutual orbit is counterclockwise, and in the Southern Hemisphere it is clockwise. The commonly quoted range of interaction is about 1,400 kilometres, with the rotation speeding up within about 650 kilometres. Merger or absorption is commonly described as occurring within about 300 kilometres. These are operational ranges, not switches.

The common centre is weighted by the strength of each circulation and need not lie halfway between the eyes; a background drift toward the north-west, known as beta drift, also acts on each storm during the interaction. The outcome depends on separation, relative size and intensity, background steering flow, storm structure and the time the pair spends over warm water. The possible outcomes are mutual orbit, absorption of the weaker by the stronger, elastic deflection and separation, straining or partial merger, and escape. A merged circulation can be larger without being more intense, so the interaction is best understood as a change in track and a loss of forecast certainty rather than a simple strengthening. The Mains question asks for both the movement and the intensity effects, so the answer must state the track effect as the most reliable signal and qualify the intensity effect.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for tropical cyclones and their formation. The PYQ list links three items: Mains 2024 GS-I Q4 (sea-surface temperature and cyclone formation; 10 marks), Mains 2026 GS-I Q4 (Fujiwhara effect; 10 marks) and Prelims 2020 Q99, which is shared with Lesson 15 and whose eye part is taught here. The Mains approaches are in the PYQ index.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a warmer ocean must produce more cyclones. The reply is that warming raises the ceiling on intensity and the rain rate, while the number depends on shear and disturbance, which warming does not guarantee to improve.

**India-centric example and its limit.** The Bay of Bengal storm surge is the Indian case. It shows that exposure and bathymetry, not only wind speed, drive the loss. The limit is that warning and evacuation have reduced mortality in recent decades, so the vulnerability picture is not fixed, and the example does not measure a particular storm's track.

**UPSC application.** Mains answers on cyclones start from the heat-engine mechanism and the genesis conditions, then separate intensity from frequency and track from intensity. Trap: saying two cyclones always merge or that the Fujiwhara effect always strengthens a storm.

- A tropical cyclone is a warm-core heat engine fuelled by latent heat over warm ocean.

### Concept check

**Question:** A scientist says warmer seas will make more cyclones. Explain what the mechanism supports and what it does not.

**Model answer:** The mechanism supports a higher potential intensity and heavier rain rates for storms that form, because more evaporation feeds the inflow and the warm layer stays deep. Read the diagram as a load balance. The updraft supports water and ice. When it weakens or is overwhelmed, the load falls. A cloudburst is a load released at once over a small area, and hail is a load of ice released as stones.

Start with the three severe events. Cloudbursts, tornadoes and hailstorms are all produced by deep, vigorous convection, but they differ in scale, in the way the load is released and in the place where they occur. Separating them is the core skill of this lesson.

A cloudburst is defined by rate and concentration, not by total volume. A working figure of 100 millimetres of rain in an hour, over a small area, is widely used in India to describe a cloudburst, although definitions differ between countries. The defining feature is intensity in a small space. A cloudburst can devastate one valley while the district total looks ordinary.

The mechanism is a deep, moisture-laden convective column, in which strong updrafts hold a large water load aloft. When the updraft can no longer support the load, or when more moisture is forced into the same cell, the water is released in a short time. Himalayan valleys are the classic setting, for three reasons. Monsoon moisture is forced up steep relief, which adds orographic lift to convective instability. The valleys are narrow and steep, so runoff concentrates within minutes. The slopes carry loose debris, so the runoff becomes a debris flow rather than clear water. Settlement occupies the valley floor and the fans, which is precisely where the debris is deposited.

Cloudbursts are hard to forecast. The cells are small and short-lived, so they lie below the resolution of most observation networks and model grids. Dense radar, automatic stations and nowcasting help, but warning alone cannot protect people on a valley fan. The control that geography supplies is land use: the restriction of settlement and construction on the valley floor and the fan surfaces.

The tornado is a violently rotating column extending from a convective cloud to the ground. It is much smaller and shorter-lived than a tropical cyclone and is a land-based severe thunderstorm product rather than a warm-ocean phenomenon. A waterspout is a tornado over water, and a dust devil is a small, short-lived whirl driven by intense surface heating, not by a thunderstorm. Its ingredients are five. Warm, very moist low-level air supplies buoyancy. Cool, dry air aloft or intruding creates steep instability and a moisture boundary called the dryline where it meets the moist air. A capping stable layer delays convection, so instability builds and is then released explosively. Strong vertical wind shear with directional change creates horizontal rotation, which the updraft tilts into a vertical vortex, producing the supercell and its mesocyclone. A lifting trigger, such as a front or a terrain feature, breaks the cap.

The concentration of tornadoes in the central United States has a geographical explanation. The Gulf of Mexico supplies warm, moist air northward across an open plain. The Rocky Mountains supply dry air at elevation and a lee trough. Cold air can sweep south because no east-west mountain barrier crosses the continent, and the mid-latitude jet supplies the shear. The configuration assembles all five ingredients repeatedly in one place. The limit is that this is one explanation of a concentration, not a complete account of every tornado.

India has tornadoes too, most often in the eastern and northeastern belt in the pre-monsoon season, linked to severe thunderstorms of the Nor'wester and Kalbaisakhi family. They are less frequent and generally less intense than on the American plains. The comparison does not reduce to the Himalaya alone. The Himalaya's east-west extent affects the flow of cold air, and that effect is one factor among several, including the moisture supply, the shear and the trigger. A claim that the Himalaya explains the absence of a tornado belt is too simple.

Hail forms in the same kind of cell. Strong updrafts carry supercooled water and ice particles upward and around the cloud, so the particles grow in layers. When a stone becomes too heavy for the updraft to hold, it falls. Hail damage is concentrated where the storm is most intense, which makes it a localised hazard for standing crops.

The PYQ list links two Mains items: 2024 GS-I Q6 (cloudbursts; 10 marks) and 2024 GS-I Q16 (twisters and the Gulf of Mexico; 15 marks). Their approaches are in the PYQ index. A related Mains item from 2022 on cyclone warnings is taught in the Disaster Management topic.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a cloudburst is simply very heavy monsoon rain. The reply is that the defining feature is rate and small area, which concentrates runoff on steep ground. The same total rain spread over a day and a wide area produces a very different outcome.

**India-centric example and its limit.** Himalayan valleys show the cloudburst mechanism and its debris-flow consequence. The limit is that the cells cannot be reliably forecast, so the lasting response is land-use control on valley floors and fans.

**In the answer.** Mains answers on severe weather begin with the load and release mechanism, then the geography of exposure. Trap: treating a cloudburst as a total-rainfall event, or calling a tornado a small cyclone.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two places record the same daily rainfall total. One is a cloudburst site and the other a broad plain. Why could the cloudburst site suffer a disaster that the plain does not?

**Model answer:** A cloudburst delivers the rain at a high rate over a small area, so runoff concentrates faster than the ground can absorb it. On steep, debris-laden slopes this becomes a flood with debris, and where settlement occupies the valley fans, the losses are severe. The same total on a broad plain spreads across a wide area and infiltrates more slowly into the soil, so the runoff is smaller and less concentrated. The disaster is produced by rate, concentration and exposure together, not by the total alone.

```text
NORTH ----------------------------------------------------------------- SOUTH
 POLE        polar-front jet (roughly 40-60 N in winter)
  ~~~~~~~~~ ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
                      ^ wind speed rises with height ^
    subtropical westerly jet (roughly 25-35 N, winter, north of the tropics)
   ==========================================================

```

The bands are schematic. Jets are narrow, fast currents of the upper troposphere. Their positions shift with the season, and the two westerly jets lie on either side of the subtropical high region. The wavy line marks the polar-front jet, the one that meanders most.

Start with a question. Why does a belt of wind at ten kilometres move faster than the air near the ground, and why is it so narrow? The answer is temperature contrast. Jets form where the horizontal temperature contrast is steepest. A steep contrast between warm and cold air implies a strong change in wind speed with height, and that is the thermal wind relation: the vertical change in the westerly wind is proportional to the north-south temperature gradient. Where the contrast is greatest, the wind increase with height is greatest, and the wind peaks as a narrow core.

The subtropical westerly jet lies over the subtropics, north of the tropical belt and south of the mid-latitudes. In winter it lies over northern India and the Himalayan foothills at roughly 25 to 30 degrees north, and it is the conveyor that carries western disturbances across northwest India. It moves north in spring and summer and retreats from the Indian region before the summer monsoon establishes its circulation. This northward shift is a well-known feature of the seasonal cycle and is linked to monsoon onset in the monsoon topic.

The polar-front jet is a separate, usually stronger band. It lies near the boundary between cold polar air and warmer mid-latitude air, roughly over 40 to 60 degrees, and in winter it lies to the north of the subtropical jet over Asia. Its position follows the polar front. It is not the same as the subtropical jet, and students confuse the two because both are westerly.

The tropical easterly jet is an upper-tropospheric easterly current in summer, located over the tropics and linked to the summer monsoon circulation. Its existence reminds you that jets are not only westerly. Its strength and position depend on the summer heating and the monsoon, so it cannot be used as a single switch for the monsoon.

Now the wave logic. The westerly flow is not straight. It forms large north-south meanders called Rossby waves. They arise because the Coriolis effect varies with latitude, so a parcel displaced poleward or equatorward is turned back, and the flow oscillates. The waves are stable in some patterns and not in others. When the meanders have low amplitude, the westerlies run nearly straight from west to east and weather systems move quickly. When the amplitude is high, deep ridges and troughs form and move slowly, or stall. A stalled ridge brings persistent heat under it, and a stalled trough brings persistent cold or rain under it. Persistence, not intensity alone, is what converts ordinary weather into a heatwave, a cold wave or a prolonged flood. This pattern is called blocking when a large, slow high interrupts the westerly flow and steers passing systems around it. Beneath the block, subsidence brings clear skies and accumulating heat in summer, or trapped cold and fog in winter, while the flanks of the block can steer storms repeatedly over one area.

The polar jet develops meanders by the same logic. Its wave amplitude is one of the most studied dynamic questions. A claim that a particular jet waviness causes a specific Indian extreme is a hypothesis under active study, and it should be presented as such, not as settled causation.

The Tibetan plateau is a large elevated heat source in summer. Its heating drives an upper-tropospheric anticyclone over the region, in the layer near 100 to 200 hectopascals, which is roughly 12 to 16 kilometres up. This upper anticyclone supports the summer monsoon circulation and the tropical easterly jet. The plateau also obstructs flows, so its effect on the cold air to the north is part of the picture. The degree to which the barrier blocks cold air is debated, and it should not be presented as the single cause of India's winter warmth or of any tornado pattern. The plateau is one of several controls. A further point is open: some accounts describe the winter subtropical jet as splitting into two branches over South Asia, one passing north and one south of the high ground of the plateau. That branching is not verified here and is treated as an open question, not as an established feature of the winter jet.

**Syllabus and PYQ boundary.** The syllabus asks for jet streams and upper-air circulation. Its eye part is in Lesson 13.

**Objection and reply.** A student may argue that a jet stream is simply strong surface wind. The reply is that a jet forms high in the troposphere where the temperature contrast is steepest, and surface winds are shaped by friction and terrain rather than the same balance.

**India-centric example and its limit.** The subtropical westerly jet in winter steers western disturbances into northwest India. The limit is that its position is seasonal and variable, and a single winter's track cannot prove a permanent jet shift.

**UPSC application.** Mains answers on the upper air begin with the thermal wind and the jets, then the Rossby waves that explain persistence. Trap: confusing the subtropical and polar-front jets, or treating a jet as a single switch for the monsoon or the rain.

Revision notes:

- Jet streams form where horizontal temperature contrast is steepest, and their wind increases with height up to the core.
- The subtropical westerly jet lies over northern India in winter and steers western disturbances.
- The polar-front jet is a separate band near the polar front, poleward of the subtropical jet.
- The tropical easterly jet is a summer upper-tropospheric easterly, not a westerly.
- Rossby waves are large meanders of the westerlies, arising from the latitude-varying Coriolis effect.
- Low-amplitude waves give fast systems; high-amplitude waves give slow, stalled systems.
- Persistence under a stalled ridge or trough turns weather into heatwave, cold wave or flood.
- The Tibetan plateau heats in summer and supports an upper anticyclone near 100 to 200 hectopascals.
- The plateau's role is one control, and its barrier effect on cold air is debated.

Read the map: in one winter, the subtropical jet sits farther south than usual and its meanders are deep. What would you expect for the duration of the rain systems over northwest India?
*Answer pointer:* Slower, persistent systems, because high-amplitude meanders stall the troughs that steer the rain, so rain or cold conditions last longer.

Next, the jets and waves describe the upper air. Lesson 16 applies them to the system that matters most for India's winter: the western disturbance and the crops it feeds.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why does a jet stream form where the temperature contrast is steepest, and how does that link to the wind increasing with height?

**Model answer:** The thermal wind relation links the change of the westerly wind with height to the horizontal temperature gradient. Where the north-south temperature contrast is steepest, the wind increases most rapidly with height, and the wind peaks as a narrow, fast core. A jet is therefore the upper-air expression of a strong temperature contrast, not a surface wind that happens to be strong.

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat a jet as a surface wind or assume that its speed is set by friction. It is set by the temperature contrast and the balance of forces aloft.

### Mains practice

**Practice question:** Explain how Rossby waves control whether an upper-air pattern produces short-lived or persistent weather, and what this implies for India's extreme weather. (10 marks)

**Model answer, 10 marks:** Rossby waves are large meanders of the westerly jet, produced by the variation of the Coriolis effect with latitude. When the meanders are of low amplitude, the flow is nearly straight, and weather systems move quickly through the region, so no single weather type lasts long. When the amplitude is high, ridges and troughs deepen and move slowly or stall. A stalled ridge holds heat or dry conditions beneath it, and a stalled trough holds cold or rain, so weather becomes persistent. Persistence turns ordinary weather into heatwaves, cold waves or prolonged floods. The link between a specific Indian extreme and wave amplitude remains an active research question, so the claim should be stated as a plausible mechanism rather than a settled cause.

**Rubric (10 marks):** Rossby waves defined and their cause, 2 marks. Low versus high amplitude contrast, 2 marks. Stalled ridges and troughs and their weather, 2 marks. Persistence as the link to extremes, 2 marks. Explicit caution about the research status, 2 marks.

Read the figure as a travelling trough. The upper trough carries the system; the surface low and cloud band are where ascent produces rain. The moisture comes from the west, not from the monsoon.

Start with the winter of northwest India. Its plains receive rain and snow on the hills from a source that is not the monsoon. Where does that moisture come from, and what carries it over the mountains? The answer is an extra-tropical storm that begins over the Mediterranean region and travels east.

A western disturbance is an extra-tropical cyclonic system embedded in the westerlies, the subtropical westerly flow. It is not a monsoon depression, and it does not depend on the summer heating of the land. It forms in the Mediterranean region and travels eastward through West Asia and Iran into Pakistan and northwest India. Its moisture comes largely from the Mediterranean, the Caspian and the Black Seas, and in some events from the Arabian Sea as well. The system is steered by the subtropical westerly jet that lies over northern India in winter, as Lesson 15 explained.

The mechanism is an upper-air trough. In the mid and lower troposphere, the system appears as a trough or cyclonic circulation, and at the surface it often appears as a low-pressure area. Ahead of the trough, air is lifted, and the lift cools the moist air to saturation, producing cloud and precipitation. Beyond this step, the physics is more detailed. Atmospheric vorticity, the local rotation of air, is typically positive ahead of an upper trough, and the pattern of divergence aloft supports upward motion. This step is general atmospheric physics, offered as an explanation of the lift rather than as a fixed figure for any system.

The effects are the winter rain of the plains (recorded most often in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and Uttar Pradesh), the snowfall over the hills (Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand) and the cloudy, slightly warmer nights that cloud cover produces. The rain is valuable. It supplies the moisture for Rabi crops such as wheat, mustard and gram during their growing period, and it is one reason winter agriculture in the northwest is productive. The same system can overshoot. Intense or untimely rain and hail damage standing crops, and heavy snowfall or avalanches threaten the hills. A stronger western disturbance is not always better, because the same storm that brings useful rain can also destroy a harvest.

The winter calendar follows the jet. The subtropical jet lies over northern India in mid-winter, when western disturbances are most frequent and active over the northwest. In early winter the systems are fewer and the jet is farther north. In spring the jet begins to retreat north, and western disturbances become less frequent, so the rain season ends before the summer heat. The sequence from December through spring is therefore a movement of the jet and of the systems that ride it, not a fixed set of dates. The dates vary from year to year.

Two claims need caution. The first is that Arctic warming and jet waviness are linked to specific Indian extremes. This is an active research question, and it should be presented as a hypothesis. The second is that El Niño, the warm phase of the tropical Pacific, tends to weaken the southwest monsoon. It is a tendency, not a certainty, and it concerns the monsoon, not the western disturbance. The two should be kept apart.

Mission Mausam is a national programme for better observation, modelling and nowcasting of weather, and it is not the Ministry of Culture's Project Mausam. Its role is to improve the measurement and forecasting of systems such as the western disturbance, not to assert that the systems have changed.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

These checks combine lessons across each block. Answer each one before reading its model answer.

### Cumulative check A - energy to temperature (Lessons 1 to 3)

**Question:** A coastal station and an inland station at the same latitude receive equal insolation on a given day. Trace how their temperatures can still differ, from the energy budget to the isotherm pattern.

**Model answer:** The difference begins with the energy absorbed, not the energy arriving. Albedo and cloud reflect part of the incoming shortwave, and the surface type decides how much is absorbed. The coastal surface, which is water, has high specific heat, mixes its heat downward and loses energy by evaporation, so it changes temperature slowly. The inland land surface heats a thin skin and does not mix, so it swings widely. Over a season, the same contrast bends the isotherms, and the coast's annual range is smaller. The chain runs from absorbed energy, through surface properties, to the seasonal pattern.

### Cumulative check B - vertical structure and stability (Lessons 4 to 6)

**Question:** A layer near the ground is heated strongly on a sunny afternoon, but the air above it is colder than it would be on a normal day. Explain whether the layer is unstable, and what pressure pattern would follow.

**Model answer:** Heating from below only makes instability possible. The outcome depends on the environmental lapse rate compared with the dry rate of about 9.8 degrees per kilometre and the moist rate. If the air above is cold, the environmental lapse rate may be steep enough for instability, so the answer depends on the profile and not on heating alone. If the layer is warm and the environment cools slowly, the air remains stable. Warm air also reduces surface pressure, producing a thermal low, while cold dense air above produces a high. The pressure contrast then drives air along the gradient, as Lesson 6 showed.

### Cumulative check C - forces, belts, local winds and moisture (Lessons 7 to 10)

**Question:** Explain why air rising at the equator does not produce a surface wind that blows straight into the equatorial low, and how local winds and moisture can modify the pattern.

**Model answer:** Surface air converges toward the equatorial low, pushed by the pressure gradient. Coriolis deflects the flow, but at the equator it is near zero, so the convergence is nearly direct and the winds are light. Away from the equator, the trade winds turn under Coriolis, and the pattern is shaped by the belts of Lesson 8. Local winds such as sea breezes, mountain winds and Foehn winds modify the flow along coasts and slopes, as Lesson 9 showed. Moisture, from Lesson 10, controls whether the rising air condenses and how much rain falls. The chain is the gradient, the deflection, the belt, then local modification and condensation.

### Cumulative check D - severe systems and the upper air (Lessons 11 to 16)

**Question:** A single year brings a cloudburst in a Himalayan valley, a strong western disturbance over northwest India and an unusually persistent upper trough. Explain how these three events relate, and what separates them.

**Model answer:** They are linked by the upper-air circulation and separated by their mechanisms and scales. The upper trough is a jet-stream feature, and its persistence depends on the meanders of Lesson 15. The western disturbance is an extra-tropical trough, carried by the subtropical jet and fed by Mediterranean moisture, producing winter rain and snow, as in Lesson 16. The cloudburst is a small, intense convective event from monsoon-season moisture lifted by relief, as in Lesson 14. The upper trough can influence the other two by steering or stalling them, but the cloudburst does not require the western disturbance. The answer must separate scale, moisture source and mechanism rather than merging the three events.

## 15-mark practice 1 - distinguish three storms

**Question:** Distinguish a tropical cyclone, a tornado and a cloudburst by their energy source, their scale and their typical hazard, and explain why the planning response differs for each. (15 marks)

**Model answer, 15 marks:** A tropical cyclone draws its energy from latent heat released over a warm ocean, with a warm core and a scale of hundreds of kilometres. Its dominant hazards are storm surge and inland flooding, so the response is coastal evacuation and warning over a large area. A tornado is a land-based column spun up in a supercell, drawing energy from the thunderstorm's updraft and from shear. A cloudburst is a rate event, in which deep convection releases a large water load in about an hour over a small area; the hazard is runoff and debris flow on steep ground, so the response is land-use control on valley floors and fans. **Question:** Assess whether dense radar and automatic-station networks can substitute for land-use control in a cloudburst-prone Himalayan valley. (15 marks)

# REMEDIATION

### Misconception 1 - the atmosphere is heated mainly by sunlight passing through it
The surface absorbs shortwave first, emits longwave, and warms the air from below (Lesson 2).

### Misconception 2 - albedo and the greenhouse effect are the same process
Albedo reflects incoming shortwave; greenhouse trapping holds outgoing longwave. A cloud can do both (Lessons 2 and 11).

### Misconception 3 - a heated layer is automatically unstable
Stability compares the environmental lapse rate with the dry and moist rates; heating from below only makes instability possible (Lessons 4 and 5).

### Misconception 4 - an inversion is an instability, and every front is an inversion
An inversion is the extreme stable case. A front is a boundary between air masses; inversions occur only in some frontal settings (Lessons 5 and 12).

### Misconception 5 - high relative humidity means high moisture
Relative humidity measures closeness to saturation. Cold air can be saturated with little vapour (Lesson 10).

### Misconception 6 - Coriolis starts the wind, or acts at the equator
The pressure gradient starts the wind. Coriolis deflects it and vanishes at the equator (Lessons 6 and 7).

### Misconception 7 - trade winds blow away from the equator; the equatorial low is a high
Trades blow toward the equator; the Doldrums are a low where air rises (Lesson 8).

### Misconception 8 - the Loo is a Foehn wind because both are hot and dry
The Loo is a surface-heating wind. Foehn and Chinook share one descent mechanism. A dry Foehn can occur when little rain falls on the windward side (Lesson 9).

### Misconception 9 - a cloudburst is heavy rain, and a warmer sea makes more cyclones
A cloudburst is defined by rate over a small area; a warmer sea raises the intensity ceiling and rain rate, not the number of storms by itself (Lessons 13 and 14).

### Misconception 10 - Fujiwhara interaction always merges or strengthens storms
Outcomes range from orbit to separation. Track change is the reliable signal (Lesson 13).

### Misconception 11 - western disturbances are part of the monsoon
They are extra-tropical systems carried by the subtropical westerlies (Lesson 16).

# COMPLETE CONSOLIDATED REGISTER NOTES

**Weather and elements.** Weather is short-term; climate is the long-run average. The core elements are temperature, pressure, wind, humidity and precipitation, recorded with cloud.

**Energy budget.** Insolation is largest under the sun. Albedo reflects shortwave; greenhouse trapping holds longwave. The atmosphere is warmed mainly from below.

**Temperature and the surface.** Isotherms join places of equal temperature. Land changes temperature quickly; water slowly. January isotherms bend toward the equator over cold land and toward the pole over warm ocean.

**Vertical atmosphere and stability.** The environmental lapse rate averages about 6.5 degrees per kilometre; a dry parcel cools at about 9.8. The tropopause is higher in the tropics. Stability compares the environmental rate with the parcel rates; an inversion is strongly stable.

**Pressure, forces and wind.** The pressure gradient points from high to low. Coriolis deflects right in the Northern Hemisphere, is zero at the equator, and balances the gradient aloft. Friction at the surface lets the wind cross the isobars.

**Global circulation and local winds.** Three cells form the belts. Trades blow toward the equator; westerlies toward the poles. Sea and land breezes reverse daily. Foehn and Chinook warm by descent; the Loo is a surface-heating wind.

**Moisture, clouds and rain.** Relative humidity measures closeness to saturation; the dew point is reached by cooling. Thick low cloud cools by day; thin high cloud warms, especially at night. Rain needs lift.

**Air masses and fronts.** Air masses take their character from their source regions. Warm fronts bring extensive stratiform cloud; cold fronts bring narrow tall cloud.

**Tropical cyclones and Fujiwhara.** A warm-core heat engine fed by latent heat. Genesis needs warm water through a deep layer, latitude beyond about 5 degrees, low shear, a disturbance and moist mid-levels. Mutual steering is counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere.

**Severe convection.** Cloudbursts are a rate criterion over a small area; a working figure of about 100 millimetres per hour is widely used in India. Tornadoes need moisture, instability, a cap, shear and a trigger.

**Jets and western disturbances.** Jets form where temperature contrast is steepest. The subtropical jet steers western disturbances over northern India in winter. These systems bring winter rain to the plains and snow to the hills, supporting Rabi crops and sometimes damaging them.
