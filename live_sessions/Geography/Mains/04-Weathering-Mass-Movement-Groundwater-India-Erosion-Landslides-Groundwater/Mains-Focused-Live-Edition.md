# Weathering, Mass Movement and Groundwater / India Erosion, Landslides and Groundwater — Mains-Focused Live Edition

This topic links four dependent systems: rock weathering, soil development/degradation, slope
movement and groundwater. Weathering can create residual material and secondary porosity; soil and
slope response then depend on relief, drainage, structure and land use; aquifer behaviour depends on
connected pores, fractures, conduits and hydraulic boundaries.

Verified demands include urban water harvesting, Himalayan–Western Ghats landslide causation,
black-soil parent material, groundwater-withdrawal statistics, rainfall chemistry and
porosity/permeability. Objective references remain answer-neutral.

**Current evidence cutoff: 5 October 2026.** Current controls are USDA-NRCS Soil Taxonomy,
ICAR-NBSS&LUP soil mapping, GSI Bhusanket, NRSC/ISRO's *Landslide Atlas of India*, USDMA's
Joshimath report repository, CGWB GWRA-2025 and NAQUIM 2.0, WDC-PMKSY 2.0 and dated Atal Jal
implementation evidence. Book-era classifications and figures are used only as historiography where
their date and limitations matter.

**Scope boundary.** The lessons teach physical mechanisms, Indian spatial application and bounded
land-use measures. Detailed disaster administration, groundwater pricing/procurement, coastal
regulation, river interlinking, wetland ecology, desertification treaties, karst-landform inventories,
liquefaction and avalanche doctrine require their respective evidence bases.

## Lesson 1 — Denudation, weathering and the vocabulary an examiner can actually check

These processes interact and may occur simultaneously; the historical four-phase sequence is a useful
teaching scheme, not a compulsory temporal order.

### 1. The question this lesson answers, and why it must come first

⚠️ Surface exposure changes temperature, pressure, water, gas and biological conditions. Minerals and
rock fabrics respond differently; not every exposed rock formed at great depth.

⚠️ Granite illustrates re-equilibration well: unloading can open sheets/joints, oxygen permits
oxidation and water drives hydrolysis. Sedimentary and volcanic rocks require their own mineral,
fabric and exposure histories.

For answer writing, derive the dominant process from mineralogy, water, temperature, structure,
drainage and exposure history rather than assigning one process mechanically to each climate.

### 2. Denudation's four phases, and what each phase contributes

✅ Leong's four-part denudation scheme—weathering, erosion, transportation and deposition—is retained
as historiography. Modern usage often includes transport within erosion and defines regolith more
broadly as unconsolidated material above bedrock, whether residual or transported.

⚠️ Each phase adds exactly one thing, and naming what it adds is the discipline:

| Phase | What it adds that the previous phase did not | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Weathering** | In-situ physical/chemical alteration | Did alteration occur before removal? |
| ✅ **Erosion** | Detachment and removal; transport commonly follows | Was material removed from its source? |
| ✅ **Transportation** | Movement by water, wind, ice, waves or gravity | How and how far did it move? |
| ✅ **Deposition** | **Arrest** — the agent loses competence and the load comes to rest | Has the material stopped, and begun to accumulate? |

⚠️ Mass movement is gravity-driven downslope transfer. Water may add mass and raise pore pressure.
Some geomorphological conventions include mass movement within erosion; this topic distinguishes it
when analysing slope mechanics.

### 3. The four-way separation

Keep distinct: weathering/erosion; movement/material; porosity/permeability; aquifer/aquitard;
water table/potentiometric surface; susceptibility/hazard/exposure/vulnerability/risk.

⚠️ The first three can be fixed with a single worked example rather than three definitions. Take a jointed Himalayan sandstone cliff above a road cutting:

- **Weathering** is the freeze-thaw cycle opening the joints over successive winters. Nothing has yet moved.
- **Mass movement** is the block, once detached, falling to the road under gravity. No agent carried it.
- **Erosion** is the monsoon stream at the foot of the cliff detaching and carrying away the shattered debris, and simultaneously **undercutting the toe** so that the next block is less supported than the last.
- **Denudation** is the broader lowering of relief by interacting weathering, erosion and mass transfer.

❗ The processes are not mutually exclusive and need not follow one fixed order.

### 4. What "in situ" actually excludes, and the two errors it prevents

⚠️ *In situ* is doing more work than it appears to. It excludes two things.

❌ **First error: treating any surface breakdown as weathering.** A boulder rounded by abrasion in a river bed has been **eroded**, not weathered; the rounding was done by impact during transport. ⚠️ The test is positional — was the material at rest on its parent when it broke down?

❌ **Second error: treating weathering as merely preparatory.** Residual parent material can undergo
pedogenesis, and weathering can create storage/transmission zones in hard rock; neither outcome is
automatic or spatially uniform.

### 5. The scale control, which is a separate discipline

⚠️ Scale control: grain/pore, profile, hillslope, catchment, aquifer and assessment unit are distinct.
National groundwater categories cannot diagnose one well or slope.

### 6. India hook — Deccan basalt weathering and transfer

🇮🇳 Deccan landscapes show residual basalt weathering, erosion on scarps and sediment transfer into
valleys. Black soils are strongly associated with basaltic parent material, but are not simply
downstream deposits of one plateau sequence.

❗ This is a process illustration, not a measured sediment budget or a claim that basalt behaves
uniformly across the province.

### Comparison, objection and the residual

| | What it explains | What it does not explain |
|---|---|---|
| Denudation's four-phase scheme | Why loose material exists, how it travels and where it stops | **Rates** — the scheme is a sequence, not a budget, and no phase in it carries a velocity |
| The in-situ criterion | Why weathering is a distinct process with its own products | The **depth** to which it reaches, which needs the controls of Lesson 4 |
| The agent-versus-gravity test | Why mass movement is neither weathering nor erosion | Why a particular slope fails **when** it does; that is pore-water pressure, at Lesson 10 |

**Strongest objection.** This is vocabulary policing. An examiner marks substance, not whether a candidate wrote "erosion" where a purist would have written "weathering".

**Reply.** The objection would hold if the terms were synonyms with different registers. They are not: they identify **different physical operations with different controls, different geographies and different policy responses**. ⚠️ The cost of conflating them is concrete and shows up in answers. If erosion and weathering are the same thing, then afforestation and terracing look like weathering controls, which they are not — they control **detachment and transport**, and they barely touch the rate of in-situ decay. ❗ Worse, the conflation destroys the topic's best argument, because the claim *"weathering manufactures the aquifer"* is only intelligible if weathering is understood as the process that **leaves material where it is**. An eroded mantle is not a reservoir; it is a sediment load.

⚠️ **Residual.** The boundary between weathering and erosion is clean in principle and genuinely fuzzy at the margin. **Rainsplash** detaches grains from a surface that is simultaneously decaying chemically; **biotic prising** by a root both breaks the rock and displaces the fragment. ❗ The sources do not resolve these cases, and an honest answer treats the four-phase scheme as an **analytical separation of a continuous process**, not as four observable stages with sharp edges in the field.

- **Traps:** calling a river-rounded boulder "weathered"; describing weathering as preparation for erosion and stopping there; treating mass movement as a kind of erosion; mixing scales by diagnosing a district with a national figure; using "denudation" as a grand synonym for erosion.

Revision notes:

- Leong's four-part denudation sequence is historical, not a compulsory order.
- **Regolith** includes residual and transported unconsolidated material above bedrock.
- Weathering alters material in place; not every rock formed at great depth.

### Concept check

**Question:** A candidate writes: "Denudation means erosion. The Himalayan rivers denude the mountains by eroding them and depositing the sediment in the plains." Identify what is wrong, and give a corrected statement of the same length that would earn marks.

**Model answer:** Denudation is broader than river erosion: it describes landscape lowering through
interacting weathering, erosion and mass transfer. Weathering alters rock in place; rivers detach,
remove and transport material; gravity can move rock or debris downslope; deposition occurs where
transport capacity falls. These processes need not follow one compulsory order. A corrected statement
is: “Himalayan relief is lowered by in-situ weathering, gravity-driven slope movement and fluvial
erosion; rivers transport part of the resulting load and deposit it downstream.”

**Misconception to avoid:** Do not treat "denudation" as a formal synonym for "erosion" merely because both describe wearing down. The difference is that denudation includes the in-situ phase, and it is precisely that phase which produces soil and the weathered aquifer — the two things this topic is mostly about.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "Weathering is not merely the first step towards erosion." Examine. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** Weathering is more than preparation for erosion. It changes mineralogy
and fabric in place, creates residual parent material for pedogenesis and can develop secondary
porosity in hard rock. Erosion may later remove that material, but on stable surfaces organisms,
organic matter and water movement can organise it into soil. In crystalline terrain, weathered
saprolite may store groundwater while fractures transmit it. These outcomes are not automatic:
relief, drainage, structure, climate and time determine whether material is retained, stripped or
hydrologically connected. Weathering therefore supplies sediment, soil parent material and part of
some aquifer systems, while erosion and mass movement govern subsequent transfer.

**Rubric (quantified, 10 marks):** 2 for weathering defined as in-situ with regolith named as its product; 2 for the preparatory framing conceded as valid where debris is removed; 3 for both independent products — soil and the weathered aquifer — stated as outcomes of the same process; 2 for relief identified as the variable that selects between the outcomes; 1 for a graded rather than absolute verdict. Deduct 2 for defining weathering by reference to erosion alone, and 1 for using "denudation" as a synonym for erosion.

### 1. Plain intuition before the terminology

⚠️ Mechanical weathering includes several mechanisms: ice or salt growth can exert stress, thermal
gradients can fatigue surfaces, unloading can open sheet joints, and roots can widen fractures.
Block/granular disintegration also reflects inherited fabric. Tensile fracture is common, but expansion
is not one universal mechanism.

Physical disintegration works through several mechanisms: thermal stress/fatigue, freeze-thaw, unloading
and stress release, salt-crystal growth, wetting-drying or shrink-swell in susceptible materials, and
biotic wedging. Ask which stress or structural change operates under the local moisture, temperature,
rock-fabric and exposure conditions; do not reduce every process to internal expansion.

### 2. Frost action, and the figure that must be corrected as it is quoted

✅ The basic canonical states the physics plainly: **water expands by about 9 per cent on freezing** and can prise apart jointed, saturated rock. ✅ Majid Husain places the process in its climatic home: frost action is powerful in **humid micro-thermal, sub-arctic, polar and high-altitude** climates.

⚠️ Three preconditions must all hold, and naming them is what separates an explanation from a label: **water must be present**, **the temperature must cross the freezing point repeatedly** rather than merely be low, and **the rock must be jointed or porous enough to admit the water**. ❗ A permanently frozen, permanently dry or unjointed rock is not frost-shattered. This is why the process peaks in the **shoulder seasons and at the diurnal scale**, not in the depth of winter, and why high-altitude India is a better frost-weathering environment than much of the Arctic.

⚠️ Water expands by about **9 per cent** on freezing. No universal frost-pressure figure is used:
fracture geometry, water supply, confinement and repeated cycles control effectiveness.

### 3. Insolation weathering, exfoliation and the long-running doubt

⚠️ Thermal cycling can contribute to surface flaking and granular breakdown, commonly with moisture
and salts. Large exfoliation domes/sheet joints are better explained primarily by unloading and
topography-parallel fracturing than by daily heating alone. Scree is a slope deposit, not a unique
product of exfoliation.

⚠️ **The honest qualification, and it is worth a mark.** Classic laboratory work found that pure temperature cycling of **dry** rock produced disappointingly little breakdown, and that adding even small amounts of moisture changed the result sharply. ⚠️ The modern reading is therefore that insolation weathering in deserts is usually **assisted by moisture — dew, occasional rain and salt-bearing solutions — rather than purely thermal**. ❗ State it as a qualification, not as a refutation: the process is real, its purely thermal version is weaker than textbooks imply, and the practical consequence is that **desert weathering is less exclusively mechanical than the simple scheme suggests**.

⚠️ Use scale and structure: small flakes/spalls can record surface stress; metre-scale sheets parallel
to topography commonly record unloading and joint development.

### 4. The two processes that climate does not select

⚠️ **Block and granular disintegration are selected by the rock, not by the sky.** Whether a rock breaks into rectangular blocks or into a sandy residue depends on whether it has a **through-going joint grid** or a **coarse mixed-mineral fabric**. A well-jointed basalt yields blocks in the Deccan and blocks in Iceland. A coarse granite yields grit in Rajasthan and grit in Malaysia.

⚠️ **Pressure release is selected by erosion history.** The sheet joints that split exhumed granite are a record of how much overburden has been removed, which is a statement about the **denudation history** of Lesson 1, not about the current climate. ❗ This is the cleanest available demonstration that weathering has **structural and historical controls alongside climatic ones** — which is exactly the six-control argument of Lesson 4, previewed here so that it does not arrive unprepared.

### 5. India hook — the Himalayan freeze-thaw belt against the Thar, one country, two regimes

🇮🇳 High-Himalayan zones with liquid water and repeated freezing can favour frost cracking. In the
Thar, thermal stress and salt weathering can contribute where moisture/salts are available. Neither
setting has one exclusive product.

❗ **Limitation, stated with the hook.** ⚠️ The contrast is a **regime statement, not a rate comparison**: no source available to this topic gives a weathering rate for either setting, and none is asserted. ❗ Nor is the Himalayan case a complete account of Himalayan slope instability — frost shattering supplies the **material**, while Lessons 10 and 12 supply the **mechanics and the causes**, and attributing a landslide to frost action alone would commit exactly the single-trigger error this topic spends Lesson 12 correcting.

### Comparison, objection and the residual

| | Mechanical weathering | Chemical weathering (Lesson 3) |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | **Size and surface area**; mineralogy is unchanged | **Mineralogy itself**; new minerals and dissolved ions are produced |
| Climatic tendency | Freeze-thaw where water cycles through freezing; salt/thermal stress where conditions permit | Commonly rapid in warm, moist, well-drained settings |
| Relationship | **Prepares the ground** for chemical attack by multiplying surface area | **Accelerates mechanical failure** by weakening grain boundaries |
| Depth reached | Shallow; it follows joints and the surface | Can reach tens of metres where the regolith is deep (Lesson 4) |

**Strongest objection.** The division into mechanical and chemical weathering is artificial. In the field the two always occur together, so teaching them apart teaches a fiction.

**Reply.** The objection is correct as a description of the field and wrong as a conclusion about method. ⚠️ The two **do** always occur together, and they are **mutually reinforcing** — mechanical breakdown multiplies the surface area available for chemical attack, while chemical decay of grain boundaries lowers the strength the mechanical processes must overcome. ❗ But they have **different climatic controls and different products**, and those differences are what make the geography of weathering predictable at all. A candidate who cannot separate them cannot explain why the Himalayan debris is angular and the Malabar regolith is a deep clay, and that explanation is the examinable content.

⚠️ **Residual.** The relative contribution of purely thermal cycling to desert weathering is **genuinely unsettled**, and the textbook account is stronger than the experimental evidence supports. ❗ The correct posture is to teach insolation weathering as real but moisture-assisted, and to avoid asserting that deserts weather "mechanically" without qualification.

### 1. Plain intuition before terminology

⚠️ Chemical weathering is easier than its vocabulary suggests. **Minerals formed at high temperature and pressure, in the absence of free oxygen and liquid water, are unstable when they meet all three.** The hotter a mineral formed, the less stable it is at the surface — which is why olivine and calcium feldspar decay fastest and quartz, which crystallises last and coolest, survives to become sand. ⚠️ Chemical weathering is therefore the rock's **return journey**: minerals move back towards the low-temperature, hydrated, oxidised forms that are stable where we live.

❗ That single idea predicts three things an answer can use. **One:** the residue of intense chemical weathering is quartz plus clay plus iron and aluminium oxides, because those are what is left when everything less stable has gone — which is the composition of laterite. **Two:** chemical weathering must be strongest where water is abundant and warm, because both accelerate reaction. **Three:** it leaves a **different product** from mechanical weathering — not smaller pieces of the same rock, but **new minerals**.

### 2. The four reactions, as the sources state them

✅ The basic canonical and Majid Husain both give **four** chemical processes: **oxidation, carbonation, solution and hydrolysis**. Each is set out below as a transformation.

**Oxidation.** Oxidation-state changes in iron-bearing minerals contribute red/yellow staining and can
weaken altered material. Colour alone does not establish a fixed oxide composition or taxonomic class.

✅ **Carbonation.** Water combined with carbon dioxide forms **weak carbonic acid**, which dissolves carbonate minerals, producing **limestone dissolution**. ⚠️ The reaction converts insoluble calcium carbonate into soluble calcium bicarbonate, and the bicarbonate is then **carried away in solution**. ⚠️ Where that solution later evaporates, the carbonate is **redeposited** — which is the mechanism of calcification and the origin of the **kankar** nodules that Lesson 7 finds in older alluvium and in black soil.

✅ **Solution.** Soluble substances are dissolved directly and their **ions are removed in water**. ⚠️ Halite, gypsum and nitrates go straight into solution; carbonates need carbonation's acid first. ❗ Solution is the mechanism behind the **salt efflorescences** of Lesson 9 — except that there the solution moves **upward** by capillary action and evaporates at the surface instead of being exported.

✅ **Hydrolysis.** Water reacts with susceptible silicate minerals; feldspars commonly alter to clay
minerals while dissolved constituents are released. It is important in many continental weathering
profiles, but no single reaction “manufactures” every regolith or aquifer.

### 3. Why rainwater is naturally acidic — the step the 2024 demand turns on

⚠️ Rainwater is not neutral before it reaches the rock, and the reason is not pollution. **Atmospheric carbon dioxide dissolves in falling rain to form carbonic acid**, so rain arrives mildly acidic in the cleanest air on Earth. ⚠️ It then becomes **more** acidic, because soil air is strongly enriched in carbon dioxide by root respiration and microbial decay, so water percolating through a soil picks up far more carbon dioxide than it carried from the sky. ⚠️ Organic acids released by decaying litter add to this.

This explains why soil water can intensify chemical alteration along some flow paths through dissolved
carbon dioxide and organic ligands. Effects still depend on infiltration, oxygen, drainage and material;
chemical weathering may occur throughout a profile rather than at one compulsory surface.

⚠️ **Prelims precision target (answer-neutral).** The 2024 GS-I objective demand concerns rainfall,
rainwater composition and weathering. Revisit natural and soil-zone carbon chemistry and distinguish
rainfall amount from chemistry. No statement verdict, option mapping or key is supplied here.

### 4. The corrections, declared rather than merged

❌ **Correction — limestone does not melt during weathering.** Carbonate dissolution transfers ions
into water; later degassing, evaporation or mixing may precipitate carbonate again. The precipitate can
be the same mineral species with a different texture or setting, so “different mineral” is not the test.

❌ ✅ **Correction 2 — Leong's three-fold scheme.** G.C. Leong gives **three** chemical processes — solution, oxidation and decomposition by organic acids — and does not separately list carbonation or hydrolysis. ❗ **Teach four.** ⚠️ Leong's scheme is named here as an **older and narrower classification**, not as an error: it folds carbonation inside solution and treats hydrolysis implicitly within organic decomposition. ❗ Quote it as that author's scheme if quoting it at all, and do not present three and four as competing current positions.

❌ ✅ **Correction 3 — hydration's disputed home.** Majid Husain classifies hydration as physical.
Modern treatments commonly classify mineral hydration as chemical because water enters the mineral
structure, although the resulting expansion can also cause mechanical disruption.

### 5. India hook — laterite and Buchanan's 1807 Malabar account

🇮🇳 Francis Buchanan described and named laterite in his **1807** Malabar journey. Lateritic profiles
reflect strong tropical weathering, leaching/desilication and relative iron–aluminium enrichment; they
vary by parent material, drainage, landscape position and duration.

⚠️ Some lateritic materials harden strongly on exposure/drying and have long been quarried as building
stone; this is not a universal property of every mapped “laterite soil”.

❗ Strong leaching is central; seasonal drying can favour hardening, but a compulsory wet–dry
alternation and a fixed rainfall threshold are not universal definitions.

### 1. The question, and why it is the most productive in the weathering block

⚠️ The same rock can develop contrasting profiles because structure, water/temperature, relief,
organisms, exposure time and land use interact.

❗ These controls explain local aquifer and soil variation; they do not generate one deterministic

### 2. The six controls

The six-factor book framework remains useful, but modern control treats all six as interacting and
does not rank climate as universally decisive.

⚠️ Each control is worth one precise sentence rather than a paragraph:

- **Rock structure and hardness.** ⚠️ The operative variable is **joint density**, not hardness in the scratch sense. Joints are the pathways by which water reaches the interior, so a closely jointed hard rock weathers faster than a massive soft one. ❗ This is the control that produces corestones in section 4 below, and the control that writes Lesson 16's province map.
- **Climate.** Temperature and moisture influence reaction and transport, but do not select one
exclusive family everywhere.
- **Topography and slope.** ⚠️ A **steep slope exports the regolith**, continually exposing fresh rock and keeping the mantle thin; a **gentle interfluve retains it**, so the profile deepens and the weathering front is progressively insulated from fresh water. ❗ This is why deep regoliths are found on **plateau surfaces and gentle pediments**, not on scarps — a fact Lesson 16 uses directly.
- **Natural vegetation.** ✅ Treated in section 3, because its effect inverts the obvious expectation.
- **Time.** Longer exposure can deepen alteration where erosion, uplift and drainage permit; depth is
not a simple clock.
- **Land use by man.** ✅ Named by the source as a control in its own right, which is unusual and useful. ⚠️ Quarrying and road cutting expose fresh rock; tillage mixes and aerates; irrigation changes the moisture regime and introduces salts; deforestation strips the cover that control four describes. ❗ This is also the control that makes Lesson 8's human factors and Lesson 12's man-made causes continuous with the weathering lesson rather than an afterthought.

### 3. Soil cover and the weathering front

Soil can enrich percolating water in carbon dioxide and organic ligands, but it can also limit
infiltration, oxygen or renewal where horizons are clay-rich or poorly drained. The effect is conditional.

⚠️ Lesson 3 supplied the mechanism, and it is worth restating as a chain because the chain is the answer: **vegetation → litter and roots → respiration and decay → soil air strongly enriched in carbon dioxide and organic acids → percolating water is far more aggressive than the rain that fell → the weathering front at the base of the profile is attacked by the most chemically loaded water in the system.**

❗ Three consequences follow, and each is examinable.

- ⚠️ Chemical alteration occurs throughout profiles, often strongly near reactive fronts and flow paths.
- ⚠️ Profile development is not automatically self-deepening; drainage, erosion and supply of fresh
reactants constrain it.
- ⚠️ **Removing the vegetation does not protect the rock; it changes which process attacks it.** Chemical attack falls, but detachment and transport rise sharply — which is Lesson 8's entire subject.

### 4. Differential weathering, joint density and corestones

✅ Leong's **Fig. 4.1** shows the structural control working at outcrop scale: **differential weathering produces corestones, and the control is joint density.** ⚠️ The mechanism is geometric. Water enters along joints, so a joint-bounded block is attacked from **all its faces and edges at once**. Edges and corners have more exposed surface per unit volume than faces, so they decay fastest, and the block is progressively **rounded from the outside in**. Where joints were closely spaced, the whole block is consumed and becomes part of the clay-rich matrix; where joints were widely spaced, an unweathered **kernel survives** at the centre. ⚠️ Those kernels are corestones, and a field of them emerging from a stripped regolith is the origin of the boulder-strewn landscapes of weathered granite terrain.

Leong’s Tampin, Negri Sembilan example illustrates differential granite weathering: feldspar alteration
can leave a quartz-rich sandy residue. Stable warm-moist surfaces may develop deep regolith, subject to
drainage, erosion and structure.

Profiles can record additions, losses, transformations and translocations. Eluviation–illuviation is
one important pair, not the explanation of every A and B horizon.

### 1. Plain intuition before terminology

⚠️ Soil develops from residual or transported parent material through additions, losses,
transformations, translocations and biological activity. A strongly differentiated horizon sequence is
not required before a young alluvial material can function as soil.

❗ Soil is not merely crushed rock, but horizon differentiation is not a universal threshold.

✅ Khullar supplies the definition and the name: soil is the thin surface layer formed by rock break-down plus decayed organic material, living organisms, water and air, and the process is **pedogenesis**.

### 2. The soil as a system, and its four components

✅ Majid Husain treats soil as a **system with four components** — mineral matter, organic matter, water and air — and this framing is more useful than a definition because it makes the failure modes obvious. ⚠️ If the air fraction is displaced by water, the system goes anaerobic, which is the gleying of waterlogged ground and Lesson 7's peaty and marshy soils. If the organic fraction falls, structure collapses and the mineral fraction is exposed to detachment, which is Lesson 8. If the water fraction carries salts upward instead of downward, the system salinises, which is Lesson 9.

✅ **Humus** is the decomposed, dark, colloidal fraction of the organic component, and it is doing disproportionate work: it supplies nutrient-holding capacity, binds mineral particles into aggregates, and holds water. ⚠️ Organic matter reflects productivity, decomposition, drainage, fire and management. Many old,
highly leached equatorial upland soils store limited readily available nutrients, but volcanic,
alluvial and young soils are important exceptions.

### 3. The horizons, and the transfer that makes them

Read the horizon sequence as interacting transfers rather than a fixed list:

| Horizon | ✅ Content | ⚠️ What the arrows do here | ✅ Exam significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **O** | Surface organic material | Addition/decomposition/removal | Thickness varies widely |
| **A** | Mineral matter mixed with organic matter | May show eluviation | Agriculturally important surface mineral horizon |
| **E** | Strongly leached, pale | Maximum eluviation | **Diagnostic of podzolisation** under cool, wet, acidic conditions |
| **B** | Subsurface horizon modified by pedogenesis | May show illuviation or in-place alteration | Diagnose from properties, not one assumed process |
| **C** | Weathered parent material | Little transfer; this is Lesson 4's regolith | **Links the soil to its rock** |
| **R** | Unweathered bedrock | None | The base of the profile |

⚠️ Eluviation removes material from an eluvial horizon; illuviation accumulates it elsewhere. E
horizons express eluviation most clearly.

Three implications follow. Horizons are linked by transfers, surface erosion disproportionately removes
biologically active topsoil, and upward capillary transport can dominate where evaporative demand and
a shallow water table favour salt accumulation.

### 4. The pedon, texture and structure — three precision tools

✅ **The pedon** is the smallest volume that can properly be called a soil: a three-dimensional column extending from the surface down to the parent material, and large enough in plan to display the full horizon sequence and the lateral variability within it. ⚠️ The concept matters because it fixes the **scale** at which a soil is an object — below it there are only particles, above it there are soil associations and map units. ❗ This is Lesson 1's scale control applied to soil.

✅ **Texture** is the proportion of sand, silt and clay, and Majid Husain presents it through the **USDA soil texture triangle**, dated **1975**, with a worked example of a **Miami silt loam from Indiana**. ⚠️ The triangle is a genuinely useful visual because texture controls four properties at once — water retention, drainage, aeration and workability — and because it shows that the named classes are **regions of a continuum**, not natural kinds.

⚠️ Black soils commonly plot in clay-rich texture classes, but exact clay percentage and hydraulic
behaviour vary by profile; no one book figure controls the group.

✅ **Structure** is the aggregation of particles into **peds** — the units that give a soil its crumb, blocky, platy or prismatic character. ⚠️ Structure is the property that **management changes fastest**: organic-matter loss destroys aggregates, tillage shatters them, and raindrop impact on a bare surface seals the surface into a crust that sheds water. ❗ Lesson 8's sheet erosion begins there, which is why structure is taught here and not in an agronomy file.

### 5. Why soil is the hinge between physical and economic geography

⚠️ Soil properties influence agricultural possibilities but do not determine population density;
water, technology, markets and history intervene.

The usable chain is: **parent material plus environmental controls → pedogenic processes → soil
properties → conditional land-use implications**. Human choices and technology prevent deterministic
crop or settlement conclusions.

❗ **The necessary qualification, and it is where the marks are.** ⚠️ **Soil sets probabilities, not destiny.** Irrigation, fertiliser, terracing and drainage repeatedly override the natural suitability — at a cost, and often at the cost of the degradation pathways of Lesson 9. ❗ An answer that treats soil as determinative has over-claimed; an answer that states the probability and then names the overriding technology and its cost has given the examiner both halves.

### 6. India hook — the tarai, where a high water table rewrites the profile

🇮🇳 ✅ The tarai belt along the Himalayan foot is the cleanest Indian demonstration that **the water regime, not the parent material, can control the profile**. ✅ Khullar's groundwater account supplies the mechanism: in the **bhabar** belt, streams emerging from the mountains **disappear into unassorted, coarse, highly permeable material**; downslope, in the **tarai**, that water **seeps out again and the water table is high**.

⚠️ The pedological consequence is direct. ⚠️ Where the water table stands within the profile, percolation is impeded and the downward arrows of this lesson's visual are truncated; the lower horizons remain saturated and anaerobic, iron is chemically reduced rather than oxidised, and the profile develops the grey-blue, mottled character the source names as **gleying**. ⚠️ Organic matter accumulates because decay is slowed by the lack of oxygen, which is the direction that at its extreme produces Lesson 7's peaty and marshy soils. ❗ Two adjacent belts on the same parent material, differing only in their water table, therefore produce very different soils — which is the five-factor model demonstrated under controlled conditions.

❗ **Limitation, stated with the hook.** ⚠️ The pedological description above is an **inference from the hydrology that the source states**, not a soil-survey description. The bhabar–tarai contrast is a hydrological boundary with soil and spring consequences; aquifer productivity still varies locally.

### 2. Broad groups, honestly qualified

Use broad groups for regional explanation, not as a modern formal taxonomy.

Regional terms for saline/sodic-affected land include **reh, kallar, usar** and **thur**. Near-surface

irrigation-induced salinity require separate diagnosis.

sufficiently shallow water table. Irrigation/seepage without adequate drainage can create or intensify
those conditions, but it is not the only cause.

Indian degradation includes erosion, organic-matter/nutrient decline, waterlogging, salinity/sodicity,

### Concept check

**Question:** A planner proposes extending canal irrigation into a desert-soil tract on the argument that the soil's only limitation is water. Using the soil's recorded properties, state what the proposal gets right, what it misses, and what single engineering provision would decide the outcome.

**Model answer:** Irrigation can relieve moisture limitation, but outcome depends on soil texture,
initial salinity/sodicity, water quality, seepage, drainage and evaporative demand. Canal leakage or

salts. The decisive provision is not irrigation alone but drainage and salt balance, supported by
monitoring. Sandy material may infiltrate readily yet still face salinity where outlets are poor.
The proposal is therefore conditionally valid, not universally beneficial.

**Misconception to avoid:** Salinisation is a transport/balance outcome; irrigation can contribute but
does not establish the diagnosis or sole cause.

**Original Mains practice [10 marks]:** "India's soil map is, to a first approximation, a map of its rocks." Examine. *(Examine — answer in about 150 words.)*

**Model (about 150 words):** Parent material strongly influences mineralogy and texture where it is

hypothesis, not a deterministic national rule.

```text
EROSION PROCESSES — RELATED, NOT A COMPULSORY LADDER

raindrop detachment + diffuse runoff -> sheet loss
concentrated shallow flow           -> rills
persistent incision/headward growth -> gullies
coalesced dissection                -> ravines/badlands

```

*Caption: classify the active process and evidence; do not infer severity from the most visible form.*

### 1. Comparative definition

Soil degradation by erosion occurs when removal outpaces profile renewal and acceptable land function.
Diffuse sheet loss can be serious without gullies because it selectively removes fine particles,
organic matter and surface structure. Repeat measurements, runoff/sediment evidence, profiles and
remote sensing are stronger than a single visual inspection.

### 2. Water erosion

Rainfall intensity, antecedent moisture, surface sealing, runoff volume, slope length/gradient and flow
concentration interact. Rills may be erased by tillage; gullies persist and grow by incision, sidewall
failure and headward erosion. Ravines are deeply dissected terrain, not a universally fixed depth class.
Chambal–Yamuna badlands and Shiwalik *chos* are regional examples; historical area/rate estimates are
not used as current control.

### 3. Wind erosion

Suspension carries very fine particles; saltation moves sand in short hops and can dislodge other
grains; surface creep rolls coarser particles. Moisture, aggregation and cover constrain all three.
Do not reproduce a precise national “limit of wind erosion” from an old textbook sketch as a current
boundary.

### 4. Off-site and cross-system effects

Eroded sediment may silt reservoirs/channels, increase turbidity, block drainage and damage downstream
land. Surface sealing or land-cover change can alter infiltration, but erosion does not automatically
lower every water table or cause every landslide. Toe/gully erosion can destabilise particular slopes;
catchment and geological evidence are required.

### 5. India map use

A defensible sketch may locate the Chambal–Yamuna ravine tract, Shiwalik *chos*, north-eastern areas
where shifting cultivation can expose slopes, and the arid north-west for wind erosion. Boundaries
should remain schematic unless supported by a current mapped product.

### Concept check

**Question:** A district has no gullies. Can an officer conclude that erosion is minor?

**Model answer:** No. Sheet erosion can remove the fine, organic-rich surface layer diffusely and leave
no dramatic channels. Rills may also be removed by tillage between inspections. Severity requires
repeat profile/surface measurements, runoff and sediment evidence, land-cover history and imagery.
Gully absence only rules out one visible form; it does not measure total soil loss or functional decline.

**Misconception to avoid:** Visibility is not a reliable ranking of erosion severity.

### Revision notes

- Erosion is problematic when removal exceeds renewal/acceptable function.
- Sheet, rill, gully and ravine are related forms, not a compulsory sequence.
- Rainfall intensity and runoff concentration matter; annual total alone is insufficient.
- Rills may be temporary; gullies persist and may grow headward.
- Ravines/badlands have no universal depth threshold.
- Wind transport: suspension, saltation and surface creep.
- Cover, moisture and aggregation constrain wind erosion.
- Chambal–Yamuna ravines and Shiwalik *chos* are regional examples.
- Off-site effects include sedimentation, turbidity and drainage blockage.
- Groundwater/landslide links are conditional, not automatic.
- Obsolete national totals and precise textbook boundaries are excluded.

### Mains Practice 8

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why soil-erosion assessment must combine process and scale.

**Model (about 150 words):** Erosion appears as diffuse sheet loss, rills, gullies, ravines or wind
transport, each requiring different evidence and treatment. A field visit may miss cumulative sheet
loss, while a national sketch cannot diagnose a farm or headcut. Assessment should link rainfall/wind
energy, cover, erodibility, moisture, slope and flow concentration to repeat profiles, runoff/sediment
measurements and imagery. Off-site sediment effects also occur beyond the eroding plot. Therefore
regional maps screen patterns, catchment analysis traces sediment pathways and site surveys design
interventions. Unsupported legacy national totals or universal thresholds create false precision.

```text
DIAGNOSIS -> MECHANISM -> RESPONSE

erosion       -> runoff/cover/slope pathway -> cover + contour/catchment treatment
waterlogging  -> recharge exceeds drainage  -> drainage + irrigation/seepage management
salinity      -> soluble salts accumulate    -> drainage + suitable-quality leaching
sodicity      -> exchangeable Na disperses   -> calcium amendment where tested + drainage/leaching
nutrient/SOC  -> removals exceed renewal     -> biomass/organic inputs + balanced nutrients
```

*Caption: similar surface symptoms can require different remedies; laboratory and hydrological evidence matter.*

### 1. Salinity is not sodicity

Saline soils contain excessive soluble salts; sodic soils have excessive exchangeable sodium and often
dispersed structure. Electrical conductivity, pH, exchangeable-sodium indicators and water quality
distinguish them. Drainage and leaching may address salinity where outlets and water quality permit;
a suitable calcium amendment such as gypsum may be needed for sodicity after diagnosis. No universal
dose or soil-depth specification is safe.

### 2. Waterlogging and capillary salinisation

Canal seepage, over-irrigation, flat relief and inadequate drainage can raise a water table. Capillary
rise and evaporation then transport and concentrate salts near the surface. Surface drainage manages
ponding/runoff; subsurface or vertical drainage acts on the water table. Pumping can reclaim a shallow
water table in one setting yet contribute to depletion in another—the desired hydraulic state decides.

### 3. Conservation by mechanism

Contour cultivation/bunds, vegetative strips, cover crops, terraces, check structures, gully-head works,
regulated grazing and afforestation are not interchangeable. Design depends on slope, soil, runoff,
sediment, outlet stability and maintenance. Detaining runoff may improve infiltration in some settings,
but groundwater recharge depends on vadose and aquifer properties; it is not an automatic benefit.

### 4. Current watershed context

**WDC-PMKSY 2.0** is the current central framework for watershed development in rainfed/degraded
landscapes. Use its current guidelines rather than historical Plan-era asset totals. Programme evidence
should distinguish treatment completed from measured changes in soil loss, moisture, recharge,
vegetation or livelihoods.

### Concept check

**Question:** A canal-command field develops white crusting. Is heavy leaching alone a sufficient remedy?

**Model answer:** No. First test whether the problem is salinity, sodicity or both and assess irrigation
water, water-table depth and drainage. Leaching saline soil needs suitable-quality water and a safe
outlet; without drainage, salts may return through capillary rise. Sodic structure may require an
appropriate calcium amendment before leaching. Irrigation/seepage management and monitoring are also
needed. A surface crust is the visible end of a water-and-salt transport system.

**Misconception to avoid:** Salinity and sodicity are not synonyms and do not share one universal treatment.

### Revision notes

- Diagnose salinity and sodicity with appropriate soil/water tests.
- Salinity = soluble-salt problem; sodicity = exchangeable-sodium/structure problem.
- Waterlogging reflects recharge/inflow exceeding drainage/outflow.
- Capillary rise plus evaporation can concentrate salts at the surface.
- Leaching requires suitable water and drainage.
- Gypsum is conditional on sodic-soil diagnosis; no universal dose applies.
- Surface and subsurface/vertical drainage act on different pathways.
- Conservation practices must match slope, soil, runoff, sediment and outlet.
- Infiltration improvement does not guarantee recharge of the pumped aquifer.
- WDC-PMKSY 2.0 is current watershed context; outcomes matter more than asset counts.

### Mains Practice 9

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** “The same tubewell can be a reclamation instrument and a depletion
instrument.” Explain.

**Model (about 150 words):** In a waterlogged command, pumping may lower a shallow water table, restore
the root zone and assist salt management when paired with drainage and safe disposal/use. In a stressed
aquifer, cumulative pumping can exceed annual extractable resource and draw down storage and heads.
The technology is unchanged; aquifer state, budget, water quality and objective differ. Therefore a
tubewell should be judged through water-level trends, recharge/inflow, abstraction, quality and impacts,
not labelled inherently beneficial or harmful.

## Lesson 10 — Slope stability: effective stress and factor of safety

Progress: 10 / 19  |  Stage: Core  |  Subtopic: Driving stress, shear strength, pore pressure and human modification

━━━ PRE-TEACH CHECKLIST ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Book context: G.C. Leong, local-scan pp.34–36, slump and slope-water examples; D.R. Khullar, printed
p.268 (local-scan p.151), mass-wasting context. Modern effective-stress mechanics controls the lesson.
CA search: “India slope stability pore pressure landslide official 2026”
CA found: terrain-specific GSI evidence is applied in Lessons 12–13.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

```text
SHEAR STRENGTH (effective-stress form)

τf = c′ + (σn - u) tan φ′

rain / leakage / blocked drainage -> u rises -> effective normal stress falls
cutting -> slope angle/toe support worsens
loading / saturation -> driving stress rises
roots / anchors / toe works -> resistance may rise

Factor of safety = available resistance / mobilised driving stress
```

*Caption: water mainly changes effective stress and weight; “lubrication” alone is incomplete.*

### 1. Driving and resisting components

Gravity supplies downslope driving stress. Slope steepening, toe removal, crest loading and increased
water weight can move a slope toward failure. Resistance comes from material cohesion, friction under
effective normal stress, intact rock bridges, root reinforcement and engineered support where relevant.
Factor of safety is a model ratio, not a directly observable universal threshold for a mapped region.

### 2. Why pore pressure matters

Pore-water pressure **u** subtracts from total normal stress in the friction term. Prolonged/intense rain,
leaking utilities, reservoir change or blocked drainage may therefore reduce strength even without new
excavation. Antecedent moisture matters because two storms of equal intensity can start from different
hydraulic states. Hydraulic response depends on permeability, layering, fractures and drainage.

### 3. Failure geometry

Rotational slumps commonly involve curved surfaces in relatively coherent soil/debris, but no single
permeable-over-clay arrangement is compulsory. Translational slides follow planar weaknesses; rock
falls detach along discontinuities after loss of support or weathering; flows involve greater internal
deformation. Field evidence—not name alone—must establish mechanism.

### 4. Western Ghats application

Deep weathered material, steep escarpments and intense monsoon/antecedent saturation can create adverse
conditions in some sectors. Quarrying, road cuts, building loads and drainage alteration may change the
same equation. No universal regolith depth, cohesion, friction angle or rainfall threshold is asserted.

### Concept check

**Question:** Two similar slopes receive the same storm; one has been cut at the toe and has blocked
drains. Why can it fail while the other remains stable?

**Model answer:** Toe cutting can steepen the effective geometry and remove support, while blocked
drainage raises pore pressure. Higher **u** lowers effective normal stress and frictional resistance;
saturation may also add weight. The modified slope may already have a factor of safety close to unity,
so the storm crosses the threshold. The unmodified slope retains greater resistance. Rain explains the
timing but the altered geometry and drainage explain the unequal response.

**Misconception to avoid:** Equal rainfall does not imply equal pore pressure or slope stability.

### Revision notes

- Shear strength: `τf = c′ + (σn − u) tan φ′`.
- Rising pore pressure lowers effective normal stress and frictional resistance.
- Saturation may also increase driving weight.
- Toe cutting removes support; crest loading adds driving stress.
- Factor of safety compares available resistance with mobilised driving stress.
- Antecedent moisture influences storm response.
- Rotational, translational, fall and flow mechanisms need field evidence.
- Clay layers can matter but are not compulsory for slumping.
- Vegetation effects depend on roots, depth, hydrology and decay.
- No universal Indian slope parameters or rainfall threshold apply.

### Mains Practice 10

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Explain why “rain lubricated the slope” is an inadequate landslide
mechanism.

**Model (about 150 words):** Rainfall can infiltrate and raise pore-water pressure, which reduces
effective normal stress and the frictional part of shear strength. Saturation may also add weight, and
surface runoff may erode a toe. The response depends on permeability, fractures, layering, antecedent
moisture and drainage. Thus rain may trigger failure, but slope geometry, material, discontinuities,
cutting and loading establish susceptibility. Effective-stress language identifies the governing terms;
“lubrication” hides them and cannot explain why neighbouring slopes respond differently.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

| Movement | Dominant kinematics | Common material description | Field clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fall | detachment and free fall/bounce | rock/debris | fresh scar and talus/run-out |
| Topple | forward rotation | blocks/columns | pivoting about a base |
| Slide | movement on a discrete surface | rock/debris/earth | scar and coherent displaced mass |
| Spread | extension and lateral displacement | coherent mass over weaker layer | fissures/lateral movement |
| Flow | distributed internal deformation | debris/earth/mud | lobate/channelised deposit |
| Creep | very slow persistent deformation | soil/debris/rock | tilted posts, bent trees, cracks |
| Complex | changes/combines modes | varied | sequence documented in field |

### 1. Names carry mechanisms

A “landslide” is not one movement type. Material and kinematics should be stated separately. Speed and
water content refine description but do not alone define all classes. Solifluction/gelifluction should be
reserved for slow flow of saturated near-surface material associated with seasonally frozen/periglacial
conditions; a warm monsoon debris flow is not solifluction.

### 2. Creep and event failure

Creep is slow deformation that can tilt trees, walls or utilities without a single catastrophic event.
It may precede faster movement but does not inevitably do so. Falls detach from steep faces along
joints/bedding after weathering, shaking, freeze-thaw or toe undercutting. Slides retain more coherence;
flows deform internally.

### 3. Liquefaction and susceptible materials

Liquefaction is a loss of effective stress/strength in saturated, loose, contractive granular material
during cyclic or rapid loading. Susceptibility is not restricted to one sand size and does not make every
saturated soil liquefiable; density, gradation, fines characteristics, drainage and loading matter.

### 4. Subsidence is separate

Subsidence is predominantly vertical ground lowering caused by mechanisms such as compaction,
dissolution/collapse, mining or fluid withdrawal. It should not be labelled a slow landslide unless a
slope-movement mechanism is established.

### Concept check

**Question:** Correct two descriptions: a warm Kerala monsoon debris flow called “solifluction,” and
slowly tilting poles called “a small landslide.”

**Model answer:** The Kerala event should be described from observed material and movement—for example,
a debris flow—rather than with a periglacial solifluction label. The tilting poles indicate creep: slow,
persistent deformation that may lack a discrete event or slip surface. Each correction changes the
implied mechanism, climate and evidence required, not merely the apparent speed.

**Misconception to avoid:** Movement names are not interchangeable severity labels.

### Revision notes

- Classify by movement and material; add speed/water qualifiers where evidenced.
- Falls detach; topples rotate; slides move on discrete surfaces.
- Flows deform internally; spreads extend laterally.
- Creep is slow deformation, not necessarily a small landslide event.
- Complex events can change movement type during run-out.
- Solifluction/gelifluction has a periglacial/frozen-ground context.
- Liquefaction requires susceptible saturated contractive material and loading.
- Grain size alone does not determine liquefaction.
- Subsidence requires a separate vertical-deformation diagnosis.
- Use scar, deposit, kinematics and material evidence to name events.

### Mains Practice 11

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why is a material–movement classification superior to a simple
slow/rapid or dry/wet division of mass movement?

**Model (about 150 words):** Speed and water content describe state but may combine unlike mechanisms.
A material–movement scheme distinguishes detachment/fall, rotation/topple, discrete-surface slide,
lateral spread, internally deforming flow and creep, while identifying rock, debris or earth. The name
therefore predicts field evidence and suitable analysis. A dry rock fall and saturated debris flow may
both be rapid but require different controls; creep and subsidence may both be slow but are not the same
process. Water and velocity remain useful qualifiers after mechanism is established.

*Caption: the trigger may explain timing; susceptibility explains why failure was possible.*

### 1. Preparatory and triggering factors

Preparatory controls include weak/weathered material, adverse discontinuities, steep/over-steepened
slopes, old deposits, toe erosion, loading, vegetation/drainage change and poor engineering. Triggers
may include intense/prolonged rain, earthquakes, rapid drawdown, sudden toe loss or other short-term
changes. The distinction is analytical, not absolute: one factor may prepare and trigger at different
timescales.

### 2. Human modification

Road cuts, quarrying, excavation, spoil dumping, construction loads, leaking services and blocked
natural drainage can change driving/resisting terms. Do not use one universal hill-road debris volume or
assume every project has the same effect. Site geometry, material, drainage and design determine impact.

### 3. Susceptibility, hazard and risk

- **Susceptibility:** relative spatial propensity based on terrain conditions.
- **Hazard:** probability/intensity of damaging movement over a stated period/area.
- **Exposure:** people/assets present.
- **Vulnerability:** their propensity to suffer loss.
- **Risk:** interaction of hazard, exposure and vulnerability; a conceptual relationship, not a literal
  universal multiplication of incomparable scores.

### 4. Joshimath method

The USDMA repository contains agency-specific Joshimath reports. Attribute a statement to the named
report and method; do not merge documents into a synthetic consensus or invent a fixed count of causes.
A defensible analysis tests geological/legacy material, drainage and subsurface water, construction and
loading, slope/river geometry and deformation evidence together. “Subsidence” should not automatically
be relabelled a landslide.

### 5. Incident reports as bounded evidence

GSI’s June 2026 Chanmari West preliminary assessment is useful as a dated site investigation, not as a
national causal template. Preliminary reports may be revised and do not establish one cause for all
Mizoram or north-eastern slopes.

### Concept check

**Question:** A committee concludes that a fatal slide was “caused by exceptional rain.” What must a
defensible attribution add?

**Model answer:** It must establish susceptible material/structure, slope geometry, antecedent moisture,
drainage and human modification; show how rainfall changed pore pressure, weight or toe conditions; and
rule or qualify competing mechanisms. Compare neighbouring slopes and use site observations, movement
records and hydrological/rainfall evidence. Rain may explain timing, but the terrain and modifications
explain why that slope crossed failure while others did not.

**Misconception to avoid:** Trigger magnitude alone does not establish causal weight.

### Revision notes

- Separate preparatory controls from short-term triggers.
- Material, structure, morphology and antecedent water create susceptibility.
- Rain can change pore pressure, weight and erosion but is not a complete cause.
- Human cutting, loading, spoil and drainage alteration act through identifiable mechanics.
- No universal hill-road debris volume is valid.
- Susceptibility, hazard, exposure, vulnerability and risk are distinct.
- Risk is an interaction, not a compulsory arithmetic product of arbitrary scores.
- Subsidence needs its own mechanism before landslide terminology is used.
- Joshimath findings must retain report/agency attribution.
- Incident reports are site- and date-bounded, especially when preliminary.

### Mains Practice 12

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** “A trigger explains when; terrain history explains why.” Critically
examine in landslide attribution.

**Model (about 150 words):** Intense rain, shaking or rapid drawdown may provide the final change that
initiates movement, so trigger evidence is essential for timing and warning. Yet failure also requires
susceptible material, discontinuities, slope geometry and antecedent conditions, often altered by
cutting, loading or drainage. Equal rainfall can therefore produce unequal outcomes. The aphorism is
useful but not absolute: some triggers also reshape terrain, and causal roles vary by timescale. A sound
attribution combines site geology, morphology, hydrology, modification, movement evidence and trigger
data and states uncertainty.

A slide may block a river and create an unstable debris dam, damage roads/railways and utilities,

single-cause event narratives are excluded. For incident examples, attribute findings to the named
agency report; the June 2026 GSI Chanmari West report is a dated assessment, not a national template.

### 5. Physical response matched to mechanism

- Drainage reduces pore pressure; toe/retaining works add resistance where appropriately designed.
- Land-use control avoids loading, toe cutting and blocked drainage in susceptible terrain.
- Vegetation may reduce erosion and reinforce shallow soil, but does not stabilise every deep failure.
- Monitoring and rainfall thresholds add time-sensitive evidence; site decisions need fine-scale
  investigation and competent authority.

**Model answer:** No. A 1:50,000 map screens regional terrain propensity; it does not supply event time,

**Misconception to avoid:** A map’s existence does not authorise its use at every scale or for every variable.

```text
GROUNDWATER PROPERTY MATRIX

                    Lower conductivity            Higher conductivity
Higher porosity     clay: stores much, yields      connected sand/gravel:
                    slowly; high retention         storage + transmission
Lower porosity      fresh massive crystalline      connected fractures/joints:
```

*Caption: well performance depends on connected voids, scale and boundary conditions—not saturation alone.*

### 1. Core properties

- **Porosity** is the fraction of void space.
- **Hydraulic conductivity** describes ease of flow through a saturated material under a gradient.
- **Specific yield** is the drainable fraction; **specific retention** remains held after gravity drainage.
- An **aquifer** stores and transmits usable groundwater; an **aquitard/confining unit** transmits much
  more slowly. Avoid perfectly impermeable aquiclude/aquifuge boxes in field reasoning.

Clay can have high porosity but low conductivity and specific yield. Fresh granite has little primary
porosity, yet weathered or connected fractured zones can store/transmit water. These are tendencies,
not universal hand-specimen rules.

### 2. Saturated and unsaturated zones

The water table is the surface where pore-water pressure equals atmospheric pressure in an unconfined
system. Above it, the unsaturated zone can contain soil water and a capillary fringe; below it, connected
voids are saturated. In confined systems use the **potentiometric surface**—the hydraulic-head level to
which water rises in a tightly cased well. It is a measurable head surface, not “imaginary water.”

### 3. Aquifer heterogeneity and anisotropy

Conductivity varies with grain size/sorting, fractures, bedding, weathering and flow-unit contacts and
can differ by direction and scale. A local fracture can give high bore-well yield without making the
entire rock mass a productive aquifer. Conversely, high porosity without connectivity may yield poorly.
Groundwater is not literally present everywhere at usable depth or quality.

### 4. India application

Peninsular hard-rock aquifers commonly rely on weathered zones and connected fractures. Deccan basalt
varies among compact flow interiors, interflow/vesicular zones, fractures, dykes, weathered horizons and
local sedimentary interbeds. Do not model the Deccan as one uniform impermeable slab.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why can saturated clay yield little water while a nearby fractured granite bore succeeds?

**Model answer:** Saturation and porosity do not establish conductivity or drainable yield. Clay has
small pores and strong retention, so flow to a well may be slow. Fresh granite has little primary pore
space, but a bore that intersects connected weathered or fractured zones can tap secondary storage and
transmission. Compare logs, fracture connectivity, pumping tests and heads rather than inferring yield
from rock name or saturation alone.

**Misconception to avoid:** High porosity does not necessarily mean high well yield.

### Revision notes

- Porosity measures void fraction; conductivity measures ease of flow.
- Specific yield is drainable water; specific retention remains held.
- Clay often has high porosity but low conductivity/yield—not zero flow universally.
- Fresh crystalline rock has low primary porosity; weathering/fractures create secondary pathways.
- Aquifers yield usable water; aquitards transmit slowly.
- Water table applies to unconfined systems; potentiometric surface represents confined head.
- Unsaturated zones may contain water and a capillary fringe.
- Hydraulic properties are scale-dependent and may be anisotropic.
- Groundwater presence, yield and quality vary spatially.
- Deccan basalt performance follows flow-unit architecture, structures and weathering.

### Mains Practice 14

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** “In hard-rock groundwater, the rock mass matters more than the hand
specimen.” Explain.

**Model (about 150 words):** A hand specimen may show little primary pore space, but the rock mass can
contain weathered zones, joints, faults and connected contacts that store and transmit groundwater.
Thus neighbouring wells in granite or basalt may differ sharply. In Deccan basalt, compact interiors,
interflow/vesicular horizons, fractures, dykes and weathering create heterogeneous hydraulic units.
Lithology still matters because it influences weathering and fracture behaviour, but it cannot alone
predict yield. Aquifer mapping, bore logs, pumping tests, water levels and quality are required before
selecting abstraction or recharge measures.

```text
GROUNDWATER HEAD AND DISCHARGE

                                      |
                           confinement changes head response

Unconfined well: standing level approximates water table
Confined well: standing level follows potentiometric surface
```

*Caption: springs and artesian flow are expressions of hydraulic gradients, not compulsory landforms.*

### 1. Springs

A spring is natural groundwater discharge at the land surface. It may reflect intersection of a water
table, a perched zone, a fracture or fault, a lithological contact, a conduit/resurgence or confined
head. Names such as scarp-foot, dip-slope, joint, dyke/barrier and resurgence describe geometry; they do
not imply that every spring is simply the regional water table meeting the surface.

The Bhabar–Tarai transition illustrates focused loss and re-emergence at a regional permeability
contrast, but discharge and exact pathways must be established locally.

### 2. Artesian conditions

An artesian aquifer is confined and has sufficient hydraulic head for water in a well to rise above the
top of the aquifer. A flowing artesian well occurs only where that potentiometric level lies above local
ground. A syncline, chalk/limestone, two clay beds and edge-only recharge are not compulsory. Confining
units may leak, and recharge can occur through outcrop, boundaries, overlying/underlying units or
managed wells. Artesian water may still be saline, hot or contaminated.

### 3. Groundwater budget

For a stated area and period:

```text
Recharge + lateral inflow - natural discharge - abstraction = change in groundwater storage
```

Terms and methods depend on the assessment framework. In CGWB dynamic assessment, stage of extraction
is annual extraction divided by annual extractable groundwater resource. “Over-exploited” is a dated
assessment-unit category, not shorthand for every negative water balance and not proof about water
quality. Administrative units may cross aquifer boundaries; one annual result may conceal seasonal or
long-term storage decline.

### 4. Human use and depletion

A well does not automatically deplete groundwater. Effects depend on cumulative abstraction, recharge,
lateral flow, capture of natural discharge and storage response. Managed recharge is one possible
input, but it cannot guarantee balance if abstraction expands or water quality is unsuitable.

### Concept check

**Question:** A block is categorised as not over-exploited. Why might an aquifer within it still require
caution?

**Model answer:** The category is a dated result for an assessment unit under a stated methodology.
Administrative boundaries may cross aquifers and aggregate unlike conditions. Annual accounting may
conceal seasonal stress, long-term storage decline or local cones of depression, and quantity status does
not establish water quality. Review aquifer geometry, time-series heads/storage, abstraction, recharge
and contaminants before making a local conclusion.

**Misconception to avoid:** An assessment-unit category is not a complete physical description of each aquifer.

### Revision notes

- Springs are groundwater-discharge points controlled by varied hydraulic/geological settings.
- Perched, fracture, contact and conduit springs need not mark the regional water table.
- Artesian = confined system with head; flowing artesian = head above local ground.
- No particular syncline, rock pair or edge-only recharge geometry is compulsory.
- Potentiometric surface represents hydraulic head, not a body of water.
- Artesian pressure does not establish usable quality.
- A groundwater budget needs area, period, boundary and storage change.
- CGWB stage of extraction uses extraction divided by annual extractable resource.
- Administrative assessment units may differ from aquifer boundaries.
- Wells do not inherently deplete groundwater; cumulative system response decides.

### Mains Practice 15

**Model (about 150 words):** A groundwater budget specifies an area, period and boundary and includes

### 1. Recharge is a hydrogeological intervention

Managed aquifer recharge (MAR) intentionally adds water to an aquifer for later recovery or
environmental benefit. It works only where source water is available and acceptable, unsaturated
storage/hydraulic capacity exists, and the receiving aquifer is suitable. A high water table, swelling
clay, contaminated runoff or poor outlet conditions can make a standard structure ineffective or
harmful.

### 2. Match intervention to mechanism

| Setting/problem | Candidate approach | Essential checks |
|---|---|---|
| Permeable surface over unconfined aquifer | spreading basin/percolation structure | infiltration rate, sediment, water table |
| Thick low-permeability cover over receptive aquifer | shaft/well recharge | source-water quality, pretreatment, head, clogging |
| Seasonal drainage and eroding catchment | check structure/watershed treatment | design flow, sediment, stability, downstream needs |
| Urban roof runoff | storage and/or recharge | first flush, roof quality, sewer separation, maintenance |
| Coastal aquifer | cautious recharge/demand management | head and salinity monitoring; avoid contaminant migration |

Direct injection deserves stricter controls because it bypasses part of the soil’s filtration function.
Recharge can raise levels yet fail to produce net recovery if pumping simultaneously increases.

### 3. Urban harvesting — verified 2018 GS-I Q14

The question asks how effective urban rainwater harvesting can be. Keep the prompt answer-neutral;
do not embed an official solution. A sound framework tests: rainfall/catchment yield, storage versus
recharge choice, aquifer capacity, runoff quality, first-flush/filters, maintenance, monitoring, building
and neighbourhood scale, and demand management. “Mandate structures” alone is not an effectiveness
argument.

### 4. Governance evidence

- **NAQUIM 2.0** supports issue-focused aquifer characterisation and management plans.
- **Atal Bhujal Yojana** used community water budgets and Gram-Panchayat Water Security Plans in
  selected water-stressed areas. Programme results are dated evidence, not universal proof of recharge.
- **WDC-PMKSY 2.0** is the current central watershed framework relevant to rainfed/degraded land and
  soil-water conservation. It does not replace aquifer diagnosis.

### 5. Monitoring closes the loop

Track pre/post-monsoon water levels, recharge volumes, recovery, turbidity/conductivity and relevant
contaminants, clogging, nearby waterlogging and abstraction. A rising level immediately after recharge
is not sufficient evidence of sustainable gain. Compare against baseline and a meaningful time window.

### Concept check

**Question:** A city orders identical recharge pits for every building. Identify the scientific flaw and
replace the rule.

**Model answer:** The order assumes uniform runoff, soil, vadose-zone thickness, aquifer capacity and
water quality. Some sites may contain clay, shallow groundwater, contaminated runoff or no useful
hydraulic connection. Replace the dimension rule with a performance process: estimate clean source
water; map aquifer and unsaturated storage; choose storage, spreading, shaft or well accordingly;
provide first-flush and pretreatment; monitor heads, quality and clogging; maintain the system; and
control abstraction. Technical approval should be site- or hydrogeological-zone specific.

**Misconception to avoid:** Structure dimensions are not a substitute for site diagnosis.

### Revision notes

- MAR is intentional recharge, not any water-conservation structure.
- Site diagnosis precedes dimensions and structure choice.
- Aquifer capacity, vadose zone, source yield and quality are core filters.
- Direct injection needs stronger pretreatment and monitoring.
- Sediment and biological/chemical clogging can destroy performance.
- Urban systems require first-flush, clean drainage and maintenance.
- Recharge without demand management may not reverse decline.
- Coastal MAR requires salinity/head evidence.
- NAQUIM 2.0, Atal Jal and WDC-PMKSY 2.0 serve different functions.
- 2018 GS-I Q14 remains answer-neutral.

### Mains Practice 18

**Question (10 marks, 150 words):** Why should India replace structure-counting with aquifer-based
performance monitoring in groundwater recharge programmes?

**Model (about 150 words):** Counting pits or check structures measures construction, not recharge.
Performance varies with rainfall yield, sediment, source-water quality, vadose-zone thickness, aquifer
capacity, hydraulic connection and abstraction. A standard structure may clog, overflow, contaminate an
aquifer or worsen waterlogging. Aquifer-based planning uses NAQUIM-style characterisation to select
surface spreading, storage, shafts or wells and requires first-flush, pretreatment and maintenance.
Monitoring should compare baseline and post-intervention heads, recharge/recovery, quality, clogging and
nearby impacts over suitable seasons. Demand must also be budgeted, because added recharge can be
cancelled by added pumping. Thus programme success is a sustained, safe aquifer response—not an asset
count.

### Concept check

**Question:** Why is “heavy rain caused the landslide” usually an incomplete answer?

**Model answer:** Rain may explain timing by increasing pore-water pressure, reducing effective stress,

structure, weathered or unconsolidated cover, slope form, antecedent saturation, drainage and cutting or
loading determine whether the rainfall becomes critical. Compare those preparatory controls before
assigning causation. The correct chain is terrain susceptibility plus antecedent condition plus trigger,
not trigger alone.

**Misconception to avoid:** Temporal coincidence does not establish a complete terrain-causation chain.

**Question (15 marks, 150 words):** Differentiate the causes of landslides in the Himalayan region and

alone is not a complete cause. Terrain-specific material, structure, morphology, antecedent water and
human modification must be evaluated together, using susceptibility maps and site investigation at the
appropriate scale.

# CUMULATIVE CONCEPT CHECKS

### Cumulative check 1 — regolith to salinity

**Question:** Connect weathering to canal-command salinity without assuming that every soil follows the
same pathway.

**Model answer:** Weathering produces regolith; organisms, climate, relief, parent material and time can
organise part of it into soil. In a poorly drained irrigated command, seepage may raise the water table.
Capillary rise then carries dissolved salts upward, and evaporation concentrates them near the surface.
The sequence is conditional: sufficient salts, shallow groundwater, capillary continuity, evaporation
and inadequate drainage are required. Salinity differs from sodicity, so diagnosis must precede leaching,
drainage or calcium amendment.

**Misconception to avoid:** A surface salt crust is not proof that canal seepage is the only cause.

### Cumulative check 2 — storm and unequal slope response

**Question:** One storm crosses a district, but only some slopes fail. Explain.

**Model answer:** Rain can add weight and raise pore pressure, reducing effective stress and shear
strength. Failure nevertheless depends on pre-existing susceptibility: material, discontinuities,
weathering, slope geometry, toe support, drainage, antecedent saturation and human cutting/loading.
Slopes with factors of safety near unity may cross the threshold; stronger slopes do not. Falls, slides,
flows and creep also reflect different materials and movement mechanisms. Thus rainfall may explain
when an event occurred, but site conditions explain why that slope could fail.

**Misconception to avoid:** Uniform rainfall cannot justify a uniform landslide-hazard label.

### Cumulative check 3 — productive aquifer, severe stress

**Question:** Why can a productive aquifer become severely stressed?

**Model answer:** High storage and transmissivity make wells reliable and inexpensive, enabling dense
abstraction. If cumulative pumping exceeds the dated extractable resource, storage and heads decline
even in a productive alluvial aquifer. Crop pattern, irrigation efficiency, energy/incentives and dry-
season demand shape the imbalance. Large storage may delay visible failure rather than prevent it.
Diagnosis requires assessment-unit category, aquifer boundaries, water-level/storage trends and quality;
a national average cannot settle the case.

**Misconception to avoid:** Resource richness is not immunity from over-abstraction.

### Cumulative check 4 — physical process and governance choice

**Question:** Where does physical geography constrain policy, and where does it stop deciding outcomes?

**Model answer:** Physical geography identifies feasible pathways: rock and weathering influence soils
and aquifers; effective stress explains slope response; aquifer geometry limits recharge and pumping.
It can rule out unsuitable measures such as standard recharge pits on every site. It cannot by itself
predict maintenance, crop choice, exposure, compliance or distributional effects. Sound policy therefore
combines physical diagnosis with social and institutional evidence, while monitoring tests whether the
chosen intervention changes the intended state safely.

**Misconception to avoid:** Recognising human choice does not make terrain and aquifer constraints optional.

# ORIGINAL 10-, 15- AND 20-MARK MAINS MODEL PRACTICE

## Q1 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Distinguish weathering, erosion and mass movement and show one Indian linkage among them.

**Model answer:** Weathering is in-situ physical breakdown or chemical alteration of rock. Erosion is
detachment and removal by an agent such as running water, wind, ice or waves. Mass movement is
downslope movement under gravity; water may modify weight and pore pressure but need not be the
transporting agent. In a Western Ghats slope, deep chemical weathering may create regolith; intense
rain and altered drainage can raise pore pressure; a slide then transfers material downslope, after which
streams erode and transport the debris. The sequence is possible, not compulsory: each process can
occur without completing a fixed denudation cycle.

## Q2 — 10 marks, 150 words

**Question:** Why are uniform artificial-recharge specifications scientifically unsafe?

**Model answer:** Recharge performance varies with catchment yield, source-water quality, soil and
vadose-zone properties, aquifer capacity, hydraulic connection, water-table depth and abstraction.
Identical pits may clog in sediment-rich runoff, fail over clay, worsen waterlogging where storage is
small, or convey contaminants downward. Site diagnosis should determine whether storage, surface
spreading, shafts or wells are appropriate and specify first-flush, pretreatment, maintenance and
monitoring. Success is a sustained, safe aquifer response—not the number of structures built.

## Q3 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Compare the causes of landslides in the Himalaya and Western Ghats.

**Model answer:** The common physics is loss of stability when driving stress exceeds shear resistance,
often after pore pressure rises. The regional inputs differ. Himalayan belts are young, tectonically
active, deformed and deeply incised; fractured rock, thrust/shear zones, steep relief, unconsolidated
legacy deposits and locally snowmelt/freeze-thaw combine with road cuts, loading, toe erosion and
drainage disruption. The Western Ghats are an older escarpment/plateau margin where deep chemical
weathering, regolith or lateritic cover, joints and intense monsoon/antecedent saturation are prominent;
quarrying, construction, vegetation change and blocked drainage can amplify failure. Neither region is
uniform and rainfall alone is not a complete cause. Comparison should use material history, structure,
morphology, water input and human modification, followed by terrain-specific mapping, drainage and
slope measures.

## Q4 — 15 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Explain India’s groundwater stress through aquifer type, demand and quality.

**Model answer:** Aquifer type controls storage and transmission: alluvial systems may be thick and
productive; crystalline rocks depend on weathered/fractured zones; Deccan basalt varies by flow units,
fractures and weathering; mountain communities often use springs; coastal aquifers face salinity risks.
Demand then determines pressure. Irrigation, crop pattern, urban and industrial abstraction can exceed
annual extractable resource and draw down storage. GWRA-2025 reports a national extraction stage of
60.63%, but its 6,762 assessment units show strong spatial contrast, so the national ratio is not a local
diagnosis. Quantity and quality are separate: arsenic, fluoride, nitrate, salinity and other contaminants
need place-, depth- and year-specific monitoring. Management must combine aquifer mapping, demand
budgets, quality protection, appropriate recharge and time-series monitoring.

## Q5 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** “Soil degradation, landslides and groundwater decline are connected expressions of land-
water mismanagement.” Discuss.

**Model answer:** The processes share a regolith–slope–water system. Vegetation loss, cultivation or
construction can expose soil, accelerate runoff and erosion and reduce infiltration. Toe cutting,
loading and poor drainage can weaken slopes; intense rain may then trigger failure. Canal seepage and
inadequate drainage may raise water tables and salinise soils, whereas heavy pumping may lower heads,
reduce well reliability and alter baseflow. Yet the links are conditional, not universal: conservation
does not automatically recharge an aquifer, erosion does not always cause landslides, and groundwater
stress also depends on demand and aquifer properties. Responses must match mechanism and scale—soil
cover and contour/watershed treatment, slope drainage/support and land-use control, aquifer-based water
budgets, demand management, safe managed recharge and monitoring. Current evidence should use GSI/
NRSC terrain products and CGWB aquifer/resource/quality products rather than obsolete national zones.

## Q6 — 20 marks, 250 words

**Question:** Evaluate the proposition that maps and national statistics improve geographical answers
only when their scale and method are stated.

**Model answer:** Maps and statistics are modelled observations, not self-explanatory facts. GSI’s
1:50,000 susceptibility baseline identifies broad relative propensity; 1:10,000 meso-scale and finer
than 1:2,000 micro-scale work serve progressively more detailed decisions. None alone predicts event
time or replaces site investigation. NRSC’s Landslide Atlas supports exposure screening, not a claim
that every mapped district is equally hazardous. Similarly, GWRA-2025’s national stage of extraction
and assessment-unit categories screen spatial stress but do not diagnose an individual aquifer, well or
quality problem. Good answers therefore state institution, product, year, scale/denominator and the
inference permitted. They avoid drawing exact boundaries from schematic maps, comparing differently
defined historical percentages, or converting susceptibility into forecast. Properly qualified evidence
sharpens arguments; unqualified evidence creates false precision.
